Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges a ruling from the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board denying registration of its trademarks. As the Board made errors regarding its evaluation of two factors under the likelihood-of-confusion framework, we partially affirm, partially vacate, and remand the Board’s decision.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. The company owns Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark as used for credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve’s method of offering credit card services with the ASPIRE mark involves partnering with a bank, which in turn issues credit cards and corresponding accounts bearing the ASPIRE brand to customers. J.A. 6930. These accounts are maintained by CC Serve and its related entities. Id. Apex Bank is a retail banking institution incorporated in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. Operating 18 branches, Apex offers products including individual checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgage loans, as well as both consumer and business loans. Id. Apex does not provide credit card services. Id. Apex intends to introduce an online bank under a new brand identity, using the ASPIRE BANK word and design trademarks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, specifying “banking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve filed a letter of protest alleging that Apex’s proposed marks were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s own mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this protest, the examining attorney permitted the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to proceed to publication, and the marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board granted CC Serve's opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that there was a probable likelihood of consumer confusion between the marks. J.A. 63. Apex appealed from this decision. Our authority to hear the appeal arises under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We examine the Board’s legal determinations without deference, and review its factual findings to ensure they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). In trademark opposition proceedings brought under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, registration must be denied when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The question of whether a likelihood of confusion exists constitutes a legal issue grounded upon factual findings as established by the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to analyze every one of the DuPont factors, but only those that are pertinent and present in the record. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in its evaluation of the likelihood of confusion, particularly concerning its treatment of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board found that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position and determined that the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We examine each of these factors in sequence.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how similar the goods or services offered by each party are. The goods or services do not have to be the same; the key inquiry is whether "the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source." Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). Here, the Board compared the credit card services provided by CC Serve with the banking and financing services offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Since "credit card services" had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board began by interpreting the scope of these services. The Board found that “credit card services” includes "issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. It concluded that, because definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance" include extending credit or supplying funds through the issuance of credit cards, the services provided by Apex and CC Serve are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also reviewed third-party registrations that encompass both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services to bolster its finding that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Due to the significant similarity between the services in question, the Board found that the second DuPont factor strongly supports a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends the services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” highlighting that CC Serve collaborates with banks to operate credit card programs rather than offering banking services itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We find Apex’s position unpersuasive. The Board gave thorough consideration to each party’s service descriptions, and its determination that the services are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. We uphold the Board’s conclusion regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. According to Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” In a marketplace dense with similar marks, the rationale is that consumers become more skilled in distinguishing among such marks and thus confusion is less likely. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). When evidence suggests that consumers have learned to differentiate between similar marks, this tends to demonstrate limited commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Apex presented multiple exhibits before the Board, documenting instances of third-party marks incorporating the term “Aspire”. J.A. 40–46. These marks used “Aspire” in relation to credit card services and the broader financial services field. The Board determined that, because the services offered by Apex and CC Serve overlap, the proper audience consists of “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Accordingly, the Board considered only those marks used in connection with credit card services and considered marks associated with other services to be “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board found evidence of nine Aspire-related marks for credit card services; it deemed this amount insufficient to establish the kind of “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use that would evidence weakness, citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2 (noting “voluminous evidence” of paw print design element use and highlighting fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”) and Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (referring to 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). Thus, the Board found that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and determined CC Serve’s mark warrants “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

Apex, on appeal, argues that the Board erred by restricting the relevant public to consumers of credit card services and should have considered the additional 42 third-party Aspire and Aspire-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board committed a legal error in its analysis. The sixth DuPont factor obliges the Board to assess similar marks for similar goods and services. When analyzing the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board recognized that the parties’ services are highly similar, and consequently found the second factor weighed heavily in favor of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. But in considering the sixth Dupont factor, the Board confined its analysis to marks associated only with credit card services, excluding those connected to other banking and financial services. This was erroneous. Our precedent holds that the sixth DuPont factor requires only similarity, not identity, of goods. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. The Board’s construction of “similarity” was thus too restrictive here. Since the Board already determined, in its analysis of the second DuPont factor, that the services at issue are highly similar—indeed, at least partially legally identical—J.A. 27–28, it should have adopted the same breadth of scope when assessing similarity under the other DuPont factors. We find no justification for applying a narrower or heightened legal standard for the sixth DuPont factor. Therefore, we vacate the Board’s determination regarding the sixth DuPont factor and remand for further consideration of the scope of third-party marks appropriate for review in light of its finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor examines how the marks compare or differ overall in terms of their look, sound, meaning, and the commercial impression they make. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This assessment centers on the general commercial impression the marks produce and the likelihood that consumers might be confused about the origin of the services associated with the respective marks. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the commercial impression is critical to the evaluation under the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s findings in this regard, as a new analysis of the sixth DuPont factor could yield a different conclusion regarding the mark’s commercial strength or weakness, thereby impacting the overall commercial impression. As a result, we remand for the Board to reassess the first factor as well.

After reviewing the parties’ other arguments, we find them unconvincing. We uphold the Board’s assessment on the second DuPont factor because there was no error in its legal reasoning and substantial evidence backs the Board’s determination that the services offered by the parties are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions concerning DuPont factors six and one and remand so the Board may reassess both the number and type of similar marks used for related goods, as well as the appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression of the marks, considering its determination that the services in question are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED

Costs to appellant.
