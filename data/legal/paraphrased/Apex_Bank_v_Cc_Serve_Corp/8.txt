Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s denial of registration for its marks. As the Board made errors concerning two factors in the likelihood-of-confusion assessment, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which it uses for credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve’s credit card offerings under the ASPIRE mark are provided through partnerships with banks, where the bank issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and accounts to consumers, and CC Serve and its affiliates handle account servicing. J.A. 6930. Apex Bank, a retail bank incorporated in Tennessee, operates 18 branch locations and provides personal checking and savings accounts, business checking, home mortgage loans, as well as consumer and business loans. J.A. 7029. Apex Bank does not issue credit cards. Id. Apex intends to establish an online bank under a separate brand, using the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use trademark applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for use with “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While these applications were pending, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, contending that Apex’s marks were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s existing mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the examining attorney authorized the publication of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, and the marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), determining that there was a probable risk of consumer confusion between the marks. J.A. 63. Apex appealed this decision. Our court possesses jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

We examine the Board’s legal determinations anew, while we review its factual determinations to ensure they are supported by substantial evidence. M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence means “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition must result in denial of registration when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). “Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, predicated upon factual findings, specifically those arising from consideration of the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors constitutes a factual inquiry, and we assess findings on those factors for substantial evidence if challenged on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to analyze every DuPont factor, but only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

On appeal, Apex contends that the Board made mistakes in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its treatment of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board found that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will consider each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor examines the degree of similarity between the goods and/or services offered by the parties. The services at issue do not need to be identical; rather, the relevant inquiry is whether “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [whether] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board analyzed the likeness between credit card services provided by CC Serve and the banking and financing services offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Because the “credit card services” entry had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual following CC Serve’s registration, the Board initially had to interpret the scope of the specified services. It concluded that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board found that dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” encompass the extension of credit or provision of funds through issuing credit cards, and therefore, that the services of Apex and CC Serve are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Additionally, the Board considered third-party registrations that include both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services as further support that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. The Board ultimately determined that, due to the strong similarity between the services of the parties, the second DuPont factor strongly favored a likelihood of confusion finding. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the parties’ services “are not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve collaborates with banks to administer credit card programs and “not providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are unpersuaded by Apex’s contention. The Board thoroughly examined the parties’ service descriptions, and substantial evidence backs the Board’s conclusion that their services are highly similar. Accordingly, we uphold the Board’s determination on the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor addresses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As explained in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” When a marketplace features numerous similar marks, the assumption is that consumers become adept at distinguishing among them, thereby reducing the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence indicating that consumers have learned to differentiate between such marks tends to show limited commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex presented to the Board multiple exhibits evidencing third-party use of “Aspire” marks, as documented at J.A. 40–46. These marks involved the term “Aspire” in association with credit card-related services and more generally within the financial services sector. The Board determined that due to the overlapping nature of the services offered by Apex and CC Serve, the correct relevant public was defined as “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Accordingly, the Board concentrated exclusively on marks designated for credit card services, disregarding marks that used Aspire for other types of services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. Ultimately, the Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks pertaining to credit card services, concluding that this number fell short of the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use sufficient to establish weakness as found in prior cases. J.A. 47 (referencing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2, which discussed “voluminous evidence” regarding registration and use of paw print designs and highlighted fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”; Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (considering 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”)). The Board concluded that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and thus determined that CC Serve’s mark deserves the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred by confining the relevant public to only consumers of credit card services, asserting that the 42 other third-party Aspire and Aspire-formative marks should have been considered as well. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s approach was legally incorrect. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to evaluate similar marks for similar goods and services. In the course of considering the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board determined that the services at issue are highly similar, resulting in a finding that this factor weighed heavily in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. However, when the Board evaluated the sixth DuPont factor, it limited its consideration to marks relating solely to credit card services and excluded those pertaining to other banking and financial services, which constitutes error. Precedent establishes that the sixth DuPont factor does not require the goods to be identical, but rather similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this case, we conclude that the Board construed similarity too restrictively. Having already factually determined in analyzing the second DuPont factor that the services are highly similar—indeed, in part, legally identical—the Board should have adopted the same scope of similarity under the sixth DuPont factor and other relevant factors. We find no basis for adopting a distinct and more rigorous standard of similarity for purposes of the sixth DuPont factor. C

The initial DuPont factor examines the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the marks’ overall commercial impression and assesses whether there is a likelihood of confusion regarding the source of the services associated with the respective marks. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Given that commercial impression is central to the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s analysis of this factor, as a reassessment of the sixth DuPont factor could alter the perceived commercial strength or weakness of the mark and thereby impact the commercial impression as a whole. Accordingly, we also remand for further consideration of the first DuPont factor.

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s analysis concerning the second DuPont factor, as the Board applied the correct legal standard and there is substantial evidence to support its conclusion that the services offered by the parties are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations regarding DuPont factors six and one, and we remand the case so that the Board may reassess the number and type of similar marks used on comparable goods, as well as the visual, phonetic, connotative, and commercial similarities between the marks, taking into account its determination that the services at issue are highly similar. AFFIRMED-IN-PART, VACATED-IN-PART, AND

REMANDED
