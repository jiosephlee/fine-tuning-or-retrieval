Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges a ruling by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board that denied registration of its marks. Since the Board made errors regarding two factors in the likelihood-of-confusion assessment, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand the case.

I

CC Serve is a business that provides credit card services to clients. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which it uses for its credit card offerings. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998 and claims priority from October 17, 1996. Id. In connection with the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve collaborates with a bank, which then issues credit cards and opens associated accounts under the ASPIRE brand for customers. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its related entities are responsible for servicing these accounts. Id. Apex Bank, which is incorporated in Tennessee as a retail bank, has a network of 18 branches and provides a variety of products including personal checking and savings accounts, business checking, home mortgages, as well as consumer and commercial loans. J.A. 7029. Apex does not currently offer credit cards. Id. Apex’s plans include launching an internet-based bank under a new brand, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for use in “banking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While these applications were under examination, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, claiming that Apex's proposed marks were likely to be confused with its own. J.A. 4576. Despite the protest, the examining attorney approved both the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, and these marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that consumer confusion between the marks was probable. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal. Our jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We evaluate the Board’s legal determinations de novo, and its factual findings under the substantial evidence standard.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence consists of “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark registration must be refused where “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The determination of likelihood of confusion constitutes a legal issue, founded upon subsidiary factual findings under the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors involves a factual question, which we review for substantial evidence if challenged on appeal. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to analyze all DuPont factors, but rather only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in its evaluation of the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its consideration of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex, while the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We examine each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how similar the goods and/or services offered by the parties are. It is not necessary for the services to be the same—what must be shown is that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board evaluated the comparability between credit card services provided by CC Serve and the banking and financing services offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Noting that “credit card services” had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first clarified the scope of the services as identified. According to the Board, “credit card services” covers activities such as “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board then found that, since the dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include extending credit and making funds available through credit card issuance, there is a partial legal identity between Apex and CC Serve’s offerings. J.A. 27–28. To reinforce its determination that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark,” the Board also reviewed third-party registrations covering both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services. J.A. 33–34. Consequently, the Board held that, given the significant similarity between the services, the second DuPont factor favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve is working with banks to handle credit card programs rather than itself “providing banking services.” Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s position persuasive. The Board thoroughly reviewed the service descriptions for both parties, and its conclusion regarding the high similarity of the services is supported by substantial evidence. Accordingly, we uphold the Board’s determination concerning the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As explained in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods” is significant, as it demonstrates a mark’s relative weakness and warrants affording it a limited protective scope. In situations where numerous analogous marks exist in a given field, it is generally assumed that consumers become more skilled at distinguishing between such marks, thus reducing the risk of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). When consumers have been trained to differentiate between marks, this is probative of limited commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Apex provided several exhibits to the Board demonstrating third-party usage of “Aspire” marks. J.A. 40–46. These marks incorporated “Aspire” in relation to credit card-associated services as well as the broader financial services sector. The Board determined that, because Apex’s and CC Serve’s services overlapped, the proper target audience consisted of “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. The Board confined its consideration to those marks associated specifically with credit card services, dismissing marks using Aspire for other kinds of services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. Ultimately, the Board found nine Aspire-formative marks for credit card services but concluded that this number fell short of the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use that would establish weakness in a mark. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2; Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1337 n.1, 1339).

The Board decided that although “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and it held that CC Serve’s mark merited “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred by restricting the relevant public to credit card service consumers and failing to include consideration of 42 additional third-party marks that use “Aspire” or related marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We find merit in Apex’s position, as the Board’s approach was legally erroneous. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to consider marks used on similar—rather than only identical—goods and services. In its examination of the second DuPont factor (see infra Section III.A), the Board had found that the parties’ services were highly similar, heavily weighing this factor in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Nevertheless, in applying the sixth Dupont factor, the Board narrowed its analysis to marks solely connected to credit card services, excluding those related to other financial or banking services—this was mistaken. Prior case law confirms that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand identity between goods, only similarity. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338.

Accordingly, we find the Board’s construal of “similarity” was too limited. Since the Board had previously found the services to be highly similar—at times factually and legally indistinguishable—under the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, it was inappropriate to change the standard when considering the other factors, including the sixth DuPont factor. There is no justification for applying a narrower, heightened standard in this context. Therefore, we vacate the Board’s finding under the sixth DuPont factor and remand the matter, instructing the Board to re-examine which third-party marks should be considered, consistent with its factual determination that the parties’ services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates how alike or different the marks are overall, with respect to their appearance, sound, meaning, and the commercial impression they create. See DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the broader commercial impression conveyed by the marks and assesses whether it is probable that consumers might be confused about the origin of the services associated with each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Given that commercial impression is integral to the assessment under the first DuPont factor, we are compelled to set aside the Board’s analysis in this case; reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor could alter the assessment regarding the mark’s commercial strength or weakness, and consequently influence the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we also remand for the Board to revisit its analysis of the first factor.

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor, as the Board committed no legal error and substantial evidence supports its conclusion that the services offered by the parties are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations relating to DuPont factors six and one, and we remand so that the Board may reconsider the quantity and character of comparable marks used with similar goods, as well as the similarity in appearance, sound, meaning, and commercial impression of the marks, taking into account its conclusion concerning the high similarity of the parties’ services. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
