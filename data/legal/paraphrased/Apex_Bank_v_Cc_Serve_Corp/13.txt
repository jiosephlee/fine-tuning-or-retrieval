Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s decision denying registration of its trademarks. Since the Board made mistakes regarding two factors in the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand the matter.

I

CC Serve is a provider of credit card services to its clients. J.A. 6927–28. The company owns Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, which is used in association with credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. Through its ASPIRE mark, CC Serve collaborates with a banking institution, which issues credit cards and related accounts under the ASPIRE brand to consumers. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its affiliates manage these accounts. Id. Apex Bank, a retail bank chartered in Tennessee, operates 18 branch locations and provides a range of services including personal and business checking and savings accounts, home mortgages, as well as loans to both individuals and businesses. J.A. 7029. Apex Bank does not, however, provide credit card services. Id. Apex Bank intends to launch an online banking platform under a separate brand, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use trademark applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking registration for the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks in connection with “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the examination of these applications, CC Serve submitted a letter of protest, contending that Apex’s proposed marks were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s own mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this protest, the examining attorney allowed the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to proceed to publication, which occurred on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s marks, contending that there existed a likelihood of confusion with its standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board, relying on Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), upheld the opposition, determining that the marks were likely to cause confusion among consumers. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently appealed. Our court’s jurisdiction is proper pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We evaluate the Board’s legal determinations de novo, while we assess its factual findings based on the substantial evidence standard.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark application must be refused registration where “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The determination of “likelihood of confusion” is a legal issue, which depends on factual determinations made in light of the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors is a question of fact, the findings regarding which are reviewed for substantial evidence on appeal when disputed. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to address all DuPont factors but is only obliged to consider those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made mistakes in its evaluation of the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its consideration of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We now examine each factor individually.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the parties’ goods and/or services are. It is not required that the services be identical—rather, the record must show that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services (offered by CC Serve) and banking and financing services (offered by Apex). J.A. 24–28. Because “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve obtained its registration, the Board first interpreted the scope of these designated services. According to the Board, “credit card services” includes activities such as “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board further observed that dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include the provision of credit or funds by issuing credit cards, and as such, determined that Apex and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Additionally, the Board took into account third-party registrations covering (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services as further evidence that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Based on the significant degree of similarity between the parties’ services, the Board determined that the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor “weighed heavily in favor of finding likelihood of confusion.” J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex asserts that the services in question “are not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” with particular emphasis on the fact that CC Serve collaborates with banks to operate credit card programs and “not providing banking services” on its own. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s position persuasive. The Board thoroughly considered both parties’ service descriptions, and substantial evidence supports the finding that their services are highly similar. We therefore uphold the Board’s determination on the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. The presence of third-party use of analogous marks on related goods is pertinent in demonstrating that a particular mark is relatively weak and should be afforded a limited range of protection. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In a marketplace crowded with comparable marks, it is believed that consumers become more proficient at telling these marks apart, reducing the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). When there is evidence that the public can differentiate among such marks, this typically signifies that the mark lacks commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex submitted various exhibits before the Board, displaying third-party use of marks incorporating the word "Aspire." J.A. 40–46. These marks involved "Aspire" in association with credit card-related services and, more generally, within the financial services sector. The Board concluded that, in light of the overlapping services offered by Apex and CC Serve, the relevant group comprised “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. The Board’s review focused exclusively on the marks that identified credit card services and discounted as "essentially irrelevant" those employing Aspire with other services. J.A. 47. The Board determined that there were nine marks using an Aspire-formative for credit card services, but also concluded that nine instances did not amount to the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use that would show weakness in the mark. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (discussing “voluminous evidence” for the use and registration of paw print designs and citing fourteen significant third-party registrations and uses); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (referring to 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board held that "[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and determined that CC Serve's mark warranted “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred by narrowing the relevant public to only credit card services consumers and that the Board should have included the 42 additional third-party marks that employ Aspire or Aspire-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We conclude that the Board’s analysis was legally incorrect. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to evaluate similar marks for similar goods and services. In its review of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board already found that the parties’ services are highly similar, which led to a conclusion that the second factor strongly favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. However, in assessing the sixth Dupont factor, the Board limited its evaluation to marks related only to credit card services and disregarded those marks associated with other banking and financing services, which constituted an error. Our precedent establishes that the sixth DuPont factor does not necessitate the goods be identical—merely similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. We determine that the Board’s interpretation of similarity was overly restrictive. Since the Board previously established that the services were highly similar—and even in certain respects, legally identical—during its evaluation of the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, it ought to apply this same scope in determining similarity under other factors. We find no justification for setting a different, more rigorous legal standard regarding similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This assessment emphasizes the overall commercial impression conveyed by the marks and examines whether consumers are likely to be confused about the origin of the services provided under the respective marks. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Because the analysis of commercial impression is central to the first DuPont factor, and since a revised analysis of the sixth DuPont factor could potentially alter the finding as to the mark’s commercial strength or weakness—thus impacting the overall commercial impression—we must likewise vacate the Board’s analysis of the first factor. Accordingly, we remand for reconsideration of the first DuPont factor as well.

We have reviewed the additional contentions put forth by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s reasoning concerning the second DuPont factor, concluding that there was no legal error and that substantial evidence supports the determination that the services offered by the parties are highly alike. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions as to DuPont factors six and one, remanding for the Board to reevaluate the quantity and character of similar marks employed for similar goods, along with the marks’ appearance, pronunciation, meaning, and overall commercial impression, taking into account its previous finding regarding the high similarity of the parties’ services. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
