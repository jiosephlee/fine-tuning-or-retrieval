Case Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket Number: No. 2023-2143
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Hughes

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's denial of registration for its trademarks. Since the Board incorrectly assessed two factors within the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. It owns Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which is used in relation to credit card services. Id. The ASPIRE mark was registered in 1998, with a priority date effective as of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve delivers credit card services under the ASPIRE mark by partnering with a bank, which issues credit cards and accounts under the ASPIRE brand to consumers. J.A. 6930. Servicing of these accounts is handled by CC Serve and its related companies. Id. Apex Bank, meanwhile, is a retail bank chartered in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. Apex operates 18 branches and provides products including personal and business checking and savings accounts, mortgages, and various types of loans. Id. Apex does not currently provide credit card offerings. Id. Apex has intentions to launch an internet bank under a different name, and for this purpose, plans to use the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use trademark applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office for registration of the ASPIRE BANK marks, describing the services as “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the examination of these applications, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, alleging that Apex’s proposed marks were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s existing mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the examining attorney found the ASPIRE BANK marks suitable for publication, and they were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP., CC Serve filed an opposition proceeding against Apex's trademarks in February 2020, claiming that Apex's marks were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board, applying Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), found in favor of CC Serve, determining there was a probable likelihood of confusion among consumers regarding the marks. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently filed an appeal. This court has jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

We conduct a de novo review of the Board’s determinations on legal issues, while reviewing its factual findings to ascertain whether they are supported by substantial evidence. See M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition is justified if there is a “likelihood of confusion due to the concurrent use of the marks by an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Determining likelihood of confusion constitutes a legal question based on the resolution of relevant underlying factual questions, specifically the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors constitutes a factual issue, and, when disputed on appeal, our review examines whether substantial evidence supports such findings. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to evaluate every DuPont factor, but only those that are pertinent and supported by the record. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex, on appeal, contends that the Board made errors in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its consideration of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, whereas the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We examine each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates the degree of similarity between the goods or services offered by the parties. It is not necessary for the services to be identical; it suffices if the evidence shows that "the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source." Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services provided by CC Serve with banking and financing services offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Since the designation “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve registered, the Board first interpreted the scope of the services as identified. The Board concluded that “credit card services” included “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board further determined that dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include the extension of credit or provision of funds via issuance of credit cards, making Apex’s and CC Serve’s services “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Additionally, the Board reviewed third-party registrations that covered both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services to reinforce its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Ultimately, the Board found that the high level of similarity between the services weighed strongly in favor of a likelihood of confusion under the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 DuPont factor. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the parties’ services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” arguing in particular that CC Serve is involved in partnerships with banks to manage credit card programs, rather than offering banking services itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s argument persuasive. The Board thoroughly analyzed the descriptions of both parties’ services, and substantial evidence underpins its determination that the services are highly similar. Accordingly, we uphold the Board’s decision regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. According to Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” In situations where the marketplace is saturated with comparable marks, customers are considered more capable of distinguishing among those marks, thereby reducing the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Demonstrating that consumers have developed the ability to differentiate between marks can point to a lack of commercial strength for the mark in question. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex provided the Board with several exhibits displaying third-party usage of marks containing the word “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks involved “Aspire” in association with credit card-related services as well as within the financial services sector more broadly. The Board found that due to the overlap in services between Apex and CC Serve, “consumers of ‘credit card services’” constituted the relevant public. J.A. 47. In its assessment, the Board restricted its focus solely to marks identified with credit card services and excluded marks using Aspire for other financial or banking services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. Ultimately, the Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks used with credit card services, but determined that this level of use did not reach the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” threshold of use recognized as evidence of weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2, which discusses “voluminous evidence” of use and registration, including fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”; see also Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (twenty-six third-party marks deemed “a considerable number”)). The Board determined that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” concluding CC Serve’s mark should receive “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. Apex argues on appeal that the Board mistakenly limited the relevant public to credit card service consumers and should have incorporated the additional 42 third-party Aspire and Aspire-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We find merit in Apex’s argument and determine that the Board’s approach was legally flawed. The sixth DuPont factor instructs that marks for similar goods and services must be considered. In its evaluation of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board had concluded the parties’ services were highly similar, weighing that factor strongly in favor of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Nevertheless, while evaluating the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its analysis to marks associated exclusively with credit card services and disregarded those tied to other financial and banking services, such as those relevant in APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 7. This approach was incorrect. Precedent clarifies that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand the goods be identical—similarity suffices. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this instance, we determine the Board's concept of similarity was overly restrictive. Given that the Board already found the services at issue highly similar—even partially legally identical—under the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, it should apply the same breadth when assessing similarity for the sixth factor and beyond. There is no justification for holding the sixth factor to a stricter legal standard for similarity. Therefore, we vacate the Board's determination regarding the sixth DuPont factor and remand

C

The initial DuPont factor examines the degree of similarity or difference between the marks when viewed in their entireties, including aspects such as appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression. See DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This evaluation emphasizes the total commercial impression conveyed by the marks, particularly whether they are likely to cause confusion regarding the origin of the services associated with each mark. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the analysis of commercial impression is critical to the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s evaluation at this stage; reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor could potentially lead to a new assessment of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness, thereby influencing the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand for further consideration of the first DuPont factor as well.

We have reviewed the additional arguments raised by the parties and do not find them convincing. Regarding the second DuPont factor, we uphold the Board’s reasoning, as it correctly applied the law and its determination that the services offered by the parties are highly similar is backed by substantial evidence. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions concerning DuPont factors six and one and remand the case for further consideration. On remand, the Board must evaluate both the quantity and nature of similar marks used on comparable goods and assess the marks’ appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression, taking into account its determination that the services in question are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
