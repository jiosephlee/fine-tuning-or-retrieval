Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges a ruling by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board denying the registration of Apex’s trademarks. Since the Board made mistakes in evaluating two of the likelihood-of-confusion factors, we partly affirm, partly vacate, and remand the case for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides customers with credit card services. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which is used in relation to credit card services. Id. The ASPIRE registration was granted in 1998, and the priority date is October 17, 1996. Id. In offering credit card services under the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve partners with a bank that issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and corresponding accounts to its clients. J.A. 6930. Servicing of these accounts is handled by CC Serve along with its affiliates. Id. Apex Bank, a retail bank based in Tennessee, operates 18 branches and provides a range of products such as personal checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgages, and loans for both individuals and businesses. J.A. 7029. However, it does not provide credit cards. Id. Apex intends to launch an online bank utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use trademark applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for services described as “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While these applications were under review, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, contending that Apex’s marks were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s existing mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the examining attorney approved the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, with the marks being published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex's trademarks, claiming they were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board granted the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding there was a likelihood that consumers would be confused by the marks. J.A. 63. Apex then filed an appeal. Our court has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“Our review of the Board’s legal determinations is de novo, while its factual findings are assessed for substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition requires denial of registration if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The “likelihood of confusion” determination constitutes a legal issue dependent on findings regarding the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to examine every DuPont factor, but rather only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

On appeal, Apex contends that the Board made mistakes in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, pointing in particular to its treatment of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while it found that the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will examine each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how similar the goods and/or services of the parties are. It is not necessary for the services to be the same; rather, the evidence must show that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board analyzed the relationship between credit card services offered by CC Serve and Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Due to the removal of “credit card services” from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first addressed the meaning of the listed services. The Board concluded that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Additionally, the Board determined that because dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include extending credit or funding via issuance of credit cards, the services offered by Apex and CC Serve are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board further reviewed third-party registrations encompassing (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services to substantiate its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. In light of the significant similarity between the parties’ services, the Board found that the second factor from APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 strongly favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the parties’ services “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” highlighting that CC Serve works with banks to manage credit card programs and thus is “not providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s contentions. The Board thoroughly evaluated the service descriptions for each party, and substantial evidence underpins the Board’s determination that the services are very similar. We therefore affirm the Board’s decision regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. Evidence that third parties use comparable marks for related goods is important because it can demonstrate that a particular mark is not strong and, therefore, deserves only limited protection. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). When numerous similar marks exist in a particular market, the reasoning is that consumers become more adept at differentiating among them, reducing the potential for confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence showing that consumers have learned to differentiate between such marks typically indicates that the mark lacks significant commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Apex provided several exhibits to the Board documenting third-party use of “Aspire” marks, J.A. 40–46, with those marks being used both for credit card-related services and more generally within financial services. The Board found the relevant public to be “consumers of ‘credit card services,’” due to the overlap in services between Apex and CC Serve. J.A. 47. In reaching its conclusion, the Board only evaluated those marks associated with credit card services, excluding any marks that employed “Aspire” for other service offerings as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. It determined that there were nine “Aspire”-derivative marks being used for credit card services but considered this evidence insufficient to rise to the level of “considerable” or “ubiquitous” third-party usage sufficient to indicate mark weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2 and Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015)). According to the Board, “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” deciding that CC Serve’s mark merited the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

On appeal, Apex argues that the Board improperly confined its relevant public definition to only credit card service consumers and should have also reviewed the 42 additional third-party marks featuring “Aspire” and Aspire-related variants. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We conclude that the Board’s approach was legally mistaken. The sixth DuPont factor obliges the Board to review similar marks used in connection with similar—not identical—goods and services. In its analysis of the second DuPont factor, as discussed infra Section III.A, the Board found that the parties’ services were highly similar, which supported its finding that this factor strongly favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Nevertheless, in assessing the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its inquiry to only those marks associated with credit card services, excluding marks used in other banking and financial service contexts. This limitation constituted error.

Our precedent holds that the sixth DuPont factor encompasses similar, not solely identical, goods. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In the present case, we determine that the Board’s conception of similarity was unduly restrictive. Because the Board already determined as a factual matter in the second DuPont factor analysis that the parties’ services are highly similar—even partially legally identical, J.A. 27–28—it should have applied the same breadth to its analysis of similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. There is no basis for applying a narrower or more exacting similarity standard under this factor. Accordingly, we vacate the Board’s decision as to the sixth DuPont factor and remand for further consideration as to the proper scope of relevant third-party marks, taking into account the factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor examines whether the marks, viewed in their entireties, are alike or different regarding appearance, sound, meaning, and the general commercial impression they impart. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This evaluation centers on the overall commercial impression the marks produce and whether there is a likelihood that consumers will be confused about the origin of the services provided under each mark. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the commercial impression is a crucial component under the first DuPont factor, it is necessary to set aside the Board’s analysis on this point as well, because reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may alter the assessment regarding the mark's commercial strength or weakness and thereby influence the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we also remand for a fresh evaluation of the first factor.

We have reviewed the additional arguments raised by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board's evaluation regarding the second DuPont factor, concluding that the Board applied the law correctly and that substantial evidence supports its determination that the services provided by the parties are very similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations concerning DuPont factors six and one, and we remand the matter for the Board to reconsider both the number and type of similar marks used with analogous goods, as well as the overall appearance, pronunciation, meaning, and commercial impression of the marks, considering the established similarity between the parties’ services. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED

Costs to appellant.
