Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank seeks review of a ruling by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board denying the registration of its trademarks. As the Board made errors concerning its evaluation of two factors under the likelihood-of-confusion framework, our disposition is to affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve is a provider of credit card services to consumers. J.A. 6927–28. The company holds Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, which it uses for its credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date going back to October 17, 1996. Id. In its business operations, CC Serve collaborates with a bank that issues credit cards and maintains accounts bearing the ASPIRE mark, while CC Serve and its affiliates handle servicing of these accounts. J.A. 6930. Apex Bank is a retail banking institution incorporated in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. The bank operates 18 branch offices and provides services such as personal and business checking accounts, savings accounts, home mortgages, and both consumer and business loans. Id. Apex does not provide credit card products. Id. However, Apex intends to launch an online bank under a separate brand, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design trademarks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, contending that Apex’s proposed trademarks were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s existing mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the examining attorney authorized the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, which occurred on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition proceeding against Apex's trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve's registered standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board granted the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that the similarity between the marks was likely to cause consumer confusion. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently appealed. Our court possesses jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

We examine the Board’s legal determinations without deference, while its factual findings are reviewed to determine if they are supported by substantial evidence. M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). “Substantial evidence” is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, an opposition to trademark registration must be sustained if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The issue of “likelihood of confusion” constitutes a legal question informed by factual findings, specifically those related to the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. The thirteen individual DuPont factors each raise factual issues, and when those findings are appealed, the appropriate standard of review is substantial evidence. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is only required to address those DuPont factors that are material and present in the evidentiary record. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

In its appeal, Apex contends that the Board made errors in assessing the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor considers the resemblance between the goods or services provided by the parties. It is not necessary for the services to be exactly the same; rather, it is sufficient that "the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source." Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board evaluated whether credit card services (offered by CC Serve) are sufficiently similar to banking and financing services (offered by Apex). J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual following CC Serve’s registration, the Board first interpreted the meaning of these identified services. It concluded that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board found that, because the definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include issuing credit or providing funds through credit cards, Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. To bolster this conclusion, the Board looked at third-party registrations that encompass both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services, indicating that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. The Board ultimately decided that the significant similarity between the parties’ services caused the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor to weigh strongly in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services at issue “are not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” highlighting that CC Serve cooperates with banks to administer credit card programs rather than providing banking services itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s argument persuasive. The Board conducted a thorough review of each party’s service descriptions, and there is substantial evidence supporting the Board’s determination that the services are highly similar. We therefore uphold the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. “Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is pertinent to establish that a mark is comparatively weak and warrants only a limited scope of protection.” Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In circumstances where the market contains numerous similar marks, it is presumed that customers can more easily distinguish between them, reducing the chance of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). When there is evidence suggesting consumers have become accustomed to differentiating such marks, this weighs against commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex presented several exhibits before the Board showing instances where third parties used marks containing the word “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks involved “Aspire” in connection with credit card services as well as in the broader financial services arena. The Board determined the relevant public consisted of “consumers of ‘credit card services’” given the service overlap between Apex and CC Serve. J.A. 47. Consequently, the Board focused exclusively on marks in the credit card services sphere, considering marks used for other financial services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-related marks connected to credit card services but concluded that nine examples did not amount to “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use, which would be necessary to indicate mark weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2; Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015)). The Board further stated, “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” concluding that CC Serve’s mark warranted “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues the Board erred by confining the relevant public to credit card service consumers and contends that all 42 third-party Aspire and Aspire-formative marks should have been considered. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We determine the Board’s approach constitutes a legal error. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to weigh similar marks for similar goods and services. In its consideration of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board found the parties’ services to be highly similar and relied on this to hold the second factor favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet, during its analysis of the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its review to marks tied only to credit card services and omitted those involving other banking and financial services. This approach was mistaken. Our precedent states that identical goods are not required for the sixth DuPont factor—goods must merely be similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this case, we hold the Board’s interpretation of similarity was overly restrictive. Since the Board already found the services at issue to be highly similar—indeed, partly legally identical—during the analysis of the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, the same definition of similarity should apply when evaluating the other factors. There is no justification for adopting a narrower and stricter legal standard for similarity solely under the sixth DuPont factor. Accordingly, we vacate the Board’s conclusion as to the sixth DuPont factor and remand so the Board can reassess the proper scope of third-party marks that may be considered, in light of its prior factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates how alike or different the marks are in their entirety with respect to appearance, sound, meaning, and commercial impression. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry emphasizes the general commercial impression created by the marks and examines whether there is a probable risk of consumer confusion regarding the source of the services linked to the respective marks. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). As the assessment of commercial impression is central to the first DuPont factor, we must also vacate the Board's consideration of this factor because a reevaluation of the sixth DuPont factor could lead to a changed finding regarding the mark's commercial strength or weakness and thereby influence the commercial impression overall. Accordingly, we order a remand for the Board to reconsider the first DuPont factor as well.

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by the parties and do not find them convincing. Regarding the second DuPont factor, we uphold the Board’s reasoning, as its legal analysis was correct and there is substantial evidence to support its conclusion that the services offered by the parties are very similar. However, concerning DuPont factors six and one, we set aside the Board’s determinations and remand for further proceedings so the Board may address both the quantity and characteristics of similar marks used in connection with similar goods, as well as the respective marks’ appearance, pronunciation, meaning, and overall commercial impression, taking into account its determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
