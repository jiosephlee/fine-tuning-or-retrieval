Case Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket Number: No. 2023-2143
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Hughes

Apex Bank brings this appeal challenging the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s refusal to register Apex’s trademarks. Since the Board incorrectly evaluated two factors in the likelihood-of-confusion assessment, we affirm the decision in part, vacate it in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve is a provider of credit card services to its clients. J.A. 6927–28. The company holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark in association with its credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve delivers credit card services under the ASPIRE mark by partnering with a bank, which then issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and establishes related accounts for consumers. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its affiliates manage these accounts. Id. Apex Bank is a retail bank incorporated in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. The bank operates 18 branches and provides services such as personal checking and savings accounts, business checking, home mortgage loans, and personal as well as business loans. Id. Apex does not provide credit card offerings. Id. The bank intends to launch an online bank under a different brand name, employing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use trademark applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While these applications were pending, CC Serve submitted a protest letter claiming that Apex’s proposed trademarks were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this submission, the examining attorney allowed the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to proceed to publication, and the marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex's trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board granted the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that the marks were likely to confuse consumers. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently filed an appeal. This court possesses jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We assess the legal determinations of the [B]oard without deference, while we review its factual findings to determine if they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). In accordance with Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, an opposition to trademark registration must be upheld if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The determination of “likelihood of confusion is a question of law, predicated on findings of the underlying relevant facts, specifically findings pertaining to the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors raises a factual question, and on appeal, any findings on those factors are evaluated under the substantial evidence standard.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is only required to analyze those DuPont factors “that are relevant and of record”; not every factor must be addressed in its consideration. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in its assessment of likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex's position, while the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the goods or services offered by the parties are. The services do not have to be exactly the same; it suffices for the evidence to show that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services (provided by CC Serve) with banking and financing services (offered by Apex). J.A. 24–28. Because “credit card services” had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual following CC Serve’s registration, the Board first clarified what the listed services entailed. According to the Board, “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board found that since the dictionary meanings of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include extending credit or issuing funds through credit cards, the services provided by Apex and CC Serve are, “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also took into account registrations by third parties that include both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services as evidence that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Therefore, the Board determined that the considerable similarity between the parties’ services made the second DuPont factor weigh significantly in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve partners with banks to run credit card programs and is “not providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s position. The Board conducted a thorough review of each party’s service descriptions, and there is substantial evidence to uphold the Board’s finding of substantial similarity between their services. We affirm the Board’s conclusion on the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor addresses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. Evidence that third parties have used comparable marks on similar products is pertinent in demonstrating that a mark is relatively weak and therefore entitled to a limited range of protection. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In cases where a marketplace contains many comparable marks, it is assumed that consumers are more capable of distinguishing between them and less prone to confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). When evidence suggests that the public has learned to discern such marks, this is indicative of a lack of commercial strength for the mark at issue. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex introduced several exhibits before the Board evidencing third-party use of marks featuring the word “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks incorporated “Aspire” in relation to credit card services and, more broadly, the financial services sector. The Board concluded that, due to the overlap in services provided by Apex and CC Serve, the relevant audience was “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Consequently, the Board limited its focus to marks identified for credit card-related services, considering uses of Aspire for other services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks associated with credit card services but concluded this number was insufficient to reach “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use that would establish weakness, as discussed in Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2, and Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (where 14 and 26 third-party marks, respectively, were considered significant numbers). The Board determined that, “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and found that CC Serve’s mark is entitled to the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred by restricting the relevant public to only consumers of credit card services and by failing to consider 42 additional third-party Aspire or Aspire-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We find legal error in the Board’s approach. The sixth DuPont factor obliges the Board to evaluate similar marks used for similar goods and services. In addressing the second DuPont factor, as discussed in Section III.A infra, the Board found that the parties' offerings were highly similar, which was the basis for weighing that factor strongly in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet, in evaluating the sixth DuPont factor, the Board narrowed its examination to only those marks pertinent to credit card services, disregarding marks related to other banking and finance services, which was improper. Our precedents clarify that identical goods are not required—only similarity. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. We conclude the Board’s determination of similarity was unduly restrictive. Where the Board has already found, as part of its analysis of the second DuPont factor, that the parties’ services are highly similar—even partially identical as a matter of law—see J.A. 27–28, it must apply the same standard in considering similarity in relation to other factors. There is no justification for applying a distinct or more exacting legal threshold for similarity in assessing the sixth DuPont factor. Accordingly, we vacate the Board’s decision regarding the sixth DuPont factor and remand for reevaluation of which third-party marks should properly be considered, consistent with the Board's prior finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates how similar or different the marks are as a whole in terms of their look, sound, meaning, and the commercial impression they create. See DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the general commercial impression to determine whether use of the marks on the respective services is likely to cause confusion about their source. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the assessment of commercial impression is integral to this first DuPont factor, we must set aside the Board’s analysis here as well, given that a reevaluation of the sixth DuPont factor could lead to a revised view regarding the mark’s strength or weakness, thereby impacting the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand for reconsideration of this first factor too.

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by both parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s decision regarding the second DuPont factor, as its legal reasoning was correct and the record contains substantial evidence to support its conclusion that the services offered by the parties are very similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations regarding DuPont factors one and six and remand the matter for the Board to further evaluate both the quantity and nature of similar marks used with comparable goods, as well as to reassess the look, sound, meaning, and commercial impression of the marks, considering its prior finding that the services at issue are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
