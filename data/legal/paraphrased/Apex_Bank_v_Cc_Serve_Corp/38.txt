Case Name: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket Number: No. 2023-2143
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Hughes

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's denial of registration for its trademarks. We conclude that the Board made errors regarding its consideration of two elements within the likelihood-of-confusion framework. Therefore, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve is a business providing credit card services to its clients. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which is utilized in association with credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. Through partnerships with banks, CC Serve provides credit card services bearing the ASPIRE mark, with the partnered bank issuing ASPIRE-branded credit cards and corresponding accounts to consumers. J.A. 6930. These accounts are managed by CC Serve along with its related entities. Id. Apex Bank is a retail banking institution based in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. With 18 branches, it provides services such as personal and business checking accounts, personal savings accounts, mortgages, and both consumer and business loans. Id. It does not currently issue credit cards. Id. Apex intends to launch an internet bank under a new brand that will utilize the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register the ASPIRE BANK marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While these applications were under review, CC Serve filed a letter of protest contending that the proposed marks were likely to cause confusion with its own. J.A. 4576. Nevertheless, the examining attorney permitted the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to proceed to publication, and the marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP., CC Serve filed an opposition in February 2020 to Apex’s trademarks, asserting there was a likelihood of confusion with its own ASPIRE standard character mark. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board granted the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding a probable likelihood that consumers would be confused by the similarity of the marks. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently filed an appeal. Our court’s jurisdiction is provided by 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

We assess the Board’s determinations on legal issues using de novo review, while we evaluate its factual findings under the substantial evidence standard. See M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition must result in denial of registration where “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Whether there exists a likelihood of confusion is a legal question, which depends on underlying factual findings, specifically those established pursuant to the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors is a factual issue, and when the findings on these factors are challenged on appeal, we review them for substantial evidence. See Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to address every DuPont factor, but only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board committed errors in its likelihood-of-confusion determination, focusing in particular on the treatment of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex's position, while it found the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We examine each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how alike the goods or services offered by each party are. It is not necessary for the services to be identical; rather, there only needs to be evidence that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services, which are CC Serve’s offerings, to banking and financing services provided by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve's mark was registered, the Board first clarified what these services include. The Board determined that “credit card services” encompasses activities such as issuing credit cards for purchase financing, giving advice on credit card debt, handling credit card transactions and payments, authorizing credit cards, monitoring and sending alerts about credit cards, managing credit card accounts, and offering access to credit scores. J.A. 25. The Board found that dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include extending credit and providing funds via credit cards, and therefore concluded that Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are, at least in part, “legally identical.” J.A. 27–28. In addition, the Board looked at third-party registrations covering both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services, bolstering its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Ultimately, because of the strong resemblance between the parties’ services, the Board found that the second DuPont factor heavily favored a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends the parties' services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve partners with banks to manage credit card programs rather than “providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s argument. The Board’s detailed analysis of the parties’ service descriptions is supported by substantial evidence, and we uphold its finding regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As held in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” In instances where a marketplace contains a large number of similar marks, it is presumed that consumers can distinguish among them more readily, reducing the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Further, proof that consumers have learned to differentiate between such marks is evidence that the mark in question is lacking in commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex provided multiple exhibits before the Board establishing third-party usage of the term “Aspire” in marks. J.A. 40–46. These marks utilized “Aspire” in association with services relating to credit cards as well as the broader financial services sector. The Board determined that, due to the overlapping services of Apex and CC Serve, the relevant consumer base should be identified as those seeking “credit card services.” J.A. 47. Accordingly, the Board considered only those marks tied directly to credit card services and disregarded those involving other financial services, declaring them “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. Upon its review, the Board identified nine Aspire-related marks within the credit card domain, but found that such a number fell short of the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use described as sufficient to demonstrate a mark’s weakness. J.A. 47 (referencing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2; Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015)). The Board concluded that while “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength,” Apex likewise had not demonstrated that CC Serve’s mark was weak, either commercially or conceptually, so as to warrant only a narrow scope of protection. The Board accordingly ruled that CC Serve’s mark qualified for the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex asserts that the Board improperly confined the relevant consumer group to those seeking credit card services, and should have also reviewed the 42 other third-party marks incorporating “Aspire” or similar formulations. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s assessment reflects a legal error. The sixth DuPont factor mandates consideration of similar marks as applied to similar—not necessarily identical—goods and services. As referenced in the Board’s evaluation under the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, it concluded the parties’ offerings are highly similar, and so gave significant weight in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Nevertheless, in applying the sixth DuPont factor, the Board restricted its consideration solely to credit card service marks, disregarding those related to other areas of banking and financial services, which constituted an error. The law does not require an exact match in goods for application of the sixth DuPont factor, but instead extends to similar goods. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. Here, we determine that the Board unduly limited the concept of similarity. Given the Board’s prior factual finding under the second DuPont factor that the parties’ services are highly similar, even legally identical in part (J.A. 27–28), the scope for evaluating similarity under all DuPont factors should have remained consistent. We see no justification for applying a narrower or more rigorous standard of similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. Consequently, we vacate the Board’s ruling as to the sixth DuPont factor and remand for further analysis of the full range of third-party marks that may be pertinent, given the factual finding that the parties offer highly similar services.

C

The initial DuPont factor addresses how alike or different the marks are when considered as a whole with respect to their appearance, sound, meaning, and the overall impression they convey in the marketplace. See DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This evaluation is centered on the general commercial impression and assesses whether there is a probability that consumers will be confused about the origin of the services provided under the respective marks. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the commercial impression is central to the first DuPont factor, the Board's assessment of this factor must likewise be set aside, as a reevaluation of the sixth DuPont factor could alter the determination regarding the mark’s commercial strength or weakness, which in turn might impact the overall commercial impression. Consequently, we order the Board to reconsider the first factor as well on remand.

After reviewing the additional arguments put forth by the parties, we conclude that they are unconvincing. We uphold the Board’s evaluation regarding the second DuPont factor, as the Board correctly applied the law and its determination that the parties' services are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations relating to the sixth and first DuPont factors and remand the case for further consideration by the Board regarding the quantity and character of similar marks in use on comparable goods, as well as the similarity of the marks in terms of appearance, sound, meaning, and overall commercial impression, in light of its prior finding on the high similarity of the services. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
