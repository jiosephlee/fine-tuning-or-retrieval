Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s refusal to register its trademarks. We conclude that the Board made errors in evaluating two factors under the likelihood-of-confusion framework, and therefore we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand the case.

I

CC Serve is a provider of credit card services to consumers. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which it uses in relation to its credit card service offerings. Id. The ASPIRE trademark registration was granted in 1998 and has an established priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve’s method of providing credit card services under the ASPIRE mark involves collaborating with a bank, which then issues credit cards and related accounts branded with the ASPIRE name to users. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its affiliates manage these accounts. Id.

Apex Bank is a retail banking institution incorporated in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. It operates 18 branch offices and provides personal checking accounts, savings accounts, business checking accounts, residential mortgages, as well as consumer and commercial loans. Id. The bank does not currently provide credit cards. Id. Apex has plans to introduce an online banking platform under a separate brand, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for use in “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve lodged a letter of protest claiming that the proposed marks from Apex would likely create confusion with CC Serve’s mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this objection, the USPTO examining attorney determined the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks were suitable for publication, and they were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex’s trademarks, claiming that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s registered standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board found in favor of CC Serve under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), determining that a likelihood of consumer confusion existed between the respective marks. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently appealed. Our jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We evaluate the Board’s determinations regarding questions of law without deference, while we review its factual findings to ensure they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). The standard of substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, registration must be refused in a trademark opposition if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The determination of likelihood of confusion is a legal issue, which depends on subsidiary factual findings, specifically the facts established under the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors constitutes an issue of fact, and we assess the Board’s findings regarding these factors for substantial evidence when those findings are contested on appeal. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to analyze each DuPont factor, but must only address those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made mistakes in assessing the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its evaluation of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will consider each of these factors separately.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how similar the goods or services of the parties are. It is not necessary for the services to be exactly the same; the relevant standard requires that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board evaluated the likenesses between credit card services offered by CC Serve and banking and financing services provided by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual following CC Serve’s registration, the Board first interpreted the scope of this service. The Board concluded that the term “credit card services” includes activities such as “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. It further determined that the dictionary meanings of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” entail extending credit or making funds available through the issuance of credit cards, and therefore Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are, to some extent, “legally identical.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also reviewed registrations from third parties that encompass both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services, which bolstered its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. The Board found that the substantial similarities between the services meant that the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor strongly supported a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve works in partnership with banks to administer credit card programs rather than offering banking services itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We disagree with Apex’s position. The Board gave due consideration to the parties’ service descriptions, and its determination that the services are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. We therefore affirm the Board’s decision as to the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor addresses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As established in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), evidence of third-party use of comparable marks on similar products is pertinent to demonstrating that a mark is relatively weak and consequently warrants only limited protection. In cases where an area is populated by many similar marks, the rationale is that consumers will be better at differentiating among the marks, reducing the likelihood of confusion between them. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Proof that consumers are able to distinguish between marks in a crowded field tends to demonstrate weaker commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Apex provided several exhibits to the Board documenting third-party uses of marks that feature the word “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks incorporated the term “Aspire” in relation to credit card services and, more broadly, within the financial services sector. The Board found that, due to the overlapping nature of Apex’s and CC Serve’s services, the pertinent audience should be defined as “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Consequently, the Board limited its analysis exclusively to those marks associated with credit card services and dismissed other “Aspire” marks used for different services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine marks with the “Aspire” root in the credit card services space but concluded that these nine instances did not constitute “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use as would be needed to demonstrate mark weakness, citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2 (where “voluminous evidence” involved registration and use of paw print designs, including fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”); see also Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (considering 26 third-party marks a “considerable number”).

The Board determined: “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” ultimately finding that CC Serve’s mark was entitled to “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

On appeal, Apex argues the Board erred in confining the relevant consumers to those using credit card services and asserts that the 42 additional third-party marks employing Aspire or similar variations should have been considered. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s analysis suffers from a legal flaw. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to examine similar marks for similar goods and services. In the course of evaluating the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board determined the parties’ services were highly similar, leading to a conclusion that this factor weighed strongly in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. However, when turning to the sixth DuPont factor, the Board narrowed its consideration to only marks associated with credit card services, excluding those related to broader banking and finance services—an error.

As previously recognized, the sixth DuPont factor does not require the goods to be identical, only similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. Here, the Board’s construction of similarity is overly restrictive. When the Board has made a factual determination in the second DuPont factor’s analysis that the parties’ services are highly similar, or even partially legally identical (J.A. 27–28), it should apply that same scope in assessing similarity for other factors, including the sixth. There is no justification for enforcing a more rigid legal test for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. Accordingly, we vacate the Board’s determination regarding the sixth DuPont factor and remand for further consideration of which

C

The initial DuPont factor addresses “the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties with respect to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This evaluation emphasizes the total commercial impression conveyed by the marks and the likelihood that consumers would be confused as to the origin of the services associated with each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Because commercial impression is integral to the first DuPont factor, and the Board’s conclusion regarding the sixth DuPont factor could alter the perceived commercial strength or weakness of the mark—thus impacting the overall commercial impression—we must set aside the Board’s analysis on this point as well. Accordingly, we remand so that the Board may also reconsider the first DuPont factor.

After reviewing the additional arguments presented by the parties, we do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s conclusion regarding the second DuPont factor, as there was no error in its application of the law and substantial evidence exists to support its determination that the services offered by the parties are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions pertaining to DuPont factors one and six and remand the case so the Board can evaluate both the quantity and character of similar marks in use on similar goods, as well as the overall appearance, pronunciation, meaning, and commercial impression of the marks, all in light of its determination regarding the similarity of the parties’ services. AFFIRMED-IN-PART, VACATED-IN-PART, AND

REMANDED
