Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank seeks review of a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board decision denying the registration of Apex’s trademarks. As the Board committed errors in assessing two factors within the likelihood-of-confusion framework, our decision is to affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand the matter.

I

CC Serve is a provider of credit card services to its clients. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which is registered in relation to credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998 and its priority date is October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve delivers credit card services under the ASPIRE mark by partnering with a bank, which issues credit cards and maintains accounts under the ASPIRE branding for customers. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its affiliates handle servicing of these accounts. Id. Apex Bank is a retail banking institution incorporated in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. With 18 branch offices, it provides products such as personal and business checking accounts, personal savings accounts, home mortgage loans, and both consumer and business loans. Id. Credit card services are not among Apex’s offerings. Id. Apex intends to establish an online banking platform using a separate brand, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use trademark applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks in connection with “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the trademark examination, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, contending that Apex’s proposed marks were likely to be confused with its own mark. J.A. 4576. Notwithstanding this protest, the examining attorney allowed the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to proceed to publication, which occurred on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, asserting that they would likely cause confusion with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board found in favor of CC Serve and upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), determining that there was a likelihood that consumers would be confused by the similarity of the marks. J.A. 63. Apex sought appellate review. Our jurisdiction is established by 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We consider the Board’s conclusions of law without deference, and we assess its factual determinations to see if they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence has been defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act directs that an application for trademark registration must be denied if there is a likelihood of confusion arising from simultaneous use of the applicant’s and the prior user’s marks on their goods. Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The “likelihood of confusion” inquiry constitutes a legal determination founded upon factual findings concerning the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “[E]ach of the [thirteen] DuPont factors is a factual issue, and we evaluate findings regarding them for substantial evidence on appeal if they are challenged.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is required to evaluate only the DuPont factors that are pertinent and have evidentiary support in the record. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in its assessment of the likelihood of confusion, focusing in particular on its evaluation of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, whereas the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors separately below.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how similar the goods and/or services of the parties are. It is not necessary for the services to be identical; rather, the record must demonstrate that "the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source." Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services, offered by CC Serve, with the banking and financing services provided by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Since "credit card services" was removed from the Trademark ID Manual post CC Serve's registration, the Board initially interpreted the scope of those services. The Board found "credit card services" to include “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Referencing definitions of "banking," "bank," and "finance," which include extending credit or furnishing funds through credit card issuance, the Board concluded that Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. In addition, the Board reviewed third-party registrations involving both credit card and banking/financing services to reinforce its finding that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Because the two parties’ services are so similar, the Board held that the second DuPont factor strongly supported a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex maintains that the respective services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” arguing that CC Serve collaborates with banks to manage credit card programs, and thus does not itself provide banking services. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not agree with Apex’s position. The Board thoroughly analyzed both parties’ service descriptions, and the record provides substantial evidence supporting the Board’s conclusion that the services are highly similar. Therefore, we uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor addresses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. Evidence showing third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is pertinent as it can demonstrate that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only limited protection. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In situations where a market features numerous similar marks, it is assumed that consumers have developed skills to differentiate among marks and are consequently less prone to confusion by similar marks. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Documentation demonstrating that the public distinguishes between marks this way supports the conclusion that the mark in question lacks significant commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex presented a series of exhibits to the Board that evidenced third-party uses of marks containing the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks involved the use of “Aspire” in relation to credit card services and, more broadly, services within the financial sector. The Board determined that, due to the overlap of the services offered by Apex and CC Serve, the appropriate audience for analysis consisted of “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Limiting its review to marks associated specifically with credit card services, the Board considered marks employing “Aspire” for other financial services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. Ultimately, the Board found that there were nine “Aspire”-formative marks used with credit card services, but concluded that these nine instances did not amount to the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” usage indicative of a weak mark, as discussed in Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2 (examining “voluminous evidence” concerning registration and use of paw print design elements, including fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”) and Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (identifying 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board accordingly determined: “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and therefore assigned CC Serve’s mark “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

Apex argues on appeal that the Board erred by confining the relevant public to consumers of credit card services and that it should have evaluated the additional 42 third-party marks using “Aspire” or similar formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We find merit in Apex’s contention that the Board’s analysis was legally erroneous. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to take into account similar marks as used on similar goods and services. In its analysis of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board concluded that the parties’ services are highly similar, supporting its conclusion that the second factor weighed heavily in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. However, in considering the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its review to marks connected to credit card services and disregarded marks relevant to other banking and financial services. This was incorrect. Our precedent has established that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand the goods to be identical—only similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this instance, the Board’s definition of similarity was overly restrictive. After the Board found as a matter of fact that the parties’ services are highly similar—in part, legally identical—when analyzing the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, that finding should be reflected consistently across consideration of the other DuPont factors. There is no reason to apply a more exacting legal standard regarding similarity when analyzing the sixth DuPont factor. Accordingly, we set aside

C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates the degree of similarity or difference between the marks as a whole with respect to their appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression. See DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. The pertinent inquiry centers on the collective commercial impression made by the marks and whether confusion concerning the source of the services associated with those marks is probable. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Given that commercial impression is a key component of the first DuPont factor, we likewise must set aside the Board’s analysis in this respect, as a reevaluation of the sixth DuPont factor may alter the finding regarding the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and, consequently, the resulting commercial impression. Accordingly, we also remand for a reassessment of the first factor.

We have reviewed the other arguments presented by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s decision regarding the second DuPont factor, as the Board’s legal reasoning was correct and substantial evidence supports its conclusion that the parties’ services are very similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations concerning DuPont factors one and six, remanding the case so that the Board may reassess the quantity and nature of comparable marks used on analogous goods and evaluate the marks’ visual appearance, aural impression, meaning, and commercial impact, considering its determination that the services at issue are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
