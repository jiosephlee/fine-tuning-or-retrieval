Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank seeks review of the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s ruling denying registration of its trademarks. Since the Board made errors regarding its evaluation of two factors in the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we affirm the decision in part, vacate it in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides customers with credit card services. J.A. 6927–28. It owns Registration No. 2126948 for the trademark ASPIRE, which is registered for use in relation to credit card services. Id. This registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. In its business, CC Serve partners with a bank, which issues credit cards and related accounts under the ASPIRE brand to customers. J.A. 6930. These accounts are managed by CC Serve and its affiliated entities. Id. Apex Bank is a retail bank based in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. It operates 18 branches and provides a range of services, including personal checking and savings accounts, business checking, home mortgages, and both consumer and business loans. Id. Credit card services are not currently offered by Apex. Id. Apex intends to launch an online bank under a separate brand, utilizing both the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex filed applications based on intent-to-use with the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register these ASPIRE BANK marks for use in “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the prosecution of these applications, CC Serve lodged a letter of protest on the basis that the marks Apex proposed bore confusing similarity to the mark owned by CC Serve. J.A. 4576. Nevertheless, the examining attorney granted approval for the publication of the ASPIRE BANK marks, and they were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP., CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex’s trademarks in February 2020, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding a likelihood that consumers would be confused by the similarity of the marks. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently appealed the decision. Our jurisdiction is based on 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We consider the [B]oard’s interpretations of law de novo, while we examine its factual determinations to determine if they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). The standard of substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition warrants refusal of registration when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The question of “likelihood of confusion” is a legal one that rests upon findings of underlying facts, in particular those addressing the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors raises a factual inquiry, which we review for substantial evidence if challenged on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to analyze every DuPont factor, but need only address those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

In its appeal, Apex contends that the Board made mistakes in its assessment of the likelihood of confusion, focusing in particular on its evaluation of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors separately below.

A

The second DuPont factor concerns the degree of similarity between the goods and/or services offered by the parties. The services do not have to be exactly the same; it is sufficient to show that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services provided by CC Serve with the banking and financing services provided by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Due to the removal of “credit card services” from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first sought to clarify what services were referenced. The Board interpreted “credit card services” to include activities such as “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board found that, since the dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include the extension of credit or provision of funds via credit cards, the services rendered by both Apex and CC Serve are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also evaluated evidence of third-party registrations encompassing both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services to bolster its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Concluding that the parties’ services exhibited a significant level of similarity, the Board found the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor weighed strongly toward a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. Apex contends on appeal that the services provided by each party are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” particularly emphasizing that CC Serve collaborates with banks to manage credit card programs rather than delivering banking services itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s position. The Board’s analysis of the parties’ respective service descriptions was thorough, and there is substantial evidence in the record supporting the Board’s determination that the services are highly similar. Accordingly, we uphold the Board’s finding as to the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. According to Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), evidence showing that third parties use comparable marks with related products is pertinent to establishing that a particular mark is relatively weak and only deserves a limited scope of protection. In situations where a marketplace contains numerous similar marks, it is reasoned that consumers become more adept at distinguishing between them, which, in turn, reduces the likelihood of confusion from similar marks. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Demonstrating that consumers can differentiate among marks in this manner tends to reflect a lack of commercial strength in a given mark. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). In this matter, Apex presented various exhibits to the Board indicating third-party uses of marks containing the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks incorporated “Aspire” in association with credit card services, as well as more broadly within the financial services sector. The Board determined that, due to the overlapping nature of Apex and CC Serve’s services, the appropriate audience was “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Accordingly, the Board limited its focus to marks used specifically for credit card services, discounting marks associated with other types of financial services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-based marks for credit card services but decided that such evidence did not reach the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” level required to demonstrate weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (addressing “voluminous evidence” regarding paw print design registrations and use, highlighting fourteen significant examples); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (identifying 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board ultimately found that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” thus awarding CC Serve’s mark “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues that the Board improperly restricted the relevant public to consumers of credit card services and should have evaluated the additional 42 third-party marks using “Aspire” and similar forms. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s approach was legally erroneous. The sixth DuPont factor compels consideration of similar marks on similar goods and services. Within its evaluation of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board had previously found that the parties’ services are highly similar, leading to the conclusion that this factor strongly favored finding a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Despite this, in addressing the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its assessment to only marks connected to credit card services, thereby excluding marks related to broader banking and finance services. This constitutes a mistake. Our precedent does not mandate that the goods be identical under the sixth DuPont factor—only that they are similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this instance, the Board defined “similarity” too restrictively. Because the Board, within its analysis of the second DuPont factor, already found the services to be highly similar, if not partially legally identical, J.A. 27–28, this same definition of similarity should apply consistently throughout the remaining factors. There is no justification for a more restrictive legal standard under the sixth DuPont factor. Therefore, we vacate the Board’s determination regarding the sixth DuPont factor and remand so that the Board may reconsider the proper universe of third-party marks to assess, consistent with its own finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This evaluation emphasizes the total commercial impression of the marks and assesses whether there is a likelihood of confusion regarding the origin of the services associated with each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Given that commercial impression is central to the inquiry under the first DuPont factor, we must also vacate the Board’s analysis of this factor, since a revised analysis of the sixth DuPont factor could lead to a different assessment of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness, which in turn may alter the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand for the Board to reevaluate the first factor.

After reviewing the additional arguments advanced by the parties, we find them unconvincing. We uphold the Board’s determination on the second DuPont factor, as there was no legal error and substantial evidence supports the conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions relating to the sixth and first DuPont factors, and remand the case for the Board to reexamine the number and nature of similar marks used in connection with similar goods, as well as to reevaluate the appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression of the marks, considering its determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
