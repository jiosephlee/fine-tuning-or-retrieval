Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's decision denying registration of Apex’s trademarks. As the Board made mistakes in evaluating two factors within the likelihood-of-confusion assessment, we affirm the decision in part, vacate it in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its clients. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which is used with its credit card services. Id. This registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998 and claims a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. In its business, CC Serve collaborates with a banking institution; the bank then issues credit cards and account services branded as ASPIRE to consumers, while servicing of these accounts is managed by CC Serve along with its affiliates. J.A. 6930. Apex Bank, a retail bank organized under Tennessee law, operates 18 branch locations where it provides services including personal checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, mortgages, and both consumer and business loans. J.A. 7029. Apex does not currently issue credit cards. Id. The bank intends to establish an online bank under a separate brand identity using the ASPIRE BANK word and design trademarks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office for the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, listing the services as “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve lodged a letter of protest, claiming that the marks proposed by Apex would likely cause confusion with CC Serve’s own mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this objection, the examining attorney allowed the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to be published, and publication occurred on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there existed a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board granted the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that the marks were likely to cause confusion among consumers. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal. Our jurisdiction is pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We conduct a de novo review of the Board’s determinations on questions of law, while we evaluate its factual findings to determine if they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence consists of “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). When an opposition to trademark registration is made under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, registration must be denied if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The determination of likelihood of confusion constitutes a legal issue, which is based upon factual findings, particularly those under the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors is a factual matter, and findings with respect to those factors are reviewed for substantial evidence on appeal when contested.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obliged to analyze all of the DuPont factors, but rather only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in evaluating likelihood of confusion, focusing particularly on its treatment of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor was not favorable to Apex and found that both the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor examines whether the goods and/or services offered by the parties are similar. It is not necessary for the services to be identical; the standard requires only a showing that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board considered the resemblance between credit card services (provided by CC Serve) and banking and financing services (offered by Apex). J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first interpreted the scope of the services specified in the registration. The Board concluded that “credit card services” includes activities such as issuing credit cards for financing purchases, counseling about credit card debt, processing payments and transactions involving credit cards, authorizing credit cards, monitoring and issuing alerts for credit cards, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores. J.A. 25. The Board determined that the dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” embrace the extension of credit or provision of funds through the issuance of credit cards and, accordingly, found that Apex’s and CC Serve’s services were “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. To further support its finding, the Board cited third-party registrations covering both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services, indicating “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. The Board ultimately decided that the substantial similarity between the services rendered the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor strongly supportive of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the parties’ offerings are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve merely partners with banks to manage credit card programs and does not itself “provid[e] banking services.” Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We find Apex’s argument unpersuasive. The Board thoroughly evaluated each party’s service descriptions, and the record contains substantial evidence backing the Board’s conclusion that the services are highly similar. We therefore uphold the Board’s determination on the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. "Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is pertinent to demonstrate that a mark is relatively weak and therefore entitled to a limited scope of protection." Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In markets crowded with similar marks, the underlying premise is that consumers are more adept at distinguishing among different marks, which lowers the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence that the public has been trained to discern among such marks suggests a lack of significant commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex presented multiple exhibits before the Board reflecting third-party uses of marks containing the term "Aspire." J.A. 40–46. These marks used "Aspire" in association with credit card-related services and, more generally, the financial services industry. The Board determined that, given the overlap in services offered by Apex and CC Serve, the relevant consumer base was "consumers of 'credit card services.'" J.A. 47. Limiting its analysis, the Board only considered those marks identified for credit card services, deeming marks that applied Aspire for other types of financial services to be "essentially irrelevant." J.A. 47. The Board found nine marks containing Aspire used for credit card services, but concluded that nine instances did not reach the threshold of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use required to evidence mark weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (discussing "voluminous evidence" of use of paw print design and referencing fourteen "notable examples of third-party registration and use"); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (noting 26 third-party marks as "a considerable number"). The Board concluded that "[CC Serve] has not demonstrated its mark possesses significant commercial strength, but [Apex] has not established that [CC Serve]'s mark is commercially or conceptually weak so as to warrant a narrower scope of protection," finding instead that CC Serve's mark is deserving of the "normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks." J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred by restricting the relevant public to credit card services consumers, and should have also taken into account the 42 additional third-party marks that employ Aspire or related forms. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We agree that the Board’s approach was legally improper. The sixth DuPont factor requires consideration of similar marks for similar goods and services. In reviewing the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board concluded that the parties’ services are highly similar, resulting in the second factor strongly favoring a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. However, in its analysis of the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its review to only those marks associated with credit card services and excluded marks relevant to other banking and financial offerings. This constitutes error. Our precedent holds that the sixth DuPont factor does not necessitate identical goods—mere similarity suffices. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this matter, we find the Board’s application of similarity unduly narrow. Where the Board has already found the services to be highly similar—indeed, in part, legally identical—in evaluating the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, it should use a consistent standard when considering similarity under other relevant factors. There is no justification to apply a more restrictive legal test for similarity in the context of the sixth DuPont factor. The initial DuPont factor examines how alike or different the marks are as a whole regarding their look, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This evaluation emphasizes the total commercial impression of the marks and assesses whether there is a likelihood that consumers will be confused about the source of the services provided under the respective marks. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since commercial impression is a key element in considering the first DuPont factor, we are required to set aside the Board’s analysis on this point as well, because a renewed analysis of the sixth DuPont factor could lead to a different view regarding the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and thereby alter the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand for reconsideration of the first factor as well.

We have reviewed the additional arguments raised by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s assessment concerning the second DuPont factor, as there was no error in the Board’s legal reasoning, and substantial evidence backs the conclusion that the services offered by the parties are very similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations regarding DuPont factors six and one and remand so that the Board may reconsider the quantity and type of similar marks used in connection with similar goods, as well as the appearance, pronunciation, meaning, and overall commercial impression of the marks, particularly in view of its determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
