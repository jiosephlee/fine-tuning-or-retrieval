Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges a ruling by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board that denied registration of its trademarks. As the Board made mistakes in evaluating two of the likelihood-of-confusion factors, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand the matter for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve is a business that provides credit card services to its clients. J.A. 6927–28. It owns Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, which is registered for use with credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date effective as of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve delivers credit card services under the ASPIRE mark by partnering with a bank, which then issues credit cards and manages accounts bearing the ASPIRE brand to customers. J.A. 6930. The servicing of these accounts is handled by CC Serve and its related entities. Id. Apex Bank is a retail banking institution based in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. It operates 18 branches and provides products such as personal and business checking accounts, personal savings accounts, home mortgages, as well as consumer and business loans. Id. Apex does not issue credit cards. Id. The bank intends to launch an online banking platform under a separate brand, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve submitted a letter of protest, claiming that Apex’s proposed marks were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s own mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the examining attorney accepted the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, which were then published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP., CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks in February 2020, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding a likelihood of consumer confusion between the marks. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently appealed. Our jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We evaluate the Board’s determinations of law anew, while reviewing its factual findings to determine if they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition requires denial of registration if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). “Likelihood of confusion constitutes a legal question, which is predicated on findings regarding the relevant facts, specifically findings made under the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors constitutes a factual question, and when appealed, the findings regarding these factors are reviewed for substantial evidence.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to analyze every DuPont factor, but rather only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

On appeal, Apex contends that the Board made mistakes in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its consideration of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex, while the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will examine each factor individually.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the goods and/or services of the parties are. The services offered do not have to be the same; it is sufficient to show that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared the credit card services provided by CC Serve with the banking and financing services offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first sought to define the nature of the identified services. The Board concluded that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Additionally, the Board found that dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include the extension of credit or the provision of funds via credit card issuance, leading to the determination that Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also reviewed third-party registrations that encompassed both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services, which reinforced the conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. The Board therefore concluded that the strong similarity between the services weighed heavily in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion under the second DuPont factor. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services in question “are not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve partners with banks to manage credit card programs and does not itself “provid[e] banking services.” Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s position persuasive. The Board thoroughly examined the service descriptions from each party, and substantial evidence supports the Board’s conclusion that the services are highly similar. We therefore uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As noted in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), "[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection." The existence of numerous similar marks in the marketplace suggests that consumers may be more proficient in distinguishing between marks, which diminishes the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). When there is evidence that the public has been trained to differentiate between such marks, this supports a finding of weak commercial strength for the mark. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex submitted multiple exhibits to the Board evidencing third-party use of the term "Aspire" in marks. J.A. 40–46. These marks utilized the term "Aspire" in connection with credit card-related offerings and, more broadly, within the financial services industry. The Board found that, given the overlap between Apex's and CC Serve’s services, the relevant public should be defined as “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Consequently, the Board focused exclusively on marks associated with credit card services, discounting marks using "Aspire" for other services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. It determined there were nine Aspire-formative marks in use for credit card services but concluded that this quantity did not reach the level of "considerable" or "ubiquitous" use required to demonstrate weakness, referencing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2 (discussing “voluminous evidence” and identifying fourteen notable examples of third-party use and registration) and Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1337 n.1, 1339 (characterizing 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board further stated, “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and therefore found that CC Serve’s mark is entitled to the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred by restricting the relevant public to credit card service consumers and asserts that the Board should have considered the 42 additional third-party marks employing Aspire or Aspire-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We find merit in this argument and hold that the Board’s approach was legally erroneous. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to take into account similar marks for similar goods and services. In its analysis under the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board determined that the parties’ services were highly similar, which led it to find that the second factor weighed strongly toward likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Nevertheless, when analyzing the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its consideration to marks connected solely to credit card services and omitted marks related to other banking and financial services, including APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 7. This was erroneous. Our precedent establishes that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand that the goods be identical—mere similarity suffices. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. We conclude that the Board’s conception of similarity was unduly restrictive. Since the Board had already determined that the parties’ services are highly similar—and, to some extent, legally identical—when evaluating the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, that same scope of similarity should have been applied in its evaluation of the remaining factors. There is no justification for applying a narrower or more demanding legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. Accordingly, we vacate the Board’s decision concerning the sixth DuPont factor and remand for further consideration of the proper

C

The initial DuPont factor assesses whether the marks, when viewed in their entireties, are similar or different in terms of appearance, sound, meaning, and overall commercial impression. See DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This evaluation centers on the general commercial impression created by the marks and the likelihood that consumers may be confused regarding the origin of the services associated with each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Because the commercial impression is integral to the analysis of the first DuPont factor, it is necessary to vacate the Board’s findings here as well, since a new consideration of the sixth DuPont factor could alter the perceived commercial strength or weakness of the mark and, consequently, its overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we also remand for the Board to reevaluate the first factor.

After reviewing the additional arguments presented by the parties, we do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor, concluding that the Board correctly applied the law and that substantial evidence supports its conclusion that the services offered by the parties are very similar. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions concerning DuPont factors six and one and remand the matter for further consideration by the Board regarding the quantity and character of similar marks in use for similar goods, as well as the look, sound, meaning, and overall commercial impression of the marks, taking into account its determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
