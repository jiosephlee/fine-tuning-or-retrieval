Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the decision issued by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board, which denied registration of Apex’s trademarks. We conclude that the Board made errors in its evaluation of two of the likelihood-of-confusion factors. Accordingly, our decision is to affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides customers with credit card services. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which is registered for use with credit card services. Id. The ASPIRE trademark was registered in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. To provide services using the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve partners with a bank, allowing the bank to issue credit cards and related accounts under the ASPIRE name, which CC Serve and its affiliates then service. J.A. 6930. Apex Bank is a retail bank incorporated in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. With 18 branch offices, Apex offers individual and business checking and savings accounts, home mortgages, and both consumer and business loans. Id. Apex does not issue credit cards. Id. The bank intends to launch an online banking platform under a separate brand, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use trademark applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks in connection with “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the examination process, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, arguing that Apex’s marks were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this protest, the examining attorney allowed the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to proceed to publication, and the marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that there was a probable likelihood of consumer confusion between the marks. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal. This court’s jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We conduct a de novo review of the Board’s conclusions of law, while evaluating its factual findings under the substantial evidence standard.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act mandates that an application must be refused if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Determining whether there is a likelihood of confusion is a legal question dependent on underlying factual determinations, specifically findings based on the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors constitutes a factual issue, and, where those are disputed on appeal, our review is limited to whether substantial evidence supports the Board’s findings. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to address every DuPont factor, but rather only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made mistakes in its evaluation of the likelihood of confusion, focusing in particular on the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while both the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor concerns the resemblance between the goods and/or services provided by each party. It is not necessary that the services be exactly the same—what must be shown is that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board evaluated the overlap between credit card services provided by CC Serve and banking and financing services offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. As “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board initially had to construe the scope of those services. The Board concluded that “credit card services” includes activities such as “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Considering dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance,” which cover the extension of credit or funding through credit card issuance, the Board found that certain aspects of Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Additionally, the Board looked to third-party registrations that encompassed both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services, thereby bolstering its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Given the substantial similarity between the services offered by each party, the Board determined that the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor strongly supported a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services of both parties are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve partners with banks for credit card program management and is “not providing banking services” directly. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s argument persuasive. The Board meticulously reviewed how the parties’ services were characterized, and there is substantial evidence to back the Board’s determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. We thus uphold the Board’s finding with respect to the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As observed in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” In circumstances where numerous similar marks exist in a particular field, courts recognize that consumers may become skilled at distinguishing between marks and are less likely to be confused by marks that resemble one another. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence indicating that the public has learned to discern between these marks weighs in favor of a finding of commercial weakness. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Apex presented several pieces of evidence before the Board that illustrated third-party uses of marks featuring the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These uses of the “Aspire” element appeared in connection with both credit card services and more broadly throughout the financial services sector. The Board determined that, due to the overlap in services offered by Apex and CC Serve, the relevant consumer group comprised purchasers of “credit card services.” J.A. 47. Accordingly, the Board’s evaluation focused exclusively on marks identified for credit card services and regarded marks utilizing “Aspire” for other services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board found nine Aspire-formative marks associated with credit card services but concluded that the presence of only nine did not equate to the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use necessary to demonstrate weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (discussing “voluminous evidence” of registration and use of paw print design elements, and noting fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (mentioning 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board further held that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and thus found that CC Serve’s mark was entitled to the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

Apex, on appeal, argues that the Board erred by narrowing the relevant consumer group to credit card services, and asserts that the 42 other third-party marks involving “Aspire” and variations thereof should have also been considered. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s approach was legally erroneous. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to evaluate similar marks for similar, not necessarily identical, goods and services. In the Board’s treatment of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, it had already found the services of both parties to be highly similar, leading the Board to weigh the second factor in favor of likely confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet, in examining the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its consideration to marks tied solely to credit card services, excluding those related to broader banking and financing offerings. This constituted a mistake. Our precedent clarifies that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand identity of goods, but only similarity. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. We find that the Board’s conception of similarity was overly restrictive. When the Board has already determined, based on the record, that the services in question are not only highly similar but at least partially legally identical under the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, that scope should inform its assessment of similarity under other factors as well. C

The initial DuPont factor examines how similar or different the marks are in their entirety with regard to appearance, sound, meaning, and overall commercial impression. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This evaluation is centered on the general commercial impression of the marks and assesses whether there is a probable confusion regarding the origin of the services associated with each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the first DuPont factor relies on commercial impression, it is necessary to vacate the Board’s analysis of this factor as well, as a new consideration of the sixth DuPont factor may alter the conclusion regarding the commercial strength or weakness of the mark and influence its overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we also remand for further consideration of the first factor.

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s conclusions concerning the second DuPont factor, as the Board did not make errors in its legal reasoning and its determination that the services provided by both parties are highly similar is backed by substantial evidence. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions relating to the sixth and first DuPont factors and remand the case for the Board to reexamine both the quantity and type of similar marks used in connection with similar goods, as well as the marks’ visual and auditory appearance, meaning, and commercial impression, taking into account the Board’s determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
