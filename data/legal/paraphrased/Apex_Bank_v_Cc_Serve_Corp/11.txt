Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges a ruling from the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board that denied registration of its marks. As the Board made mistakes concerning its evaluation of two elements within the likelihood-of-confusion assessment, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve is a business engaged in providing credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which it uses in association with credit card services. Id. The ASPIRE mark was registered in 1998, with its priority date set as October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve’s credit card services under the ASPIRE mark are conducted through partnerships with banks; these partner banks issue credit cards and open accounts bearing the ASPIRE name for customers. J.A. 6930. The servicing of these accounts is handled by CC Serve along with its affiliates. Id. Apex Bank, chartered in Tennessee, operates as a retail bank. J.A. 7029. It maintains 18 branches and provides services including personal and business checking accounts, personal savings accounts, mortgages for homes, and loans for both consumers and businesses. Id. However, Apex Bank does not currently provide credit cards. Id. Apex has intentions to launch an online bank utilizing a separate brand, specifically the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use trademark applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “banking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During this trademark prosecution process, CC Serve filed a letter of protest alleging that the ASPIRE BANK marks proposed by Apex were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s existing mark. J.A. 4576. Nonetheless, the trademark examiner accepted the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, which subsequently occurred on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In February 2020, CC Serve opposed Apex Bank’s marks, asserting that they were likely to be confused with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board found in favor of CC Serve under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), determining that there was a probable likelihood of confusion for consumers between the marks. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently appealed this decision. Our jurisdiction is established by 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We evaluate the Board’s determinations of law with de novo review, while assessing its factual findings under the substantial evidence standard.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). In a trademark opposition proceeding pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, registration must be denied if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Determining “likelihood of confusion” constitutes a legal issue, grounded upon subsidiary factual findings regarding the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors is a question of fact, for which our review is limited to whether substantial evidence supports the Board’s findings when contested on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to address all DuPont factors, but must consider only those that are “relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

On appeal, Apex contends that the Board made mistakes in its assessment of the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its evaluation of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the goods and/or services offered by the parties are. The services at issue do not need to be exactly the same; it is enough to demonstrate that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services (provided by CC Serve) with banking and financing services (provided by Apex). J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve registered, the Board initially analyzed the meaning of the designated services. The Board interpreted “credit card services” broadly to include “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board further found that dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” all include extending credit or providing funds by issuing credit cards, such that Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Additionally, the Board looked to third-party registrations including both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services to bolster its determination that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Based on the significant similarities between the parties’ offerings, the Board determined the second DuPont factor weighed strongly in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. Apex contends on appeal that the parties’ services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve collaborates with banks to run credit card programs, rather than itself “providing banking services.” Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We disagree with Apex’s contentions. The Board thoroughly evaluated the service descriptions of both parties, and there is substantial evidence supporting the Board’s finding of high similarity between the parties’ services. We therefore affirm the Board’s conclusion as to the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor assesses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. “Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Where a marketplace contains numerous similar marks, it is believed that consumers become more skilled at distinguishing among marks, resulting in a lower likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Proof that customers have been taught to tell apart marks in this manner suggests a lack of commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex presented a number of exhibits to the Board depicting third-party uses of marks containing the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks employed the word “Aspire” for services related to credit cards and, in a broader sense, the financial services sector. The Board found that, given the overlap between the services offered by Apex and CC Serve, the correct group for analysis consisted of “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. The Board limited its analysis to marks used specifically in credit card services, considering marks involving Aspire for other services to be “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board concluded that nine Aspire-formative marks existed for credit card services, and that this number did not amount to the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” presence necessary to demonstrate weakness, distinguishing from circumstances with substantial evidence, such as the “voluminous evidence” regarding paw print marks in Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2, and the 26 third-party marks viewed as “a considerable number” in Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015). The Board stated that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and determined that CC Serve’s mark warrants “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex asserts that the Board mistakenly restricted the relevant public to credit card service consumers and should have included the 42 other third-party marks that utilize Aspire and related formulations. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We find the Board’s approach to be legally erroneous. The sixth DuPont factor obliges the Board to examine similar marks used in relation to similar goods and services. In its analysis of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board concluded the parties’ services are highly similar, which resulted in that factor strongly supporting a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet in its review of the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited itself to marks related only to credit card services, disregarding those tied to other banking and financial services. This limitation was a mistake. Precedent establishes that the sixth DuPont factor does not require that the goods be identical—similarity suffices. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. Here, the Board defined similarity too narrowly. When the Board has found as fact that the services are highly similar or even partially legally identical in its evaluation of the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, it should maintain a consistent scope of analysis with respect to similarity under other factors. No justification exists for a distinct and stricter standard of similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. Therefore, we vacate the Board’s determination as to the sixth DuPont factor and remand for a reevaluation of which third-party marks should be considered, consistent with the Board’s factual finding that the parties offer highly similar services.

C

The first DuPont factor evaluates whether the marks, viewed in their entireties, are similar or dissimilar with respect to appearance, sound, meaning, and the impression they convey in commerce. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the total commercial impression and whether there is a likelihood that consumers would be confused about the origin of the services associated with the respective marks. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Because the analysis of commercial impression is tied to the first DuPont factor, and since reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may yield a different view regarding the commercial strength or weakness of the mark, thus impacting the overall commercial impression, we likewise set aside the Board’s findings on the first factor. Accordingly, we remand for the Board to reevaluate the first DuPont factor.

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s conclusions regarding the second DuPont factor, as the Board’s legal reasoning was correct and there is substantial evidence supporting its determination that the services offered by the parties are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions concerning DuPont factors six and one, and remand the case so the Board may properly assess both the number and nature of similar marks in use for comparable goods, as well as evaluate the visual, auditory, conceptual, and commercial aspects of the marks, given the Board’s finding that the services involved are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
