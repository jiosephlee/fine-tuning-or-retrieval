Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board's denial of registration for its marks. As the Board made mistakes regarding two factors in the likelihood-of-confusion assessment, we partially affirm, partially vacate, and remand the case.

I

CC Serve provides customers with credit card services. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, which it uses for credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve collaborates with a bank that issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts to consumers, and CC Serve along with its affiliates handle the servicing of these accounts. J.A. 6930. Apex Bank, meanwhile, is a retail bank based in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. Apex operates 18 branches, providing products such as personal and business checking and savings accounts, home mortgages, and both consumer and business loans. Id. Apex does not provide credit card services. Id. The bank intends to launch an internet bank using the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks as its brand. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register these ASPIRE BANK marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While Apex’s applications were under examination, CC Serve filed a letter of protest on the basis that Apex’s proposed marks were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this protest, the examining attorney allowed the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to be published, and these marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board, invoking Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), agreed with CC Serve and found it likely that consumers would confuse the marks, thus sustaining the opposition. J.A. 63. Apex has filed an appeal, and our court’s jurisdiction is conferred by 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

We evaluate the Board’s legal determinations using de novo review, while its factual determinations are reviewed to determine if substantial evidence supports them. See M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). The standard for substantial evidence is “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, registration is denied in a trademark opposition proceeding when the concurrent use of the applicant’s and the prior user’s marks on their goods is likely to cause confusion. Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The issue of likelihood of confusion constitutes a legal question that depends upon factual findings, in particular those assessed through application of the DuPont factors. See M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to address every DuPont factor, but must only evaluate the factors that are relevant and present in the record. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

On appeal, Apex contends that the Board made errors in its assessment of the likelihood of confusion, particularly concerning its treatment of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will consider each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the goods and/or services offered by the parties are. The services do not have to be identical; it is sufficient if “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board examined whether there is similarity between credit card services, provided by CC Serve, and Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” was deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve registered, the Board first interpreted the scope of the services described. It determined that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Citing dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” as involving providing credit or funds via credit card issuance, the Board held that Apex’s services and those of CC Serve are, in part, “legally identical.” J.A. 27–28. In addition, the Board reviewed third-party registrations that include both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services, using this to bolster its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Given the strong degree of similarity in the services, the Board found that the second DuPont factor weighed substantially in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. Apex contends on appeal that the two parties’ services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” particularly since CC Serve only manages credit card programs in partnership with banks, rather than providing banking services independently. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s position. The Board gave thorough consideration to each party’s description of its services, and substantial evidence underpins the Board’s determination that their services are highly similar. Accordingly, we uphold the Board’s conclusion regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. “Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Where many similar marks coexist in a given field, the rationale is that consumers become skilled in distinguishing among these marks and are therefore less likely to confuse them. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Demonstrating that consumers are accustomed to differentiating between such marks generally points to a lack of commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex provided multiple exhibits to the Board illustrating third-party usage of marks incorporating the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks used “Aspire” in connection not only with credit card-related services but also within the broader financial services sector. The Board held that, because Apex and CC Serve’s services overlapped, the pertinent group to consider was “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. The Board then limited its evaluation to marks tied to credit card services, deeming uses of “Aspire” for other financial or banking services “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks relating to credit card services but found this number insufficient to show the kind of “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use that evidences mark weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (noting the presence of “voluminous evidence” for paw print design marks and highlighting fourteen notable third-party uses and registrations); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (describing 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board determined that while “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” ultimately affording CC Serve’s mark “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues the Board wrongly confined the relevant public to credit card service consumers, and that it should have reviewed the other 42 third-party marks that incorporated “Aspire” or similar formations. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s approach was legally erroneous. The sixth DuPont factor mandates evaluation of similar marks on similar—not merely identical—goods and services. In its analysis of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board determined the parties’ services were highly similar, weighing the factor heavily in favor of a likelihood of confusion finding. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. However, for the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its consideration exclusively to marks concerning credit card services and omitted marks linked to other banking and financing activities. This limitation was improper. Precedent establishes that the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods, only goods or services that are similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. We conclude that the Board’s interpretation of “similarity” was unduly narrow. Where the Board has previously found the parties’ services highly similar, or even partly legally identical, under the second DuPont factor (J.A. 27–28), it should apply the same scope for similarity under the sixth factor as well. There is no justification to employ a stricter legal standard for the sixth factor. Accordingly, we vacate the Board’s determination regarding the sixth DuPont factor and remand for further analysis consistent with the Board’s previous factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor examines the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This assessment centers on the marks’ overall commercial impression and whether consumers are likely to be confused about the origin of the services provided under each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). As the analysis of commercial impression is integral to the first DuPont factor, we are compelled to vacate the Board’s consideration of this factor as well, since reevaluation of the sixth DuPont factor may alter the conclusion regarding the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and thereby impact the overall commercial impression. Consequently, we remand for further examination of the first factor.

We have reviewed the other arguments raised by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor, as the Board correctly applied the law and its conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar is backed by substantial evidence. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions concerning the sixth and first DuPont factors, and we remand so the Board may assess both the quantity and character of comparable marks used for similar products, as well as the visual, phonetic, connotative, and commercial characteristics of the marks, in view of its finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
