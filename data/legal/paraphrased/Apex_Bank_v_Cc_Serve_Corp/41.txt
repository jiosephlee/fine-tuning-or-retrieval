Case Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 25, 2025  
Docket No.: 2023-2143  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Hughes

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s denial of its trademark registration applications. As the Board made errors concerning two factors in the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides customers with credit card services. J.A. 6927–28. The company owns Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, which it uses in relation to credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. In its business, CC Serve collaborates with a bank that issues credit cards and corresponding accounts under the ASPIRE brand, with CC Serve and its affiliates handling servicing of those accounts. J.A. 6930. Apex Bank, which is a retail bank incorporated in Tennessee, maintains 18 branch offices and provides personal checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgage products, and consumer as well as business loans. J.A. 7029. Apex Bank does not currently provide credit card products. Id. Apex intends to launch an online banking service under a different brand name that incorporates the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to cover “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While those applications were under examination, CC Serve filed a letter of protest contending that Apex’s intended marks were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this objection, the examining attorney authorized the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, and they were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex’s trademark applications, contending that there existed a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board found in favor of CC Serve, determining pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), that there was a probable risk of consumer confusion between the marks. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal, and our court possesses jurisdiction in accordance with 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We evaluate the Board’s determinations of law de novo, while its factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act mandates refusal of trademark registration if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The determination of “likelihood of confusion constitutes a legal question, grounded in findings regarding underlying facts, specifically the findings related to the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors constitutes a factual question, and our review tests these findings for substantial evidence when their accuracy is challenged on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to analyze every DuPont factor, but only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

In its appeal, Apex contends that the Board made errors in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, pointing particularly to its treatment of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, whereas the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We proceed to consider each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how similar the goods and/or services of the parties are. It is not necessary for the services to be exactly the same—what must be shown is that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board analyzed the degree of similarity between CC Serve’s credit card services and Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual following CC Serve’s registration, the Board first interpreted the scope of the services identified. The Board found that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board further concluded that since the dictionary meanings of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include extending credit or providing funds via credit cards, the services offered by Apex and CC Serve are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Additionally, the Board reviewed third-party registrations encompassing both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services, which supported its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. As a result of the substantial similarity between the services, the Board determined that the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor strongly supported a likelihood of confusion finding. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services of the parties are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve collaborates with banks to manage credit card programs and does not itself “provid[e] banking services.” Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s argument persuasive. The Board thoroughly evaluated the service descriptions for both parties, and substantial evidence underpins the Board’s conclusion that the services are highly similar. We uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. Evidence demonstrating third-party use of comparable marks on similar goods is pertinent because it may indicate that a particular mark is relatively weak and thus deserving of a limited scope of protection. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). When there is a proliferation of similar marks in a given field, it is assumed that consumers have become more capable of distinguishing among them, reducing the probability of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Where there is proof that consumers are accustomed to telling the difference between such marks, it generally suggests a lack of significant commercial strength for the mark at issue. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). In this case, Apex provided the Board with several exhibits evidencing third-party use of marks incorporating the word “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks used “Aspire” in association with credit card-related services, and more generally within the financial services sector. Based on the overlap in services offered by Apex and CC Serve, the Board found the relevant public to be consumers of “credit card services.” J.A. 47. The Board limited its analysis to marks pertaining to credit card services, considering marks used for other services “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-based marks for credit card services, but held that this number did not constitute the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” usage that courts have recognized as evidence of a weak mark. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2; referencing “voluminous evidence” such as fourteen significant third-party examples, and Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1337 n.1, 1339 (twenty-six third-party marks as “a considerable number”)). The Board ultimately concluded: “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and therefore found that CC Serve’s mark warranted “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. Apex argues on appeal that the Board improperly confined the relevant public to those using credit card services and should have included consideration of the 42 other third-party marks that feature Aspire or Aspire-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We agree with Apex that the Board’s reasoning was legally erroneous. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to review similar marks for similar goods and services. In its analysis of the second DuPont factor (see infra Section III.A), the Board previously determined that the services provided by the parties are highly similar, which led to a strong finding of likely confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Despite this, in its consideration of the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited the scope only to marks relating to credit card services, omitting marks connected to other banking and financial services. This limitation was a mistake. Our precedents establish that the sixth DuPont factor does not necessitate identical goods, but rather similar ones. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. We find that the Board construed the concept of similarity too narrowly. Given the Board’s own finding under the second DuPont factor that the parties' services are not just highly similar, but at least in part legally identical (J.A. 27–28), the Board should have maintained a consistent scope of similarity analysis across all DuPont factors. There is no justification for applying a more restrictive standard for similarity when analyzing the sixth factor. Consequently, we vacate the Board’s determination with regard to the sixth DuPont factor and remand for reconsideration, instructing the Board to reevaluate which third-party marks are pertinent given the factual finding that the services provided by the parties are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates the degree of similarity or difference between the marks when considered in their entireties, including aspects of appearance, pronunciation, meaning, and overall commercial impression. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the total commercial impression left by the marks and whether such an impression is likely to lead to confusion regarding the origin of the services provided under each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Given that the commercial impression is integral to the analysis under the first DuPont factor, the Board’s consideration must be vacated here as well, since a reexamination of the sixth DuPont factor could change the assessment of the strength or weakness of the mark and consequently alter the resulting commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand for a new analysis of the first factor.

We have reviewed the additional arguments put forth by the parties and do not find them convincing. With respect to the second DuPont factor, we uphold the Board’s reasoning because its legal analysis was correct and there is substantial evidence supporting its determination that the services at issue are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations regarding DuPont factors six and one, and remand so that the Board may assess the quantity and character of comparable marks in use on similar goods, as well as evaluate the visual, aural, connotative, and commercial impressions of the marks, in view of its conclusion that the services provided by the parties are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
