Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the ruling of the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board, which denied registration of its trademarks. We determine that the Board made errors with respect to two elements of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis; accordingly, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand the matter.

I

CC Serve provides customers with credit card services. J.A. 6927–28. It owns Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which it uses for its credit card service offerings. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. In connection with the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve works together with a bank; that bank issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and establishes accounts for customers, with CC Serve and its affiliates managing these accounts. J.A. 6930. Apex Bank is a retail bank based in Tennessee, holding a charter in that state. J.A. 7029. With its 18 branch offices, Apex Bank provides services such as personal and business checking, personal savings, home mortgage lending, and both consumer and business loans. Id. Apex Bank does not, however, issue credit cards. Id. Apex intends to create an online bank under a separate brand and plans to use the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office in order to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve filed a letter of protest arguing that Apex’s proposed marks were confusingly similar to CC Serve’s existing mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this protest, the examining attorney authorized the publication of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, and those marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there existed a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), determining that a likelihood of consumer confusion between the marks was present. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal. Our court possesses jurisdiction pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We assess the Board’s conclusions regarding legal matters without deference, while we examine its factual findings to determine whether they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). The standard of substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition must result in refusal to register if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Whether a likelihood of confusion exists is a question of law that is determined by underlying factual findings, specifically those evaluated under the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors constitutes a factual determination, and appellate review as to those findings is conducted for substantial evidence. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to analyze every DuPont factor but must address those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

In its appeal, Apex contends that the Board made mistakes in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, particularly in its consideration of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex, while the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will examine each factor individually.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how alike the goods and/or services offered by the parties are. The services do not need to be exactly the same; it is sufficient if “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board examined the resemblance between the credit card services provided by CC Serve and the banking and financing services offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Since the Trademark ID Manual had deleted “credit card services” following CC Serve’s registration, the Board first interpreted the meaning of those designated services. The Board concluded that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board further found that the dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include extending credit or making funds available by issuing credit cards, thereby concluding Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. In addition, the Board evaluated third-party registrations covering both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services to further support the conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. The Board determined that due to the substantial similarity between the parties’ offerings, the second factor in APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. weighed significantly in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” in particular because CC Serve collaborates with banks to manage credit card programs rather than “providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s position. The Board thoroughly considered the respective descriptions of services, and substantial evidence supports the Board’s determination that the services are highly similar. We thus uphold the Board’s finding with respect to the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As explained in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” The reasoning behind this is that when many similar marks populate a field, it is presumed that customers become more skilled in differentiating among them and are consequently less likely to confuse such marks. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence that consumers have been accustomed to distinguishing between such marks can indicate a lack of commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex provided multiple exhibits to the Board, highlighting third-party usage of marks containing the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks utilized “Aspire” in the context of credit card-related offerings and, more generally, the financial services sector. The Board determined that, due to the overlap between Apex’s and CC Serve’s respective services, the pertinent audience comprised “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. It concentrated exclusively on marks pertaining to credit card services, regarding those employing Aspire for other services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks relating to credit card services but determined that this number fell short of the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use needed to show weakness as recognized in precedent. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2, referring to “voluminous evidence” and noting fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”; Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (finding 26 third-party marks to constitute a “considerable number”)). As a result, the Board found that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and thus afforded CC Serve’s mark the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

On appeal, Apex asserts that the Board erred in limiting the relevant public to only consumers of credit card services, arguing that the Board should have also evaluated the 42 other third-party marks using Aspire and related forms. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We conclude that the Board committed legal error. The sixth DuPont factor requires that the Board consider similar marks used with similar goods and services. In the Board’s evaluation of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, it concluded that the parties’ services are highly similar—leading it to weigh that factor strongly in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet, for the sixth DuPont factor, the Board narrowed its review only to marks connected with credit card services, excluding those involving other banking and financial services. This was mistaken. We have established that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand identical goods—only similar ones. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this instance, we find the Board’s interpretation of similarity to be unduly restrictive. Since the Board has already factually determined—within its analysis of the second DuPont factor—that the services at issue are highly similar, indeed in part legally identical, J.A. 27–28, that same standard should guide its review of similarity for the other factors. There is no justification to adopt a more demanding legal test for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. We therefore vacate the Board’s decision as to the sixth DuPont factor and remand for the Board to reconsider the appropriate range of third-party marks that may be taken into account,

C

The initial DuPont factor examines the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This evaluation centers on the overall commercial impression generated and whether it is probable that consumers will be confused regarding the origin of the services associated with the respective marks. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the overall commercial impression is integral to the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we are compelled to vacate the Board’s assessment in this instance as well; reassessment of the sixth DuPont factor could potentially lead to a different conclusion about the mark’s commercial strength or weakness, thereby influencing the commercial impression as a whole. Accordingly, we also remand for the Board to reconsider the first factor.

We have reviewed the additional arguments raised by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s analysis concerning the second DuPont factor, as the Board correctly applied the law and relied on substantial evidence in concluding that the parties’ services are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations related to the first and sixth DuPont factors and remand the case, instructing the Board to reevaluate the number and nature of similar marks in use on related goods as well as the visual appearance, phonetic sound, meaning, and commercial impression of the marks, taking into account its prior finding that the services in question are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
