Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s decision denying registration of Apex’s marks. We affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand, as the Board made errors in its consideration of two factors within the likelihood-of-confusion assessment.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its clients. J.A. 6927–28. The company owns Registration No. 2126948, which covers the word mark ASPIRE as applied to credit card services. Id. The ASPIRE mark was registered in 1998, with its effective priority date being October 17, 1996. Id. In connection with the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve partners with a bank, which issues credit cards and maintains accounts branded as ASPIRE for its customers, while CC Serve and its affiliates manage these accounts. J.A. 6930. Apex Bank, on the other hand, is a Tennessee-chartered retail bank with 18 branch offices, providing offerings such as personal and business checking accounts, savings accounts, home mortgages, and various loan products, but it does not provide credit card services. J.A. 7029. Apex intends to launch an online bank using a separate brand, specifically under the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While these applications were pending, CC Serve lodged a letter of protest, contending that the marks Apex proposed were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s existing mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the examining attorney granted approval for publication of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, and these marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In the case of APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP., CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex’s trademarks in February 2020, asserting there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that there was a probable likelihood of confusion among consumers between the marks. J.A. 63. Apex then brought this appeal. Our jurisdiction is established under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We examine the Board’s legal determinations anew, without deference, and assess its factual findings for substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition requires denying registration when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Determining “likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to evaluate every DuPont factor, but only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in its assessment regarding likelihood of confusion, particularly in how it evaluated the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors separately below.

A

The second DuPont factor involves examining how similar the goods or services offered by the parties are. The services at issue do not have to be exactly the same; it is sufficient if the evidence demonstrates that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board considered whether there were similarities between credit card services, which are provided by CC Serve, and Apex’s services of banking and financing. J.A. 24–28. Because the Trademark ID Manual removed the entry for “credit card services” after CC Serve registered its mark, the Board first interpreted what was meant by the referenced services. According to the Board, “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. It also determined that based on dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance”—all of which include activities such as granting credit or furnishing funds through issuing credit cards—the services of Apex and CC Serve are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board further looked to third-party registrations that cover both credit card services and banking and financing services, which reinforced its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Ultimately, the Board held that due to the high level of similarity in the parties’ services, the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor strongly favored a likelihood of confusion finding. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services offered by the parties are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” especially since CC Serve collaborates with banks to administer credit card programs rather than engaging in banking services itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s position persuasive. The Board thoroughly analyzed the descriptions of the parties’ services, and substantial evidence supports its determination that those services are highly similar. Accordingly, we uphold the Board’s findings on the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. According to Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), third-party usage of comparable marks on comparable goods is significant as it tends to indicate a mark is relatively weak and only meriting a limited degree of protection. In crowded marketplaces featuring many similar marks, the premise is that consumers will be more skilled at differentiating among marks, thereby decreasing the chance of confusion between similar marks. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence suggesting consumers can distinguish between marks accordingly is generally probative of a lack of commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). In this matter, Apex presented the Board with several exhibits demonstrating third-party use of marks incorporating “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks featured “Aspire” in association with credit card-related services, as well as broader financial services. The Board determined that, due to the overlap between Apex’s and CC Serve’s offerings, the proper relevant public was “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. The Board limited its analysis to marks specifically related to credit card services and found marks employing “Aspire” in connection with other services to be “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. Ultimately, the Board found there were nine “Aspire”-based marks used for credit card services, but deemed that this did not constitute “considerable” or “ubiquitous” third-party use to evidence mark weakness, referencing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2 (discussing “voluminous evidence” of paw print registrations and highlighting fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”) and Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1337 n.1, 1339 (identifying 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board further held that, while “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” concluding that CC Serve’s mark “is entitled to the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. 

On appeal, Apex argues the Board erred by defining the relevant public solely as consumers of credit card services and asserts the Board should have accounted for the 42 other third-party marks using “Aspire” or related formulations. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that there was a legal error in the Board’s analysis. The sixth DuPont factor obliges the Board to take into account similar marks for similar goods and services. In its evaluation of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board determined the parties’ services are highly similar, which supported its finding that the second factor strongly favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Despite this, the Board restricted its sixth DuPont factor review to only those marks associated with credit card services and excluded those related to other banking and financial services. This was incorrect. We have previously established that the sixth DuPont factor does not require that the goods be identical—only that they be similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this case, we find the Board’s standard for similarity to be unduly restrictive. Since the Board had already made a factual determination that the parties’ services are highly similar—even partially legally identical—when addressing the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, it should have maintained the same breadth for similarity in reviewing other DuPont factors. There is no basis to impose a distinct and more stringent legal threshold for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. C

The initial DuPont factor examines “the similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. The principal concern in this analysis is the overall commercial impression conveyed and whether there is a likelihood of confusion regarding the origin of the services offered under the respective marks. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since commercial impression is integral to the evaluation under the first DuPont factor, it is necessary to vacate the Board’s current analysis because a renewed assessment of the sixth DuPont factor could produce a different evaluation regarding the strength or weakness of the mark, thereby influencing the overall commercial impression. Consequently, the case is remanded for the Board to also reconsider the first factor.

We have reviewed the other arguments presented by the parties and conclude that they lack merit. We uphold the Board’s reasoning regarding the second DuPont factor, determining that the Board correctly applied the law and that there is substantial evidence supporting its conclusion that the services offered by the parties are very similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations on DuPont factors six and one, remanding the case so that the Board may reconsider the quantity and character of similar marks used for similar goods, as well as assess the visual, aural, and commercial impressions of the marks, given its finding that the services in question are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
