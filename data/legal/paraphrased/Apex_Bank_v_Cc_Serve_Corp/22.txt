Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank seeks review of the decision by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board that denied registration for its marks. Since the Board committed errors concerning two factors within the likelihood-of-confusion evaluation, we uphold the decision in part, set aside in part, and send the case back for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve is a provider of credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark as used in relation to credit card services. Id. The ASPIRE mark was registered in 1998 with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. To provide credit card services under the ASPIRE brand, CC Serve collaborates with a bank, which then issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and related accounts to its customers. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its affiliates are responsible for servicing these accounts. Id. Apex Bank is a Tennessee-chartered retail bank. J.A. 7029. With eighteen branch offices, Apex Bank offers products including personal checking and savings accounts, business checking, home mortgages, and consumer and business loans. Id. Apex does not provide credit card services. Id. Apex intends to launch an online bank under a separate brand, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office for registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “banking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While those applications were under examination, CC Serve filed a letter of protest arguing that Apex’s proposed marks closely resembled CC Serve’s mark and would cause confusion. J.A. 4576. Nevertheless, the examining attorney allowed the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to move forward, and the marks were published for opposition on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, asserting there was a likelihood that consumers would confuse them with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding a probable risk of confusion between the respective marks. J.A. 63. Apex then filed an appeal. Our jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We consider the Board’s determinations of law on a de novo basis, while we assess its factual findings under the substantial evidence standard.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). In trademark opposition proceedings brought under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a registration must be refused where “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Determination of a “likelihood of confusion” constitutes a legal question, which is premised on findings of fact made pursuant to the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors raises an issue of fact, which, when appealed, are reviewed under the substantial evidence standard. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to address every DuPont factor, but rather only those that are relevant and supported by the record. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its consideration of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex, while it found the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will examine each factor individually.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how alike the parties’ goods and/or services are. There is no requirement that the services be the same; it is enough to show that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board analyzed the degree of similarity between credit card services (offered by CC Serve) and banking and financing services (offered by Apex). J.A. 24–28. After “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual following CC Serve’s registration, the Board first clarified the meaning of those services. The Board concluded that “credit card services” covers “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. In addition, the Board found that definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include extending credit or supplying funds through issuing credit cards, so Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also reviewed third-party registrations involving both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services, supporting the conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. The Board ultimately determined that due to the strong similarity between the parties’ services, the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor strongly favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the parties’ services “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve collaborates with banks to administer credit card programs rather than “providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s assertion. The Board gave thorough consideration to each party’s service descriptions, and there is substantial evidence for the Board’s conclusion that the services are highly similar. We uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. According to Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” Where an area features numerous comparable marks, it is believed that customers can more easily distinguish among them, making confusion less likely. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence that consumers have been conditioned to differentiate such marks tends to demonstrate the mark’s lack of commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex provided several exhibits before the Board, indicating third-party usage of marks incorporating the word “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks deployed “Aspire” in connection with both credit card-related services and the broader financial services sector. The Board determined that, due to the overlapping nature of Apex’s and CC Serve’s offerings, the correct relevant public comprised “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. The Board limited its assessment solely to marks registered for credit card services and regarded those for other types of services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. Ultimately, the Board concluded that there were nine Aspire-formative marks in connection with credit card services, but found this number fell short of the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use required to establish weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (addressing “voluminous evidence” of registration and use of paw print designs and noting fourteen “notable examples”); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (referring to twenty-six third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board further determined that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and determined that CC Serve’s mark receives the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argued that the Board erred by restricting the relevant public only to consumers of credit card services, and contended the Board should have taken into account the other forty-two third-party marks using Aspire or Aspire-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We agree with Apex that the Board’s legal analysis was flawed. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to take into consideration similar marks applied to similar goods and services. As discussed in the Board’s analysis under the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board had concluded the parties’ services are highly similar, leading it to determine the second factor strongly favored finding a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. In analyzing the sixth DuPont factor, however, the Board only examined marks associated with credit card services, excluding those connected to other banking and financial services, which was improper. Our precedents clarify that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand exact identity of goods, only similarity; see, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this instance, the Board’s standard for similarity was unduly restrictive. Having already determined as a factual matter that the parties’ services are highly similar—indeed, in some respects legally identical—when analyzing the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, the Board ought to maintain an equivalent scope when evaluating similarity for other factors. There is no justification to apply a separate and stricter legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression,” as stated in DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This determination centers on the overall commercial impression conveyed by the marks and whether consumers are likely to be confused about the origin of the services associated with each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since commercial impression is a key component of the first DuPont factor, we likewise must vacate the Board’s findings on this issue because the outcome of the sixth DuPont factor, upon reconsideration, could alter the assessment of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and, in turn, the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we also remand for the Board to reevaluate the first DuPont factor.

After reviewing the parties' additional arguments, we are not persuaded by them. We uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor, as the Board’s legal conclusions were correct and there is substantial evidence supporting the determination that the services offered by the parties are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions regarding DuPont factors six and one, remanding the matter so the Board may reevaluate the number and type of similar marks used with similar goods, as well as reassess the marks’ visual, auditory, and conceptual qualities and their commercial impressions, considering its conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
