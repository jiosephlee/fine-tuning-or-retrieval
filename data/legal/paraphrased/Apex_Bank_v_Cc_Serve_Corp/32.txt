Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit  
Date: September 25, 2025  
Docket: No. 2023-2143  
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank seeks review of a Trademark Trial and Appeal Board ruling that denied registration of its trademarks. As the Board incorrectly evaluated two factors in the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we partly affirm, partly vacate, and send the matter back for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to customers. J.A. 6927–28. The company owns Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark in connection with credit card services. Id. The ASPIRE mark was registered in 1998, carrying a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. In its business, CC Serve partners with a bank to offer credit card services under the ASPIRE mark, where the partnering bank issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and corresponding accounts to customers, while CC Serve and its affiliates are responsible for servicing these accounts. J.A. 6930. Apex Bank, a retail institution chartered in Tennessee, operates 18 branches and provides services such as personal checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgage products, as well as consumer and commercial loans. J.A. 7029. Apex Bank does not currently provide credit card services. Id. However, Apex intends to launch an online banking platform under a distinct brand name, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use trademark applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office for the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to be used in connection with “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application review process, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, claiming Apex’s proposed marks bore a likelihood of confusion with its own mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this protest, the trademark examining attorney approved Apex’s ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, which were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), determining that the marks were likely to cause confusion among consumers. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal. This court possesses jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We examine the Board’s legal determinations without deference, while reviewing its factual findings to determine whether they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act precludes registration where “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The issue of “likelihood of confusion” constitutes a legal determination based upon factual findings regarding the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact,” and such factual findings are reviewed under the substantial evidence standard when contested on appeal. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to analyze every DuPont factor, but only those that are “relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its treatment of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. According to the Board, the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will consider each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how similar the goods or services offered by each party are. The requirement is not for services to be exactly the same; it suffices to show that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared CC Serve’s provision of credit card services to Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve registered, the Board first interpreted the scope of those services. The Board found that “credit card services” includes activities such as issuing credit cards for purchase financing, offering advice about credit card debt, processing payments and transactions, authorizing credit cards, monitoring and alerting about credit cards, managing accounts, and giving credit score access. J.A. 25. Relying on definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance,” which all encompass providing credit or funds through credit card issuance, the Board determined there was partial legal identity between Apex and CC Serve’s services. J.A. 27–28. The Board also took into account third-party registrations that covered both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services as evidence that such services may originate from a single provider under one trademark. J.A. 33–34. Consequently, the Board found that the marked similarity between the two parties’ services made the second DuPont factor strongly favor a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. Apex, on appeal, contends that the services differ and are not in direct competition, arguing that CC Serve manages credit card programs in partnership with banks rather than providing banking services itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not agree with Apex. The Board analyzed the description of both parties’ services thoroughly, and there is substantial evidence supporting its determination that their services are highly similar. We therefore uphold the Board’s conclusion regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. The presence of third-party marks similar to the one at issue and used on comparable goods suggests that the mark in question is weak and thus entitled only to limited protection. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In marketplaces crowded with comparable marks, customers are believed to better distinguish between brands and are less susceptible to confusion from similar marks. Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence indicating that customers are able to differentiate between such marks supports a conclusion that the mark lacks commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Apex presented several exhibits before the Board evidencing third-party uses of marks containing “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks featured “Aspire” in the context of credit card-related offerings and, more generally, within the financial services sector. The Board determined that, due to the overlap in the services provided by Apex and CC Serve, the correct relevant public consists of “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Consequently, the Board examined only the marks related to credit card services and regarded those linked to other types of services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks utilized for credit card services but concluded that this did not constitute “considerable” or “ubiquitous” third-party usage sufficient to demonstrate weakness in the mark. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2 and Juice Generation, 794 F.3d at 1337 n.1, 1339). Accordingly, the Board concluded: “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” thus affording CC Serve’s mark the standard protection provided to inherently distinctive marks. J.A. 51.

On appeal, Apex challenges the Board’s decision to limit the relevant public to credit card service consumers and argues that the Board should have reviewed the 42 additional third-party marks containing “Aspire” and Aspire-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We agree that the Board’s approach was legally incorrect. The sixth DuPont factor mandates analysis of similar marks associated with similar, not necessarily identical, goods and services. As analyzed under the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board previously determined that the parties’ services were highly similar, resulting in that factor strongly supporting a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Nevertheless, during its sixth DuPont factor analysis, the Board confined its focus only to credit card service marks and disregarded marks associated with other banking and financial services, which was erroneous.

Our precedent holds that the sixth DuPont factor encompasses similar, not exclusively identical, goods. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. Here, the Board applied too narrow an interpretation of similarity. Given that the Board previously found the services to be highly similar, and in some respects legally identical, under the second DuPont factor (J.A. 27–28), the Board should maintain the same breadth in assessing similarity for other DuPont factors. There is no justification for applying a stricter legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. Consequently, we vacate the Board’s decision regarding the sixth DuPont factor and remand for it to reevaluate the scope of third-party marks it should consider, consistent with its factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor assesses the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This evaluation centers on the overall commercial impression and whether there is a likelihood of confusion regarding the origin of the services provided under the respective marks. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Because commercial impression is critical to the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must likewise vacate the Board’s analysis in this instance, as reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor could result in a different finding regarding the commercial strength or weakness of the mark, thereby impacting the total commercial impression. Accordingly, we also remand for reevaluation of the first factor.

Upon review of the additional arguments presented by the parties, we conclude that they lack merit. We uphold the Board’s evaluation regarding the second DuPont factor, as the Board applied the correct legal standard and its conclusion—that the services offered by the parties are very similar—is supported by substantial evidence. However, with regard to the Board’s determinations on DuPont factors six and one, we set those aside and remand for further proceedings so the Board may reassess the number and character of similar marks used for similar goods, as well as the marks’ appearance, sound, meaning, and commercial impression, in consideration of its determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED

COSTS

Costs to appellant.
