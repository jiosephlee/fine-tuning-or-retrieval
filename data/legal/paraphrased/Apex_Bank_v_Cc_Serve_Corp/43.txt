Case Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket Number: No. 2023-2143
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Hughes

Apex Bank seeks review of the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s denial of registration for its marks. As the Board incorrectly evaluated two factors relevant to the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand the case for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides customers with credit card services. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, which is used with these services. Id. This ASPIRE registration was granted in 1998 and claims priority from October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve's credit card offerings under the ASPIRE mark involve a partnership with a bank, which issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and associated accounts to clients. J.A. 6930. Servicing of these accounts is handled by CC Serve and its related entities. Id. Apex Bank is a retail bank headquartered in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. Apex operates 18 branches and provides services such as personal and business checking and savings accounts, home mortgages, and various loan products for consumers and businesses. Id. Apex does not provide credit card services. Id. Apex intends to launch an internet banking platform under a new name, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex applied to the United States Patent and Trademark Office for registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, specifying use in connection with “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During examination of these applications, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, arguing that Apex’s marks were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s mark. J.A. 4576. However, the examining attorney ultimately approved the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, with the marks being published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex's marks, asserting that they were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve's standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that there was a probable likelihood of consumer confusion between the respective marks. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently filed an appeal. Our jurisdiction is established by 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We assess the Board’s legal determinations without deference and examine its factual findings under the substantial evidence standard.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). The term substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition demands denial of registration if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The determination of “likelihood of confusion” constitutes a legal question resting on factual findings, which are derived from the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors raises a distinct factual issue, and, if contested on appeal, findings on those factors are evaluated for substantial evidence. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to address all DuPont factors, but must only consider those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in its likelihood-of-confusion determination, particularly regarding its evaluation of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board found that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, whereas it determined that the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates the degree of similarity between the goods or services offered by the parties. The requirement is not that the services be exactly the same; it is sufficient to show that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board examined the connections between credit card services, provided by CC Serve, and banking and financing services, offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. After “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual following CC Serve’s registration, the Board first interpreted the scope of the described services. It concluded that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Further, the Board found that the definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include providing credit or issuing credit cards, and thus Apex’s services and those of CC Serve are, to some extent, “legally identical.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also looked at third-party registrations encompassing both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services to bolster its conclusion that such services may originate from a single provider under one mark. J.A. 33–34. Based on the significant similarity between the services offered by the parties, the Board found that the second DuPont factor strongly favored finding a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the parties’ services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” highlighting that CC Serve collaborates with banks to manage credit card programs and does not itself offer banking services. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s arguments persuasive. The Board undertook a careful analysis of both parties’ service descriptions, and substantial evidence exists to support its conclusion that their services are closely related. We thus affirm the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As explained in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods” helps determine whether a mark is relatively weak, thus meriting only a limited scope of protection. When an area is crowded with comparable marks, the rationale is that consumers are more skilled at distinguishing among marks and will be less likely to be confused by similarities. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence suggesting that the consuming public is used to differentiating between marks tends to indicate that the mark in question does not possess substantial commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Apex provided several exhibits before the Board showing use of marks containing the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks used “Aspire” in connection with credit card services and, more broadly, throughout the financial services sector. The Board determined that, given the overlap of services between Apex and CC Serve, the relevant public at issue comprises “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Limiting its focus only to marks for credit card services, the Board disregarded marks using “Aspire” for unrelated services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. It then found that only nine “Aspire”-formative marks existed for credit card services, determining that such a number did not constitute the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use necessary to demonstrate weakness as found in prior precedent. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (regarding “voluminous evidence” of paw print design marks and noting fourteen significant examples); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1337 n.1, 1339 (identifying twenty-six third-party marks as a “considerable number”). The Board ultimately determined that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” thus finding CC Serve’s mark entitled to the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred by restricting the relevant public to credit card service consumers and contends that the Board should have evaluated the additional 42 third-party “Aspire” and “Aspire”-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We agree that the Board’s analysis was legally incorrect. The sixth DuPont factor obliges the Board to consider similar marks for similar—not identical—goods and services. As discussed in the Board’s analysis of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board found the parties’ services highly similar and weighed that finding heavily in favor of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet, for the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its consideration to only credit card service-related marks, excluding those associated with other banking or financial services—a limitation we find erroneous. Our case law holds that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand identical goods, only similar ones. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this situation, the Board’s standard for similarity was overly restrictive. When, as here, the Board has found in its analysis of the second DuPont factor that the services at issue are highly similar, even legally identical in part (J.A. 27–28), it should employ a similar scope of similarity across other factors. There is no justification for applying a different or stricter legal criterion regarding similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. C

The first DuPont factor examines how similar or different the marks are as a whole in terms of appearance, sound, meaning, and the general commercial impression they convey. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the overall commercial impression of the marks and assesses whether there is a likelihood of confusion regarding the origin of the services offered under each mark. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the commercial impression is central to the evaluation under the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s analysis on this point; a revised consideration of the sixth DuPont factor may yield a different evaluation of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness, thereby impacting the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand for the Board to reconsider the first factor as well.

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by the parties and do not find them convincing. Regarding the second DuPont factor, we uphold the Board’s analysis because there was no error in its legal reasoning and substantial evidence backs the Board’s conclusion that the services offered by the parties are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations as to the sixth and first DuPont factors and remand the case for the Board to reassess the number and nature of similar marks in use on comparable goods, as well as the look, sound, meaning, and commercial impression of the marks, in view of its finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
