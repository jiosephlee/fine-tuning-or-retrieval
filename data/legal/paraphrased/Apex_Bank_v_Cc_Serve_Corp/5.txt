Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s denial of its trademark registration applications. As the Board made mistakes in considering two elements of the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, we partially affirm, partially vacate, and remand the case for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. The company owns Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark in connection with credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. Under the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve partners with a bank, which issues credit cards and accounts bearing the ASPIRE brand to customers. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its affiliates handle servicing of these accounts. Id. Apex Bank is a retail bank based in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. It operates 18 branch locations, providing personal checking and savings accounts, business checking, home mortgage products, and loans to both consumers and businesses. Id. Apex does not issue credit cards. Id. Apex intends to launch an online bank under a separate brand, using the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, claiming Apex’s marks would be confusingly similar to CC Serve’s own mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the examining attorney approved the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, and they were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there existed a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that there was a probable risk of confusion among consumers regarding the marks. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently appealed the decision. Our jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We examine the Board’s determinations of law without deference, while we assess its factual findings to determine whether substantial evidence supports them.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act mandates denial of registration where “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The determination of “likelihood of confusion” constitutes a legal question that rests on findings of underlying fact, specifically findings as to the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to address all DuPont factors, but rather only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

In its appeal, Apex contends that the Board made mistakes in its likelihood-of-confusion determination, particularly concerning its evaluation of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex's position, while the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will examine each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the goods and/or services of the parties are. It is not necessary for the services to be exactly the same; rather, the relevant inquiry is whether “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). In its analysis, the Board compared the credit card services offered by CC Serve with the banking and financing services provided by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Because “credit card services” had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first analyzed the scope of the identified services. The Board found that “credit card services” included “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Relying on dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance,” the Board concluded that these terms include providing credit or funds by means of issuing credit cards, and thus that Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also looked to third-party registrations that included both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services, supporting its determination that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Ultimately, the Board determined that the substantial similarity between the parties’ services meant the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor strongly favored finding a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex asserts that the services at issue are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve partners with banks to administer credit card programs rather than “providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s contentions persuasive. The Board undertook a thorough examination of both parties’ service descriptions, and there is substantial evidence supporting the Board’s conclusion that the services are highly similar. Accordingly, we uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor assesses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As the Federal Circuit has explained, “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In circumstances where the marketplace is crowded with comparable marks, the rationale is that consumers are more skilled at distinguishing among them, which decreases the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). When evidence demonstrates that the public has learned to differentiate among such marks, this usually suggests the mark is commercially weak. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex presented multiple exhibits before the Board evidencing third-party use of marks incorporating the word “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks were used in association with credit card-related services, as well as within the broader financial services sector. The Board determined that, due to the overlap between Apex’s and CC Serve’s offerings, the proper definition of the relevant public was “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. The Board then considered only those marks registered for credit card services and regarded marks using Aspire for other types of services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. It concluded there were nine Aspire-formative marks in use for credit card services but found this number insufficient to constitute the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” usage indicative of mark weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (describing “voluminous evidence” of paw print design registrations and noting fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (referring to twenty-six third-party marks as “a considerable number”)). The Board further held that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” thus affording CC Serve’s mark “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred in narrowing the relevant public to consumers of credit card services, and contends that the Board should have also considered forty-two additional third-party marks utilizing Aspire and Aspire-formative elements. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We agree with Apex that the Board’s approach was legally incorrect. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to consider similar marks in connection with similar goods and services. In analyzing the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board determined the parties’ services are highly similar, leading it to conclude the second factor weighed significantly toward a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. But, when considering the sixth DuPont factor, the Board restricted its review to marks used solely for credit card services, omitting marks used for other banking and financial services. This was a mistake. Our precedents establish that the sixth DuPont factor does not require the goods to be identical—only similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this instance, the Board adopted too restrictive a definition of similarity. Having already determined as a factual matter in its second DuPont factor analysis that the services at issue are highly similar—even legally identical in part—J.A. 27–28, the Board should apply that same breadth to its evaluation of similarity under the remaining factors. There is no justification for adopting a narrower, more rigorous standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. C

The first DuPont factor evaluates the degree of similarity or difference between the marks when considered in their entireties, including aspects such as appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression. See DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This examination emphasizes the overall commercial impression created by the marks and assesses whether there is a likelihood that consumers would be confused regarding the origin of the services associated with each mark. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Given that commercial impression is central to the inquiry under the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s determination on this factor, as reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may change the evaluation of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and, in turn, influence the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand for the Board to reexamine the first factor as well.

After reviewing the additional arguments raised by the parties, we do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s conclusions regarding the second DuPont factor, as the Board correctly applied the law and its determination that the parties’ services are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations as to DuPont factors six and one and remand the case for the Board to evaluate both the number and nature of similar marks used in connection with similar goods, as well as the marks’ appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression, taking into account its finding that the services at issue are highly similar. Accordingly, the decision is AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
