Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the determination by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board, which denied registration of Apex’s trademarks. Since the Board made mistakes in evaluating two aspects of the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we uphold the decision in part, set it aside in part, and send the case back for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve operates as a provider of credit card services to its clientele. J.A. 6927–28. The company holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which it uses in relation to its credit card service offerings. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve delivers its credit card services under the ASPIRE mark by partnering with a bank that issues credit cards and account services bearing the ASPIRE brand to customers. J.A. 6930. Servicing of these accounts is performed by CC Serve and its affiliated entities. Id. Apex Bank, a Tennessee-chartered retail bank, is described at J.A. 7029. Apex maintains 18 branch locations, offering services including personal and business checking and savings accounts, mortgages, and various loans to consumers and businesses. Id. Apex does not provide credit card services. Id. The bank intends to introduce an online bank, which will employ the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks as its branding. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office, seeking registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to cover “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve lodged a letter of protest arguing that the marks Apex proposed bore a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s own mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the USPTO examining attorney granted approval for the publication of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, and those marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, claiming that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that there was a probable likelihood of consumer confusion between the involved marks. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal. This court possesses jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We conduct a de novo review of the Board’s legal determinations and assess its factual findings to determine if they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). In trademark oppositions brought under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, registration must be refused if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Whether a likelihood of confusion exists is a question of law that depends on findings regarding the underlying facts set out by the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors raises a factual question, and when these findings are challenged on appeal, they are subject to review for substantial evidence. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is required to address only those DuPont factors that are both relevant and part of the record, and not every factor must be considered in every case. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in its evaluation of the likelihood of confusion, focusing in particular on its consideration of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board found that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex's position, while the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates the degree of similarity between the goods and/or services offered by the parties. It is not necessary for the services to be identical; the record need only demonstrate that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). In its analysis, the Board compared CC Serve’s credit card services with Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual following CC Serve’s registration, the Board first interpreted the scope of the identified services. It determined that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board further found that dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” cover the extension of credit or provision of funds via credit cards, and therefore concluded that certain aspects of Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also referenced third-party registrations encompassing both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services, to bolster its conclusion that such services may originate from a single source using one trademark. J.A. 33–34. Based on the pronounced similarity between the parties’ services, the Board determined that the second factor from APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. weighed strongly in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the respective services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” arguing that CC Serve collaborates with banks to oversee credit card programs rather than offering banking services itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We find Apex’s contention unpersuasive. The Board thoroughly examined the service descriptions provided by each party, and substantial evidence supports its conclusion regarding the significant similarity of the parties’ services. We therefore affirm the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As established by Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), third-party use of analogous marks on comparable goods is relevant as it tends to demonstrate that a mark is relatively weak and thus only entitled to limited protection. When many similar marks are present in a particular marketplace, customers are thought to develop a greater ability to distinguish among marks, reducing the risk of confusion from similarities. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence that consumers learn to differentiate between such marks generally suggests the mark possesses limited commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Apex provided the Board with several exhibits highlighting the third-party use of marks containing the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks were utilized in connection with credit card services and more generally within the financial services sector. The Board determined, given the overlap in services between Apex and CC Serve, that the correct relevant public should be "consumers of 'credit card services.'" J.A. 47. Consequently, the Board only considered those marks related to credit card services, disregarding marks associated with other services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board found that, although nine Aspire-related marks for credit card services were present, this did not equate to the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” level of use deemed necessary to show the mark’s weakness as recognized in prior cases. J.A. 47 (referencing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2, which addressed "voluminous evidence" of registration and use of a paw print design, highlighting fourteen notable third-party examples; and Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015), discussing 26 third-party marks described as "a considerable number"). The Board thus concluded that, while "[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]'s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection." As a result, the Board deemed CC Serve’s mark deserving of the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

On appeal, Apex argues the Board erred by limiting the relevant public solely to credit card service consumers and that the Board should have included the 42 other third-party “Aspire” and Aspire-formative marks in its consideration. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We find merit in Apex’s position and agree the Board’s analysis was flawed as a matter of law. The sixth DuPont factor requires an assessment of similar marks for similar, not just identical, goods and services. In evaluating the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board had already concluded the services of the parties were highly similar, which heavily favored a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. However, in its analysis of the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its review to only those marks concerning credit card services, disregarding marks related to additional banking and financial offerings. This limitation constituted error. The law does not require the sixth DuPont factor to involve only identical goods—similarity is sufficient. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. Here, the Board’s approach to similarity was inappropriately narrow. Having already determined that the parties’ services are highly similar (in some respects, even legally identical) under the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, the Board should have maintained the same breadth when analyzing similarity across other DuPont factors. There is no justification to apply a narrower or more demanding legal standard regarding similarity under the sixth factor. C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates how alike or different the marks are overall with regard to their appearance, sound, meaning, and commercial impression. See DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the overall commercial impression produced by the marks, specifically examining whether consumers are likely to be confused about the origin of the services provided under the respective marks. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the assessment of commercial impression is central to the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s findings on this factor, as a new evaluation of the sixth DuPont factor could alter the determination regarding the commercial strength or weakness of the mark and, consequently, the total commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand so that the Board may also reconsider the first factor.

After reviewing the additional arguments presented by the parties, we find them unconvincing. We uphold the Board's determination regarding the second DuPont factor, as the Board applied the correct legal standard and its conclusion that the services offered by the parties are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions relating to DuPont factors six and one, and remand the matter so that the Board may evaluate both the prevalence and characteristics of comparable marks on related goods, as well as assess the marks’ visual appearance, phonetic similarity, meaning, and overall commercial impression, taking into account its finding of high similarity between the parties’ services. The judgment is AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
