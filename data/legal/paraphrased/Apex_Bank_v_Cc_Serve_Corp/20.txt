Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges a ruling by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board that denied registration of Apex’s trademarks. As the Board made errors regarding two factors in the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we affirm the decision in part, vacate it in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. It owns Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, which is registered for use with credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998 and claims a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve collaborates with a banking partner, whereby the partner bank issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and corresponding accounts to consumers, while CC Serve and its affiliates handle servicing of these accounts. J.A. 6930. Apex Bank is a Tennessee-chartered retail banking institution. J.A. 7029. Apex operates 18 branches and provides a variety of products including personal checking and savings accounts, business checking, mortgages, and both consumer and business loans. Id. Notably, Apex does not issue credit cards. Id. Apex intends to launch an online bank under a different brand name, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use trademark applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office for registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, designating the marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, contending that the proposed Apex marks were likely to cause confusion with CC Serve’s mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this protest, the examining attorney allowed the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to move forward to publication, and the marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s ASPIRE standard character mark. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that the marks were likely to cause confusion among consumers. J.A. 63. Apex has appealed this decision. Our authority to hear the appeal is based on 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

We examine the Board’s determinations of law under a de novo standard, while its factual findings are assessed to determine if substantial evidence supports them. See M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). The standard for substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act precludes trademark registration when the simultaneous use of the marks in question by the applicant and a prior user on their respective goods is likely to result in confusion. Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The determination of likelihood of confusion is a legal question that is predicated on findings of underlying facts, specifically those encompassed by the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors involves a factual determination, and on appeal, these factual findings are reviewed under the substantial evidence standard. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is obligated to evaluate only those DuPont factors that are pertinent and for which there is evidence in the record, rather than all thirteen factors in every case. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made mistakes in its evaluation of likelihood of confusion, pointing particularly to the way it considered the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while it found that the first and second DuPont factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We now consider each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor examines whether the goods and/or services offered by each party are similar. It is not necessary that the services be exactly the same; rather, the evidence must demonstrate that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board evaluated the likeness between CC Serve’s credit card services and Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Because “credit card services” had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board initially determined the scope of this service. The Board concluded that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board further found that dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” cover extending credit and providing funds through issuing credit cards, leading to the conclusion that CC Serve’s and Apex’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board additionally relied on third-party registrations covering both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services to bolster its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Given the significant similarity between the parties’ offerings, the Board determined that the second factor in APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 strongly favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex maintains that the services provided by the parties are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” asserting that CC Serve’s role is to partner with banks in managing credit card programs rather than directly offering banking services. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s reasoning persuasive. The Board thoroughly reviewed the service descriptions from both parties, and its finding that the services are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. We uphold the Board’s determination on the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor assesses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. “Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In situations where a marketplace contains many similar marks, the assumption is that consumers become adept at telling marks apart, thereby reducing the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). If there is evidence showing consumers can distinguish between similar marks, this suggests the mark lacks strong commercial distinctiveness. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex submitted several exhibits to the Board showing third-party use of marks featuring “Aspire”. J.A. 40–46. These marks used “Aspire” for credit card-related services and more generally in the financial services sector. The Board found that, due to the overlap between Apex and CC Serve’s offerings, the appropriate target audience was “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. As such, the Board limited its focus to the marks identified in relation to credit card services, deeming uses for other financial services “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks for credit card services, but found that this number did not reach the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” threshold sufficient to show weakness, as established in previous cases. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2, discussing “voluminous evidence” and mentioning fourteen significant examples of third-party use; Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015), referencing 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board concluded, “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” determining instead that CC Serve’s mark warranted “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred by defining the relevant public as consumers of credit card services only, and should have considered an additional 42 third-party marks incorporating Aspire and similar elements. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We agree that the Board’s approach was legally erroneous. The sixth DuPont factor instructs the Board to consider similar marks on similar goods and services. As part of its examination of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board found that the services provided by the parties were highly similar, leading it to find that the second factor strongly favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet for the sixth DuPont factor, the Board narrowed the field, considering only those marks associated with credit card services, thereby excluding marks for other banking and financial services. This constitutes an error. Our precedent clarifies that the sixth DuPont factor requires similarity, not identical goods. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this instance, the Board’s interpretation of similarity was unduly restrictive. Once the Board made a factual determination that the services at issue are highly similar—even, in some respects, legally identical—when applying the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, it should use the same definition of similarity for the other DuPont factors. There is no justification for implementing a stricter or altered legal standard under the sixth DuPont factor. Therefore, we vacate the Board’s determination concerning the sixth DuPont factor and remand for reconsideration regarding which third-party marks may be considered, taking into account the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor examines how the marks as a whole compare or differ regarding their appearance, sound, meaning, and the commercial impression they create. See DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This assessment centers on the overall commercial impression of the marks and the likelihood that consumers might be confused about the origin of the services associated with each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since commercial impression is a key aspect of the first DuPont factor, we must likewise vacate the Board’s findings here, as a renewed review of the sixth DuPont factor could alter the evaluation of the mark’s strength or weakness and thus impact the commercial impression as a whole. Accordingly, we also remand for reconsideration of the first factor.

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by the parties and conclude that they lack merit. Regarding the second DuPont factor, we uphold the Board's analysis because it committed no legal error and its conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. However, with respect to DuPont factors six and one, we set aside the Board’s determinations and remand the matter so the Board can assess both the quantity and character of similar marks used on comparable goods, as well as the visual, aural, conceptual, and commercial attributes of the marks, taking into account its determination that the services provided by both parties are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
