Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges a ruling from the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board that denied registration of Apex’s trademarks. As the Board made errors regarding its evaluation of two factors in the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand the case.

I

CC Serve provides customers with credit card services. J.A. 6927–28. The company owns Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark in relation to credit card services. Id. This registration was granted in 1998 and claims a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve collaborates with a bank to deliver credit card services using the ASPIRE mark; the bank issues credit cards and corresponding accounts branded with ASPIRE to consumers. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its affiliates handle servicing of these accounts. Id. Apex Bank is a retail bank organized under Tennessee law. J.A. 7029. Apex operates 18 branches and provides products such as personal checking accounts, personal savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgages, and loans for consumers and businesses. Id. Credit cards are not among its offerings. Id. Apex intends to launch an online bank under a separate brand, employing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During examination, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, contending that Apex’s marks were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s existing mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this protest, the examining attorney approved the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, and they were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with its own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), determining that the marks were likely to cause consumer confusion. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal. Our jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We assess the Board’s determinations of law using a de novo standard, while we examine its factual findings to determine if they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence has been defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). In trademark opposition proceedings brought under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, registration must be denied if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The question of “likelihood of confusion” is a legal one, dependent upon factual findings, specifically findings relating to the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to address every DuPont factor, but must evaluate those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, particularly in its consideration of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board found that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex, while the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will consider each factor individually.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how similar the goods and/or services offered by the parties are. The services do not need to be the same; it is sufficient for the evidence to show that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board evaluated the degree of similarity between CC Serve’s credit card services and Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Because “credit card services” had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve registered, the Board first clarified the scope of the listed services. It concluded that “credit card services” include “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Additionally, the Board determined that dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include providing credit or funds through credit card issuance, leading to the conclusion that Apex and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also reviewed third-party registrations that list both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services to reinforce its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Based on the significant similarity between the parties’ services, the Board held that the second DuPont factor strongly supported a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services “are not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve is collaborating with banks to manage credit card programs rather than itself “providing banking services.” Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s argument. The Board thoroughly analyzed each party’s service descriptions, and substantial evidence supports its finding that the services are highly similar. We therefore affirm the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. According to Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), evidence of third parties using comparable marks for similar goods can demonstrate that a mark is relatively weak and thus deserving of only limited protection. When a market contains numerous similar marks, it is presumed that consumers will be more capable of distinguishing between these marks, reducing the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence indicating that consumers are accustomed to telling marks apart suggests that the mark in question may lack commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Apex submitted multiple exhibits to the Board reflecting third-party use of marks incorporating "Aspire." J.A. 40–46. These marks employed the term "Aspire" in relation to credit card services and, in a broader sense, across the financial services industry. The Board determined that, due to the overlap between the services offered by Apex and CC Serve, the appropriate relevant public consists of "consumers of ‘credit card services.’" J.A. 47. Consequently, the Board considered only the marks connected with credit card services, disregarding those using Aspire for other types of services as "essentially irrelevant." J.A. 47. The Board concluded there were nine Aspire-formative marks associated with credit card services but found that this quantity fell short of the "considerable" or "ubiquitous" third-party usage necessary to demonstrate mark weakness, referencing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2, which addressed "voluminous evidence" regarding registrations and use of paw print designs, including fourteen "notable examples," as well as Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015), which described 26 third-party marks as "a considerable number." The Board determined that "[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]'s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection," ultimately finding that CC Serve’s mark deserved "the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks." J.A. 51.

Apex, on appeal, argues that the Board erred in restricting the relevant public to users of credit card services and asserts the Board should have taken into account the additional 42 third-party marks containing Aspire and related formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We find merit in Apex’s position and conclude the Board’s analysis contained legal error. The sixth DuPont factor obliges the Board to evaluate similar marks for similar goods and services. In its analysis of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board previously determined that the parties’ services were highly similar, leading to the conclusion that the second factor significantly favored a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet, in addressing the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its assessment to marks connected to credit card services only, excluding those linked to other banking and financing services, which constituted an error.

This Court has established that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand the goods be identical, only similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. We find that the Board defined similarity too narrowly in this instance. Having already found the services to be highly similar—even partially legally identical—in its second DuPont factor analysis, J.A. 27–28, the Board should have applied a consistent scope of similarity to the other factors as well. We find no justification for adopting a different or stricter legal standard for similarity in connection with the sixth DuPont factor. C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates the degree of similarity or difference between the marks in their entirety, considering aspects such as appearance, sound, meaning, and overall commercial impression. See DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This assessment centers on the total commercial impression created by the marks and the likelihood that consumers might be confused regarding the origin of the services associated with each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Given that commercial impression is integral to the evaluation under the first DuPont factor, we likewise set aside the Board’s findings here, as a reexamination of the sixth DuPont factor could alter the perceived commercial strength or weakness of the mark, thereby impacting the commercial impression overall. Accordingly, we also remand for the Board to reconsider the first DuPont factor.

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s assessment concerning the second DuPont factor, as the Board applied the correct legal standard and there is substantial evidence to support its determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions regarding DuPont factors six and one, and we remand so that the Board may reassess the number and nature of similar marks used on similar goods, as well as the appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression of the marks, taking into account its finding that the services offered by the parties are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
