Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s decision denying registration of Apex’s trademarks. As the Board made mistakes in its consideration of two elements within the likelihood-of-confusion assessment, we partly affirm, partly vacate, and send the matter back for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve is a business that provides credit card services to consumers. J.A. 6927–28. The company holds Registration No. 2126948 for the trademark ASPIRE, which is registered for use with credit card services. Id. This registration was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. Through the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve collaborates with a banking partner that issues credit cards and accounts under the ASPIRE brand to its customers. J.A. 6930. These accounts are managed by CC Serve and its related entities. Id. Apex Bank, on the other hand, is a retail banking institution incorporated in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. Operating 18 branches, Apex offers various products such as personal and business checking and savings accounts, home mortgages, and loans to both consumers and businesses. Id. Apex does not provide credit card services. Id. Apex intends to launch an online bank utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks as a separate brand. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use trademark applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office for the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to be used in “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve lodged a letter of protest, arguing that Apex’s proposed marks were likely to cause confusion with its own. J.A. 4576. Nevertheless, the examining attorney approved the ASPIRE BANK marks for publication, and they were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex's trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve's own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board agreed with CC Serve and upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), determining that the marks in question were likely to cause confusion among consumers. J.A. 63. Apex has filed an appeal. This court has jurisdiction in accordance with 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

We examine the Board's determinations of law without deference, while its factual determinations are reviewed to determine if they are supported by substantial evidence. See M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, an opposition to trademark registration must be sustained where “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The question of likelihood of confusion constitutes a legal issue that turns on findings of fact with respect to the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors constitutes a distinct factual inquiry, the findings for which are reviewed under the substantial evidence standard when raised on appeal. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is only required to address those DuPont factors “that are relevant and of record,” and need not consider every factor in every case. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in its assessment of the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex's position, while the first and second factors favored CC Serve. See J.A. 62–63. We examine each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the goods and/or services of the parties are. It is not necessary for the services to be the same; rather, it is sufficient that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared credit card services, as offered by CC Serve, to banking and financing services, which are offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. In light of the deletion of “credit card services” from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve registered its mark, the Board first clarified the scope of the listed services. It concluded that “credit card services” include activities such as “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Drawing upon dictionary definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance,” which include the extension of credit or providing funding by issuing credit cards, the Board found that some aspects of Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Additionally, the Board reviewed third-party registrations that encompassed both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services, as further support that these types of services might originate from a single source under one trademark. J.A. 33–34. Ultimately, due to the considerable similarity between the parties’ services, the Board held that the second factor weighed significantly in favor of a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex asserts that the services each party provides “are not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” pointing out that CC Serve collaborates with banks to manage credit card programs, rather than independently offering banking services. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We reject Apex’s position. The Board undertook a careful examination of both parties’ service descriptions, and substantial evidence upholds the Board’s determination that their services are closely related. Accordingly, we affirm the Board’s conclusion regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor addresses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. Evidence indicating that third parties use comparable marks for comparable goods is pertinent in demonstrating a mark’s relative weakness and its correspondingly narrow scope of protection. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). Where numerous similar marks are present in a field, the rationale is that consumers become adept at distinguishing among them, thereby reducing the likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence that customers have learned to differentiate among marks in this context tends to show a lack of commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex provided the Board with several exhibits showing third-party uses of marks containing the word “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks included the term “Aspire” in relation to credit card-related offerings and, more generally, the financial services sector. The Board found that, due to the overlap in Apex’s and CC Serve’s services, the relevant public should be defined as “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. The Board limited its attention exclusively to marks used for credit card services, deeming those marks using Aspire for different services “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. It determined that only nine Aspire-formative marks existed for credit card services and concluded that this number did not rise to the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use that would establish mark weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2, discussing “voluminous evidence” of paw print design elements at issue and noting fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”; Juice Generation, 794 F.3d at 1337 n.1, 1339, describing 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board concluded that while “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, [Apex] has not demonstrated that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak so as to warrant only a narrow scope of protection,” and it ruled that CC Serve’s mark is entitled to “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues the Board erred by limiting the relevant consumer group to credit card service users and asserts the Board should have included the other 42 third-party Aspire and Aspire-formative marks in its analysis. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We find merit in Apex’s argument, as the Board’s analysis suffers from a legal error. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to take into account similar marks used for similar goods and services. In evaluating the second DuPont factor (see infra Section III.A), the Board found that the parties’ services are highly similar, leading to the conclusion that the second factor strongly supported a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. However, during its sixth DuPont factor analysis, the Board restricted its consideration to only those marks associated with credit card services, disregarding marks related to other banking and financing offerings. This was erroneous. This court has stated that the sixth DuPont factor does not mandate the goods to be identical; they need only be similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, 794 F.3d at 1338. In this matter, we deem the Board’s definition of similarity unduly limited. Given that the Board’s factual findings on the second DuPont factor concluded that the services are highly similar—and in part legally identical—J.A. 27–28, that same scope of similarity should apply in consideration of the remaining factors. There is no justification for imposing a different, more demanding standard of similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. Consequently, we vacate the Board’s decision on the sixth DuPont factor and remand for the Board to reassess the applicable scope of third-party marks in view of its determination that the parties’ services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates the "similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties regarding appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression." DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the overall commercial impression created by the marks and whether this may lead to consumer confusion regarding the source of the services provided under the respective marks. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Because the overall commercial impression is a critical consideration in assessing the first DuPont factor, we find it necessary to also set aside the Board’s analysis on this point; reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor could produce a different view as to the mark’s commercial strength or weakness, thereby impacting the commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand for further examination of the first factor as well.

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor, concluding that there was no legal error and that the Board’s determination that the services offered by the parties are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. However, we set aside the Board’s findings concerning DuPont factors six and one, remanding the case so that the Board may evaluate the quantity and character of similar marks used in connection with similar goods, as well as the visual, phonetic, connotative, and commercial impressions of the marks, taking into consideration its finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. The decision is AFFIRMED-IN-PART, VACATED-IN-PART, AND

REMANDED
