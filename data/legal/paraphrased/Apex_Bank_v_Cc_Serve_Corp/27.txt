Case Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket Number: No. 2023-2143
Opinion by Circuit Judge Hughes

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s denial of registration for its trademarks. As the Board incorrectly evaluated two elements within the likelihood-of-confusion analysis, we partially affirm, partially vacate, and remand the Board’s decision.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. The company holds Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, which is registered for use with credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998 and carries a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. In using the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve collaborates with a bank, and that bank issues credit cards and maintains customer accounts under the ASPIRE brand. J.A. 6930. Servicing for these accounts is handled by CC Serve and its related companies. Id. Apex Bank is a retail bank based in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. With 18 branches, it provides products such as personal checking and savings accounts, business checking accounts, home mortgage loans, as well as consumer and business loans. Id. Apex does not currently offer credit card products. Id. Apex intends to introduce an internet bank under a distinct brand name, employing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use trademark applications with the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for use with “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While these applications were being examined, CC Serve submitted a protest letter, alleging that Apex’s applied-for marks created a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s existing mark. J.A. 4576. Nevertheless, the examining attorney permitted publication of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, which appeared on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In the matter of APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP., CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex's trademarks in February 2020, asserting that they would create a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve's standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding a probable likelihood of consumer confusion between the marks. J.A. 63. Apex filed an appeal. This Court has jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

We conduct a de novo review of the Board's determinations on legal issues, while examining its factual findings to determine whether they are supported by substantial evidence. See M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, trademark registration must be denied in an opposition proceeding when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The issue of “likelihood of confusion” constitutes a legal question that is predicated on factual determinations pertaining to the DuPont factors. See M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors involves a factual inquiry, and when those findings are challenged on appeal, we evaluate them under the substantial evidence standard. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to address every DuPont factor, but must only address those factors “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

On appeal, Apex contends that the Board made errors in its evaluation of likelihood of confusion, particularly concerning its consideration of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board found that the sixth DuPont factor did not favor Apex, while the first and second DuPont factors supported CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the goods and/or services offered by the parties are. There is no requirement that these services be identical; it is sufficient if the evidence demonstrates that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared CC Serve’s credit card services with Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Noting that “credit card services” was removed from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve obtained its registration, the Board began by interpreting what the designated services encompass. It determined that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. Referring to dictionary definitions, the Board concluded that the terms “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” comprise activities like extending credit or offering funds through credit cards, and therefore, Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board further reviewed third-party trademark registrations covering both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services, finding this supported the conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Given the significant overlap between the parties’ services, the Board found that the second DuPont factor strongly favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex claims that the services at issue are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” highlighting that CC Serve collaborates with banks to manage credit card programs, rather than “providing banking services” itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find this argument persuasive. The Board meticulously reviewed the specific descriptions of each party’s services, and there is substantial evidence in the record to support the Board’s determination that the services are highly similar. We therefore uphold the Board’s conclusion with respect to the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines "[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods." DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. "Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection." Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In situations where the market contains numerous similar marks, it is presumed that consumers are more skilled at differentiating between those marks, which reduces the possibility of confusion between them. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). When evidence demonstrates that consumers have learned to discern among similar marks, it suggests that the mark in question lacks strong market presence. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015).

Apex provided several exhibits to the Board, showing third-party uses of marks containing the term "Aspire." J.A. 40–46. These included use of "Aspire" in association with credit card-related services and, in a broader context, within the financial services sector. The Board held that, given the overlap between the services offered by Apex and CC Serve, the relevant public should be defined as "consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. In its analysis, the Board considered only the marks associated with credit card services and discounted those used in connection with other financial services as "essentially irrelevant." J.A. 47. It ultimately found that there were nine marks using "Aspire" or variants thereof for credit card services, but concluded that the presence of only nine such marks did not amount to "considerable" or "ubiquitous" third-party use indicating weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (addressing "voluminous evidence" of registration and use of paw print designs, noting fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use"); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (referring to 26 third-party marks as "a considerable number").

The Board concluded, "[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]'s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection," and determined that CC Serve's mark is deserving of the "normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks." J.A. 51.

Apex, on appeal, asserts that the Board erred by limiting the relevant public to only consumers of credit card services, and that the Board should also have taken into account the additional 42 third-party marks employing "Aspire" or formative uses thereof. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28.

We concur that the Board’s analysis contained a legal flaw. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to examine similar marks used on similar goods and services. As noted in the Board's evaluation of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board found that the services provided by the parties are highly similar, leading it to conclude that this factor weighed strongly in favor of a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet, in considering the sixth DuPont factor, the Board narrowed its consideration only to marks associated with credit card services, excluding those linked to other banking and financing services. This limitation constituted an error. Our precedent establishes that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand the goods be identical—similarity is sufficient. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. Here, we find the Board’s definition of similarity overly restrictive. Where the Board has already found the services to be highly similar—indeed, at times even legally identical—under the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, the Board should apply the same standard of similarity when assessing the sixth factor. We see no justification for imposing a more stringent test for similarity under

C

The first DuPont factor evaluates the degree of similarity or difference between the marks as a whole, taking into account their appearance, sound, meaning, and overall commercial impression. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the total commercial impression the marks create and whether that impression is likely to lead to confusion about the origin of the respective services. In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the commercial impression plays a pivotal role in the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s analysis under this factor because reevaluation of the sixth DuPont factor could yield a different conclusion regarding the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and, therefore, could alter the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand for the Board to reconsider the first factor as well.

We have reviewed the remaining contentions raised by the parties and do not find them convincing. The Board’s reasoning concerning the second DuPont factor is affirmed, as there was no legal error and its conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. However, we set aside the Board’s determinations relating to DuPont factors one and six, and remand for the Board to reevaluate the number and nature of similar marks in use on comparable goods, as well as the look, sound, meaning, and overall commercial impression of the marks, taking into account the Board’s determination regarding the high similarity of the services. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
