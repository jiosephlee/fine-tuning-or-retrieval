Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges a ruling from the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board that denied the registration of Apex’s trademarks. As the Board made errors regarding its evaluation of two elements in the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we partially affirm, partially vacate, and remand the matter.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark in connection with those services. Id. The ASPIRE mark was registered in 1998, with an effective priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve offers its credit card services under the ASPIRE mark by partnering with banks, which issue ASPIRE-branded credit cards and corresponding accounts to clients. J.A. 6930. CC Serve and its affiliates manage these accounts. Id. Apex Bank, a retail bank incorporated in Tennessee, operates 18 branch offices and provides various products such as personal checking and savings accounts, business checking, home mortgages, and consumer and commercial loans. J.A. 7029. Credit cards, however, are not among its offerings. Id. Apex intends to launch an online banking platform under a separate brand, adopting the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “banking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While these applications were pending, CC Serve lodged a letter of protest claiming that Apex’s marks were likely to be confused with its own. J.A. 4576. Despite this protest, the examining attorney approved both the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, and these marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there existed a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s ASPIRE standard character mark. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board found in favor of the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), determining that the marks were likely to cause confusion among consumers. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently filed an appeal. Our court’s jurisdiction is grounded in 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We conduct a de novo review of the Board’s legal determinations, while its factual findings are reviewed to determine if they are supported by substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence constitutes “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). According to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition requires the denial of registration where “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The issue of “likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors presents a factual issue, with findings on these factors subject to review for substantial evidence on appeal when challenged. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to analyze all the DuPont factors, but only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

In its appeal, Apex contends that the Board made mistakes in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its assessment of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex, while the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how alike the parties’ goods or services are. The services in question need not be the same; rather, the relevant evidence must demonstrate that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). Here, the Board evaluated the similarity between the credit card services provided by CC Serve and the banking and financing services offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Due to the removal of the “credit card services” entry from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board initially clarified the scope of the referenced services. The Board interpreted “credit card services” to include “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board then observed that since the definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” involve extending credit or supplying funds via credit card issuance, Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Additionally, the Board reviewed third-party registrations encompassing both (1) credit card and (2) banking and financing services to reinforce its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. In light of this significant overlap between the parties’ services, the Board determined that the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor weighed strongly in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. Apex, on appeal, contends that the parties’ services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve’s activities consist of collaborating with banks to manage credit card programs—rather than “providing banking services” directly. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s position. The Board thoroughly assessed the parties’ service descriptions, and there is substantial evidence to uphold the Board’s determination that their services are highly similar. We therefore affirm the Board’s finding regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. “Evidence of third-party usage of comparable marks on comparable goods is pertinent to establishing that a mark is comparatively weak and thus deserving of only a narrow scope of protection.” Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). In a marketplace filled with similar marks, the principle is that consumers become proficient at distinguishing between marks, resulting in a reduced likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence demonstrating that consumers can differentiate between such marks typically reflects limited commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex presented several exhibits before the Board, documenting third-party uses of marks incorporating the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks utilized “Aspire” with reference to credit card services, as well as more broadly within the financial services sector. The Board decided that, due to the overlap of Apex’s and CC Serve’s services, the proper relevant public consisted of “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. It limited its analysis to marks associated with credit card services, discounting marks employing “Aspire” for other types of services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks related to credit card services but determined this number fell short of the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” usage required to establish weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (noting “voluminous evidence” of registrations and use of paw print designs and pointing to fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (discussing 26 third-party marks as constituting a “considerable number”). The Board thus concluded that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and found that CC Serve’s mark warranted “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erroneously restricted the relevant public to only consumers of credit card services, and that the Board was also obliged to consider the additional 42 third-party marks incorporating “Aspire” and Aspire-formative components. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We find merit in Apex’s argument that the Board’s approach was legally incorrect. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to assess similar marks covering similar goods and services. In its evaluation of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board found the parties’ services were highly similar, which weighed heavily toward likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. However, in analyzing the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its review to marks associated strictly with credit card services, disregarding those connected to other financial and banking services. This was a mistake. Our precedent establishes that the sixth DuPont factor requires only similarity in goods—not identity. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this instance, the Board adopted an overly restrictive standard of similarity. Having previously found, in its assessment of the second DuPont factor, that the services are highly similar or even partially legally identical, J.A. 27–28, the Board was required to maintain an equivalent scope when considering similarity under the other factors. There is no justification for imposing a narrower and more demanding legal threshold for similarity for purposes of the sixth DuPont factor. The initial DuPont factor evaluates the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the overall commercial impression and assesses whether the use of the respective marks on the identified services is likely to cause confusion about their source. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the first DuPont factor's analysis is informed by commercial impression, and since a reassessment of the sixth DuPont factor could lead to a new conclusion regarding the strength or weakness of the mark that in turn influences the overall commercial impression, we must also set aside the Board’s analysis on this point. Accordingly, we remand so that the Board may also reconsider the first DuPont factor.

We have reviewed the additional arguments put forward by the parties and do not find them persuasive. We uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor, concluding that the Board’s legal reasoning was sound and that substantial evidence supports its finding that the parties offer highly similar services. However, we set aside the Board’s findings concerning DuPont factors one and six, and remand the matter so that the Board may reconsider the number and nature of similar marks used in connection with similar goods, as well as assess the appearance, sound, meaning, and commercial impression of the marks, given its determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
