Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s denial of registration for Apex’s marks. As the Board committed errors regarding its evaluation of two specific factors in the likelihood-of-confusion assessment, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to customers. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the trademark ASPIRE as used with credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, with a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve delivers credit card services under the ASPIRE mark by partnering with a bank, which issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and accounts to its clients. J.A. 6930. These accounts are managed by CC Serve and its affiliates. Id. Apex Bank, a retail bank headquartered in Tennessee, has 18 branches and provides products such as personal checking and savings accounts, business checking, home mortgage loans, and consumer and business loans. J.A. 7029. Apex Bank does not issue credit cards. Id. Apex intends to launch an internet banking service under a separate brand, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office for registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks in connection with “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During examination, CC Serve lodged a letter of protest claiming that Apex’s proposed marks bore a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s existing mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this objection, the trademark examiner approved Apex’s ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for publication, which occurred on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition to Apex's trademarks, claiming there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve's own standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board granted the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that there was a probable risk of consumer confusion between the marks. J.A. 63. Apex then appealed, and our court exercises jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We apply de novo review to the legal determinations of the [B]oard, while its factual determinations are evaluated under the substantial evidence standard.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence is defined as “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, an opposition to a trademark must result in refusal of registration if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Determining a “likelihood of confusion” constitutes a question of law that relies upon factual findings pertaining to the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to analyze every DuPont factor, but only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

On appeal, Apex contends that the Board made errors in evaluating the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its treatment of the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex’s position, while both the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates the likeness between the goods and/or services offered by the parties. It is not necessary for the services to be identical; rather, the evidence must demonstrate that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board analyzed the resemblances between the credit card services provided by CC Serve and the banking and financing services of Apex. J.A. 24–28. Due to the removal of “credit card services” from the Trademark ID Manual after CC Serve’s registration, the Board first interpreted the listed services’ scope. It concluded that “credit card services” included activities such as issuing credit cards for financing purchases, advising about credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, authorizing credit card use, monitoring and providing alerts for credit cards, managing credit card accounts, and enabling access to credit scores. J.A. 25. Referring to the dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance,” which encompass extending credit or offering funds through credit card issuance, the Board found that the services of Apex and CC Serve were “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. In addition, the Board reviewed third-party registrations for both credit card services and banking and financing services to bolster its conclusion that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Based on the significant similarity between the parties’ services, the Board determined that the second factor in APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 substantially favored a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services offered are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” emphasizing that CC Serve operates credit card programs in partnership with banks rather than independently offering banking services. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s position persuasive. The Board thoroughly assessed the descriptions for each party’s services, and substantial evidence underpins its determination that the services are highly similar. We uphold the Board’s finding concerning the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. According to Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), third-party use of similar marks on related goods can demonstrate that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a limited scope of protection. When many similar marks are used in the same field, it is presumed that consumers are better able to differentiate among them and, as a result, there is less likelihood of confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence showing that consumers have learned to tell apart such marks often indicates that the mark lacks significant commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex presented multiple exhibits to the Board reflecting third-party uses of marks that incorporate the word “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks used “Aspire” in connection with credit card services and, more broadly, within the financial services sector. The Board determined that, given the overlap in Apex and CC Serve’s offerings, the correct relevant public consisted of “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. It concentrated solely on marks linked to credit card services and considered those employing Aspire for other types of services to be “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-based marks for credit card services but found that only nine instances did not amount to the “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use demonstrating weakness, as described in Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2 (noting “voluminous evidence” of registration and use of similar paw print designs and pointing to fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”), and Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (citing 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board concluded that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and found that CC Serve’s mark should receive the “normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51.

On appeal, Apex argues that the Board erred in limiting the relevant consumers to those of credit card services and asserts that the Board should have taken into account the 42 additional third-party marks utilizing Aspire and Aspire-formative marks. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We agree that there was a legal flaw in the Board’s analysis. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to review similar marks that are used for similar goods and services. In evaluating the second DuPont factor (see infra Section III.A), the Board found that the parties’ services are highly similar, ultimately giving that factor substantial weight in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Nevertheless, when it addressed the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its examination to marks related exclusively to credit card services and omitted those concerning other banking and financial services. This was incorrect. Our precedent establishes that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand identical goods, only goods that are similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. We find the Board’s construction of similarity here to be overly restrictive. Once the Board has determined that the services in question are highly similar—indeed, in some respects legally identical—under the second DuPont factor, J.A. 27–28, it should use that same degree of similarity when considering the sixth factor. There is no justification for applying a more rigorous legal test for similarity in the context of the sixth DuPont factor. C

The initial DuPont factor examines the extent to which the marks are alike or unalike in their overall appearance, pronunciation, meaning, and the commercial impression they create. See DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the total commercial impression conveyed by the marks and whether there is a likelihood that consumers will be confused regarding the origin of the services associated with each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the evaluation of commercial impression is integral to the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s assessment on this point, as a reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor could potentially lead to a different conclusion regarding the mark's commercial strength or weakness, thereby influencing the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we remand the matter for reevaluation of the first factor as well.

We have reviewed the additional contentions raised by the parties and conclude that they lack merit. We uphold the Board’s reasoning with respect to the second DuPont factor, as the Board’s legal conclusions were correct and its determination that the parties’ services are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions on DuPont factors six and one, and remand for the Board to evaluate the number and character of analogous marks used on comparable goods, as well as the marks’ visual appearance, pronunciation, meaning, and overall commercial impression, considering its finding regarding the high similarity of the parties’ services. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
