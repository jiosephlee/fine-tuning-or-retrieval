Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s denial of registration for its marks. Since the Board made errors regarding two elements of the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we affirm in part, vacate in part, and remand for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve is engaged in providing credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which is used in association with credit card services. Id. This registration was granted in 1998, with a priority date effective as of October 17, 1996. Id. CC Serve’s credit card services under the ASPIRE mark involve collaboration with a bank that issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards and corresponding accounts to consumers. J.A. 6930. Servicing of these accounts is handled by CC Serve and its affiliates. Id. Apex Bank, a retail banking institution incorporated in Tennessee, maintains 18 branch offices and provides services including personal checking and savings accounts, business checking, home mortgages, and both consumer and business loans. J.A. 7029. Apex Bank does not, however, offer credit cards. Id. Apex intends to establish an online banking platform under a separate brand, utilizing the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking to register the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, arguing that Apex’s proposed marks were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s own mark. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the examining attorney permitted the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks to move forward for publication, and the marks were published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, claiming that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s ASPIRE standard character mark. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), determining that consumers were likely to be confused by the marks. J.A. 63. Apex then appealed. Our jurisdiction is proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We assess the Board’s conclusions of law de novo and review its factual determinations for substantial evidence.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence means “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition must be sustained when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The determination of likelihood of confusion is a legal question informed by subsidiary factual findings, specifically those made pursuant to the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors is a factual question, and findings on these points are reviewed for substantial evidence on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obligated to address every DuPont factor, but rather only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

In its appeal, Apex contends that the Board made mistakes in assessing the likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding the first, second, and sixth DuPont factors. According to the Board, the sixth DuPont factor does not support Apex's position, while the first and second factors favor CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We will consider each of these factors individually.

A

The second DuPont factor examines how similar the parties’ goods and/or services are. The services in question do not need to be exactly the same—what must be shown is that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board analyzed the resemblance between CC Serve’s credit card services and Apex’s banking and financing services. J.A. 24–28. Because the Trademark ID Manual entry for “credit card services” was removed following CC Serve’s registration, the Board first clarified the meaning of those listed services. The Board concluded that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. It further found that, since definitions for “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include the extension of credit or providing funds via credit card issuance, Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also reviewed third-party registrations encompassing both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services to reinforce its determination that “the services are of a type that may emanate from a single source under one mark.” J.A. 33–34. Given the significant overlap between the parties’ services, the Board determined the second APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. 5 factor strongly favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive with each other,” particularly because CC Serve works with banks to operate credit card programs and “not providing banking services” on its own. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s position persuasive. The Board thoroughly examined both parties’ service descriptions, and substantial evidence supports its conclusion that the services are highly similar. We therefore affirm the Board’s finding on the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor evaluates “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. “Evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to demonstrate that a mark is comparatively weak and thus entitled to only a limited scope of protection.” Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005). The rationale is that, in a marketplace crowded with comparable marks, consumers are more likely to be able to distinguish between marks and thus are less likely to be confused by their similarities. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Evidence that consumers have learned to differentiate marks in this manner supports a finding of reduced commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex submitted multiple exhibits to the Board, demonstrating third-party uses of marks that incorporate the term “Aspire.” J.A. 40–46. These marks featured the word “Aspire” in association with credit card services and, more generally, within the financial services sector. The Board determined that, due to the overlap between the services provided by Apex and CC Serve, the relevant public is “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. Accordingly, the Board confined its analysis to marks specifically identified with credit card services and disregarded marks using “Aspire” for other types of services as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. The Board identified nine Aspire-formative marks for credit card services, but concluded that the existence of only nine such uses did not reach the threshold of “considerable” or “ubiquitous” usage sufficient to demonstrate weakness, as discussed in Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2 (noting “voluminous evidence” of paw print mark registrations and usage, with fourteen “notable examples”), and Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (where 26 third-party marks constituted “a considerable number”). The Board concluded, “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and accordingly determined that CC Serve’s mark merits “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex asserts that the Board erred by confining the relevant public to consumers of credit card services, and maintains that the Board should have taken into account the 42 additional third-party marks containing Aspire or Aspire-formative elements. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We agree that the Board’s approach was legally incorrect. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to consider similar marks used for similar goods and services. In its analysis of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar, leading to the conclusion that this factor strongly favored a finding of likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Nevertheless, when assessing the sixth DuPont factor, the Board narrowed the range of considered marks to only those related to credit card services, and excluded marks associated with other banking and financial services. This limitation was improper. Our precedent establishes that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand identical goods—only that the goods or services be similar. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this case, the Board’s interpretation of “similarity” was excessively restrictive. Given that the Board previously determined in the second DuPont factor analysis that the services are highly similar, and in part legally identical, J.A. 27–28, the Board should apply that same breadth of similarity to its consideration under the other DuPont factors. C

The initial DuPont factor assesses how alike or different the marks are in their entireties, taking into account their appearance, sound, meaning, and the commercial impression they create. DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the overall commercial impression of the marks and the likelihood that consumers will be confused about the origin of the services associated with each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the commercial impression is a key aspect of the first DuPont factor, we must also set aside the Board’s analysis here, as a new evaluation of the sixth DuPont factor could lead to a different assessment of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness, thereby influencing the total commercial impression. Accordingly, we also remand for the Board to reconsider the first DuPont factor.

We have reviewed the parties’ additional contentions and conclude that they lack merit. We uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor, as there was no legal error in its reasoning and the record contains substantial evidence supporting its conclusion that the services offered by the parties are very similar. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions concerning DuPont factors six and one, and remand the matter for the Board to re-examine the number and nature of comparable marks used with analogous goods, as well as to reassess the appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression of the marks, taking into account its determination that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
