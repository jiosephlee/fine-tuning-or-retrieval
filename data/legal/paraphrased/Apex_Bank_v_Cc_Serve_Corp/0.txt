HUGHES, Circuit Judge.

Apex Bank challenges a determination by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board that denied registration of its marks. Since the Board made mistakes in evaluating two factors within the likelihood-of-confusion framework, we partially affirm, partially vacate, and remand the case for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve operates as a provider of credit card services to consumers. J.A. 6927–28. The company holds Registration No. 2126948 for the word mark ASPIRE, which is registered specifically for use with credit card services. Id. The ASPIRE mark’s registration dates back to 1998 and carries a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. Through the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve delivers credit card services in collaboration with a partner bank; this bank is responsible for issuing credit cards and accounts bearing the ASPIRE brand to users. J.A. 6930. Account management for these cards is handled by CC Serve and its affiliates. Id. Apex Bank, in contrast, is a Tennessee-chartered retail banking institution. J.A. 7029. With 18 branch locations, Apex provides a variety of banking services, such as personal and business checking and savings accounts, home mortgages, and loans for both consumers and businesses. Id. Notably, Apex does not provide credit card offerings. Id. Apex intends to launch an online banking platform under a new brand that utilizes the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex submitted intent-to-use applications to the United States Patent and Trademark Office, seeking registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for use in “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. During the application process, CC Serve filed a letter of protest, arguing that Apex’s proposed marks could likely be confused with its own. J.A. 4576. Despite this objection, the examining attorney granted approval for the publication of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, and they were officially published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, claiming that they were likely to be confused with CC Serve’s standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board upheld the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that there was a probable risk of consumer confusion between the marks. J.A. 63. Apex subsequently appealed. Our jurisdiction is conferred by 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

We assess the Board’s legal determinations de novo, while its factual determinations are reviewed for substantial evidence. See M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). Substantial evidence refers to “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). According to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, registration of a trademark must be denied when “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). Whether a likelihood of confusion exists constitutes a legal issue that relies on subsidiary factual determinations based on the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. “Each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not obliged to evaluate every DuPont factor, but only those “that are relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in its assessment of likelihood of confusion, particularly regarding its consideration of the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor did not support Apex's position, whereas the first and second factors favored CC Serve. J.A. 62–63. We examine each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how closely the goods or services offered by each party resemble one another. Exact equivalence between the services is not required; rather, the evidence must demonstrate that "the respective products are related in some manner and/or [that] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source." Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). The Board compared the nature of credit card services provided by CC Serve with the banking and financing services offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” had been removed from the Trademark ID Manual following CC Serve’s registration, the Board first analyzed the scope of the services as identified in the record. It concluded that “credit card services” includes not only issuing credit cards for purchasing but also covers advising on credit card debt, handling credit card transactions and payments, authorizing credit cards, monitoring credit activity, sending alerts, managing credit card accounts, and giving access to credit scores. J.A. 25. Further, the Board found that the dictionary meanings of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” all include the extension of credit or funding via the issuance of credit cards, rendering the services of Apex and CC Serve at least “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. The Board also reviewed examples of third-party registrations that included both (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services, reinforcing its conclusion that such services could plausibly originate from the same source under a single mark. J.A. 33–34. Based on this substantial overlap, the Board determined that the high degree of similarity between the services weighed heavily in favor of finding a likelihood of confusion under the second factor in APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. J.A. 35. On appeal, Apex contends that the parties’ offerings differ and are not in direct competition, emphasizing that CC Serve partners with banks to administer credit card programs instead of providing banking services itself. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We do not find Apex’s contentions persuasive. The Board conducted a thorough review of the service descriptions, and the evidence on the record supports its conclusion that the parties’ services are closely related. Therefore, we affirm the Board’s decision regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor examines “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. According to Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), evidence of third-party use of analogous marks for similar goods is significant as it demonstrates a mark’s relative weakness and that its protective scope should be limited. In situations where many comparable marks are present in a particular commercial area, the rationale is that consumers are accustomed to discerning between such marks and are thus less likely to experience confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Proof that customers have learned to differentiate among such marks often supports a finding of limited commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). In this case, Apex provided the Board with several exhibits demonstrating third-party usage of marks featuring the word “Aspire”. J.A. 40–46. These marks were used in association with credit card services as well as, more generally, within the broader financial services sector. The Board determined that, due to the similarity of Apex’s and CC Serve’s offerings, the appropriate relevant public comprised “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. The Board consequently restricted its focus to marks designated for credit card services and dismissed uses of “Aspire” with other financial offerings as “essentially irrelevant.” J.A. 47. It further identified nine marks incorporating “Aspire” for credit card services, but concluded this did not represent a “considerable” or “ubiquitous” use as required to establish weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2, which discussed “voluminous evidence” of the registration and use of certain design elements, noting fourteen “notable examples”; and Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015), which described 26 third-party marks as “a considerable number”). The Board found that “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” and thus decided that CC Serve’s mark warranted “the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. On appeal, Apex argued that the Board erred by confining the relevant public solely to credit card service consumers, and that it should have also taken into account 42 other third-party uses of the “Aspire” mark or its variants. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s analysis was legally deficient. The sixth DuPont factor obligates the Board to account for similar marks in relation to similar, not just identical, goods and services. As reflected in its consideration of the second DuPont factor (see infra Section III.A), the Board found the parties’ services to be highly similar, and on that basis, the second factor heavily favored a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet, in addressing the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited its examination to marks associated strictly with credit card services, disregarding those related to other banking and financial activities. This was erroneous. Precedent makes clear that the sixth DuPont factor covers similar goods, not exclusively identical ones. See, for example, Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. In this context, the Board’s conception of similarity was overly restrictive. Where the Board has already established in its findings on the second DuPont factor that the parties’ services are highly similar, and in part legally identical, J.A. 27–28, its evaluation of similarity in other factors, including the sixth, should reflect the same breadth. There is no justification for imposing a narrower legal standard for similarity when addressing the sixth DuPont factor. C

The initial DuPont factor assesses the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. The key consideration is the total commercial impression created by the marks and whether consumers are likely to be confused regarding the origin of the services associated with each mark. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Given that commercial impression plays a central role in evaluating the first DuPont factor, we find it necessary to also vacate the Board’s assessment under this factor. This is because a revised consideration of the sixth DuPont factor could yield a different conclusion about the strength or weakness of the mark and alter the overall commercial impression. Consequently, we remand for the Board to revisit its analysis of the first DuPont factor.

After reviewing the parties’ additional contentions, we remain unpersuaded. We uphold the Board’s conclusions regarding the second DuPont factor, as its legal reasoning was sound and there is substantial evidence indicating a high degree of similarity between the services offered by the parties. However, with respect to the Board’s determinations on the sixth and first DuPont factors, we set aside those findings and remand for reconsideration. On remand, the Board is directed to re-examine the number and nature of comparable marks utilized with similar goods, as well as to reassess the visual, phonetic, connotative, and overall commercial impressions of the marks, taking into account its prior finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
