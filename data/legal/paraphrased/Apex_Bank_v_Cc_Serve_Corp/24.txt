Title: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit
Date: September 25, 2025
Docket: No. 2023-2143
Author: Hughes, Circuit Judge

Apex Bank challenges the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s ruling denying registration of its trademarks. Since the Board committed errors regarding its assessment of two factors in the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry, we uphold part of its decision, set aside another part, and send the case back for further proceedings.

I

CC Serve provides credit card services to its customers. J.A. 6927–28. It holds Registration No. 2126948 for the ASPIRE word mark, which is utilized in the context of credit card services. Id. The registration for the ASPIRE mark was granted in 1998, carrying a priority date of October 17, 1996. Id. In offering credit card services under the ASPIRE mark, CC Serve partners with a bank that issues credit cards and establishes accounts under the ASPIRE brand for customers. J.A. 6930. CC Serve, together with its affiliates, services these accounts. Id. Apex Bank is a retail banking institution based in Tennessee. J.A. 7029. Operating out of 18 branches, Apex Bank provides personal checking, personal savings, business checking, home mortgage services, and both consumer and business loans. Id. Apex Bank does not issue credit cards. Id. The bank intends to launch an online bank with a new brand that uses the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. J.A. 5243. In August 2019, Apex filed intent-to-use applications at the United States Patent and Trademark Office seeking registration of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “[b]anking and financing services.” J.A. 7027. While those applications were under examination, CC Serve submitted a letter of protest, claiming that Apex’s proposed marks closely resembled CC Serve’s mark in a way that could lead to confusion. J.A. 4576. Despite this, the trademark examiner authorized publication of the ASPIRE BANK word and design marks, which were subsequently published on December 17, 2019. J.A. 4569–70.

APEX BANK v. CC SERVE CORP. In February 2020, CC Serve filed an opposition against Apex’s trademarks, asserting that there was a likelihood of confusion with CC Serve’s registered standard character mark, ASPIRE. J.A. 2, 6931. The Board granted the opposition pursuant to Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), finding that there was a probable risk of confusion among consumers regarding the marks. J.A. 63. Apex sought review of this decision. Our court possesses jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B).

II

“We assess the Board’s determinations of law anew, giving no deference, while we examine its factual determinations under the substantial evidence standard.” M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006) (internal citation omitted). The substantial evidence threshold is met by “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938). Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, a trademark opposition must be sustained and registration denied if “confusion is likely because of concurrent use of the marks of an applicant and a prior user on their respective goods.” Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357, 1360 (C.C.P.A. 1973). The determination of “likelihood of confusion” constitutes a legal issue, rooted in subsidiary findings of fact addressed by the DuPont factors. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381. Each of the thirteen DuPont factors embodies a factual inquiry, and any findings as to those factors are reviewed for substantial evidence if contested on appeal. Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002). The Board is not required to analyze all DuPont factors, only those that are “relevant and of record.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

III

Apex contends on appeal that the Board made errors in its assessment of likelihood of confusion, focusing particularly on the second, sixth, and first DuPont factors. The Board determined that the sixth DuPont factor was not favorable to Apex, while the first and second factors supported CC Serve’s position. J.A. 62–63. We consider each of these factors individually below.

A

The second DuPont factor evaluates how similar the goods or services of the parties are. Exact identity is not necessary; it suffices if “the respective products are related in some manner and/or [if] the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012) (internal citation omitted). In this case, the Board analyzed the resemblance between the credit card services provided by CC Serve and the banking and financing services offered by Apex. J.A. 24–28. Since “credit card services” had been deleted from the Trademark ID Manual following CC Serve’s registration, the Board first needed to interpret the scope of those services. It determined that “credit card services” includes “issuing credit cards for use to finance purchases as well as counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board found that because standard definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” cover extending credit or issuing credit cards, the services provided by Apex and CC Serve are “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. Furthermore, the Board examined third-party registrations spanning (1) credit card services and (2) banking and financing services, which supported its conclusion that such services can originate from a single provider under one trademark. J.A. 33–34. Based on the marked similarity between the parties’ offerings, the Board found that the second DuPont factor strongly supported a likelihood of confusion. J.A. 35. Apex challenges this finding on appeal, contending that the services are “not the same and . . . are not directly competitive,” especially given that CC Serve collaborates with banks to run credit card programs and does not itself provide banking services. Appellant’s Opening Br. 37. We are not persuaded by Apex’s position. The Board conducted a thorough review of the service descriptions and the record contains substantial evidence to support its conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar. Therefore, we uphold the Board’s determination regarding the second DuPont factor.

B

The sixth DuPont factor addresses “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. As stated in Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005), “[e]vidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” In markets crowded with similar marks, consumers are generally believed to be better equipped to differentiate among those marks and less prone to confusion. See Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Showing that consumers have learned to distinguish between such marks can support a finding of diminished commercial strength. See Jack Wolfskin Ausrustung Fur Draussen GmbH & Co. KGAA v. New Millennium Sports, S.L.U., 797 F.3d 1363, 1374 (Fed. Cir. 2015). Apex provided the Board with several exhibits documenting third-party use of marks containing the term “Aspire”. J.A. 40–46. These marks employed the word “Aspire” for credit card services and more broadly across the financial services field. The Board decided that due to the overlap in services provided by Apex and CC Serve, the relevant public consisted of “consumers of ‘credit card services.’” J.A. 47. The Board then concentrated exclusively on marks used for credit card services and disregarded as “essentially irrelevant” those Aspire-formative marks applied to other services. J.A. 47. Ultimately, the Board determined there were nine third-party Aspire-formative marks in the credit card sector but concluded that this level of use did not rise to “considerable” or “ubiquitous” as required to establish weakness. J.A. 47 (citing Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373, 1373 n.2) (addressing “voluminous evidence” regarding paw print designs and specifically noting fourteen “notable examples of third-party registration and use”); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1337 n.1, 1339 (Fed. Cir. 2015) (finding 26 third-party marks “a considerable number”). According to the Board, “[CC Serve] has not shown that its mark has any particular commercial strength, but [Apex] has not shown that [CC Serve]’s mark is commercially or conceptually weak such that it is entitled to a narrow scope of protection,” so CC Serve’s mark “is entitled to the normal scope of protection accorded inherently distinctive marks.” J.A. 51. Apex argues on appeal that the Board erred by confining the relevant public to consumers of credit card services and should have taken into account the 42 other third-party marks that use Aspire or Aspire-formative terms. Appellant’s Opening Br. 28. We concur that the Board’s analysis was legally deficient. The sixth DuPont factor obliges the Board to consider similar marks on similar goods and services. In its consideration of the second DuPont factor, see infra Section III.A, the Board found the parties’ services to be highly similar, leading it to find that this factor strongly supported the likelihood of confusion. J.A. 27–28, 33–34. Yet when applying the sixth DuPont factor, the Board limited itself to marks for credit card services only, excluding those linked to other banking and financing services. That limitation constitutes error. Our precedents clarify that the sixth DuPont factor does not demand identical goods, but only similar ones. See, e.g., Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation, Inc., 794 F.3d at 1338. We determine here that the Board’s interpretation of similarity was too restricted. After finding as a matter of fact under the second DuPont factor that the services are highly similar—indeed, in part legally identical—the Board was required to use the same standard for similarity with respect to the other DuPont factors. We perceive no justification for adopting a more rigorous standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor. Therefore, we vacate the Board’s findings related to the sixth DuPont factor and remand for further proceedings to reconsider the proper scope of third-party marks in light of the Board’s determination that the parties’ services are highly similar.

C

The initial DuPont factor evaluates the “similarity or dissimilarity of the marks in their entireties as to appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression.” DuPont, 476 F.2d at 1361. This inquiry centers on the overall commercial impression created by the marks and assesses whether there is a likelihood of confusion regarding the origin of the services each mark represents. See In re I.AM.Symbolic, LLC, 866 F.3d 1315, 1323 (Fed. Cir. 2017). Since the analysis of commercial impression is integral to the first DuPont factor, we also find it necessary to vacate the Board’s findings on this factor; a reassessment of the sixth DuPont factor could potentially alter the evaluation of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and thus impact the overall commercial impression. Accordingly, we also remand for the Board to reconsider the first DuPont factor.

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by the parties and do not find them convincing. We uphold the Board’s determination concerning the second DuPont factor, as the Board made no legal error and its conclusion that the parties’ services are highly similar is supported by substantial evidence. However, we set aside the Board’s conclusions regarding DuPont factors one and six, remanding the case for the Board to reassess both the quantity and nature of analogous marks in use with similar goods, as well as the marks’ appearance, sound, meaning, and commercial impression, taking into account its determination that the services offered by the parties are highly similar. AFFIRMED IN PART, VACATED IN PART, AND

REMANDED
