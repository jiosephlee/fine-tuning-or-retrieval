Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former Congressman, challenges the final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law brought against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the network responsible for broadcasting Kimmel’s late-night program, Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. The litigation arose from an incident where Kimmel, employing several assumed identities, submitted requests through the Cameo service seeking personalized videos from Santos, all of which Santos subsequently delivered. These videos were later featured on JKL in a satirical segment known as “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court granted dismissal pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, determining that the fair use doctrine precluded the copyright claims and that the state law claims were either superseded by the Copyright Act or inadequately pled. Finding ourselves in agreement that the copyright claims are indeed foreclosed by the fair use doctrine and also concluding that none of the state law causes of action state a valid claim for relief, we affirm the District Court’s decision.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s order dismissing Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our analysis takes into account “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached as exhibits to the complaint, and documents that are incorporated by reference within the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we may take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In copyright litigation, the affirmative defense of fair use “excuses conduct that might otherwise constitute infringement, permitting courts to avoid the strict enforcement of the Copyright Act where doing so would hinder the creativity the Act is designed to foster.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, the determination of fair use involves weighing several non-exhaustive considerations: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the "purpose and character of the use" prong, our analysis centers on the extent to which the secondary use is transformative—that is, whether the new work merely replaces the original's purpose or instead introduces new content, serving a further or different purpose. Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach is grounded in the principle that "fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context." Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). In some instances, the availability of the fair use defense may be "so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" even at the pleading stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. asserts that the use lacks transformative quality, arguing that Kimmel "instigated the creation of the original Works" and therefore is the "designer[]" of their original intent. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court's determination that the intended purpose of the purportedly infringing use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, Santos contends that this was his own original purpose in making the videos. However, the inquiry into whether a secondary use is transformative depends on the perception of a reasonable observer and not on the subjective motives of the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As Santos concedes in his original allegation, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as communicating “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than a willingness to utter absurdities for financial gain. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations amount to bad faith, thereby vitiating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are not persuaded by this argument. Although the Supreme Court did state in Harper & Row that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (internal quotation marks omitted), the Court has since stepped back from this pronouncement. Moreover, the allegations in Santos’s complaint do not support the assertion that Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the complaint suggests that Defendants acted out of (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Therefore, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of fair use.

We are likewise unconvincing by Santos’s objections to the District Court’s evaluation of the other pertinent factors. Specifically, the applicable copyright registrations indicate that the videos were published simultaneously with their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, it is apparent from the face of Santos’s complaint that there has been no market harm as contemplated by the fourth fair use factor, since “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Since our determination of the fair use issue requires us to review solely "the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue," as established in Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s finding that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims even at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s dismissal of his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found the breach of contract and breach of implied contract claims to be preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos contends that these claims differ in substance from a copyright infringement claim and are therefore not preempted, citing Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). We do not reach a determination on the preemption question because, regardless, Santos’s contract-based state law claims do not adequately plead entitlement to relief under applicable state law.

We begin our analysis with Santos’s claim for direct breach of contract arising under the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos is not a signatory to these Terms of Service, which are binding on users such as Kimmel and which Santos claims were violated. Illinois law, which governs these Terms of Service, strongly disfavors extending contractual benefits to individuals who are not parties to the contract. See Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, the requirement that a contract is intended to benefit third parties must be so clearly stated as to approximate an explicit declaration. Id. (internal quotation marks omitted). In the present matter, Santos points to no “express declaration” that would make the pertinent sections of the Terms of Service applicable to him or other persons not party to the contract. On the contrary, certain other portions of the Terms of Service contain express language granting third-party beneficiary status, which demonstrates that, where the signatories meant for provisions to be enforceable by third parties, they did so unequivocally.

Turning to New York law, which controls this particular claim, courts assess “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” when determining whether an implied-in-fact contract has been created, and to what extent. Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Yet, Santos’s complaint fails to “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting the core terms of the supposed implied contract that Defendants purportedly breached). Furthermore, Santos has not credibly asserted that there was “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” such that Kimmel would be obligated to the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also contests the District Court's decision to dismiss his fraudulent inducement claim on the basis that he did not plead any actual out-of-pocket damages, as mandated by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court's finding that Santos did not claim to have incurred any concrete out-of-pocket loss as a consequence of the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court's dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

Having reviewed Santos’s additional arguments, we find them to lack merit. Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we AFFIRM the District Court’s judgment.
