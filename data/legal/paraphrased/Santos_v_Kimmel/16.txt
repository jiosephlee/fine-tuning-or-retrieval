Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former member of Congress, appeals from a final judgment entered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote) that dismissed his copyright infringement and related state law claims brought against Jimmy Kimmel; American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the network airing Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”); and ABC’s parent, The Walt Disney Company. The underlying litigation stems from events in which Kimmel, using aliases, requested personalized videos from Santos through the Cameo website. Santos completed the video requests, after which Kimmel broadcast the clips on JKL in a series labeled “Will Santos Say It?”, which parodied Santos. The District Court granted dismissal under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), finding that the fair use doctrine precluded the copyright claims and that the state law causes of action were either preempted by the Copyright Act or otherwise inadequately pled. Concluding both that the fair use doctrine bars Santos’s copyright claims and that all of Santos’s state law claims fail to state a claim upon which relief can be granted, we affirm the judgment of the District Court.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court's decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our evaluation encompasses “the facts presented in the complaint, materials appended as exhibits to the complaint, and documents explicitly incorporated by reference therein.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as recorded in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In a lawsuit regarding copyright, the affirmative defense of fair use “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). The Copyright Act provides that, in assessing whether fair use applies, courts examine these non-exclusive factors: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use,” our analysis centers primarily on the extent to which the new use is transformative; that is, we examine whether the subsequent work simply replaces the original or introduces additional elements that serve a distinct objective or exhibit a new character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach reflects our recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). Fair use can, in certain cases, be so evident from the complaint itself as to warrant dismissal of a copyright infringement suit at the pleading stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. maintains that the use lacks transformative character because Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and thus can be viewed as the “designer[]” of the original intent. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the purpose of the allegedly infringing material was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he claims that this commentary was also the motivating purpose behind the creation of his original videos. However, the question of whether the secondary use is transformative is resolved by what a reasonable observer would perceive, not by either party’s subjective motivations. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As Santos himself admitted in the initial allegation, a reasonable observer would view the original videos as communicating “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than a readiness to utter absurdities for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations constitute bad faith, thereby negating any fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are unpersuaded by this argument. While the Supreme Court observed in Harper & Row that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently moved away from emphasizing this principle. Regardless, the factual allegations in Santos’s complaint do not support an inference that Defendants sought to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). In fact, the complaint describes defendants as being driven by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first fair use factor weighs heavily in favor of finding fair use.

We also find Santos’s objections to the District Court’s evaluation of the other pertinent factors unconvincing. In particular, the pertinent copyright registrations show that the videos were both created and published on the same date. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, it is evident from the allegations in Santos’s complaint that no market injury of the type contemplated by the fourth fair use factor has occurred, since “the inquiry is not whether the second work harms the market for the first (for instance, by diminishing its value through parody or critique), but rather whether it displaces the market for the original by providing a substitute that competes with it.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he remote possibility that creators of creative works would approve licenses for critical reviews or parodies of their work excludes those uses from consideration as part of a potential licensing market.”).

Because our determination of “the question of fair use” requires consideration only of “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” as stated in Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s decision that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims even at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further contests the District Court’s decision to dismiss his state law claims of breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court dismissed the breach of contract and breach of implied contract claims, reasoning that the Copyright Act preempted them. Santos counters by asserting that his claims are "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim" and should therefore avoid preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, we do not need to address preemption because, regardless, Santos’s contract claims under state law do not present a viable claim for relief.

Turning first to Santos’s direct breach of contract claim based on the Cameo Terms of Service, Santos is not a signatory to these Terms, which are required of users such as Kimmel and which Santos contends were violated. According to Illinois law, which controls the Terms of Service, there exists a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, for a contract to apply to third parties, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, Santos does not point to anything that comes close to such an “express declaration” suggesting that the provisions at issue apply to him or other third parties. Rather, other parts of the Terms of Service do include explicit language about third-party beneficiaries, showing that the contract’s drafters specified when they wished to allow such enforcement by nonparties.

Regarding his implied contract claim governed by New York law, we evaluate “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to decide whether an implied-in-fact contract existed and its scope. Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Yet, the allegations in Santos’s complaint fail to “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (not specifying the essential terms of any alleged implied contract). Nor does Santos plausibly claim the existence of “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” by which Kimmel agreed to abide by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Lastly, Santos disputes the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement cause of action on the ground that he failed to allege an out-of-pocket loss as mandated by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos did not allege he actually suffered any out-of-pocket damages as a consequence of the alleged fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the dismissal of this claim by the District Court.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
