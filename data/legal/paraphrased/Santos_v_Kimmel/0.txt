Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos challenges a final judgment entered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims for copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. The basis for Santos’s action was that Kimmel, utilizing a variety of pseudonyms, submitted multiple requests via the Cameo platform for Santos to create personalized video messages. After Santos provided the requested videos, Kimmel broadcast them on JKL within a satirical series called “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, determining that the fair use doctrine shielded defendants from the copyright claims and that Santos’s state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to adequately allege a claim. Because we concur that the fair use doctrine bars Santos’s copyright claims, and we further determine that each of Santos’s state law claims does not state a viable claim, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court's Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal of Santos's complaint. See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our assessment encompasses “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In a copyright lawsuit, the fair use affirmative defense "justifies conduct that might otherwise qualify as infringement, permitting courts to forgo strict enforcement of the Copyright Act in situations where doing so would suppress the very creativity the Act seeks to encourage." Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). The Copyright Act instructs courts to weigh the following non-exhaustive considerations in assessing whether fair use applies: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the factor of “purpose and character of the use,” our principal inquiry is the extent to which the use can be considered transformative—that is, whether the secondary work merely replaces the aims of the original creation, or whether it introduces a new element, function, or perspective. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach is rooted in the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). There are circumstances in which fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” even at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. asserts that the use in question was not transformative on the grounds that Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and, therefore, is the “designer[]” of their initial purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the alleged infringing use’s aim was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, his argument is that this was equally his intention in producing the videos. However, the question of whether a secondary use qualifies as transformative depends on the perspective of a reasonable observer, rather than on the personal intent of either the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As Santos himself initially alleged, a reasonable observer would perceive the videos as expressing “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” and not as reflecting a willingness to say absurd things for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s allegedly false statements are evidence of bad faith, thereby vitiating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not find this argument persuasive. Although the Supreme Court in Harper & Row observed that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (internal quotation marks omitted), subsequent decisions by the Court have retreated from this position. Regardless, the allegations in Santos’s complaint undermine any assertion that Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (internal quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the complaint characterizes the defendants as acting out of (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Therefore, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first fair use factor weighs heavily in favor of a finding of fair use.

We likewise find Santos’s objections to the District Court’s treatment of the remaining fair use factors unconvincing. The applicable copyright registrations indicate that the videos were published contemporaneously with their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, Santos’s own complaint makes evident that he has not experienced the type of market harm contemplated by the fourth fair use factor, since, as the relevant inquiry requires, the issue is not whether the second work harms the market for the original—such as through parody or critique—but rather whether it supplants the market for the original by serving as a direct substitute. Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Given that “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” suffice for resolving “the question of fair use,” as stated in Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims even at the motion-to-dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further contests the District Court’s dismissal of his state law claims alleging breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court had dismissed the first two claims on the basis that the Copyright Act preempted them. In response, Santos argues that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and thus should not be preempted. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, we do not need to address the preemption argument because Santos’s contract-based claims do not, in any event, adequately plead a claim for relief under state law.

Turning first to Santos’s breach of contract claim based on the Cameo Terms of Service, it is evident that Santos is not a party to the Terms of Service, which apply to users like Kimmel and are the basis of Santos’s alleged claim. Illinois law, which governs these Terms, maintains a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, Santos does not point to anything resembling such an “express declaration” in the Terms of Service that would extend the relevant provisions to him or other non-parties. Notably, certain other sections of the Terms explicitly provide third-party beneficiary rights, which demonstrates that the parties specified when they intended to permit third-party enforcement of the contract.

With respect to New York law, which applies to the implied contract claim, the court must evaluate “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to ascertain “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). In this matter, Santos’s complaint fails to “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting the essential terms of the purported implied contract allegedly breached by Defendants). Nor does Santos adequately allege facts that would demonstrate “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel was bound by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Lastly, Santos disputes the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim for not alleging any out-of-pocket loss, a requirement under New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos did not allege having sustained any actual out-of-pocket loss resulting from the alleged fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we affirm the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
