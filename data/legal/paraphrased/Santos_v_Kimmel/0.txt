United States Court of Appeals

For the Second Circuit

Docket No. 24-2196-cv

GEORGE SANTOS, Plaintiff-Appellant,

v.

JAMES C. KIMMEL, AKA JIMMY KIMMEL, AMERICAN BROADCASTING COMPANIES, INC., THE WALT DISNEY COMPANY, Defendants-Appellees.

Before:

CABRANES, LOHIER, and SULLIVAN, Circuit Judges.

Former Congressman George Santos initiated legal action against Jimmy Kimmel, the host of the late-night program, as well as the entities responsible for producing and broadcasting Jimmy Kimmel Live!, alleging violations of copyright, breach of contract under state law, and fraudulent inducement. The litigation stemmed from instances where Kimmel, employing assumed identities, submitted multiple requests for customized video messages from Santos via the Cameo service. After Santos provided the requested videos, Kimmel incorporated excerpts from them into a recurring segment on his show, entitled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed the suit on the basis that the complaint did not present a viable legal claim, specifically determining that the copyright allegations were negated by the fair use doctrine, and that the claims arising under state law were either superseded by the Copyright Act or otherwise inadequately pleaded. Finding that the copyright claims are precluded by the fair use doctrine and that each state law claim is insufficiently supported, we AFFIRM the decision.

NATHAN SIEGEL, Davis Wright Tremaine LLP, Washington, DC (with Eric J. Feder, Davis Wright Tremaine LLP, Washington, DC, and Raphael Holoszyc-Pimentel, Davis Wright Tremaine LLP, New York, NY, appearing on the brief), represented the Defendants-Appellees.

LOHIER, Circuit Judge:

Former Congressman George Santos seeks review of a final judgment from the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), broadcaster of the late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. The dispute arose after Kimmel, using pseudonyms, submitted requests for personalized videos from Santos through the Cameo service. Santos complied with each request, after which Kimmel broadcast the resulting videos during JKL in a recurring satirical segment titled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed Santos’s complaint on the basis of Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, holding that his copyright allegations were precluded by the fair use doctrine, and that his state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or otherwise insufficiently pleaded. We concur that the copyright claims are foreclosed under the fair use doctrine and further determine that all of Santos’s state law causes of action fail to state a claim; therefore, we uphold the district court’s judgment.

DISCUSSION

Our review of the District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal of Santos’s complaint is conducted de novo. See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). In our analysis, we evaluate “the facts set forth in the complaint, any exhibits attached to the complaint, as well as documents expressly referenced within the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations as reflected in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

Within copyright litigation, the affirmative defense of fair use permits conduct that could otherwise be deemed infringing, thereby granting courts the flexibility to refrain from strictly enforcing the Copyright Act in circumstances where such rigidity would undermine the creative objectives the Act is designed to encourage. See Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (internal quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, courts assess several non-exhaustive factors to determine whether the fair use doctrine applies: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use,” our principal inquiry centers on how much the secondary use transforms the original work—that is, whether the subsequent work simply replaces the original’s function or contributes new expression, meaning, or message. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (internal quotation marks omitted). This emphasis derives from the principle that “fair use” is inherently adaptable and its implementation depends heavily on the specific factual circumstances. Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). At times, the facts alleged in a complaint may make fair use so apparent that it justifies dismissing a copyright claim at the pleadings stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. contends that the secondary use at issue cannot be transformative on the grounds that Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works,” positioning himself as the “designer[]” of the Works’ initial objectives. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not contest the District Court’s conclusion that the alleged infringing use’s purpose was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, his argument is that this motivation was identical to his own rationale for producing the original videos. Nevertheless, the analysis of whether a use qualifies as transformative is guided by the perception of a reasonable observer, rather than the personal motivation of the original author or the alleged infringer. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As set out in Santos’s initial claim, a reasonable viewer would interpret the original videos as expressing “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” as opposed to a readiness to make outlandish statements for payment. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations constitute bad faith, thereby negating any fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We find this argument unconvincing. While it is correct that the Supreme Court in Harper & Row observed that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” Id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently retreated from this proposition. Regardless, Santos’s own complaint fails to allege that the Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). In fact, the complaint portrays the defendants as engaging in (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first fair use factor weighs heavily in favor of fair use.

We also find no merit in Santos’s objections regarding the District Court’s assessment of the other pertinent factors. For example, the relevant copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos were both created and made public on the same date. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, it is apparent from the face of Santos’s complaint that he has not experienced any market injury as contemplated by the fourth fair use factor, since “[t]he proper inquiry is not whether the subsequent work might harm the market for the original by, say, diminishing its value through parody or critique, but whether the new work supplants the original by serving as a direct market substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he fact that authors of creative works are unlikely to permit licensing for critical reviews or satires of their own materials excludes such uses from the concept of a potential licensing market.”). In Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 585 n.18 (1994), while the Supreme Court noted the language from Harper & Row, it “expressed some skepticism about whether bad faith has any role in a fair use analysis.” Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc., 593 U.S. 1, 32 (2021). More recently, the Court indicated that it “find[s] this skepticism justifiable, as copyright is not a privilege reserved for the well-behaved.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). Thus, we reject Santos’s contention irrespective of whether good or bad faith is relevant.

Since our determination of "the question of fair use" requires us to evaluate solely "the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue," Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (internal quotations omitted), we uphold the District Court's finding that the doctrine of fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims even at the motion to dismiss phase.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s decision to dismiss his state law claims, which include breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court rejected the first two of these claims, finding that the Copyright Act preempted them. In response, Santos contends that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and therefore should not be preempted, citing Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nonetheless, there is no necessity to address the preemption question, as Santos’s state law contract claims independently fail to establish a viable claim under state law.

We begin by considering Santos’s claim for direct breach of contract under the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos himself is not a signatory to these Terms of Service—an agreement that users, such as Kimmel, are required to accept, and which Santos claims has been violated. Under Illinois law, which is controlling for the Terms of Service, there exists a significant presumption against extending contractual benefits to individuals who are not parties to the agreement. See Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, courts require that any inference that the contract covers non-parties must be nearly tantamount to an explicit provision. Id. (internal quotations omitted). In the present case, Santos fails to point to any provision that amounts to an explicit statement or “express declaration” conferring rights to him or to any other non-signatory. On the contrary, where the Terms of Service are intended to benefit third parties, the agreement specifically states as much, thus demonstrating that such intent must be clearly articulated. 

When applying New York law to this claim, the court’s analysis focuses on “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to evaluate “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Here, however, the complaint lodged by Santos does not contain nonconclusory allegations describing the essential terms of any purported contract, nor does it identify particular provisions that would give rise to liability. See Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting the fundamental terms of the alleged implied contract). Furthermore, Santos does not allege facts that would support the existence of a mutual understanding or “meeting of minds” between the parties to create an agreement binding Kimmel to the Terms of Service. See DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also disputes the District Court’s rejection of his fraudulent inducement claim, arguing that he was not required to specifically allege an out-of-pocket loss, as mandated by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court's determination that Santos did not plead any tangible out-of-pocket loss resulting from the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
