Case Name: Santos v. Kimmel  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket Number: No. 24-2196-cv  
Opinion by: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former member of Congress, challenges a final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote), which dismissed his federal copyright infringement and related state law claims against Jimmy Kimmel, the American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”)—which broadcasts the late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”)—and ABC’s parent corporation, The Walt Disney Company. The basis for Santos’s lawsuit stemmed from incidents in which Kimmel, operating under assumed identities, requested personalized video messages from Santos via the Cameo service. After Santos created these videos, Kimmel broadcast them during a recurring comedic segment entitled “Will Santos Say It?” on his television show. The District Court, invoking Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, found that the complaint did not state a valid claim: the copyright infringement claims were dismissed on fair use grounds, and the state law claims were either preempted by federal copyright law or failed to allege actionable conduct. Agreeing both that the fair use defense negated the copyright claims and that Santos’s state law claims were insufficient, we affirm the District Court’s decision.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review includes consideration of “the facts set forth in the complaint, any documents appended to the complaint as exhibits, and materials explicitly incorporated in the complaint by reference.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations as they appear in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In a copyright dispute, the fair use defense “provides a justification for conduct that might typically constitute infringement, thereby permitting courts to forgo inflexible enforcement of the Copyright Act in instances where such rigidity would inhibit the creative expression the Act seeks to foster.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). The Copyright Act directs courts to assess several non-exhaustive factors when evaluating fair use: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the "purpose and character of the use" element, our principal attention is on the extent to which the use can be considered transformative—that is, whether the new creation merely replaces the function of the original work, or whether it brings forth additional value with a distinct purpose or character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (internal quotation marks omitted). This approach is based on recognizing that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (internal quotation marks omitted). The fair use doctrine may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. contends that the use should not be deemed transformative on the basis that Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and, as such, is the “designer[]” of their initial purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the alleged infringing use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he asserts that his own intent in creating the videos was the same. Nonetheless, the determination of whether a secondary use qualifies as transformative is guided by the impression of a reasonable observer, rather than the personal motives of the copyright owner or the subsequent user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As indicated by Santos’s original complaint, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as communicating “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” as opposed to reflecting a willingness to say absurd things in exchange for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further asserts that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations constitute bad faith, thereby invalidating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not concur. While the Supreme Court in Harper & Row observed that “‘[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,’” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has later retreated from this pronouncement. Regardless, Santos’s own allegations refute any assertion that the Defendants intended to "supplant" her "commercially valuable right" in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Rather, the complaint characterizes the defendants as being driven by motives of (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court that the first factor weighs decisively in favor of fair use.

Santos’s objections to the District Court’s treatment of the other factors are similarly unconvincing. Notably, the pertinent copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos were both created and published on the same date. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, the complaint filed by Santos plainly shows that there is no market harm as contemplated by the fourth fair use factor. The inquiry is not whether the secondary work negatively impacts the original’s value—such as through parody or criticism—but rather whether the secondary work “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Since our analysis is confined to “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” in resolving “the question of fair use,” see Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s dismissal of his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found that the first two claims under state law were preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos asserts that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and therefore do not fall under preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nevertheless, we do not need to address this question, since Santos’s contract-based claims under state law do not satisfy the requirements for relief under state law.

We begin by considering Santos’s claim for direct breach of contract based on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos himself is not a signatory to these Terms of Service, which are binding on users such as Kimmel and which Santos asserts were violated. Illinois law, which is applicable to the Terms of Service, establishes a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). As that court explained, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (internal quotation marks omitted). Santos, in this instance, fails to point to anything resembling an “express declaration” in the pertinent provisions of the Terms of Service that would extend their application to him or to any other party not a signatory. In fact, some other sections of the Terms of Service specifically include language providing for third-party beneficiaries, which demonstrates that when the contracting parties intended enforcement by noncontracting third parties, they explicitly provided for it. 

Turning to New York law, which governs this particular claim, the evaluation turns on “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” when deciding “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Here, however, Santos’s complaint does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (not stating the required terms of the supposed implied contract that Defendants are accused of breaching). Furthermore, Santos does not plausibly set forth that there existed “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel would be bound by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also contests the District Court’s decision to dismiss his fraudulent inducement claim on the grounds that he did not plead any actual out-of-pocket loss, as mandated by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s determination that Santos did not assert any concrete out-of-pocket damages suffered as a result of the alleged fraudulent conduct. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

After reviewing Santos’s additional arguments, we determine that they lack merit. Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we AFFIRM the District Court’s judgment.
