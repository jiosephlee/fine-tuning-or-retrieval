Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos challenges a final judgment from the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law asserted against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s show Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), as well as ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. Santos initiated this litigation after Kimmel, under various pseudonyms, requested and received personalized videos from Santos through the Cameo platform. After Santos completed these requests, Kimmel featured the resulting videos on JKL within a satirical segment called “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, determining that the copyright claims were foreclosed by the fair use doctrine and that Santos’s state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to state a viable claim. Since we concur that the fair use doctrine defeats Santos’s copyright claims and additionally find that none of the state law causes of action stated a claim, we affirm the District Court’s judgment.

DISCUSSION

We undertake a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses the factual allegations in the complaint, any exhibits appended to it, and documents that the complaint incorporates by reference. Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In copyright litigation, the fair use affirmative defense "justifies conduct that could otherwise amount to infringement, enabling courts to temper a strict interpretation of the Copyright Act in situations where it might hinder the creative objectives underlying the Act." Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, our assessment of fair use involves analysis of several non-exhaustive factors: "(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work." U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the factor concerning the “purpose and character of the use,” our principal inquiry is the extent to which the work constitutes a transformative use—that is, whether the new creation simply substitutes for the original work’s objectives or introduces new expression, meaning, or message, serving a distinct purpose or embodying a different character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach is informed by the principle that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). Fair use may in certain circumstances be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” even at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. contends that Kimmel’s use was not transformative because Kimmel allegedly “instigated the creation of the original Works” and therefore is the “designer[]” of their intended purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not contest the District Court’s conclusion that Kimmel’s allegedly infringing use sought “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, he maintains that his own original intent in making the videos matched this purpose. Nevertheless, the determination of whether a secondary use is transformative depends on the perspective of a reasonable observer rather than the copyright owner’s or secondary user’s personal intentions. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). Santos’s initial allegation concedes that, for a reasonable observer, the videos would appear to express “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” and not to reflect a propensity to say absurd things for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s misrepresentations amount to bad faith, thereby invalidating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are unpersuaded by this argument. Although the Supreme Court in Harper & Row noted that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently retreated from this pronouncement. Regardless, the allegations in Santos’s complaint undercut any assertion that the Defendants acted with an intent to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Instead, the complaint describes the defendants as being driven by (mocking) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of a determination of fair use.

We remain unconvinced by Santos’s objections to how the District Court addressed the remaining factors. For instance, the pertinent copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos were published contemporaneously with their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Moreover, it is apparent from the complaint itself that Santos has not experienced market harm as defined under the fourth fair use factor. Specifically, “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (internal quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Given that the decision on “the question of fair use” requires examination of “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” alone, Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that Santos’s copyright claims are barred by fair use even at the pleading stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s decision to dismiss his state law claims involving breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found that the first two claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos asserts that his claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and therefore are not subject to preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nonetheless, this panel need not determine the preemption question because Santos’s contract-based claims do not sufficiently allege a cause of action under applicable state law.

Turning to Santos’s express breach of contract allegation under the Cameo Terms of Service, it is clear that Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service binding on users such as Kimmel, and which Santos claims were violated. The Terms of Service are governed by Illinois law, which generally disfavors extending contractual benefits to non-parties. As stated in Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016), the presumption against third-party beneficiaries is strong and may only be overcome where “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, Santos does not reference anything resembling an “express declaration” in the Terms of Service that would extend the benefits of its provisions to himself or any other non-signatory. Other sections of the Terms of Service do explicitly provide for third-party beneficiaries, reinforcing that such an intention must be expressly stated by the contracting parties.

As for the claim governed by New York law, whether an implied-in-fact contract has arisen—and if so, its terms—requires assessing both “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). In this case, however, Santos’s complaint fails to set forth, in more than conclusory terms, “the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting the requisite essential terms of the alleged implied contract with the Defendants). Furthermore, Santos does not plausibly allege “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” whereby Kimmel agreed to comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

With respect to the fraudulent inducement claim, Santos contests the District Court’s dismissal on the basis that he did not allege any out-of-pocket loss as required by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos failed to plead any actual out-of-pocket damages resulting from the supposed fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we affirm the District Court’s ruling dismissing this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
