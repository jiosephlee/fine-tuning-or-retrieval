Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former member of Congress, challenges a final judgment entered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and various state law violations against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. Santos’s action stemmed from Kimmel’s acts of submitting requests under aliases for customized videos from Santos through the Cameo service. After Santos produced the requested videos, Kimmel featured them on JKL in a satirical segment named “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court found the complaint insufficient under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, holding that the copyright allegations were precluded by the fair use doctrine, and that the state law causes of action were either preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to sufficiently allege a claim. Concluding as the District Court did that Santos’s copyright counts are barred by fair use and finding as well that none of the state law claims meets the standard to state a claim, we affirm the District Court’s decision.

DISCUSSION

We undertake a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). In our analysis, we examine not only “the facts alleged in the complaint” but also any “documents attached to the complaint as exhibits” and “documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In the context of copyright litigation, fair use operates as an affirmative defense that "excuses conduct which might otherwise constitute infringement, thereby enabling courts to prevent a strict enforcement of the Copyright Act in circumstances where such enforcement would undermine the very creativity the Act seeks to encourage." Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, courts evaluate the following non-exhaustive factors to determine if fair use applies: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use” element, our principal inquiry centers on how transformative the use is—that is, whether it merely replaces the purpose of the original work, or instead incorporates new expression, meaning, or message with a different aim or altered character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This focus arises from the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). A use may qualify as fair use based on the pleadings alone if it is “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim.” TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. argues that the use cannot be transformative since Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and is, therefore, the “designer[]” of their intended objective. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the contested use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, his position is that his own original goal in making the videos was the same. However, the assessment of whether a secondary use is transformative is based upon how a reasonable observer would view it, not on the subjective motivations of the copyright owner or the second user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). Indeed, as Santos’s own complaint indicates, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as expressing “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than an inclination to utter absurdities for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s allegedly false statements amount to bad faith, thereby negating the applicability of the fair use doctrine. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are not persuaded by this argument. While it is accurate that the Supreme Court in Harper & Row observed that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently retreated from emphasizing this principle. Regardless, the allegations in Santos’s own complaint do not support any suggestion that Defendants sought to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Instead, the complaint characterizes defendants’ actions as driven by (albeit sarcastic) critique and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first fair use factor weighs decisively in favor of fair use.

We likewise find no merit in Santos’s objections to the District Court’s consideration of the remaining factors. The pertinent copyright registrations confirm that the videos were both created and published on the same day. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, the complaint on its face demonstrates that Santos has not experienced market harm as described in the fourth factor of fair use, since “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

As we are required to examine solely “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” in determining “the question of fair use,” Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s finding that the defense of fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, even at the stage of the pleadings.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s dismissal of his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found that the breach of contract and breach of implied contract claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos contends, however, that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim,” and therefore not subject to preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nevertheless, it is unnecessary for us to address this question, as Santos’s contract-based claims under state law do not adequately state a claim for relief.

We begin by considering Santos’s claim for direct breach of contract based on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos himself is not a signatory to the Terms of Service, which users such as Kimmel are required to accept and which Santos claims were violated. According to Illinois law, which regulates the Terms of Service at issue, there exists a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In the present case, Santos points to nothing resembling an “express declaration” that the applicable provisions of the Terms of Service extend to him or other parties who are not signatories. Furthermore, certain other sections of the Terms of Service explicitly contain third-party beneficiary clauses, which suggests that when the contracting parties wanted third parties to have enforcement rights, they made that intention clear. Regarding New York law—which governs this aspect of Santos’s claim—courts analyze “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” in order to assess “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). In this matter, Santos’s complaint does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (lacking allegations of the fundamental terms of the supposed implied contract alleged to have been breached by Defendants). Additionally, Santos fails to plausibly assert any “indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” whereby Kimmel agreed to be bound by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Lastly, Santos contests the District Court’s decision to dismiss his fraudulent inducement claim due to his failure to plead any out-of-pocket loss, which New York law requires. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos did not sufficiently allege any actual out-of-pocket loss as a result of the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

After reviewing Santos’s additional arguments, we find them to be unpersuasive. Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
