Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former member of Congress, seeks review of a final judgment by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and various state law violations against Jimmy Kimmel; American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), broadcaster of the show Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”); and ABC’s parent, The Walt Disney Company. Santos’s claims stemmed from Kimmel, who, under assumed names, requested personalized video messages from Santos via the Cameo platform. After Santos produced these videos, Kimmel featured them on JKL within a comedic segment named “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed the complaint on the grounds that, pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, the copyright causes of action were precluded by the fair use doctrine, and Santos’s state law causes were either superseded by the Copyright Act or insufficiently pled. Concluding that the copyright claims are precluded by the fair use doctrine and that none of the state law claims present a viable cause of action, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts alleged in the complaint, any exhibits attached to the complaint, as well as documents incorporated by reference therein.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as they appear in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In copyright litigation, the fair use affirmative defense “excuses conduct that might otherwise qualify as infringement, thus permitting courts to circumvent inflexible enforcement of the Copyright Act in circumstances where such rigidity would impede the creativity the Act seeks to foster.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, the court assesses several non-exhaustive factors to determine if fair use is present: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use” prong, the principal consideration is the extent to which the use alters the original, specifically whether the new creation merely replaces the function of the original or instead introduces new expression, meaning, or message. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This inquiry is rooted in the principle that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). Moreover, fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the pleading stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. maintains that the use lacked transformativeness because Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and thus is the “designer[]” of their original intent. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not contest the District Court’s determination that the challenged use was intended “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he contends that this was his own purpose in creating the videos. However, the question of whether a subsequent use is transformative is determined by how a reasonable observer would perceive the work, not by the subjective motives of either the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). According to Santos’s initial complaint, a reasonable person would interpret the videos as expressing “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than as promoting a willingness to utter absurdities for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel's alleged misrepresentations amount to bad faith, thereby invalidating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not concur with this argument. While the Supreme Court noted in Harper & Row that "[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing," id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), subsequent Court decisions have moved away from relying on that assertion. Regardless, Santos’s own complaint is inconsistent with any allegation that Defendants sought to “supplant” her “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the allegations describe Defendants as being driven by (sarcastic) commentary and criticism. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Therefore, we concur with the District Court that the first statutory factor weighs strongly in favor of a fair use finding.

We also find no merit in Santos’s objections regarding the District Court’s assessment of the other relevant factors. The pertinent copyright registrations indicate that the videos’ publication occurred simultaneously with their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, the face of Santos’s complaint demonstrates that there has been no market harm as contemplated by the fourth fair use factor, since “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

As we are required to evaluate solely “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” to resolve “the question of fair use,” see Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (internal quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that Santos’s copyright claims are precluded by fair use, even at the stage of the pleadings.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s decision to dismiss his state law claims alleging breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court determined that the first two claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos contends that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and therefore are not preempted, citing Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, we do not need to address the preemption question, as Santos’s state law contract claims do not state a claim for relief under state law.

We begin by examining Santos's claim for direct breach of contract based on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos, however, is not a signatory to the Terms of Service that apply to users like Kimmel, which Santos contends have been violated. Illinois law, which is controlling for the Terms of Service, recognizes a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, the law requires that for contract terms to benefit nonparties, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, Santos fails to identify anything that resembles such an “express declaration” extending the relevant contract terms to himself or other parties not in privity with the agreement. In fact, certain other portions of the Terms of Service explicitly mention third-party beneficiaries, demonstrating that when the contracting parties intended enforcement rights for nonparties, they unambiguously included such language. 

For claims governed by New York law, which pertains here, courts focus on “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” in order to evaluate “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Yet, in the present case, Santos’s complaint fails to “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting any articulation of the fundamental terms of the supposed implied contract claimed to be breached by Defendants). Additionally, Santos does not plausibly set forth facts indicating “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel was required to follow the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also disputes the District Court’s decision to dismiss his fraudulent inducement claim on the grounds that he did not plead any out-of-pocket damages as mandated by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s finding that Santos did not allege he incurred any actual out-of-pocket loss resulting from the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of that claim.

CONCLUSION

After reviewing Santos’s additional arguments, we find them to be unpersuasive. Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we AFFIRM the District Court’s judgment.
