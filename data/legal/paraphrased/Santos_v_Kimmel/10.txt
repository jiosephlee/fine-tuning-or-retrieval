Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos challenges a final judgment from the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his allegations of copyright infringement and related state law claims against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. Santos initiated the action after Kimmel, employing several aliases, requested custom videos from Santos via the Cameo platform; Santos created and delivered each video as requested. Kimmel subsequently broadcast these videos on JKL within a satirical segment called “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court found that the complaint did not sufficiently plead a claim, dismissing it under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure on the grounds that the copyright infringement claims were precluded by the fair use doctrine, and the state law claims were either superseded by the Copyright Act or otherwise deficient. Since we concur that the fair use doctrine bars Santos’s copyright claims and also determine that all of his state law claims lack merit, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our analysis takes into account “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached as exhibits to the complaint, and documents referenced within the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In copyright litigation, the defense of fair use “excuses conduct that might otherwise constitute infringement, thereby enabling courts to avert an inflexible enforcement of the Copyright Act where such enforcement could hinder the very innovation the Act seeks to encourage.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, the following non-exhaustive criteria are assessed to determine if fair use exists: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When considering the “purpose and character of the use” prong, the key inquiry is typically how transformative the use is—that is, whether the subsequent work simply replaces the original’s objectives, or if it introduces new content, intent, or context. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (internal quotation marks omitted). This perspective embodies the principle that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). The defense of fair use may in some circumstances be so apparent from the complaint itself that it justifies dismissal of the copyright infringement action at the pleading stage. See TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. 

Santos asserts that the use was not transformative on the grounds that Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and can therefore be considered the “designer[]” of their original intention. Appellant’s Br. 11. However, Santos does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the alleged infringing use was intended “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, his argument is that this was likewise his objective when creating the videos. Whether a secondary use is transformative, however, does not hinge on the copyright owner’s or the subsequent user’s subjective intent, but rather on how a reasonable observer would interpret the work. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). Here, as recognized in Santos’s own allegations, a reasonable observer would perceive that the videos communicated “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” as opposed to expressing a willingness to say absurd things for money. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s allegedly false statements constitute bad faith, thereby invalidating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not accept this argument. While the Supreme Court did note in Harper & Row that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently retreated from that position. Regardless, the allegations set forth in Santos’s complaint run counter to any assertion that Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Rather, the pleading instead describes defendants as acting with an intent to provide (sarcastic) commentary and criticism. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Therefore, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first fair use factor weighs heavily in favor of fair use.

We also find Santos’s objections to the District Court’s reasoning regarding the other pertinent factors to be unconvincing. Specifically, the operative copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos were both created and published on the same date. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Additionally, Santos’s complaint itself makes it apparent that, as to the fourth fair use factor, he has not experienced any cognizable market harm, since “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Because our determination of fair use only requires examination of “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s finding that Santos’s copyright claims are precluded by fair use even at the pleading stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos additionally challenges the District Court’s dismissal of his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found that the first two state law claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos contends that his claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim,” and therefore should not be considered preempted. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, we do not address the preemption question, since Santos’s state law contract claims do not state a valid claim for relief under the applicable law.

We turn first to Santos’s claim for direct breach of contract premised on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service that are binding on users (such as Kimmel) and which he asserts were violated. Under Illinois law—the governing law for the Terms of Service—there exists a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). The law further holds that “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this case, Santos does not identify anything resembling such an “express declaration” extending the relevant contractual provisions to himself or to other nonparties to the contract. In fact, additional clauses within the Terms of Service expressly contain language granting third-party beneficiary status, signaling that where the parties intended certain provisions to be enforceable by third parties, they included such intent in clear terms.

With respect to his implied contract claim, New York law applies. Under New York law, to determine “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms,” courts assess “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Here, Santos’s complaint does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (failing to set forth the material terms of the alleged implied contract at issue). Nor does Santos adequately allege facts suggesting there was any “indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” whereby Kimmel agreed to comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also contests the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim on the basis that he did not allege any out-of-pocket damages, as required by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s determination that Santos failed to plead that he incurred any actual out-of-pocket loss as a consequence of the alleged fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
