Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former Congressman, challenges the final judgment of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote), which dismissed his claims for copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel; American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”); and ABC’s parent, The Walt Disney Company. Santos’s action stems from instances where Kimmel, employing pseudonyms, requested customized videos from Santos via the Cameo platform. After Santos produced the requested videos, Kimmel broadcasted them on JKL as part of a recurring segment called “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed the complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, finding that the copyright claims were defeated by the fair use doctrine, and the state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or insufficiently pleaded. Because we concur that the fair use doctrine precludes Santos’s copyright claims and also find that each of Santos’s state law claims fails to state a claim, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s dismissal of Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our assessment includes “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents appended as exhibits to the complaint, and documents that are referenced within the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In a copyright dispute, the fair use affirmative defense “permits conduct that might otherwise constitute infringement, thereby enabling courts to refrain from strictly applying the Copyright Act when such rigidity would hinder the creative activity that the Act is designed to support.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, the determination of fair use is guided by several non-exhaustive factors: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use” factor, our primary concern is the extent to which the use transforms the original work; this involves examining whether the new work simply replaces the function of the original, or whether it introduces something novel with a distinct purpose or character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach demonstrates our understanding that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). Sometimes, fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. asserts that the use lacks transformative character, since Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and is thereby the “designer[]” of their intended purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s determination that the allegedly infringing use was intended “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he contends this was also his underlying motivation in creating the videos. Nevertheless, the question of whether a secondary use is transformative is governed by the perception of a reasonable observer rather than the copyright owner’s or secondary user’s personal intent. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). Santos’s own initial claim concedes that, for a reasonable viewer, the videos would communicate “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than simply a willingness to utter absurdities for payment. App’x 15.

Santos further asserts that Kimmel’s misleading statements indicate bad faith, thereby invalidating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not concur with this argument. While it is accurate that the Supreme Court remarked in Harper & Row that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” Id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently retreated from this position. Moreover, Santos’s own complaint refutes any allegation that Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” regarding the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the complaint characterizes the defendants as acting out of (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Consequently, we agree with the District Court that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of a fair use finding.

We remain unconvinced by Santos's objections to the District Court's evaluation of the other pertinent factors. Notably, the applicable copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos were published on their respective creation dates. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, it is apparent from the face of Santos’s complaint that Santos has not incurred market harm as contemplated under the fourth fair use factor, since the inquiry is "not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) ("[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.").

As it is sufficient to examine "the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue" to resolve "the question of fair use," Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court's determination that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, including at the pleading stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court's decision to dismiss his state law causes of action alleging breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found that the first two of these state claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos asserts that his contract-related claims are "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim" and therefore should not be preempted, citing Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nonetheless, it is unnecessary for us to decide the preemption question because Santos's state law contract claims do not adequately plead entitlement to relief under state law.

We begin by considering Santos’s claim for breach of contract based directly on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos did not enter into the Terms of Service, which are agreements required for users such as Kimmel, and which Santos alleges were violated. Illinois law, the controlling law for these Terms of Service, maintains a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, under Illinois precedent, "the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration." Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this matter, Santos has not identified anything akin to an "express declaration" suggesting that relevant provisions in the Terms of Service are enforceable by him or other third parties who did not sign the agreement. Certain other sections of the Terms of Service do expressly grant rights to third-party beneficiaries, showing that when the contracting parties meant for third parties to enforce provisions, they stated so explicitly. For the claim governed by New York law, the analysis requires examination of "the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances" to assess "[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms." Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Yet, Santos’s complaint fails to "allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated." Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting allegations regarding the essential terms of the purported implied contract allegedly breached by Defendants). Furthermore, Santos has not set forth a plausible claim of "an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement" requiring Kimmel to comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also contests the District Court’s rejection of his fraudulent inducement claim, arguing that he sufficiently pled an out-of-pocket loss as required by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). However, we concur with the District Court’s finding that Santos did not adequately allege any real out-of-pocket damage resulting from the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s decision to dismiss this claim.

CONCLUSION

After reviewing Santos's additional assertions, we find them unpersuasive. Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we AFFIRM the District Court's judgment.
