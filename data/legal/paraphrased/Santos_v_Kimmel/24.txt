Case Title: Santos v. Kimmel  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket Number: 24-2196-cv  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Lohier

George Santos, a former member of Congress, challenges the final judgment entered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel; American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”); and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. The litigation originated when Kimmel, under a series of assumed identities, made multiple requests to Santos for custom-made videos through the Cameo platform. After Santos created each video as requested, Kimmel subsequently broadcast the footage on JKL in recurring satirical segments called “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed the entire complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, determining that the copyright allegations were precluded by the fair use doctrine, and that Santos’s claims under state law were either superseded by the Copyright Act or did not satisfy the standard for stating a claim. Since we concur that the fair use doctrine prevents Santos’s copyright claims and additionally find that none of his state law causes of action are adequately pled, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We undertake a de novo review of the District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal of Santos’s complaint. See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). In this review, we examine “the facts set forth in the complaint, any documents attached as exhibits, as well as documents incorporated into the complaint by reference.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as they appear in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In a suit involving copyright, the fair use doctrine provides an affirmative defense that “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). The Copyright Act sets forth several non-exhaustive criteria for assessing fair use, including: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When considering the “purpose and character of the use” element, our primary consideration is the extent to which the new use is transformative—that is, whether it simply replaces the original work’s objectives or, alternatively, introduces something new by serving a different function or bearing a novel character. Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach stems from the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). Furthermore, fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. contends that the use lacks transformative qualities because Kimmel allegedly “instigated the creation of the original Works” and thus serves as the “designer[]” of their initial purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s determination that the purpose of the accused use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, he asserts that this objective coincided with his own original purpose in producing the videos. Nonetheless, whether a use is transformative depends on the perspective of a reasonable observer, not the personal intentions of either the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As admitted by Santos in his original complaint, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as communicating “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than indicating a readiness to say absurd things for financial compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel's alleged misrepresentations reflect bad faith, which would invalidate the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not concur. Although the Supreme Court acknowledged in Harper & Row that "‘[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,’" id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has later retreated from this position. Regardless, the allegations in Santos’s complaint do not support an inference that Defendants intended to "supplant" Santos’s "commercially valuable right" in the original videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the complaint characterizes the defendants’ conduct as being aimed at (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first statutory factor weighs heavily in favor of a fair use determination.

We remain unconvinced by Santos’s objections regarding the District Court’s treatment of the remaining considerations. Notably, the applicable copyright registrations show that the videos in question were published on the very day they were created. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Moreover, from the face of Santos’s complaint, it is apparent that he has not demonstrated any harm to the market as contemplated by the fourth factor of the fair use analysis. The pertinent inquiry is “not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (for instance, by diminishing its value through parody or criticism), but whether it supplants the market for the first by providing a competing alternative.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikely scenario that authors of imaginative works would license critical commentary or parodies of their creations removes such uses from the notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Since our analysis requires us to examine solely “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” to resolve “the question of fair use,” Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, even at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s dismissal of his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found the first two claims preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos argues that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and therefore not subject to preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nonetheless, we do not address the preemption question, as Santos’s contract-based claims under state law do not meet the requirements for relief in any event.

We begin by considering Santos's claim for direct breach of contract based on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos himself did not agree to the Terms of Service that users such as Kimmel are required to accept, and which Santos asserts have been violated. Illinois law—which governs the interpretation of the Terms of Service—establishes a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, the intent for a contract to benefit third parties must appear “so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (internal quotation marks omitted). In this instance, Santos fails to point to any provision amounting to such an “express declaration” that would extend the relevant sections of the Terms of Service to him or to other parties who did not contract. Notably, some other sections in the Terms of Service do expressly mention third-party beneficiary rights, which demonstrates that when the parties wanted to allow third parties to enforce certain terms, they made such intentions explicit. 

Under New York law, which controls this particular claim, the analysis turns on “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” when determining “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). However, the complaint submitted by Santos does not set out, in nonconclusory language, “the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting the essential terms of the supposed implied contract allegedly breached by Defendants). Moreover, Santos has not plausibly claimed “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” whereby Kimmel agreed to abide by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also contests the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim on the grounds that he did not plead any out-of-pocket damages, as mandated by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s determination that Santos did not assert any actual pecuniary loss resulting from the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Consequently, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

After reviewing Santos's additional arguments, we find them unpersuasive. Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we AFFIRM the District Court’s judgment.
