Title: Santos v. Kimmel  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv  
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former Congressman, brings this appeal from a final judgment entered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote) which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), which broadcasts Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and The Walt Disney Company, the parent entity of ABC. The underlying dispute centers on Kimmel’s actions whereby, employing assorted fictitious identities, he requested custom videos from Santos via the Cameo platform. Santos completed each requested video, after which Kimmel featured the resulting clips on JKL in a satirical segment known as “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court, invoking Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, dismissed the complaint, determining that the fair use doctrine precluded the copyright claims, while concluding that the state law claims were either superseded by the Copyright Act or insufficiently pleaded. As we concur with the District Court’s assessment that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, and also determine that all of Santos’s state law claims were inadequately stated, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

Our review of the District Court’s dismissal of Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) is conducted de novo. DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). In evaluating the complaint, we examine “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In the context of copyright litigation, the fair use affirmative defense “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, four non-exhaustive factors must be assessed in determining if fair use applies: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use” prong, our analysis centers primarily on the extent to which the use is transformative; that is, we consider whether the new work simply replaces the functions of the original, or if it instead introduces new elements, purposes, or characteristics. Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (internal quotation marks omitted). This approach stems from the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (internal quotation marks omitted). Sometimes, the applicability of fair use can be so obvious from the facts alleged in the complaint that it may warrant dismissal of a copyright infringement case at the pleading phase. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. argues that the use in question was not transformative on the ground that Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and, as such, is the “designer[]” of their initial purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s determination that the contested use aimed “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, his position is that this was also his own motivation when creating the videos. However, the inquiry into whether a secondary use is transformative is governed by the perception of a reasonable observer, rather than the personal motivation of either the copyright owner or secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As stated in Santos’s original complaint, a reasonable observer would perceive that the videos expressed “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” as opposed to conveying a message of a willingness to say absurd things for money. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s misrepresentations amount to bad faith, thereby precluding any fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not find this persuasive. Although the Supreme Court in Harper & Row noted that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (internal quotation marks omitted), the Court has since moderated its position on that point. Moreover, the allegations set forth in Santos’s complaint are inconsistent with any assertion that the Defendants aimed to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the complaint characterizes the defendants as engaging in (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). We, therefore, concur with the District Court that the first fair use factor strongly weighs in favor of fair use.

We also find no merit in Santos’s objections to the District Court’s reasoning regarding the other relevant factors. Notably, the applicable copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos in question were published on the date they were created. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Additionally, the complaint itself makes it evident that Santos has not experienced market harm as contemplated by the fourth factor of the fair use analysis, since “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

As our determination of the fair use issue requires us to review solely "the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue," see Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court's finding that the doctrine of fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims even at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s dismissal of his state law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found the first two claims preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos counters that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and therefore not subject to preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nonetheless, we do not have to determine this question because Santos’s contract-based state law claims do not sufficiently allege a basis for relief under state law.

We begin our analysis with Santos’s claim of direct breach of contract pursuant to the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service that users—including Kimmel—must accept and which Santos contends were breached. Under Illinois law, the Terms of Service are subject to a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (internal quotation marks omitted). In the present matter, Santos fails to point to anything that rises to an “express declaration” indicating that the relevant portions of the Terms of Service extend to him or any other non-signatory individuals. Other clauses within the Terms of Service, by contrast, explicitly reference third-party beneficiaries, demonstrating that, when the parties wished for contractual provisions to be enforceable by third parties, they did so clearly. 

Turning to New York law, which is controlling for this claim, our examination focuses on “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to establish “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, however, Santos’s pleading does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting to allege the material terms of the alleged implied contract Defendants are said to have breached). Nor does Santos plausibly claim the existence of “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” whereby Kimmel was to abide by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also contests the District Court’s rejection of his fraudulent inducement claim on the basis that he did not allege any out-of-pocket loss, as required by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s determination that Santos did not sufficiently claim any actual out-of-pocket damages resulting from the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have reviewed the rest of Santos's arguments and find them to be unpersuasive. Accordingly, for the reasons discussed above, we AFFIRM the District Court’s judgment.
