Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former member of Congress, appeals from a final judgment rendered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel; American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program, Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”); and ABC’s parent, The Walt Disney Company. The action originated after Kimmel used various pseudonyms to request personalized video messages from Santos on the Cameo platform. Santos produced the requested videos, and Kimmel subsequently broadcast them during JKL as part of a satirical segment entitled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed the lawsuit pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, finding that the copyright allegations were precluded by the fair use doctrine, and that Santos’s state law claims were either superseded by the Copyright Act or insufficiently pled. We concur that Santos’s copyright claims are foreclosed by the fair use doctrine and also determine that each of his state law causes of action fails to state a valid claim; accordingly, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts stated in the complaint, exhibits attached to the complaint, and any documents that are incorporated into the complaint by reference.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we may take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as found in the Copyright Office’s public registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

Within copyright litigation, fair use is an affirmative defense that “excuses conduct which may otherwise constitute infringement, permitting courts to avoid a strict enforcement of the Copyright Act in situations where such rigidity would undermine the creative objectives of the Act itself.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). The Copyright Act sets forth a list of non-exhaustive factors to guide the fair use analysis: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the "purpose and character of the use" factor, the primary consideration is the extent to which the use transforms the original work—specifically, whether the new work simply replaces the function of the original or introduces new elements that serve a different purpose or possess a distinct character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach is grounded in the principle that "fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context." Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). In some cases, fair use may be "so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the motion to dismiss phase. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178.

argues that the use at issue is not transformative because Kimmel "instigated the creation of the original Works" and therefore should be considered the "designer[]" of their original purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s determination that the purportedly infringing use was intended "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." App’x 235. Instead, he contends that his initial intention in creating the videos was the same as Kimmel's alleged purpose. Nevertheless, whether a secondary use is transformative depends on the perception of a reasonable observer, rather than the subjective intentions of either the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). According to Santos’s own initial pleading, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as communicating “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than as demonstrating a willingness to say absurd things for money. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s misrepresentations exhibit bad faith, thereby invalidating any fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not concur. While the Supreme Court remarked in Harper & Row that “‘[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,’” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has later retreated from that characterization. Regardless, Santos’s own complaint undermines any suggestion that Defendants acted with the aim to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Instead, the pleadings depict the defendants’ actions as motivated by (albeit sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Consequently, we share the District Court’s view that the first fair use factor weighs decisively in favor of fair use.

We likewise find Santos’s objections to the District Court’s consideration of the additional factors unconvincing. Notably, the applicable copyright registrations indicate that the videos were both created and published on the same date. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Further, it is apparent from the face of Santos’s complaint that there is no allegation of market harm as contemplated by the fourth fair use factor, since “the inquiry is not whether the second work might harm the market for the original by reducing its value through parody or critique, but whether it replaces the original by providing a competing alternative.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he remote possibility that authors of creative works would authorize critical commentary or satire of their own works precludes those uses from being viewed as part of a potential licensing market.”).

Given that our determination of fair use need only address “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” as explained in Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s decision that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, even at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further contests the District Court’s dismissal of his state law claims, which include breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court concluded that the first two claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos counters that his claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and should not be preempted, citing Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, we find it unnecessary to decide the preemption question because Santos’s state law contract claims do not plausibly state a cause of action under applicable state law.

Turning initially to Santos’s breach of contract claim under the Cameo Terms of Service, it is evident that Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service that users like Kimmel accepted and that he contends were breached. Under Illinois law, which applies to the Terms of Service, there exists a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). Indeed, “[t]he implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this case, Santos points to no language that could be considered an “express declaration” extending the relevant provisions to himself or other nonparties. Furthermore, other sections of the Terms of Service do expressly provide for third-party beneficiary status, confirming that when the contracting parties wished to enable third-party enforcement, they did so expressly.

When analyzing the implied contract claim under New York law—the governing law for this claim—we examine “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to ascertain “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Yet, Santos’s pleading fails to “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting the essential terms of the supposed implied contract allegedly breached by Defendants). In addition, Santos does not plausibly plead facts indicating “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel would observe the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Lastly, Santos disputes the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement cause of action, arguing it was wrongly dismissed for failure to allege any out-of-pocket loss, as required by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s assessment that Santos did not allege any actual out-of-pocket loss as a result of the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). We therefore uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
