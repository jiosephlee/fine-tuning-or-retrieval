Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former member of Congress, challenges the final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims alleging copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent corporation, The Walt Disney Company. Santos initiated the action after Kimmel, employing several aliases, requested custom videos from Santos via the Cameo service. After Santos completed the requests, Kimmel broadcasted the videos on JKL within comedic segments entitled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court granted dismissal under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6), finding that the fair use doctrine precluded the copyright claims and holding that the state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to present a viable cause of action. As we concur that the fair use doctrine bars Santos’s copyright claims and also determine that all of his state law claims are insufficiently pleaded, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts set forth in the complaint, any exhibits attached to the complaint, and documents referenced therein.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we may judicially notice “copyright registrations[] as they appear in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In a suit concerning copyright, the defense of fair use “justifies conduct that could otherwise be deemed infringing, enabling courts to refrain from strictly enforcing the Copyright Act in situations where such rigidity would hinder the creative expression the Act is designed to foster.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, our analysis of fair use rests on several non-exhaustive criteria: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use,” the primary consideration is the extent to which the use is transformative—specifically, whether the new work merely replaces the function of the original, or instead introduces new expression, meaning, or message. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach is rooted in the recognition that the doctrine of fair use is inherently adaptable and its application may differ based on the specific circumstances. Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). There are instances where fair use can be determined so unequivocally from the face of a complaint that it warrants dismissal of a copyright infringement action at the pleading stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. contends the use fails to qualify as transformative because Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and is therefore regarded as the “designer[]” of their original function. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s determination that the purportedly infringing use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he maintains that his initial aim in producing the videos was the same. However, the determination of whether the secondary use is transformative is governed by the perspective of an objective reasonable observer, rather than the subjective purpose of either the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As stated in Santos’s original allegation, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as imparting “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than as demonstrating a willingness to utter absurd remarks for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s misrepresentations constitute bad faith, thereby defeating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not agree. Although it is correct that the Supreme Court noted in Harper & Row that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently retreated from this assertion. Regardless, the allegations in Santos’s complaint are inconsistent with any allegation that Defendants aimed to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Rather, the complaint describes defendants as being driven by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of fair use.

We likewise find Santos’s objections to the District Court’s treatment of the other fair use factors to be unconvincing. Notably, the pertinent copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos in question were published contemporaneously with their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, the allegations in Santos’s complaint plainly indicate that Santos has not experienced the type of market harm contemplated under the fourth fair use factor, as the inquiry is “not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

As our determination of "the question of fair use" requires review solely of "the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue," see Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s finding that the defense of fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims even at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s dismissal of his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court concluded that the breach of contract and breach of implied contract claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos argues that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and therefore not subject to preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nevertheless, we do not need to decide this preemption question because Santos’s contract claims under state law do not sufficiently plead a claim for relief.

We begin by considering Santos’s direct breach of contract allegation pursuant to the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos himself is not a signatory to these Terms of Service, which users such as Kimmel must accept and which Santos claims were violated. Under Illinois law, which is controlling for the Terms of Service, there exists a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In the present matter, Santos fails to identify any “express declaration” indicating that the relevant sections of the Terms of Service confer enforceable rights to him or to any other non-parties. On the contrary, some other portions of the Terms of Service expressly include third-party beneficiary provisions, which suggests that when the parties intended for third parties to have enforcement rights under the agreement, such intent was explicitly stated.

Turning to the governing law for this claim, New York law requires courts to examine “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to establish “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Here, however, Santos’s pleadings do not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (not alleging the necessary terms of the supposed implied contract allegedly breached by Defendants). Moreover, Santos does not plausibly claim that “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” existed whereby Kimmel would comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also contests the District Court’s decision to dismiss his fraudulent inducement claim on the grounds that he did not plead any out-of-pocket damages, a requirement under New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos did not sufficiently allege he incurred any actual out-of-pocket losses as a consequence of the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

After reviewing Santos’s other arguments, we find them unpersuasive. Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we AFFIRM the District Court’s judgment.
