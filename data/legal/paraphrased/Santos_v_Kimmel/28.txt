Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former member of Congress, challenges a final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims alleging copyright infringement as well as violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”)—the network that broadcasts Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”)—and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. The basis of Santos’s complaint was Kimmel’s use of several pseudonyms to request custom video messages from Santos through the Cameo service; Santos created and delivered each requested video, which Kimmel subsequently broadcast during JKL segments collectively referred to as “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court, applying Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, granted dismissal on grounds that Santos’s copyright allegations were precluded by the fair use doctrine and his state claims either could not survive Copyright Act preemption or otherwise did not establish a viable cause of action. Finding the District Court’s fair use analysis dispositive as to the copyright claims and determining independently that Santos’s state law theories fail to state a claim, we affirm the judgment below.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts alleged in the complaint, the exhibits appended to the complaint, as well as documents referenced within the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we may take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

Within copyright litigation, the fair use affirmative defense “excuses conduct that might otherwise infringe, permitting courts to avoid strict enforcement of the Copyright Act in instances where such enforcement would hinder the creativity the Act seeks to protect.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). The Copyright Act directs consideration of several non-exhaustive factors to assess whether fair use exists: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When considering the factor of "purpose and character of the use," our analysis centers primarily on the extent to which the use is transformative—that is, whether the subsequent work merely replaces the goals of the original or brings something novel, serving an additional or distinct aim. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach embodies the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). The issue of fair use may be "so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim" at the motion to dismiss phase. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. Kimmel argues that the use at issue lacks transformation because Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and, consequently, acted as their “designer[]” with regard to their intended purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not contest the District Court’s determination that the purpose of the challenged use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he contends that this was his original objective when producing the videos. Nevertheless, the transformative nature of a secondary use depends on the perception of a reasonable observer, rather than on the subjective intentions of the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As noted in Santos’s original complaint, a reasonable observer would perceive the videos as expressing “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” as opposed to a readiness to say absurd things for money. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations amount to bad faith, thereby precluding invocation of the fair use doctrine. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We reject this argument. While it is accurate that the Supreme Court in Harper & Row observed, “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Supreme Court has subsequently retreated from this pronouncement. Regardless, Santos’s own complaint undermines any assertion that Defendants sought to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” to the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Rather, the complaint itself describes Defendants as engaging in (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first fair use factor weighs heavily in favor of fair use.

We likewise find no merit in Santos's objections regarding the District Court's evaluation of the other relevant factors. The pertinent copyright registrations, for instance, show that the videos in question were published simultaneously with their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Moreover, it is apparent from the allegations in Santos’s complaint that he has not demonstrated market harm as contemplated by the fourth factor of the fair use analysis, since the proper inquiry is not whether the secondary work would diminish the value of the original through parody or criticism, but rather whether it supplants the original work in the relevant market by serving as a substitute. See Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Since our determination of “the question of fair use” requires examination solely of “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s finding that the doctrine of fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, even at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s decision to dismiss his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found that the first two claims under state law were barred by the preemptive effect of the Copyright Act. In response, Santos argues these claims are "qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim" and therefore not subject to preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nevertheless, we are not required to address this preemption issue, as Santos’s contract-based state law claims do not, in any event, set forth a viable claim for relief under applicable state law.

We begin our analysis with Santos’s direct breach of contract claim based on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos, however, is not a signatory to the Terms of Service, which are accepted by users such as Kimmel and which Santos claims were violated. Under the applicable Illinois law governing these Terms, courts are generally “strongly disinclined to confer benefits upon third parties not party to the contract.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, a contract’s intent to benefit third parties must be “so unequivocal as to amount almost to an express statement.” Id. (internal quotation marks omitted). Santos, however, fails to point to anything resembling such an “express declaration” making clear that the relevant parts of the Terms of Service extend to him or to other third parties. Moreover, the Terms of Service elsewhere expressly reference third-party beneficiaries, showing that, where the drafters intended to allow non-signatories to enforce contract provisions, they stated so plainly.

For the claim governed by New York law, we must evaluate “the intent of the parties and the circumstances surrounding the transaction” in order to determine “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was made, and, if so, its scope and terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (internal quotation marks omitted). Here, however, the complaint fails to set forth, without conclusory language, the essential contractual terms underlying the alleged liability, as required by Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see also App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (lacking allegations as to key terms of the purported implied contract said to be breached). In addition, Santos does not plausibly assert the existence of “an indication of a meeting of the minds constituting an agreement” that would have required Kimmel to abide by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also contests the District Court’s decision to dismiss his fraudulent inducement claim on the basis that he did not plead any out-of-pocket loss, as required by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s determination that Santos did not allege he incurred an actual out-of-pocket loss as a result of the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of the claim.

CONCLUSION

After reviewing Santos’s additional arguments, we determine they lack merit. Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we AFFIRM the District Court’s judgment.
