Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos challenges a final judgment entered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law brought against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent corporation, The Walt Disney Company. The litigation arose when Kimmel, under assumed names, requested and received personalized videos from Santos through the Cameo platform. After Santos provided these videos, Kimmel featured them on JKL in a satirical segment entitled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court, applying Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, dismissed the complaint on the grounds that the copyright infringement claims were precluded by the fair use doctrine, and that Santos’s state law claims were either superseded by the Copyright Act or failed to sufficiently allege a claim. Because we concur that the fair use doctrine defeats Santos’s copyright claims and also find that each state law claim fails to state a claim, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s dismissal of Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts set forth in the complaint, any exhibits attached thereto, as well as documents that are referenced within the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations as recorded in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In a lawsuit involving copyright, the fair use affirmative defense “permits conduct that could otherwise constitute infringement, thereby enabling courts to forgo strict enforcement of the Copyright Act in circumstances where such rigidity would hinder the creativity the Act seeks to foster.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, the determination of fair use requires an analysis of several non-exhaustive factors: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the factor concerning the “purpose and character of the use,” the principal inquiry is on how much the use transforms the original work—that is, whether the subsequent work simply replaces the original’s purpose, or instead introduces something novel, serving an additional or distinct function. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (internal quotation marks omitted). This approach is based on the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). It is possible for fair use to be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the pleading stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. argues that the use does not qualify as transformative, asserting that Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and therefore is the “designer[]” of the original intended use. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the alleged infringing use aimed “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he contends that this was also his own intended purpose in producing the videos. However, the determination of whether a secondary use is transformative depends on the perception of a reasonable observer, rather than the subjective intentions of either the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As Santos himself acknowledges in his original allegation, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as communicating “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than as evidence of a willingness to say absurd things for money. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations constitute bad faith, which would undermine the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not concur with this argument. While it is accurate that the Supreme Court in Harper & Row noted that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has since moved away from this view. Moreover, the allegations in Santos’s complaint do not support any inference that the Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the complaint describes conduct by defendants motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). We therefore concur with the District Court that the first factor weighs strongly in favor of finding fair use.

We likewise find Santos’s objections to the District Court’s assessment of the other fair use elements unconvincing. Notably, the applicable copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos in question were published on the same dates as their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Additionally, the allegations in Santos’s complaint make it apparent that there has been no market harm as contemplated under the fourth fair use factor. As explained, “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Since resolution of the fair use issue requires only an examination of “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” as held in Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that Santos’s copyright claims are barred by fair use at the pleading stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further appeals the District Court’s dismissal of his state law claims, which include breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found that the first two claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos, however, argues that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and thus should not be preempted. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nevertheless, we do not need to reach the preemption issue because Santos’s contract-based claims under state law do not present sufficient grounds for relief.

Turning first to Santos’s claim for breach of contract based on the Cameo Terms of Service, Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service that users such as Kimmel accept, which are the terms Santos alleges were breached. Under Illinois law, which governs the Terms of Service, there is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). Santos does not point to anything resembling such an “express declaration” that the pertinent terms of the agreement apply to him or to other nonparties to the contract. Notably, other sections of the Terms of Service include explicit third-party beneficiary clauses, making clear that when the parties intended to give enforcement rights to third parties, they did so specifically.

As to the implied contract claim governed by New York law, the analysis requires consideration of “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to determine whether an implied-in-fact contract existed and to ascertain its terms. Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Here, Santos’s complaint does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (not identifying the material terms of the supposed implied contract at issue). Moreover, Santos does not adequately claim there was “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” whereby Kimmel would be bound by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Finally, with respect to Santos’s appeal of the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim for failing to plead any out-of-pocket loss as required under New York law, see Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996), we concur with the District Court that Santos has not alleged any actual out-of-pocket loss resulting from the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we affirm the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
