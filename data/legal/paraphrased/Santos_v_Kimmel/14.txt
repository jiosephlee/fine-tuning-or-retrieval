Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos challenges a final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and state law violations brought against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. Santos’s allegations arose when Kimmel, using several fabricated identities, requested customized videos from Santos via the Cameo platform. After Santos completed these requests, Kimmel broadcast the resulting videos on JKL during a satirical segment labeled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, finding that the copyright allegations were negated by the fair use doctrine, and that Santos’s state law claims were either preempted under the Copyright Act or otherwise insufficient. Concluding that the fair use doctrine bars Santos’s copyright allegations and further determining that all of Santos’s state law claims fail to establish a claim, we affirm the District Court’s judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses the factual allegations set forth in the complaint, any exhibits attached to the complaint, as well as documents referenced within the complaint. Id. at 111. Additionally, we may take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In copyright litigation, the fair use affirmative defense “excuses conduct that could otherwise qualify as infringement, permitting courts to steer clear of strict enforcement of the Copyright Act where such rigidity might suppress the creativity the statute seeks to foster.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). According to the Copyright Act, courts evaluate whether fair use applies by assessing the following illustrative, not exhaustive, considerations: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the "purpose and character of the use," the principal consideration is how transformative the use is—that is, whether the subsequent work simply replaces the original or whether it introduces something new with a distinct objective or nature. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach stems from the recognition that "fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context." Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). Moreover, fair use can sometimes be evident from the face of the complaint, warranting dismissal of a copyright infringement claim at the pleading stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. Kimmel asserts that the use lacks transformative character since he "instigated the creation of the original Works" and is therefore the "designer[]" of their intended function. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court's determination that the disputed use was intended "to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money." App’x 235. Instead, he contends that this was his own original intent behind producing the videos. However, the question of whether a subsequent use qualifies as transformative depends on the perception of a reasonable observer, and not on the private intentions of either the copyright holder or the alleged infringer. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) ("[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use."). As acknowledged in Santos’s initial complaint, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than a propensity to say absurd things for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s allegedly false statements constitute evidence of bad faith, thereby precluding a fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not concur with this argument. While it is correct that the Supreme Court in Harper & Row observed that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has since moved away from reliance on that principle. Moreover, the allegations set forth in Santos’s complaint fail to suggest that Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in his videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). In fact, according to the complaint, the defendants acted with the intent to provide (albeit sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s determination that the first fair use factor weighs heavily in favor of fair use.

We are likewise unconviced by Santos’s objections to the District Court’s treatment of the remaining considerations. Notably, the pertinent copyright registrations indicate that the videos were both created and published on the same date. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, the face of Santos’s own complaint demonstrates the absence of any market harm as required by the fourth factor of the fair use analysis, since, as stated, “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Since resolution of “the question of fair use” requires only an evaluation of “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” as per Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, even at the stage of the pleadings.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further contests the District Court’s dismissal of his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court determined that the two contract-based claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos asserts that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and are therefore not subject to preemption. See Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, there is no need for us to decide the preemption question here, as Santos’s state law claims for contract breach do not adequately allege grounds for relief under state law standards.

We begin by considering Santos’s direct breach of contract claim brought under the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos himself is not a party to the Terms of Service agreement, which users such as Kimmel must accept and which Santos contends were violated. Under Illinois law—the law applicable to the Terms of Service—there exists a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, the “implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, Santos points to nothing akin to an “express declaration” that would extend the relevant provisions of the Terms of Service to him or other non-parties to the contract. On the contrary, other sections of the Terms of Service explicitly include third-party beneficiary clauses, suggesting that when the contract parties sought to authorize enforcement by third parties, they did so expressly.

As to the claim governed by New York law, we analyze “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” in determining “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Nevertheless, here Santos's complaint does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (the complaint fails to identify the essential terms of the claimed implied contract said to have been breached by Defendants). Additionally, Santos fails to plausibly assert the existence of “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel was obligated to abide by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also disputes the District Court’s decision dismissing his fraudulent inducement cause of action on the basis that he failed to plead any out-of-pocket loss as required by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos did not allege any actual out-of-pocket loss attributable to the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
