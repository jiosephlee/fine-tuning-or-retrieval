Title: Santos v. Kimmel  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit  
Date: October 6, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv  
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former member of Congress, appeals from a final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the network broadcasting Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent, The Walt Disney Company. The case arises from Kimmel’s actions in which he, under several assumed identities, requested personalized videos from Santos on the Cameo platform. Santos created and provided these videos, after which Kimmel featured them on JKL within recurring comedic segments entitled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court granted a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, finding that the copyright claims were precluded by the fair use doctrine and determining that Santos’s state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or insufficiently pleaded. Sharing the District Court’s view that the fair use doctrine bars Santos’s copyright claims, and further holding that all of the state law causes of action fail to state a viable claim, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal of Santos’s complaint. See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our assessment encompasses “the facts set forth in the complaint, any exhibits attached to the complaint, as well as documents that are incorporated by reference into the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations as reflected in the records of the Copyright Office.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In copyright litigation, the fair use affirmative defense permits conduct that might typically be seen as infringement, thereby enabling courts to prevent strict enforcement of the Copyright Act in situations where such inflexibility could inhibit the kind of creativity the Act was designed to encourage. See Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, the determination of whether fair use applies involves assessment of several non-exhaustive considerations: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When considering the "purpose and character of the use" element, our primary analysis is on how transformative the use is; specifically, we consider whether the secondary work simply replaces the original’s objectives or introduces new content, serving a different aim or adopting a new character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This focus stems from the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). Fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” even at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. contends that the use cannot be deemed transformative since Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works,” making him the “designer[]” behind the initial intended use. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not challenge the District Court’s conclusion that the aim of the allegedly infringing use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, he asserts this was likewise his own purpose for making the videos. Nonetheless, whether a subsequent use is transformative is determined by the perspective of a reasonable observer, rather than by the subjective intentions of the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). According to Santos’s own allegations, a reasonable observer would perceive the videos to express “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than a willingness to make absurd statements for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s misrepresentations amount to bad faith, thereby invalidating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not share this view. While it is accurate that the Supreme Court observed in Harper & Row that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has later retreated from this assertion. Regardless, the allegations in Santos’s complaint contradict the notion that Defendants aimed to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the material. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the complaint suggests the Defendants’ actions were prompted by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s determination that the first factor overwhelmingly weighs in favor of fair use.

We remain unconvinced by Santos’s objections to the District Court’s consideration of the other relevant factors. For example, the applicable copyright registrations indicate that the videos in question were both created and published on the same date. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Additionally, it is apparent from the allegations in Santos’s complaint that he has not experienced any market harm in the sense described by the fourth fair use factor, since “[w]e ask not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Since our analysis is limited to "the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue" for determining "the question of fair use," see Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court's finding that the doctrine of fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, even at the stage of the pleadings.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s dismissal of his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found the first two contract-based claims to be preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos contends that his contract claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and, therefore, are not preempted. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, it is unnecessary for us to address the preemption question here, since Santos’s state law contract claims do not adequately state a basis for relief under state law.

We begin by considering Santos’s direct claim for breach of contract based on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos himself did not assent to the Terms of Service, which only users such as Kimmel are required to accept and which Santos alleges were violated. Illinois law, which controls interpretation of the Terms of Service, establishes a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, there must be such a strong implication that third parties are included that it amounts “to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In the instant matter, Santos fails to identify any language approaching an “express declaration” extending the pertinent provisions of the Terms of Service to him or other parties not in privity. Notably, the Terms of Service include provisions that do refer expressly to third-party beneficiaries, which illustrates that when the contracting parties intended for the agreement’s terms to be enforceable by outsiders, they so specified. Turning to New York law—which governs this particular claim—we must look at “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to assess “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Yet here, Santos’s complaint does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (lacking the required pleading of the essential terms of the purported implied contract at issue). Additionally, Santos has not sufficiently alleged “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel would be bound by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also disputes the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim, asserting that he was not required to plead any out-of-pocket loss, which New York law mandates. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s determination that Santos did not allege any concrete out-of-pocket damages resulting from the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

After reviewing Santos’s additional arguments, we find them to be unpersuasive. Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we AFFIRM the District Court’s judgment.
