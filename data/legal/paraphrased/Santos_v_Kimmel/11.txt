Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos brings this appeal following a final judgment by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote) which dismissed his claims alleging copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”)—the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”)—as well as ABC’s parent company, The Walt Disney Company. The litigation stemmed from Kimmel’s actions in submitting requests for custom videos from Santos on the Cameo platform while using pseudonyms. Santos created the requested videos, after which Kimmel broadcast these clips on JKL during a satirical segment known as “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, finding that the copyright claims were foreclosed by the fair use doctrine and that his state law claims either fell under the preemption of the Copyright Act or were insufficiently pleaded. Sharing the District Court’s conclusion that Santos’s copyright claims are precluded by the fair use doctrine and determining further that all of his state law claims fail to state a claim, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s dismissal of Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts stated in the complaint, any exhibits attached to the complaint, and any documents that are referenced in the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as they appear in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In the context of copyright litigation, fair use serves as an affirmative defense that “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, courts evaluate whether fair use applies by analyzing these non-exhaustive factors: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When considering the factor of “purpose and character of the use,” our analysis centers primarily on how transformative the use is—specifically, whether the new creation simply replaces the original's objectives or contributes something novel with an additional purpose or a different nature. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (internal quotation marks omitted). This focus stems from the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (internal quotation marks omitted). There are circumstances in which fair use is “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. contends that the use in question was not transformative because Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and is therefore the “designer[]” of their initial intent. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not contest the District Court’s conclusion that the purportedly infringing purpose was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, he asserts that this was also the motivation behind creating the videos. Nonetheless, the transformative nature of a secondary use is assessed based on the viewpoint of a reasonable observer, rather than the subjective motives of either the original copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). According to Santos’s own allegations, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as expressing “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than indicating a willingness to say absurd things in exchange for payment. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations amount to bad faith, thereby defeating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are unpersuaded by this argument. Although the Supreme Court in Harper & Row remarked that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), it has since retreated from that position. Moreover, the allegations in Santos’s complaint do not support an inference that Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the complaint describes Defendants as acting out of a desire to criticize and comment, albeit in a (sarcastic) manner. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s assessment that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of fair use.

We likewise find no merit in Santos’s objections regarding the District Court’s consideration of the remaining fair use factors. Notably, the pertinent copyright registrations demonstrate that the videos in question were published contemporaneously with their creation date. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, Santos’s own complaint makes plain that there was no market harm as understood by the fourth factor of the fair use analysis, since “[t]he inquiry is not whether the second work harms the market for the original (such as by diminishing its value through parody or criticism), but rather whether it supplants the market for the original by acting as a competing alternative.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he improbable scenario of creators of imaginative works issuing licenses for critical reviews or satires of their own material removes such uses from the concept of a potential licensing market.”).

Since our analysis is limited to “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” for the fair use determination, as set forth in Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s ruling that the fair use doctrine precludes Santos’s copyright claims, even at the pleading stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s decision to dismiss his state law claims alleging breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court dismissed the first two claims on the basis that they were preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos asserts that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and thus should not be preempted. See Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, it is unnecessary for us to address this preemption argument because Santos’s state law contract claims do not state a valid claim for relief under state law.

We begin with Santos’s direct breach of contract claim under the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos was not a party to the Terms of Service, which must be accepted by users such as Kimmel and which Santos asserts were breached. Under Illinois law, which governs the Terms of Service, there is a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). Here, Santos does not point to anything resembling an “express declaration” that the Terms of Service’s relevant provisions are intended to benefit him or any noncontracting individual. Other parts of the Terms of Service expressly include third-party beneficiary language, which demonstrates that, where the contracting parties wanted to allow third-party enforcement, they explicitly stated so.

Turning to the claim governed by New York law, we evaluate whether an implied-in-fact contract arose by looking to “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to determine “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). However, in this instance, Santos’s pleading fails to “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (lacking allegations regarding the essential terms of any implied contract supposedly breached by Defendants). In addition, Santos does not plausibly claim that there was “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” obligating Kimmel to comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Lastly, Santos disputes the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim on the ground that he failed to allege any out-of-pocket loss as required by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos did not plead any actual out-of-pocket loss resulting from the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we affirm the District Court’s dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
