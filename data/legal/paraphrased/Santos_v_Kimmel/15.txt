Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos challenges a final judgment from the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”)—the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”)—and ABC’s parent, The Walt Disney Company. Santos filed this lawsuit after Kimmel used multiple aliases to request personalized video messages from Santos via the Cameo platform, which Santos supplied; Kimmel subsequently broadcasted these videos on JKL within a comedic segment entitled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court found the complaint insufficient under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, determining that the copyright causes of action were precluded by the doctrine of fair use, and that Santos’s state law causes of action were either superseded by the Copyright Act or failed to adequately allege a claim. We concur with the District Court that Santos’s copyright claims are prohibited by the fair use doctrine, and additionally hold that none of his state law claims are properly pleaded; thus, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision dismissing Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts set forth in the complaint, any exhibits attached to it, and any documents that are incorporated into the complaint by reference.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as they appear in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In copyright litigation, fair use operates as an affirmative defense that “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). The Copyright Act requires consideration of several non-exclusive factors to assess whether fair use applies: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use” prong, the primary consideration is the extent to which the use alters the original, specifically, whether the secondary work simply replaces the original's objectives or instead introduces something novel with a new purpose or distinctive character. Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (internal quotation marks omitted). This approach is rooted in the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). Sometimes, fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the initial pleadings stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. Kimmel argues the use was not transformative because he “instigated the creation of the original Works” and, therefore, is the “designer[]” behind their initial intent. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos, on the other hand, does not challenge the District Court’s determination that the alleged infringing purpose was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, Santos asserts that this was his own original intent in producing the videos. However, the question of whether a subsequent use is transformative depends on how a reasonable observer would view it, not on the copyright owner’s or secondary user’s subjective intentions. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). Santos’s own initial claim concedes that, from the perspective of a reasonable observer, the videos would have communicated “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than the notion of saying absurd things in exchange for money. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s allegedly false statements are indicative of bad faith, thereby vitiating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are not persuaded. While it is accurate that the Supreme Court in Harper & Row observed that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has since moderated its reliance on this pronouncement. Moreover, any assertion that Defendants sought to "supplant" Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos is undermined by the allegations in Santos’s complaint itself. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Instead, the complaint suggests that the defendants were motivated by (if sarcastic) commentary and criticism. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first statutory fair use factor weighs decisively in favor of fair use.

We remain unconvinced by Santos’s objections to the District Court’s consideration of the other fair use factors. In particular, the applicable copyright registrations show that the videos were published the same day they were created. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Further, as reflected in the face of Santos’s own complaint, Santos has not demonstrated market harm as required under the fourth factor of fair use analysis, since the proper inquiry is “not whether the second work would damage the market for the first (by, for example, devaluing it through parody or criticism), but whether it usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Given that resolution of “the question of fair use” may rest solely on reviewing “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s finding that Santos’s copyright claims are barred by fair use even at the motion-to-dismiss phase.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further contests the District Court's dismissal of his state law claims alleging breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court dismissed the first two of these state law claims on the basis that the Copyright Act preempted them. Santos contends that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and therefore not subject to preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). We find it unnecessary to decide this preemption question because Santos's state contract claims do not state a valid basis for relief under state law.

We begin by considering Santos's direct breach of contract claim predicated on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos is not a party to those Terms of Service, which users such as Kimmel must accept and which Santos asserts were violated. Illinois law governs the Terms of Service, and under Illinois law, there exists a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, a contract must imply application to third parties with such clarity that it virtually amounts to an express declaration. Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, Santos points to nothing that rises to an “express declaration” extending the applicable contract terms to him or other third parties. Indeed, other segments of the Terms of Service do include language for third-party beneficiaries, demonstrating that the contracting parties expressly provided for third-party enforcement of specific provisions when intended.

Turning to the claim of implied contract governed by New York law, we look to “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to decide “whether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Here, the allegations in Santos's complaint do not, through nonconclusory statements, set out the necessary elements of a contract between the parties, nor does he identify particular provisions upon which liability would rest. Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting essential terms of the supposed implied contract Defendants allegedly breached). Moreover, Santos does not plausibly claim any “indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel would abide by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Lastly, Santos objects to the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim, which was rejected for not stating any actual out-of-pocket loss as is required by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s finding that Santos has failed to allege any real out-of-pocket loss resulting from the alleged fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we affirm the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
