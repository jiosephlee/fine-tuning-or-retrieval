Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos appeals a final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his copyright infringement and state law claims brought against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”)—the network broadcasting Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”)—and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. The litigation stemmed from Kimmel, under several assumed names, submitting requests on the Cameo platform for customized videos from Santos, which Santos provided; Kimmel subsequently broadcast these videos on JKL within satirical segments labeled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed the case pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, determining that the copyright allegations were precluded by the fair use doctrine, and that Santos’s state law claims were either superseded by the Copyright Act or did not establish a viable cause of action. As we concur with the determination that the fair use doctrine bars Santos’s copyright claims and find that each of the state law claims likewise fails to state a valid claim, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court's dismissal of Santos's complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents appended to the complaint as exhibits, and documents that are incorporated by reference into the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In a lawsuit involving copyright, the fair use affirmative defense "permits conduct that could otherwise constitute infringement, enabling courts to avoid strictly enforcing the Copyright Act in circumstances where doing so would suppress the creative expression the Act seeks to encourage." Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, courts evaluate the following non-exhaustive factors in assessing a claim of fair use: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When considering the “purpose and character of the use” factor, the primary inquiry is on the extent to which the use transforms the original work; specifically, whether the new work simply replaces the original’s purpose or introduces new expression, meaning, or message with a distinct objective or nature. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This emphasis reflects the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). The defense may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” on a motion to dismiss. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. argues that the use fails to be transformative because Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and is, therefore, the “designer[]” of their intended purpose. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not contest the District Court’s conclusion that the accused use was aimed “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, he contends this was also his initial motivation for producing the videos. Nonetheless, the determination of whether the secondary use is transformative is based on the perception of a reasonable observer, not on the subjective intentions of either the copyright owner or the alleged infringer. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). According to Santos’s own initial assertion, a reasonable observer would understand the videos to communicate “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than a mere readiness to utter absurdities for payment. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s allegedly false statements constitute bad faith, thereby negating the applicability of the fair use doctrine. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are unpersuaded by this argument. Although the Supreme Court observed in Harper & Row that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently moved away from relying on this principle. Moreover, Santos’s own complaint undermines any suggestion that Defendants sought to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the video works. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Instead, the complaint characterizes Defendants’ actions as motivated by (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Consequently, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of a finding of fair use.

We likewise find Santos’s objections to the District Court’s evaluation of the other relevant considerations to be unconvincing. In particular, the pertinent copyright registrations confirm that the videos were published on the very dates they were created. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, it is apparent from the face of Santos’s complaint that he has not alleged the sort of market harm contemplated by the fourth fair use factor, as the inquiry focuses not on whether the subsequent work diminishes the first work’s value (such as through parody or critique), but rather on whether it supplants the original work’s market by serving as a competing alternative. Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Since our analysis need only address “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” in resolving “the question of fair use,” as noted in Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that the fair use doctrine precludes Santos’s copyright claims even at the motion to dismiss phase.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further contests the District Court’s rejection of his state law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court ruled that the initial two claims under state law were barred by preemption pursuant to the Copyright Act. Santos counters by asserting that his claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and, accordingly, should not be preempted. See Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, we find it unnecessary to address the preemption argument because, even setting preemption aside, Santos’s contract claims under state law do not meet the threshold for a viable cause of action.

Turning first to the alleged direct breach of contract under the Cameo Terms of Service, it is undisputed that Santos was not a signatory to the Terms of Service agreed upon by users, such as Kimmel, which form the basis of Santos’s breach allegation. Illinois law, which controls the interpretation of these Terms of Service, firmly disfavours recognizing rights for third parties who are not signatories. See Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). For such rights to be created, there must be a nearly express statement that the contract’s benefits apply to non-parties. Id. (quotation marks omitted). Santos has not cited anything resembling such an “express declaration” within the relevant portions of the Terms of Service. Moreover, other sections of the Terms of Service explicitly contemplate third-party beneficiaries, underscoring that whenever the contracting parties intended for third-party enforcement, they specifically provided for it.

Under New York law, which governs the implied contract theory, we examine “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to assess “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Nevertheless, the complaint falls short, failing to “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see also App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (the complaint omits essential terms purportedly forming the basis of the alleged implied contract). Santos similarly does not present a plausible allegation of “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” whereby Kimmel would comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

With regard to the fraudulent inducement claim, Santos objects to the District Court’s dismissal, which was based on his failure to allege any out-of-pocket loss—a necessary element under New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos did not set forth any facts showing he sustained actual pecuniary loss as a result of the supposed fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we affirm the District Court’s decision to dismiss this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
