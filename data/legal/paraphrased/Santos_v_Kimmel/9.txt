Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

Former Congressman George Santos challenges a final judgment issued by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”)—the network that broadcasts Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”)—and ABC’s parent, The Walt Disney Company. The litigation stemmed from Kimmel’s use of various pseudonyms to request personalized videos from Santos via the Cameo service. Santos completed these requests, after which Kimmel incorporated the videos into JKL segments mockingly entitled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court ruled in favor of the defendants, dismissing the suit pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, on the grounds that the copyright allegations were defeated by the fair use doctrine and that the state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or insufficiently pleaded. Agreeing that the fair use doctrine bars Santos’s copyright claims and determining that each state law claim fails to state a valid cause of action, we affirm the judgment.

DISCUSSION

Our review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6) is conducted de novo. See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). In this review, we examine “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In copyright litigation, the fair use affirmative defense “excuses conduct that might otherwise constitute infringement, enabling courts to avoid strict enforcement of the Copyright Act in circumstances where such enforcement would inhibit the creativity the Act seeks to encourage.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, our analysis of fair use involves consideration of several non-exhaustive factors: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When evaluating the “purpose and character of the use” factor, our primary inquiry is the extent to which the use qualifies as transformative—that is, whether it simply replaces the aims of the initial work, or, alternatively, introduces something novel with a distinct purpose or nature. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This emphasis demonstrates the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). A determination that fair use exists may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the pleading stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. Santos contends that the use at issue was not transformative, asserting that Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and, as such, is the “designer[]” of the original intent. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not contest the District Court’s conclusion that the contested use was intended “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. His argument instead is that such commentary was also his own intent in creating the videos. Nevertheless, the determination of whether a secondary use is transformative is grounded in the perception of a reasonable observer, and not the subjective aims of the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). According to Santos’s original pleading, a reasonable observer would conclude that the videos communicated “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” as opposed to merely indicating a willingness to say absurd things for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s alleged misrepresentations amount to bad faith, thereby precluding the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We are not persuaded by this argument. While it is correct that in Harper & Row the Supreme Court declared that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently taken a less stringent approach toward this principle. Moreover, Santos’s own complaint does not support any assertion that Defendants intended to “supplant” his “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the allegations present defendants as acting out of a desire to provide (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first statutory factor strongly favors fair use.

We likewise find no merit in Santos’s objections regarding the District Court’s treatment of the remaining elements. In particular, the relevant copyright registrations confirm that the videos were published concurrently with their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Additionally, the complaint itself plainly demonstrates that Santos did not experience the type of market harm contemplated by the fourth prong of the fair use analysis, since the inquiry focuses not on whether the subsequent work could lower the value of the original via parody or criticism, but rather on whether it supplants the original by serving as a direct competitor. Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he unlikelihood that creators of imaginative works will license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions removes such uses from the very notion of a potential licensing market.”).

Given that review is limited to “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue” for resolving “the question of fair use,” Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that the fair use doctrine precludes Santos’s copyright claims even at this stage of the litigation.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further contests the District Court’s dismissal of his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found the first two claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. Santos, however, argues that these claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and, therefore, are not preempted. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). We do not address this preemption argument because Santos’s contract-based state law claims do not survive under applicable state law.

We first examine Santos’s claim for direct breach of contract based on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service accepted by users like Kimmel, which are the terms Santos claims were violated. Illinois law governs the Terms of Service, and under that law there exists a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). For a contract to benefit third parties, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). Santos does not point to any “express declaration” suggesting that the relevant contract terms are intended to apply to him or other nonparties. In fact, some sections of the Terms of Service expressly address third-party beneficiaries, demonstrating that when the contracting parties sought to confer enforcement rights on third parties, they did so explicitly.

Turning to the implied contract claim under New York law, which applies here, the analysis looks to “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to decide “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, Santos’s complaint does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting the essential elements of the alleged implied contract supposedly breached by Defendants). Additionally, Santos fails to plausibly claim the existence of “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel would comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Lastly, Santos disputes the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim, which the District Court found deficient because it did not allege the requisite out-of-pocket loss under New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court that Santos did not adequately allege any actual out-of-pocket loss as the result of the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Consequently, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have considered Santos’s remaining arguments and conclude that they are without merit. For the foregoing reasons, we AFFIRM the judgment of the District Court.
