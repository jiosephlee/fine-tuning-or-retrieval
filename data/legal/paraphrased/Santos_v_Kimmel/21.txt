Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, formerly a member of Congress, appeals a final judgment entered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote), which dismissed his claims alleging copyright infringement as well as violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”)—the broadcaster of Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”)—and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. Santos initiated this litigation after Kimmel, operating under multiple assumed names, requested and received personalized video messages from Santos via the Cameo service. After Santos completed these requests, Kimmel featured the resulting videos on JKL in a satirical segment called “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court dismissed Santos’s complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, holding that the copyright allegations were precluded by the fair use doctrine, and that Santos’s state law causes of action were either superseded by the Copyright Act or failed to allege a cognizable claim. As we find that the fair use doctrine bars the copyright claims and that each of the state law claims is insufficient as a matter of law, we affirm the District Court’s judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s dismissal of Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our analysis includes “the facts set out in the complaint, any exhibits attached to the complaint, and documents incorporated by reference therein.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as they appear in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In copyright litigation, fair use serves as an affirmative defense that “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, courts evaluate the following non-exhaustive factors to determine whether fair use is present: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When considering the “purpose and character of the use” element, the analysis centers primarily on the extent to which the use alters the original, asking whether the subsequent work simply replaces the original’s function or offers additional material with a distinct purpose or character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach is grounded in the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). There are instances in which fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” at the pleading stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. contends that the use fails to be transformative because Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works” and thus acts as their original “designer[].” Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not contest the District Court’s determination that the purpose of the claimed infringing use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he asserts that his own purpose in creating the videos was the same. However, the question of whether a secondary use is transformative is judged by how a reasonable observer would view the matter, rather than by the personal motives of the copyright owner or of the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). According to Santos’s original allegations, a reasonable observer would perceive the videos as expressing “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” as opposed to being statements motivated by a willingness to say absurd things in exchange for payment. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s allegedly false statements exhibit bad faith, thereby invalidating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not accept this argument. While the Supreme Court observed in Harper & Row that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (quotation marks omitted), subsequent decisions have shown the Court moving away from that proposition. Regardless, the allegations in Santos’s complaint undermine any suggestion that Defendants sought to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Instead, the complaint suggests Defendants’ aim was (sarcastic) critique and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court that the first statutory factor weighs heavily in favor of fair use.

We also find Santos's objections to the District Court’s consideration of the remaining factors to be unconvincing. The applicable copyright registrations indicate that the videos in question were released publicly on the same day they were made. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, the face of Santos’s complaint demonstrates that no market harm, as contemplated by the fourth fair use factor, has occurred because, as explained in Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted), the inquiry “is not whether the second work would harm the market for the first (such as by devaluing it through parody or criticism), but instead whether it supplants the market for the original by supplying a competing alternative.” See also Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he remote likelihood that authors of creative works would license critical commentaries or parodies of their own material removes those types of uses from the relevant concept of a possible licensing market.”).

As our determination of “the question of fair use” requires review solely of “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s finding that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, including at the motion to dismiss stage.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s dismissal of his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court found the first two of these state law claims to be preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos contends that his claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and therefore not subject to preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, we do not reach the preemption question because, irrespective of that issue, Santos’s state law contract claims do not sufficiently allege entitlement to relief under applicable state law.

We begin by considering Santos’s straightforward breach of contract allegation premised on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos himself is not a signatory to these Terms, to which individuals such as Kimmel must assent and which Santos asserts were violated. Illinois law, which is controlling for the Terms of Service, establishes a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). In fact, to benefit a third party, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). Here, Santos fails to point to anything resembling such an “express declaration” suggesting the relevant terms of the Terms of Service extend to him or other individuals not party to the contract. In fact, the Terms of Service elsewhere expressly confer third-party beneficiary rights when intended, demonstrating that where the signatories meant for outsiders to enforce contractual terms, they made that intention clear. For the implied-in-fact contract claim, governed by New York law, the analysis looks to “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to resolve “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). But in this instance, Santos’s pleading does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting the fundamental terms of the purported implied contract supposedly breached by Defendants). Nor does Santos adequately plead facts plausibly suggesting “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that Kimmel would comply with the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also contests the District Court’s decision to dismiss his fraudulent inducement claim on the grounds that he did not plead any out-of-pocket loss, as mandated by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s determination that Santos did not claim to have incurred any real out-of-pocket loss as a result of the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

After reviewing Santos's additional arguments, we find them unpersuasive. Accordingly, for the reasons discussed above, we AFFIRM the District Court’s judgment.
