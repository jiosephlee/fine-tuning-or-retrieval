Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, formerly a member of Congress, challenges the final judgment entered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Cote, J.) which dismissed his copyright infringement and state law causes of action against Jimmy Kimmel; American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the broadcaster of Kimmel’s late-night program Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”); and ABC’s parent company, The Walt Disney Company. The dispute originated after Kimmel, using multiple aliases, submitted requests for personalized videos from Santos via the Cameo platform. Santos complied with these requests, and Kimmel subsequently broadcasted the videos on JKL in a satirical segment called “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court granted dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, determining that the doctrine of fair use precluded Santos’s copyright claims and that his state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or failed to adequately allege a claim. Upon review, as we agree that Santos’s copyright claims are foreclosed by fair use and also determine that none of his state law claims survive a motion to dismiss, we affirm the District Court’s judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts presented in the complaint, any exhibits attached thereto, and any documents that the complaint references and incorporates by reference.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations as set forth in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

Within a copyright lawsuit, fair use serves as an affirmative defense that “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). Pursuant to the Copyright Act, we analyze several non-exclusive considerations to ascertain whether fair use applies: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When considering the “purpose and character of the use” prong, our principal inquiry is on how transformative the use is; specifically, we examine whether the new work merely replaces the function of the original, or if it introduces novel elements with a distinct objective or altered character. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (internal quotation marks omitted). This emphasis is based on the notion that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). Furthermore, fair use may be “so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal of a copyright infringement claim” even at the motion to dismiss stage. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. argues that the use at issue does not constitute a transformative use, asserting that Kimmel “instigated the creation of the original Works,” making him the “designer[]” behind their intended function. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not contest the District Court’s determination that the point of the allegedly infringing use was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Rather, his argument is that this was the very motivation for his original videos. However, the assessment of whether a use is transformative is guided by what an objective, reasonable observer would conclude, not by the personal intent of either the copyright owner or secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). According to Santos’s own initial allegations, a reasonable observer in this context would interpret the videos as delivering “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” instead of a readiness to utter absurd statements in exchange for money. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s allegedly false statements amount to bad faith, thereby invalidating the fair use defense. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not find this argument persuasive. While it is correct that the Supreme Court remarked in Harper & Row that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” Id. at 562 (internal quotation marks omitted), the Court has since moderated that position. Additionally, the allegations in Santos’s complaint undermine any assertion that the Defendants aimed to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” in the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (internal quotation marks omitted). On the contrary, the complaint describes defendants as acting out of a motivation to provide (sarcastic) criticism and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s conclusion that the first factor weighs heavily in favor of fair use.

We likewise remain unconvinced by Santos’s objections to how the District Court addressed the other factors. Notably, the copyright registrations in question show that the videos were made public on the same date as their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Additionally, from the face of Santos’s own complaint, it is apparent that Santos has not demonstrated any market harm as contemplated by the fourth fair use factor, since “the inquiry is not whether the second work harms the value of the first by means such as parody or criticism, but rather whether it displaces the original by serving as a competing substitute.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he implausibility that originators of creative works will seek to license critical reviews or lampoons of their own output excludes such uses from constituting a potential licensing market.”).

Since our analysis of fair use requires review solely of “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” see Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that fair use precludes Santos’s copyright claims, including at the stage of the pleadings.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s rejection of his state law causes of action for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court concluded that the first two of these claims were barred by preemption under the Copyright Act. In response, Santos argues that these contract claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and therefore escape preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). However, it is unnecessary for us to address the preemption question, as Santos’s state law contract claims do not sufficiently allege an entitlement to relief under applicable state law.

We first consider Santos’s claim of direct breach of contract based on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos is not a signatory to the Terms of Service that users, such as Kimmel, are required to accept and which Santos asserts have been violated. The Terms of Service are governed by Illinois law, which applies a "strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties." Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). To that end, “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (quotation marks omitted). In this instance, Santos does not point to any provision approximating an “express declaration” indicating that the relevant sections of the Terms of Service are applicable to him or other non-signatories. On the contrary, the Terms of Service elsewhere use explicit third-party beneficiary language, demonstrating that where the contracting parties wished to permit third parties to enforce contractual terms, they did so unequivocally.

As for the claim governed by New York law, “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” are used to determine “[w]hether an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (quotation marks omitted). Here, however, Santos’s complaint does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (failing to plead the essential terms of the putative implied contract that the Defendants allegedly breached). Additionally, Santos does not plausibly claim the existence of “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” by which Kimmel was bound to the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also contests the District Court’s dismissal of his fraudulent inducement claim on the grounds that he did not assert any out-of-pocket loss, as mandated by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court's determination that Santos did not plead any actual out-of-pocket damages resulting from the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Accordingly, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by Santos and determine that they lack merit. Accordingly, for the reasons discussed above, we AFFIRM the District Court's judgment.
