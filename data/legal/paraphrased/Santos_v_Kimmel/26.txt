Title: Santos v. Kimmel
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit
Date: October 6, 2025
Docket: No. 24-2196-cv
Author: Lohier, Circuit Judge

George Santos, a former member of Congress, appeals from the final judgment entered by the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York (Judge Cote), which dismissed his claims of copyright infringement and violations of state law against Jimmy Kimmel, American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. (“ABC”), the network that broadcasts Jimmy Kimmel Live! (“JKL”), and ABC’s parent entity, The Walt Disney Company. Santos initiated this litigation following a series of events in which Kimmel used aliases to request personalized videos from Santos on the Cameo platform. After Santos completed the requests, Kimmel broadcast the resulting videos on JKL as a component of a satirical segment labeled “Will Santos Say It?” The District Court, acting under Rule 12(b)(6) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, dismissed the action for failure to state a claim. It determined that the fair use doctrine protected the use of the videos and precluded Santos’s copyright claims, while also holding that the state law claims were either preempted by the Copyright Act or otherwise insufficiently pleaded. As we concur that the fair use doctrine bars Santos’s copyright allegations and further determine that his state law causes of action fail to state a claim, we affirm the District Court’s judgment.

DISCUSSION

We conduct a de novo review of the District Court’s decision to dismiss Santos’s complaint pursuant to Rule 12(b)(6). See DiFolco v. MSNBC Cable L.L.C., 622 F.3d 104, 110 (2d Cir. 2010). Our review encompasses “the facts set forth in the complaint, any exhibits attached thereto, and documents that are incorporated by reference in the complaint.” Id. at 111. Additionally, we are permitted to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as they appear in the Copyright Office’s registry.” Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005); see also TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum, 839 F.3d 168, 172 (2d Cir. 2016).

I. Copyright Infringement and Fair Use

In the context of a copyright lawsuit, fair use serves as an affirmative defense that “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior, allowing courts to avoid rigid application of the Copyright Act when it would stifle the very creativity the Act is meant to promote.” Hachette Book Grp., Inc. v. Internet Archive, 115 F.4th 163, 179 (2d Cir. 2024) (quotation marks omitted). The Copyright Act instructs that courts evaluate the following non-exclusive criteria to determine the presence of fair use: “(1) the purpose and character of the use . . . ; (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used . . . ; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.” U.S.C. § 107.

When analyzing the “purpose and character of the use” element, the primary consideration is the extent to which the use can be deemed transformative—meaning whether the subsequent work simply replaces the function of the original or instead introduces new expression, purpose, or meaning. Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021) (quotation marks omitted). This approach stems from the recognition that “fair use is a flexible concept, whose application varies depending on the context.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179 (quotation marks omitted). In certain cases, fair use may be so apparent based on the allegations in the complaint that it warrants dismissal of a copyright infringement claim on the pleadings. TCA Television Corp., 839 F.3d at 178. asserts that the use cannot be transformative since Kimmel allegedly “instigated the creation of the original Works” and thus should be considered the “designer[]” of their intended function. Appellant’s Br. 11. Santos does not contest the District Court’s conclusion that the allegedly infringing purpose was “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money.” App’x 235. Instead, he contends that this also represented his own initial intention in creating the videos. Nonetheless, the determination of whether a secondary use is transformative depends on the perspective of a reasonable observer, not on the subjective intentions of either the copyright owner or the secondary user. See Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 598 U.S. 508, 544 (2023) (“[T]he subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) [does not] determine the purpose of the use.”). As Santos’s initial complaint concedes, a reasonable observer would interpret the videos as communicating “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” rather than a willingness to make absurd statements for compensation. App’x 15.

Santos further argues that Kimmel’s misrepresentations constitute bad faith, thereby vitiating the fair use doctrine. See Harper & Row Publishers, Inc. v. Nation Enters., 471 U.S. 539, 562–63 (1985). We do not concur with this position. Although the Supreme Court in Harper & Row observed that “[f]air use presupposes good faith and fair dealing,” id. at 562 (internal quotation marks omitted), the Court has subsequently taken a step back from this pronouncement. Regardless, Santos’s complaint does not support an allegation that Defendants intended to “supplant” Santos’s “commercially valuable right” to the videos. Swatch Grp. Mgmt. Servs. Ltd. v. Bloomberg L.P., 756 F.3d 73, 83 (2d Cir. 2014) (quotation marks omitted). Instead, the complaint suggests the defendants acted for the purpose of (satirical) critique and commentary. See Authors Guild v. Google, Inc., 804 F.3d 202, 215–16 (2d Cir. 2015). Accordingly, we concur with the District Court’s determination that the first factor decidedly favors a finding of fair use.

We likewise find no merit in Santos’s objections to the District Court’s evaluation of the other relevant considerations. The copyright registrations in question demonstrate that the videos were made public on the same date as their creation. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101. Furthermore, it is evident from the allegations in Santos’s complaint that Santos has not experienced harm to the market as contemplated by the fourth factor of the fair use analysis, since “the issue is not whether the second work might harm the market for the original (such as by reducing its value through parody or criticism), but whether it supplants the market for the original by serving as a direct commercial alternative.” Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 (quotation marks omitted); see Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994) (“[T]he improbability that creators of expressive works would license unfavorable commentaries or parodies of their own works takes such uses outside the core concept of a potential licensing market.”).

Since our analysis of fair use is limited to “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue,” as stated in Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) (quotation marks omitted), we uphold the District Court’s determination that Santos’s copyright claims are precluded by fair use, even when considering only the pleadings.

II. State Law Claims

Santos further challenges the District Court’s decision to dismiss his state law claims for breach of contract, breach of implied contract, and fraudulent inducement. The District Court determined that the first two of these claims were preempted by the Copyright Act. In response, Santos argues that the claims are “qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim” and are therefore not subject to preemption. Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022) (quotation marks omitted). Nonetheless, we need not address the preemption question because, irrespective of preemption, Santos’s state law contract claims do not present a plausible basis for relief under state law.

We begin by examining Santos’s claim for direct breach of contract based on the Cameo Terms of Service. Santos himself is not a signatory to the Terms of Service that users, such as Kimmel, are required to accept and that Santos claims were violated. Illinois law, which governs these Terms of Service, establishes a “strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.” Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, 68 N.E.3d 964, 976 (Ill. App. Ct. 2016). The relevant principle dictates that “the implication that the contract applies to third parties must be so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Id. (internal quotation marks omitted). In the present case, Santos fails to point to anything resembling such an “express declaration” that the provisions at issue in the Terms of Service apply to him or other individuals who are not parties to the contract. In fact, the Terms of Service elsewhere include language explicitly granting third-party beneficiary rights, showing that where the contracting parties intended for provisions to be enforceable by third parties, this was directly expressed. 

Turning to New York law, which is also relevant to this claim, the inquiry is focused on “the intent of the parties and the surrounding circumstances” to determine whether “an implied-in-fact contract was formed and, if so, the extent of its terms.” Rocky Point Props., Inc. v. Sear-Brown Grp., Inc., 744 N.Y.S.2d 269, 271 (4th Dep’t 2002) (internal quotation marks omitted). However, in this case, the complaint filed by Santos does not “allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms of the parties’ . . . contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” Caniglia v. Chi. Trib.-N.Y. News Syndicate, Inc., 612 N.Y.S.2d 146, 147 (1st Dep’t 1994); see App’x 22 ¶¶ 78-79 (omitting factual allegations that would identify the material terms of the supposed implied contract Defendants allegedly violated). In addition, Santos does not adequately allege facts plausibly demonstrating “an indication of a meeting of minds of the parties constituting an agreement” that would require Kimmel to abide by the Terms of Service. DG & A Mgmt. Servs., LLC v. Secs. Indus. Ass’n Compliance & Legal Div., 859 N.Y.S.2d 305, 306 (3d Dep’t 2008) (quotation marks omitted); see also Tjoa v. Julia Butterfield Mem’l Hosp., 612 N.Y.S.2d 676, 677 (2d Dep’t 1994).

Santos also contests the District Court's decision to dismiss his fraudulent inducement claim on the grounds that he did not allege any out-of-pocket loss, as is required by New York law. See Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc., 88 N.Y.2d 413, 421 (1996). We concur with the District Court’s finding that Santos did not assert that he incurred any real out-of-pocket loss resulting from the purported fraud. See Connaughton v. Chipotle Mexican Grill, Inc., 29 N.Y.3d 137, 142–43 (2017). Consequently, we uphold the District Court’s dismissal of this claim.

CONCLUSION

We have reviewed the additional arguments presented by Santos and determine that they lack merit. Accordingly, for the reasons stated above, we AFFIRM the District Court’s judgment.
