The Court conducted oral argument on this matter on an expedited schedule on September 3, 2025. A formal opinion will follow in due time. Meanwhile, this Order serves as a stay pending appeal and until further directive from this Court. We hereby set aside our administrative stay from July 18, 2025. Additionally, we rescind our earlier stay dated March 25, 2025, as modified on April 21 and May 9, 2025. The preliminary injunctions entered by the district court on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. The government’s emergency motion filed on July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108) is dismissed as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order No. 14163, named “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” See 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under the authority of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President found that permitting refugees to enter the country through the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) would harm the interests of the United States, and accordingly ordered the suspension of refugee entry under USRAP until additional determinations could be made. Exec. Order No. 14163, § 3(a). See also 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (stating that “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all USRAP funding, allegedly following a separate order—Executive Order No. 14169, titled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” See 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This measure temporarily ceased funding for services supporting the domestic resettlement of refugees while further evaluation was underway.

The plaintiffs consist of refugees who either have already been admitted into the United States or have been approved for resettlement but remain outside the country, along with three organizations holding cooperative agreements with the State Department for overseas processing of refugee applications and for providing resettlement services to refugees already admitted. In February 2025, these plaintiffs brought an action in the Western District of Washington, arguing that Executive Order 14163’s suspension of the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (currently codified in multiple sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and that cutting off funding for USRAP ran afoul of several provisions within the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (characterizing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court issued an oral preliminary injunction that enjoined the enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The government subsequently terminated, on February 26, every cooperative agreement related to the provision of reception and placement services for refugees in the United States, along with all except one agreement supporting USRAP processing abroad. On February 28, the district court entered a written order clarifying the coverage of its February 25 preliminary injunction, indicating that it also enjoins any suspension of funding for USRAP. On March 24, the court issued a second preliminary injunction compelling the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that had been ended after the February 25 order. The government submitted notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s February 28 order while it appealed, and a motions panel of this court partially granted that request on March 25. In referencing Trump v. Hawaii, the panel noted that the President's authority to issue Executive Order No. 14163, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), “exudes deference” toward the President and grants him significant authority “to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (citing 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nonetheless, the court denied the motion for a stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Subsequently, two further orders were entered to clarify the parameters of this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The panel instructed the parties to submit full briefing and ordered that the appeal be placed on the next available calendar by the Clerk of Court.

After briefing but before oral argument, which was scheduled for September 19, 2025, the district court, on July 14, issued an order creating a structure for the implementation of the limited carveout and assigned a magistrate judge to supervise it. On July 17, the government filed an emergency motion requesting enforcement, clarification, or modification of the stay order, which plaintiffs opposed. On July 18, 2025, the court entered an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order. Oral argument was expedited and scheduled accordingly.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties’ written submissions and hearing oral argument, the matter is now under submission for a decision. A ruling on the merits will be forthcoming. In the meantime, we hereby dissolve our administrative stay issued on July 18 and our stay from March 25, as modified on April 21 and May 9. We now impose a new stay pending the ultimate disposition of this appeal. Accordingly, the district court's orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed in full, except as expressly provided below.

The criteria for granting a stay are well established. The court evaluates four factors: (1) whether the party seeking the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether, absent a stay, the applicant will suffer irreparable harm; (3) whether other interested parties would suffer substantial harm from the issuance of a stay; and (4) the public interest considerations. See Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Among these, the first and second factors—a likelihood of success on the merits and the possibility of irreparable harm—are regarded as “the most critical.” Id. It appears that the government is likely to succeed in defending against plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions, and judicial review does not permit a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Id. at 685. “[T]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, specifically the power to suspend the “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other relevant factors, implicating both domestic and international interests of the United States—as vested in the “broad discretion” granted to the President, Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84—also favor the United States.

The government, as will be detailed more thoroughly in a forthcoming opinion, does not appear likely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ claims brought under the Administrative Procedure Act. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, specific reception and placement services must be provided by the government to refugees upon their entry into the United States. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) obliges the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—which operates under the Department of Health and Human Services—“to the extent of available appropriations”—to: (i) allocate adequate resources for employment training and placement so that refugees may achieve economic independence as expeditiously as possible, (ii) ensure that refugees have access to sufficient English language instruction to facilitate their swift integration, (iii) guarantee that the provision of cash assistance does not undermine the refugees’ drive toward self-sufficiency, in line with subsection (e)(2), and (iv) ensure women receive equal access to training and educational opportunities as men. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (outlining federal funding mechanisms for refugee assistance). For unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) stipulates: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Regarding monetary and medical aid for newly arrived refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) directs that the Secretary of Health and Human Services “shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” It is noteworthy that Congress allocated $3.178 billion in migration and refugee assistance for fiscal year 2025, see Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating appropriations from division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), subsequently reduced by $800 million via the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Additionally, § 1523 imposes a duty on the Secretary to submit a comprehensive report regarding all activities pursuant to this subchapter to the Judiciary Committees of both the House of Representatives and the Senate by January 31 following each fiscal year's end.

According to statute, these resettlement services—commonly called “reception and placement” services—may be delivered by means of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Traditionally, the United States has used cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations to facilitate such services. The plaintiff organizations are three entities with which the Department of State has maintained such cooperative agreements.

As previously noted, in February 2024, the State Department ended all existing cooperative agreements for provision of these services. Recognizing this cessation of resettlement funding, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nonetheless, at oral argument before the panel, the government stated that it did not know whether any advancement had occurred in obtaining a new agency or agencies to provide the necessary reception and placement services.

Given the government’s lack of clarity about its capacity to deliver the reception and placement services mandated by 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is ordered to restore such cooperative agreements as are required to ensure provision of the reception and placement services outlined in § 1522 to refugees admitted to the United States. These services must comprise those typical and customary offerings that were available to refugees under the previous cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President possesses broad discretion and is accorded significant deference in matters of immigration, the district court erred by issuing an injunction preventing the executive branch from carrying out the President’s policy restricting refugee admissions and corresponding services. Accordingly, I would grant a complete stay of the district court’s injunctions.

My fellow panel members and I concur that the President holds the power to suspend refugee admissions, and for that reason, we would stay the portion of the injunction relating to that issue. However, we diverge with respect to whether the federal government is legally obligated to offer services to refugees who were admitted before the suspension commenced. My colleagues interpret 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a requirement upon the government to deliver certain services. In contrast, I view the statute’s most reasonable reading as one that confers authority—not compulsion.

We begin with the statutory language: Section 1522 bears the heading “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis in original). The term "authorization" connotes permission rather than duty. AUTHORIZATION, Black's Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining the term as “permission to do something” or an “official document granting such permission”). In §1522, Congress provides for the possibility of action, but does not create a requirement.

Moreover, each of the four statutory subsections supplying funds for various refugee services—such as the “Program of initial resettlement” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—explicitly states that the “Director is authorized” to extend assistance. U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis in original). These phrases reinforce the notion that the statutory structure entrusts such decisions to the discretion of the executive branch.

In asserting that Section 1522 creates a mandate to provide funding, my colleagues rely on §1522(a), titled “Conditions and consideration,” which contains the directive “shall” as well as the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this provision is limited by the immediately preceding language: “In providing assistance under this section [referring to the permissive funding authorities in §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ” Thus, §1522(a) establishes certain required “conditions and considerations” only if the government opts to allocate funds for refugee initiatives. For instance, the Director “shall. . . make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [from § 1522(c)’s discretionary funding relating to ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible.” In essence, this presents a required condition or factor that must be satisfied if funding is chosen, rather than a standalone mandate to provide the funding.

To reinforce their interpretation of a mandatory funding requirement, my colleagues reference subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), both of which may appear to create duties for the Director. Nevertheless, neither provision convincingly rebuts the overarching function of § 1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) makes the Director legally and financially responsible for unaccompanied refugee children solely when that responsibility is deemed “necessary.” Furthermore, this provision gives the Director authority “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Although the subsection utilizes “shall,” its deference to executive judgement is evident, and it imposes no absolute directive. Additionally, the record does not clarify whether there are any unaccompanied refugee children pertinent to this dispute. Likewise, subsection (e)(7)(A) obligates that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” projects for providing cash and medical support for refugees “as needed.” The particulars of such programs are reserved for executive judgment, reflecting discretion rather than a compulsory congressional directive.

In my assessment, Section 1522 merely permits the executive branch to design programs at its discretion provided that appropriations are available, but does not impose a duty to operate those programs. Since the statute does not compel the executive branch to deliver these services, the court lacks authority to require them. I therefore respectfully dissent in part.
