Title: Pacito v. Trump

Oral argument in this matter was conducted on an expedited schedule on September 3, 2025. A formal opinion will be released in due course. Meanwhile, this Order serves as a stay during the pendency of the appeal and until further direction from this court. We hereby set aside the administrative stay that was put in place on July 18, 2025. Additionally, our previous stay dated March 25, 2025, as amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025, is also vacated. The preliminary injunctions issued by the district court on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45), and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79), are stayed in full, with the exception of any parts addressed otherwise in this Order. The government’s emergency motion filed on July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108), is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Relying on 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and ordered that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until further findings were made. Exec. Order. No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (stating in pertinent part that “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all financial support for the USRAP program, purportedly acting under a separate executive order titled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This freeze, pending additional review, also encompassed funding for domestic resettlement assistance provided to refugees who had been admitted to the United States.

The plaintiffs consist of refugees who were either previously admitted into the United States or have received approval for resettlement in the United States but remain outside its borders, along with three organizations holding cooperative agreements with the State Department to provide overseas processing for individuals seeking refugee status and resettlement services for admitted refugees. In February 2025, the plaintiffs initiated litigation in the Western District of Washington, contending that Executive Order 14163’s halt of the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified at various locations within the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and further asserting that the termination of funding for USRAP violated sections of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (describing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court verbally granted a preliminary injunction preventing enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The next day, February 26, the government ended all cooperative agreements related to reception and placement services for refugees within the United States and, with one exception, also terminated cooperative agreements for USRAP processing abroad. On February 28, the district court entered a written order clarifying the preliminary injunction from February 25, stating that it also bars the suspension of funding for USRAP. Subsequently, on March 24, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction compelling the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 order. The government submitted notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s February 28 order while its appeal was pending, and a motions panel of this court partially granted that request on March 25. Citing Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the statutory authority invoked by the President to issue Executive Order No. 14163, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), “exudes deference” to presidential decision-making and “vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nevertheless, we denied the stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. We later issued two further orders to clarify this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel also ordered comprehensive briefing and instructed the Clerk of Court to schedule the appeal for the next available hearing calendar.

After the parties had submitted briefs but prior to the argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court issued a July 14 order establishing a mechanism for implementing the limited carveout, including appointing a magistrate judge to oversee the process. On July 17, the government submitted an emergency motion seeking to enforce, clarify, or modify the stay order, which the plaintiffs opposed. On July 18, 2025, we entered an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and scheduled oral argument on an expedited timeline.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties’ briefing and oral argument, the matter is now under submission for a decision. A decision on the merits will be issued in due course. Meanwhile, we hereby dissolve both our administrative stay entered on July 18 and our stay from March 25, as modified on April 21 and May 9. A new stay is ordered pending the final determination of this appeal. The district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025 are hereby stayed in full, except where specified otherwise below.

The criteria governing the issuance of a stay are well-settled. Four elements guide our consideration: (1) whether the applicant for a stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant faces irreparable harm if a stay is not granted; (3) whether the stay would cause substantial injury to other parties in the case; and (4) the public interest considerations. See Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). The first two—likelihood of success and risk of irreparable injury—are identified as “the most critical” considerations. Id. The government has shown a strong probability of prevailing against plaintiffs’ claim regarding the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s refugee admissions suspension, and it is not within our purview to conduct “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Id. at 685. “[T]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, to bar the “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other factors—which concern both domestic and international interests of the United States, as governed by the President’s “broad discretion,” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84—also weigh in favor of the United States.

As will be set out in detail in a forthcoming opinion, the government does not appear likely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ claims brought under the APA. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government has a duty to furnish certain reception and placement services to refugees after their entry into the United States. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) obligates the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, a division of the Department of Health and Human Services, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to: (i) allocate adequate resources for employment training and job placement to facilitate the rapid economic self-sufficiency of refugees, (ii) offer opportunities for refugees to obtain sufficient English language training to ensure effective and timely resettlement, (iii) guarantee that cash assistance for refugees is distributed so as not to undermine their pursuit of economic self-sufficiency, consistent with subsection (e)(2), and (iv) ensure equal access for women and men to participate in training and educational programs. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (which detail federal funding provisions for refugee services). Regarding unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). With respect to cash and medical aid for recently arrived refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) mandates that the Secretary of Health and Human Services “shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” It should also be observed that for fiscal year 2025, Congress allocated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, see Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (adopting the appropriations found in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), which was subsequently reduced by $800 million by the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Section 1523 imposes a requirement on the Secretary to submit a comprehensive report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

Pursuant to statute, these resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be delivered through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Historically, the United States has used cooperative agreements with various resettlement entities to provide these services. The plaintiff organizations are three such entities with which the Department of State has entered into cooperative agreements.

As previously noted, in February 2024, the State Department ended all cooperative agreements related to these services. Following this cessation of funding, the government notified the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, at oral argument, the government informed the panel that it was unaware of any developments regarding the identification or selection of a new agency or agencies capable of providing these reception and placement services.

Given the government’s lack of certainty as to whether it can fulfill its statutory obligation under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 to provide reception and placement services, the government is hereby ordered to restore such cooperative agreements as are required to deliver the reception and placement services outlined in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted into the United States. These services must include those that have customarily and ordinarily been provided to such refugees pursuant to prior cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is granted broad authority and deference concerning immigration issues, the district court erroneously prohibited the executive branch from carrying out the President’s chosen policy to restrict refugee admissions and related services. Accordingly, I would grant a stay of the district court’s injunctions in full.

My fellow panel members and I concur that the President possesses the power to halt refugee admissions, and for that reason, we would stay the injunction entered by the district court on that matter. However, we disagree regarding whether the federal government is legally obligated to provide services to individuals who were admitted prior to the suspension’s effective date. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a requirement upon the government to render specified services to refugees. In my view, the statute is better understood as conferring authority, not establishing a duty.

Let us begin with the statutory language: The heading of Section 1522 reads “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis added). The word “authorization” conveys discretion rather than compulsion. AUTHORIZATION, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining the term as “permission to do something” or the “official document granting such permission”). Through § 1522, Congress permits, but does not command, such action.

Moreover, the four statutory subsections that allocate funds for distinct refugee services—such as “Program of initial resettlement” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—consistently provide that the “Director is authorized” to furnish such assistance. U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis added). These provisions likewise indicate that Congress has vested the executive branch with discretion over such funding.

In asserting that Section 1522 mandates funding, my colleagues focus on §1522(a), entitled “Conditions and consideration,” which includes the term “shall” and the clause “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this wording is limited by the preceding phrase: “In providing assistance under this section [referring to the discretionary funding measures in §1522(b)-(e)]...” Thus, §1522(a) sets forth certain mandatory “conditions and considerations” only when the government elects to finance refugee programs. For instance, the Director “shall... make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [as provided by § 1522(c)’s discretionary funding for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugees’] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible”—that is, this requirement becomes operative only if the government chooses to provide such funding.

To support their assertion of compelled spending, my colleagues point to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which could be read to create some duties for the Director. Yet neither provision effectively undermines the general functioning of § 1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) stipulates that the Director assumes legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children solely when that responsibility is deemed “necessary.” The subsection also empowers the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Despite the use of “shall,” the language confers significant discretion upon the executive and does not set forth a clear-cut requirement. Moreover, it is unclear from the current record whether any unaccompanied refugee children are at issue. Likewise, subsection (e)(7)(A) provides that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” initiatives for cash and medical assistance to refugees “as needed.” The precise scope and structure of these programs rest with the executive’s judgment, and thus do not constitute a definitive directive from Congress.

From my perspective, Section 1522 grants authority to the executive branch to design programs at its discretion so long as appropriations are provided. It does not obligate the executive to implement those programs. Since the statute does not impose a duty on the executive to deliver these services, this court cannot require them to do so. Accordingly, I must respectfully dissent in part.
