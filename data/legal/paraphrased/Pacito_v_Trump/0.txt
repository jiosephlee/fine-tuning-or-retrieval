BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order labeled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under the authority of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that allowing refugees to enter through the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) would harm national interests. Consequently, the order mandated the suspension of refugee admissions under the USRAP until additional determinations could be made. Exec. Order No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (authorizing the President, “whenever [he] finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, [to] by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all financial support for the USRAP, citing a separate executive order titled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This freeze, pending additional assessment, encompassed funding for services aiding refugee resettlement within the United States.

The plaintiffs consist of individuals recognized as refugees who were either previously admitted into the United States or have been approved for resettlement but currently reside outside the country, along with three organizations that maintained cooperative agreements with the State Department for overseas processing of refugee applications and provision of resettlement services to admitted refugees. In February 2025, these plaintiffs initiated legal proceedings in the Western District of Washington, asserting that the suspension of the refugee program by Executive Order 14163 contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (as codified in multiple sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and that the termination of funding for USRAP breached various requirements of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (characterizing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court delivered an oral preliminary injunction that barred the enforcement or execution of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The following day, February 26, the government rescinded all cooperative agreements related to the reception and placement of refugees within the United States and terminated all but one agreement for USRAP processing abroad. On February 28, the district court issued a written order clarifying that the preliminary injunction also extended to the suspension of funding for USRAP. Subsequently, on March 24, the district court granted a second preliminary injunction mandating the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated following the February 25 order. The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s February 28 order while its appeal was pending, and a motions panel from this court partially granted that request on March 25. In reference to Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the President’s issuance of Executive Order No. 14163 was grounded in the authority of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which “exudes deference” to the President and “vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (citing 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nevertheless, we declined to stay the district court’s preliminary injunction “insofar as it applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services prior to January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Subsequently, we issued two clarifying orders addressing the scope of this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel also requested comprehensive briefing and instructed the Clerk of Court to assign the appeal to the earliest available hearing date.

After the completion of briefing and prior to the argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court entered an order on July 14 to create a procedure for implementing the limited carveout and designated a magistrate judge to supervise this process. Subsequently, on July 17, the government submitted an emergency motion seeking to enforce, clarify, or modify the stay order, which was contested by the plaintiffs. On July 18, 2025, we granted an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order. We arranged for oral argument to proceed on an accelerated schedule.

DISCUSSION

Following the consideration of the parties’ briefs and the oral arguments, the matter is now under advisement for determination. A decision addressing the substantive issues will be released in due time. Meanwhile, we hereby lift the administrative stay entered on July 18 as well as the stay imposed on March 25, along with its modifications dated April 21 and May 9. A new stay is entered, which shall remain effective until this appeal is conclusively resolved. The district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025 are hereby stayed in full, except as specifically provided below.

The criteria governing the grant of a stay are well-defined in legal precedent. There are four considerations: (1) whether the party requesting the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant faces irreparable harm if the stay is denied; (3) the potential for significant harm to other interested parties if the stay is granted; and (4) the implications for the public interest. See Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Among these, the first two—the likelihood of success and the possibility of irreparable harm—are regarded as paramount. Id. The government is poised to succeed against the plaintiffs’ challenge regarding the legitimacy of Executive Order No. 14163’s halt on refugee admissions, and courts are constrained from rigorously examining the President’s stated justifications. See Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. Section 1182(f) stipulates only that the President must “find[]” that allowing the entry of specified aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” a condition that has unquestionably been met in this instance. Id. at 685. Furthermore, “[t]he language of § 1182(f) is unambiguous, and the [Executive Order] does not contravene any express statutory limitation on presidential authority,” id. at 688, in suspending the “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other factors—pertaining to the domestic and international interests of the United States and rooted in the President’s “broad discretion,” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84—also weigh in favor of the United States.

The Court will elaborate in a forthcoming opinion, but for now it is apparent that the government is unlikely to succeed against at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA claims. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government holds statutory obligations to supply specific reception and placement services for refugees following their entry into the United States. In particular, § 1522(a)(1)(A) directs the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, part of the Department of Health and Human Services, to ensure—subject to appropriations availability—the following: (i) adequate resources for employment training and job placement are provided to facilitate refugees’ rapid attainment of economic self-sufficiency; (ii) refugees receive English language instruction sufficient to permit swift and effective resettlement; (iii) cash assistance is distributed in a manner consistent with encouraging, rather than deterring, self-sufficiency in line with subsection (e)(2); and (iv) equal opportunities for participation in training and education are offered to both women and men. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq., which further sets out federal funding mechanisms for refugee services. In the case of unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) stipulates: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). For newly resettled refugees needing cash and medical support, § 1522(e)(7)(A) tasks the Secretary of Health and Human Services with designing and instituting alternative projects to serve refugees who have spent less than thirty-six months in the United States, ensuring provision of interim support, medical assistance, supplementary services, and case management as appropriate. As an additional point, it should be noted that the Congressional appropriation for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025 totals $3.178 billion (see Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11), referencing division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), with a subsequent reduction of $800 million pursuant to the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Finally, § 1523 establishes a requirement for the Secretary to submit a comprehensive report of subchapter activities to the Judiciary Committees of both Houses of Congress by January 31 each year, following the conclusion of the fiscal year.

According to statutory provisions, resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—can be administered via contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements involving “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Traditionally, the United States has utilized cooperative agreements with a range of resettlement organizations to deliver these services. The organizational plaintiffs in this context are among the entities that have established such cooperative agreements with the Department of State.

As previously noted, in February 2024, the State Department ended all cooperative agreements related to these services. Recognizing this cessation of resettlement service funding, the government notified the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, during oral argument before the panel, the government indicated that it did not know whether there had been any advancement in the process of identifying a new agency or agencies capable of providing reception and placement services.

Given the government’s doubts about its capacity to deliver the reception and placement services required by statute under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is ordered to renew any cooperative agreements needed to supply the reception and placement services delineated in § 1522 to refugees admitted into the United States. These services should encompass the standard provisions that refugees previously received pursuant to earlier cooperative agreements.

Although the President is granted broad latitude and significant judicial deference in the realm of immigration, the district court erred in prohibiting the executive branch from enforcing the President’s chosen policy restricting refugee admissions and associated services. Consequently, I would fully stay the district court’s injunctions.

My associates and I concur that the President possesses the power to halt refugee admissions, and therefore, I would grant a stay of the district court’s injunction with respect to that matter. However, our views diverge on the question of whether the federal government is legally obligated to deliver services to individuals who had already been admitted prior to the implementation of the suspension. My colleagues read 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a requirement on the government to supply specific services to refugees. In my interpretation, this statutory provision is best understood as granting permission rather than establishing a compulsory duty.

Let us begin by examining the title of Section 1522: “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis added). The term “authorization” signifies permission rather than obligation. As noted in Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024), AUTHORIZATION is defined as “permission to do something” or an “official document granting such permission.” Consequently, §1522 enables but does not compel Congress to take action.

Additionally, the four distinct statutory clauses that allocate resources for various refugee programs—such as “Program of initial resettlement” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—consistently specify that the “Director is authorized” to offer such support. U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis added). This language once more indicates congressional intent to grant the executive branch latitude in determining the distribution of funds.

In asserting that Section 1522 creates an obligation for mandatory funding, my peers refer to §1522(a)—entitled “Conditions and consideration”—which contains the term “shall” alongside the qualification “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this wording is further circumscribed by the preceding clause: “In providing assistance under this section [that is, the discretionary funding mechanisms described in §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ” In effect, §1522(a) stipulates certain obligatory “conditions and considerations” only in the event that the government opts to finance refugee initiatives. For instance, the Director “shall. . . make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [drawing on the discretionary appropriations outlined in § 1522(c) for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugees’] so as to facilitate the attainment of economic self-sufficiency among refugees at the earliest possible opportunity [in other words, this constitutes the mandatory condition or consideration to be weighed should funding be provided].”

In support of their position regarding mandatory spending, my peers point to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which could be interpreted as placing certain duties on the Director. Nonetheless, these provisions do not effectively challenge the general structure set forth by § 1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) stipulates that the program Director is to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children only when it is “necessary.” The provision further grants the Director authority “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Although the term “shall” is present, the subsection is heavily couched in deference to executive authority and falls short of articulating a concrete requirement. Furthermore, the evidence does not make it apparent whether any unaccompanied refugee children are implicated in this context. Along the same lines, subsection (e)(7)(A) prescribes that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” initiatives providing cash and medical assistance to refugees “as needed.” Ultimately, the extent and parameters of these programs are left to the judgment of the executive, signifying executive flexibility rather than explicit congressional compulsion.

From my perspective, Section 1522 grants the executive branch the latitude to establish programs at its discretion, contingent upon the availability of appropriated funds. However, it does not obligate the actual operation of these programs. Since the statute does not impose a duty on the executive branch to deliver these services, the judiciary lacks the authority to compel their implementation. Accordingly, I must respectfully dissent in part.
