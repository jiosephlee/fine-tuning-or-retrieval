Title: Pacito v. Trump

The court conducted oral argument in this matter on an expedited schedule on September 3, 2025. A formal opinion will follow in due course. Meanwhile, this Order will serve as a stay during the appeal and until further order from this court. The administrative stay imposed on July 18, 2025, is hereby set aside. The earlier stay dated March 25, 2025, as modified on April 21 and May 9, 2025, is also vacated. The preliminary injunctions granted by the district court on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45) and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79) are stayed in full, with the exception of any terms specifically addressed in this Order. The government’s emergency motion filed on July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108), is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order named “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that allowing refugees to enter through the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and therefore ordered that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until additional findings were made. Exec. Order. No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (stating, in pertinent part, that “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Then, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all financial support for the USRAP program, claiming authority from a separate executive order entitled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This suspension, pending ongoing review, encompassed funding for services aiding refugees resettling in the United States.

The plaintiffs include individuals who are refugees either previously admitted into the United States or approved for U.S. resettlement while remaining outside the country, along with three organizations that maintained cooperative agreements with the State Department for overseas processing of refugee applicants and resettlement services for admitted refugees. In February 2025, they initiated legal proceedings in the Western District of Washington, asserting that the suspension of the refugee program pursuant to Executive Order 14163 contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (as codified in sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and further, that the withdrawal of funding for USRAP violated multiple provisions within the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (noting that the Refugee Act provides “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court delivered an oral preliminary injunction restraining the enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The following day, February 26, the government terminated all cooperative agreements providing refugee reception and placement services inside the United States and, with one exception, all agreements offering USRAP processing assistance abroad. On February 28, the district court clarified the preliminary injunction through a written order, holding that it also enjoined suspension of USRAP funding. Subsequently, on March 24, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction compelling the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that had been ended after the February 25 order. The government submitted notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay pending appeal of the district court's February 28 order, which was partially granted by a motions panel of this court on March 25. Referring to Trump v. Hawaii, the court acknowledged that the President’s issuance of Executive Order No. 14163 pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) "'exudes deference' to the President and 'vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].'" Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nevertheless, we declined to stay "to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025." Id. at 1. Subsequently, two further orders were issued to clarify the scope of this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel also required comprehensive briefing and directed the Clerk of Court to schedule the appeal on the earliest available calendar.

After briefs were filed, but prior to the argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court issued a July 14 order that created a mechanism to implement the limited carveout and designated a magistrate judge to supervise this process. On July 17, the government submitted an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order, opposed by the plaintiffs. On July 18, 2025, we entered an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and arranged for oral argument on an expedited schedule.

DISCUSSION

Having considered the parties’ written submissions and oral presentations, we now deem the matter submitted for adjudication. A merits opinion will follow in due course. Meanwhile, we hereby lift the administrative stay entered on July 18, as well as the stay granted on March 25, as subsequently modified on April 21 and May 9. We grant a stay of the district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, in full, with exceptions as specified below, until this appeal is finally resolved.

The established criteria governing the grant of a stay require us to evaluate four considerations: (1) the extent to which the party seeking the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will sustain irreparable harm in the absence of a stay; (3) the potential for substantial injury to the other parties involved; and (4) the public interest. See Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Of these, the two primary factors are the probability of success on the merits and risk of irreparable injury. Id. The government has shown a probable likelihood of success in its defense against plaintiffs’ challenge to Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions, and our review does not encompass “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. Section 1182(f) establishes a single prerequisite: that the President “find[]” the entry of certain aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Here, the President has unquestionably satisfied that statutory requirement. Id. at 685. The express text of § 1182(f) is unambiguous, and the Executive Order remains within the boundaries of the President’s statutory authority, id. at 688, to suspend “the entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other considerations, implicating both internal and external concerns of the United States, and within the “broad discretion” given to the President, Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84, also weigh in favor of the United States.

The reasons underlying this conclusion will be thoroughly addressed in a forthcoming opinion; however, at this stage it appears the government is unlikely to succeed on at least one of plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act claims. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, following their admission to the United States, the government is mandated to extend certain reception and placement services to refugees. According to § 1522(a)(1)(A), the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—a division of the Department of Health and Human Services—is required, within the limitations of available appropriations, to: (i) allocate sufficient resources for employment training and job placement to enable refugees to achieve economic self-sufficiency expeditiously; (ii) ensure that refugees have the opportunity to receive adequate English language training to facilitate prompt and effective resettlement; (iii) guarantee that the provision of cash assistance to refugees does not act as a deterrent to their economic self-sufficiency, in alignment with subsection (e)(2); and (iv) ensure equitable access for women to the same training and educational opportunities as men. Refer also to 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq., which outlines the scope of federal funding available for refugee-related services. Likewise, regarding unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) stipulates: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). As to cash and medical support for recently arrived refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) obligates the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For fiscal year 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion to migration and refugee assistance, see Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating the appropriations set forth in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), although this sum was reduced by $800 million under the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Section 1523 also mandates that the Secretary submit a comprehensive annual report on activities conducted pursuant to this subchapter to the Judiciary Committees of both the House of Representatives and the Senate, with a deadline of January 31 following each fiscal year’s conclusion.

Pursuant to statute, these resettlement services—commonly termed “reception and placement” services—may be delivered via contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements involving “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Historically, the United States has established cooperative agreements with multiple resettlement organizations to carry out these services. The organizational plaintiffs are among the entities with which the Department of State previously entered into cooperative agreements.

As previously noted, the State Department, in February 2024, ended all cooperative agreements concerning the provision of these services. Following this, on March 10, 2025, the government notified the district court of the cessation of funding for resettlement services and stated that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, during oral argument, the government informed the panel that it did not know if any steps had been taken towards identifying or securing a new agency or agencies to deliver these reception and placement services.

Given the government’s lack of clarity about its capacity to provide the reception and placement services mandated by 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is ordered to restore those cooperative agreements necessary for the provision of the reception and placement services delineated in § 1522 for refugees admitted to the United States. These services must encompass the usual and customary offerings that were provided under the previous cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President possesses broad authority and is entitled to deference in immigration affairs, the district court erred by restraining the executive branch from carrying out the President’s policy choice to restrict the admission of refugees and related services. Accordingly, I would grant a full stay of the district court’s injunctions.

While my fellow judges and I agree that the President is empowered to impose a halt on refugee admissions—and would stay the lower court’s injunction as to that point—we diverge regarding the existence of a legal obligation on the federal government to supply services to recently admitted refugees prior to the effective date of the suspension. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 to compel the government to furnish specified services to refugees. From my perspective, however, the statute is most reasonably construed as providing authorization rather than imposing a duty.

To begin with, §1522 bears the heading, “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis added). An authorization connotes permission, not obligation. AUTHORIZATION, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining the term as “permission to do something” or an “official document granting such permission”). Therefore, Congress empowers the executive to act under §1522 but does not mandate it.

Moreover, each of the four distinct provisions within the statute that allocate funding for various refugee services—such as “Program of initial resettlement,” or “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—expressly provides that the “Director is authorized” to render assistance. U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis added). These phrases consistently signal that the allocation of resources remains within the executive’s discretion.

In contending that Section 1522 requires mandatory funding, my colleagues rely on §1522(a), labeled “Conditions and consideration,” which contains the term “shall” along with the qualifier “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this requirement is limited by the immediately preceding clause: “In providing assistance under this section [that is, the discretionary funding provisions within §1522(b)-(e)] . . .” Thus, §1522(a) establishes certain binding “conditions and considerations” that apply if the government opts to fund refugee initiatives. For instance, the Director is required to “make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [pursuant to the discretionary funding for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’ in §1522(c)] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible,” indicating that this mandatory consideration is triggered only if the program receives funding.

To support their assertion regarding compulsory expenditures, my colleagues refer to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), contending these place enforceable duties on the Director. Yet, neither subsection meaningfully contradicts the overall function of §1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) obligates the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children solely where such assumption is “necessary.” Further, this provision permits the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Although the word “shall” is present, the subsection primarily leaves authority with the executive, establishing no unequivocal directive. Additionally, it remains uncertain from the record whether any unaccompanied refugee children are implicated in this matter. In the same vein, subsection (e)(7)(A) directs that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” support projects for refugees offering cash and medical assistance “as needed.” The particulars of these projects are left to the executive’s judgment, not imposed by Congress as obligatory.

In my assessment, Section 1522 grants the executive branch discretion to design and manage programs as it sees fit, conditioned on available appropriations, but it does not compel that such programs be maintained. Since there is no statutory requirement that the executive deliver these services, this court has no authority to compel their provision. For these reasons, I must respectfully dissent in part.
