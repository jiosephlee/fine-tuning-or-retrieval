Title: Pacito v. Trump
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
Date: September 12, 2025
Docket: Nos. 25-1313 and 25-1939
Author: Panel order; partial concurrence and partial dissent by Lee, Circuit Judge

This case was argued before us on an expedited schedule on September 3, 2025. The court will issue its opinion in due time. Meanwhile, this Order serves as a stay pending appeal and until further direction from this court. The administrative stay that was entered on July 18, 2025, is hereby lifted. Additionally, our previous stay entered on March 25, 2025, as amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025, is also vacated. The preliminary injunctions entered by the district court on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45) and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79) are stayed in full, except as otherwise provided in this Order. The government’s emergency motion filed on July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108), is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order named “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under the authority of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that allowing “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and ordered the suspension of “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP” until further notice. Exec. Order No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (stating, in pertinent part, that “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all financial support for the USRAP program, ostensibly in accordance with a separate executive order entitled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This freeze in funding, pending further assessment, extended to financial resources for resettlement services within the United States for refugees already admitted.

The plaintiffs consist of refugees who were either previously admitted into the United States or had been approved for U.S. resettlement but were still outside the country, as well as three organizations that maintained cooperative agreements with the State Department to conduct overseas processing for refugee applicants and deliver resettlement services to refugees admitted to the United States. In February 2025, these plaintiffs brought an action in the Western District of Washington, asserting that Executive Order 14163’s suspension of the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified throughout the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and that the withdrawal of funding from USRAP breached various provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (referring to the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court delivered an oral preliminary injunction that restrained the enforcement or execution of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The next day, on February 26, the government ended all cooperative agreements for providing reception and placement services to refugees within the United States and terminated all but one of the overseas agreements supporting USRAP processing. On February 28, the district court issued a written order clarifying the extent of the preliminary injunction issued on February 25, indicating that it also barred the suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, the district court granted a second preliminary injunction, directing the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 order. The government submitted notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay pending its appeal of the district court’s order from February 28, which a motions panel of this court partially granted on March 25. Referring to Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the President’s authority to issue Executive Order No. 14163 under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nonetheless, we denied the stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Subsequently, we issued two further orders to clarify this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel also required full briefing and instructed the Clerk of Court to schedule the appeal at the earliest possible opportunity.

After the briefs were filed, but prior to the argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court, on July 14, entered an order creating a framework to implement the limited carveout and assigned a magistrate judge to supervise the process. On July 17, the government submitted an emergency motion seeking to enforce, clarify, or modify the stay order, which plaintiffs contested. On July 18, 2025, we granted an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and set the matter for expedited oral argument.

DISCUSSION

After consideration of the parties’ submissions and oral argument, the matter is now submitted for a decision. An opinion addressing the merits will follow in due course. Meanwhile, we hereby lift our administrative stay entered on July 18, as well as the stay issued on March 25 and subsequently modified on April 21 and May 9. We now impose this stay, which will remain effective until this appeal is conclusively resolved. Accordingly, the district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed in full, except as otherwise specified below.

The criteria governing the grant of a stay are well settled. Our analysis rests on four factors: (1) whether the party requesting the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of prevailing on the merits; (2) whether the applicant faces a risk of irreparable harm if a stay is denied; (3) whether the issuance of a stay would cause significant injury to other parties in the litigation; and (4) where the public interest lies. Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Among these, the first two—the likelihood of success on the merits and the prospect of irreparable injury—are considered “the most critical.” Id. It appears the government is likely to succeed against plaintiffs’ contest of the legality of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions, and it is not the court’s role to undertake “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. Section 1182(f) establishes that the President must merely “find[]” that admitting the specified aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” That prerequisite has plainly been satisfied in this instance. Id. at 685. The statute’s language is explicit, and the Executive Order “does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, to suspend the “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other relevant considerations, which encompass both the domestic and international interests of the United States as determined by the President’s “broad discretion,” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84, also weigh in favor of the United States.

As will be detailed more thoroughly in a forthcoming opinion, the government is unlikely to succeed regarding at least one of the plaintiffs’ claims brought under the APA. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is obligated to deliver specified reception and placement services to refugees subsequent to their entry into the United States. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) mandates that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—housed within the Department of Health and Human Services—“to the extent of available appropriations,” must: (i) allocate adequate resources for employment training and placement to facilitate refugees' rapid attainment of economic self-sufficiency; (ii) provide refugees with opportunities to receive sufficient English language instruction so that they may become effectively resettled promptly; (iii) ensure that cash assistance to refugees is distributed in a way that does not impede their economic self-sufficiency, as outlined in subsection (e)(2); and (iv) guarantee that women have equal access to training and instructional opportunities as men. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (defining the scope of federal funding for refugee programs). In addition, for unaccompanied refugee minors, § 1522(d)(2)(B) stipulates: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Regarding financial and medical assistance for refugees recently resettled, § 1522(e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” It should be observed that for fiscal year 2025, Congress allotted $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, see Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating funds appropriated by division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), an amount subsequently reduced by $800 million through the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Section 1523 further imposes an accountability measure, requiring the Secretary to submit a comprehensive report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

Pursuant to statute, resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be delivered via contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). The United States has traditionally used cooperative agreements with a variety of resettlement organizations for the provision of these services. The organizational plaintiffs are three such entities that have had cooperative agreements with the Department of State.

As previously noted, in February 2024, the State Department ended all cooperative agreements related to these services. Recognizing the cessation of resettlement services funding, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nonetheless, at oral argument before the panel, the government stated that it did not know whether any steps had been taken to identify a new agency or agencies capable of providing reception and placement services.

Given the government’s lack of certainty about its capacity to deliver the reception and placement services required by 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby ordered to reestablish those cooperative agreements necessary to offer the reception and placement services outlined in § 1522 to refugees admitted into the United States. These services must encompass the standard services historically provided to such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is generally granted broad discretion and significant deference in the area of immigration, the district court improperly enjoined the executive branch from executing the President’s policy restricting the admission of refugees and the provision of services to them. Accordingly, I would grant a complete stay of the district court’s injunctions.

While my fellow panel members and I concur that the President possesses the authority to pause refugee admissions and would therefore stay the district court’s injunction concerning that matter, we disagree regarding whether the federal government is legally obligated to continue providing services to refugees who had already been admitted prior to the effective date of the suspension. My colleagues read 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a requirement on the government to supply certain services to refugees. In my view, however, the statute is more reasonably construed as granting permission, rather than imposing an obligation.

To begin, the title of § 1522 is “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis added). “Authorization” connotes a permissive, rather than a compulsory, grant of power. See AUTHORIZATION, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining the term as “permission to do something” or “official document granting such permission”). By enacting § 1522, Congress has permitted such actions but has not made them mandatory.

Moreover, each of the four detailed statutory sections that allocate funding for various refugee-related services—for instance, “Program of initial resettlement” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—specify that the “Director is authorized” to extend such assistance. See U.S.C. § 1522(b)-(e) (emphasis added). These provisions again indicate that the executive branch retains discretion in the allocation of funding for these services.

In contending that Section 1522 mandates funding, my colleagues reference §1522(a)—captioned “Conditions and consideration”—which employs the term “shall” along with the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this wording is limited by the phrase immediately before it: “In providing assistance under this section [i.e., the discretionary funding provisions found in §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ” In effect, §1522(a) establishes certain obligatory “conditions and considerations” only when the government opts to allocate funding to refugee programs. For instance, the Director “shall. . . make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [pursuant to §1522(c)’s discretionary funding for “Project grants and contracts for services for refugee”] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible [that is, this is a mandatory condition or consideration only when the government elects to fund it].”

To reinforce their argument that spending is required, my colleagues point to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which might be read as imposing certain duties on the Director. Nevertheless, neither of these provisions overrides the discretionary framework of §1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) obliges the program Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children solely when such responsibility is deemed “necessary.” Additionally, the provision authorizes the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Despite utilizing the word “shall,” this subsection is replete with deference to executive judgment and lacks any unambiguous requirement. Furthermore, the record does not indicate whether any unaccompanied refugee children are implicated in this case. In the same vein, subsection (e)(7)(A) directs that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” projects offering cash and medical assistance to refugees “as needed.” The scope and particulars of these programs are subject to executive discretion, which does not amount to a directive from Congress.

From my perspective, Section 1522 grants the executive branch the authority to design and implement programs at its discretion as long as appropriations are available. It does not compel the operation of such programs. Since the statute does not obligate the executive to furnish these services, this court lacks the authority to order their provision. Accordingly, I must respectfully dissent in part.
