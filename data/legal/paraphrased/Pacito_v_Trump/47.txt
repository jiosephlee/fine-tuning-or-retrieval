On September 3, 2025, the court held expedited oral arguments in this matter. A formal opinion will follow at the appropriate time. Meanwhile, this Order serves to impose a stay pending appeal until further directive from this court. The administrative stay previously entered on July 18, 2025, is now vacated. Additionally, the court vacates its earlier stay dated March 25, 2025, including amendments made on April 21 and May 9, 2025. The preliminary injunctions entered by the district court on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45), and on March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under the authority of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that the admission of refugees through the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and thus ordered that the “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until further determinations are made. Exec. Order No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (stating in pertinent part: “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). On January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all funding to the USRAP, claiming authority under a separate executive order titled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). As part of this temporary measure, funding was also suspended for domestic resettlement assistance to refugees already admitted to the United States, pending additional review.

The plaintiffs comprise individuals who are refugees either previously admitted into the United States or approved for resettlement but currently residing outside the country, along with three organizations that maintained cooperative agreements with the State Department to assist with overseas processing for refugee status applicants and resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States. In February 2025, they commenced litigation in the Western District of Washington, contending that Executive Order 14163’s suspension of the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified in various provisions of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and arguing that the withdrawal of funds from USRAP breached multiple requirements of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (characterizing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court delivered an oral preliminary injunction that forbade the enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The following day, on February 26, the government terminated all cooperative agreements for the provision of reception and placement services for refugees within the United States and all but one agreement related to USRAP processing support abroad. Subsequently, on February 28, the district court issued a written order clarifying the February 25 preliminary injunction, expressly prohibiting also the suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, the district court granted a second preliminary injunction directing the State Department to reinstate every cooperative agreement that had been ended after the February 25 order. The government lodged notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s February 28 order while its appeal was pending, and a motions panel of this court partially granted that request on March 25. In reliance on Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that Executive Order No. 14163 was issued pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nevertheless, we denied the stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Subsequently, we entered two orders further clarifying the contours of this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel ordered full briefing on the matter and instructed the Clerk of Court to schedule the appeal for the earliest available argument calendar.

After the briefs were filed, but prior to the September 19, 2025 argument date, the district court issued an order on July 14 setting forth a process to implement the limited carveout and assigned a magistrate judge to supervise the process. The government responded on July 17 by filing an emergency motion seeking enforcement, clarification, or modification of the stay order, which plaintiffs opposed. On July 18, 2025, we entered an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and arranged for oral argument to proceed on an expedited schedule.

DISCUSSION

After considering the written and oral submissions from the parties, the case is now taken under submission for a decision. An opinion addressing the merits will follow in due course. Meanwhile, we hereby dissolve both our July 18 administrative stay and our stay of March 25, as modified on April 21 and May 9. We now enter a new stay that shall remain in place until a final determination in this appeal. The entirety of the district court’s February 28 and March 24, 2025 orders is stayed, save as otherwise specified below.

The established criteria for granting a stay require consideration of four elements: (1) Whether the party requesting the stay has demonstrated a strong likelihood of prevailing on the merits; (2) whether, absent a stay, the applicant will suffer irreparable harm; (3) whether imposing the stay will result in substantial injury to other parties with interests in the case; and (4) the location of the public interest. Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). The first two factors—the likelihood of success on the merits and risk of irreparable injury—are “the most critical.” Id. The government appears poised to succeed against plaintiffs’ challenge to Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions, and it is not within our purview to undertake “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. Section 1182(f) requires only that the President “find[] that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Id. at 685. Moreover, “the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, to bar “the entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other factors, which implicate both domestic and international concerns as determined by the President’s “broad discretion,” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84, also weigh in the United States’ favor.

As will be detailed more thoroughly in a forthcoming opinion, the government does not appear likely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ claims brought under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, upon a refugee’s admission into the United States, the government is obligated to supply certain reception and placement services. Specifically, § 1522(a)(1)(A) mandates that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—an entity within the Department of Health and Human Services—must, “to the extent of available appropriations,”: (i) allocate adequate resources for employment training and job placement to enable refugees to reach economic self-sufficiency as quickly as practicable, (ii) afford refugees access to adequate English language instruction to facilitate effective and prompt resettlement, (iii) ensure that cash assistance is delivered to refugees in a manner consistent with promoting economic independence, as specified in subsection (e)(2), and (iv) guarantee that women are provided the same opportunities as men for training and educational programs. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (regarding the scope of federal financial support for refugee programs). In the case of unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Regarding financial and medical assistance for refugees who have been recently resettled, § 1522(e)(7)(A) obligates the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For Fiscal Year 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee aid, see Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating the funding stipulated in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), although this amount was subsequently reduced by $800 million through the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Section 1523 requires the Secretary to submit a comprehensive report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

Pursuant to statute, these resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be delivered through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Historically, the United States has utilized cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations to facilitate such services. The organizational plaintiffs in this case are three entities with which the Department of State has maintained cooperative agreements.

As noted previously, in February 2024, the Department of State ended all cooperative agreements concerning the provision of these services. Recognizing this cessation of funding for resettlement services, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nonetheless, during oral argument before this panel, the government indicated that it did not know whether any advancement had been made in retaining a new agency or agencies to offer such services.

Given the government’s lack of certainty as to its capacity to deliver the reception and placement services required under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby ordered to reinstate those cooperative agreements necessary to deliver the reception and placement services enumerated in § 1522 to refugees admitted into the United States. These services must encompass the standard and usual assistance previously provided under the former cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is accorded significant discretion and courts traditionally grant deference in immigration-related decisions, the district court mistakenly prevented the executive from enacting the President’s chosen approach to restricting the admission of refugees and furnishing them with assistance. Consequently, I would grant a full stay of the district court’s injunctions.

There is agreement among my colleagues and myself that the President possesses the legal power to suspend refugee admissions, leading us all to favor staying the district court’s injunction regarding that matter. However, we diverge concerning the question of whether the federal government is legally compelled to offer services to refugees who were admitted just prior to the imposition of the suspension. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as obliging the government to deliver specific services to such refugees. In contrast, I read the provision more straightforwardly as conferring permission, rather than imposing a directive.

To elaborate: The heading of § 1522 is “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis added). The term "authorization" indicates allowance, not compulsion. See AUTHORIZATION, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (“permission to do something” or the “official document granting such permission”). In other words, under § 1522, Congress permits, but does not compel, the executive branch to act.

Moreover, all four particular statutory subsections that allocate funds for various types of refugee services—such as the “Program of initial resettlement,” “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—consistently declare that the “Director is authorized” to render such assistance. See U.S.C. § 1522(b)-(e) (emphasis in original). This repeated language further indicates congressional intent for executive discretion in the allocation of these resources.

In contending that Section 1522 establishes a requirement for mandatory funding, my colleagues rely on §1522(a), labeled “Conditions and consideration,” which contains the terms “shall” and “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this wording is restricted by the immediately preceding clause: “In providing assistance under this section [referring to the discretionary funding authorizations found in §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ” In effect, §1522(a) introduces certain obligatory “conditions and considerations” only if the government elects to finance refugee initiatives. For instance, it obliges the Director to “make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [pursuant to the discretionary funding mechanism set forth in § 1522(c) for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’] to promote economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible [that is, this is the mandatory criterion or factor that comes into play only if the government chooses to allocate funds].”

To reinforce their assertion of compelled expenditures, my colleagues reference subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which could be interpreted as imposing some duties on the Director. Nonetheless, neither subsection effectively challenges the broader functional structure of §1522. Under subsection (d)(2)(B), the Director is only tasked with assuming legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee minors if such responsibility proves to be “necessary.” Moreover, the section authorizes the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” While the subsection employs “shall,” it exhibits significant latitude in deference to executive judgment and fails to articulate a concrete obligation. The record also does not clarify whether there are any unaccompanied refugee children pertinent to this case. Similarly, subsection (e)(7)(A) directs that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” programs providing cash and medical assistance to refugees “as needed.” The specifics and scope of such initiatives remain subject to executive discretion, which undermines the claim of a congressional imperative.

Based on my assessment, Section 1522 grants authority to the executive branch to establish such programs at its discretion, contingent upon the presence of appropriations, but does not obligate their operation. Since the statute does not require the executive branch to provide these particular services, it is beyond our court’s purview to command their implementation. Accordingly, I respectfully dissent in part.
