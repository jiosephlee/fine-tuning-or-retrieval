Title: Pacito v. Trump
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
Date: September 12, 2025
Docket: Nos. 25-1313 and 25-1939
Author: Panel order; partial concurrence and partial dissent by Lee, Circuit Judge

We heard oral argument in this case on an expedited basis on September 3, 2025. An opinion will issue in due course. In the interim, this Order shall constitute a stay pending appeal and further order of this court. We hereby vacate our administrative stay issued July 18, 2025. We vacate our prior stay issued March 25, 2025, as amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025. The district court’s February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45) and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79) preliminary injunctions are stayed in their entirety except as specified in this Order. The government’s emergency motion of July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108) is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued an executive order entitled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President determined that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and directed that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” pending further findings. Exec. Order. No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (providing in relevant part that “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). On January 24, 2025, the Department of State suspended all funding of the USRAP program, purportedly pursuant to a different executive order entitled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). Pending further review, this suspension included funding for domestic resettlement services for refugees admitted to the United States.

The plaintiffs consist of individuals who are refugees either previously allowed entry into the United States or who have been authorized for resettlement within the United States but continue to reside abroad, along with three organizations that maintained cooperative agreements with the State Department for providing overseas processing for refugee applicants and resettlement assistance to refugees already admitted to the country. In February 2025, plaintiffs initiated litigation in the Western District of Washington, asserting that Executive Order 14163’s pause of the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (as codified in multiple sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), as well as alleging that the discontinuation of USRAP funding breached several provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (characterizing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court verbally issued a preliminary injunction enjoining enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. On February 26, the government rescinded all cooperative agreements for reception and placement services for refugees in the United States and all but one cooperative agreement for USRAP processing assistance overseas. On February 28, the district court released a written order clarifying the reach of its February 25 preliminary injunction, stating that it also blocks the cessation of USRAP funding. On March 24, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction directing the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that were terminated subsequent to the February 25 decision. The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s February 28 order pending appeal, which a motions panel from this court partially granted on March 25. Citing Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the President’s issuance of Executive Order No. 14163 derived from authority found in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), which “exudes deference” to the President and “vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). However, we rejected the government’s request for a stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. We later issued two further orders to clarify the scope of this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel ordered full briefing on the appeal and directed the Clerk of Court to calendar the case at the earliest possible date.

After the parties completed briefing but prior to the oral argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court, on July 14, issued an order that created a framework for enforcing the limited carveout and appointed a magistrate judge to supervise its implementation. The government, on July 17, submitted an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay, which plaintiffs opposed. On July 18, 2025, we granted an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order. Oral argument was then scheduled on an accelerated timetable.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties' briefs and oral argument, the case is now under submission for a decision. An opinion addressing the merits will be issued in due course. Meanwhile, we hereby lift the administrative stay entered on July 18, as well as the stay entered on March 25 and subsequently modified on April 21 and May 9. A new stay is hereby imposed pending the ultimate resolution of this appeal. The district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed in full, except as specifically provided below.

The established criteria for granting a stay require evaluation of four factors: (1) whether the applicant has demonstrated a strong likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant would suffer irreparable harm if the stay is not granted; (3) whether issuing the stay would cause substantial harm to other parties to the litigation; and (4) where the public interest lies. See Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). The two most significant considerations are the applicant’s showing of probable success on the merits and the possibility of irreparable injury. Id. The government appears poised to prevail regarding plaintiffs’ contest to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions, and we are precluded from conducting “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Id. at 685. Moreover, “the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, permitting suspension of the “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The remaining factors, involving both the internal and foreign affairs of the United States and informed by the “broad discretion” Congress has vested in the President, see Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84, also weigh in favor of the United States.

As will be discussed at length in a forthcoming opinion, the government does not appear likely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act claims. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is obligated to furnish specified reception and placement services to refugees subsequent to their entry into the United States. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) obliges the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—housed within the Department of Health and Human Services—“to the extent of available appropriations” to: (i) allocate adequate resources for employment training and job placement aimed at enabling refugees to reach economic self-sufficiency promptly; (ii) afford refugees access to sufficient English language training so they can resettle effectively in the shortest possible time; (iii) ensure cash assistance is provided to refugees in a manner that does not undermine their economic self-sufficiency, consistent with subsection (e)(2); and (iv) guarantee women are afforded the same opportunities as men to participate in training and educational programs. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (detailing federal funding for refugee services). Regarding unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) stipulates: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Additionally, in relation to cash and medical assistance for recently resettled refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For fiscal year 2025, it is noted that Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, as set forth in Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating appropriations found in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), with an $800 million reduction pursuant to the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Furthermore, § 1523 mandates the Secretary to submit a comprehensive report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

By statute, these resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be delivered through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Traditionally, the United States has relied on cooperative agreements with different resettlement organizations to offer these services. The organizational plaintiffs are three entities with which the Department of State has established such cooperative agreements.

As previously noted, in February 2024, the State Department terminated all cooperative agreements relating to these services. Recognizing this cessation of funding for resettlement services, the government notified the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, at oral argument, the government informed the panel that it lacked knowledge regarding any progress in identifying a new agency or agencies qualified to provide these services.

Given the government’s acknowledged uncertainty about fulfilling its obligation to offer the reception and placement services required under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is ordered to restore those cooperative agreements needed to deliver the reception and placement services outlined in § 1522 to refugees admitted to the United States. Such services must encompass the typical and standard offerings previously provided to such refugees under earlier cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is accorded broad discretion and substantial deference concerning immigration issues, the district court erred by prohibiting the executive branch from carrying out the President’s chosen policy to restrict refugee admissions and the related provision of services. Therefore, I would grant a complete stay of the district court’s injunctions.

My fellow judges and I concur that the President possesses the authority to temporarily halt refugee admissions, and for that reason would stay the district court’s injunction as it pertains to that matter. Our views diverge, however, regarding whether the federal government is legally obligated to furnish services to those refugees who entered the country immediately prior to the moratorium’s commencement. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a duty upon the government to supply certain services to refugees. In my estimation, this statutory provision is best read as a grant of authority rather than an imposition of a requirement.

To begin with, the title of Section 1522 reads: “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis in original). As understood, “authorization” is permissive in nature and does not create a mandatory duty. See AUTHORIZATION, Black's Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (“permission to do something” or the “official document granting such permission”). Section 1522 thus permits actions by Congress; it does not compel them.

Additionally, each of the four discrete statutory sections that allocate funds for specific refugee-related services—for instance, the “Program of initial resettlement,” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—specify that the “Director is authorized” to render such assistance. U.S.C. § 1522(b)-(e) (emphasis supplied). Once more, these provisions suggest that the executive branch holds discretionary authority over such funding decisions.

In asserting that Section 1522 mandates funding, my colleagues rely on §1522(a)—entitled “Conditions and consideration”—which employs the term “shall” as well as the clause “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this wording is limited by the directly preceding language: “In providing assistance under this section [meaning the discretionary funding measures set forth in §1522(b)-(e)] . . .” Therefore, §1522(a) sets out certain compulsory “conditions and considerations” applicable only if the government elects to fund refugee initiatives. For instance, the Director “shall... make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [in reference to § 1522(c)’s discretionary grant funding for “Project grants and contracts for services for refugee”] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as rapidly as possible [indicating this requirement is only triggered should the government opt to fund such services].”

To further support their argument that funding is obligatory, my colleagues refer to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which arguably impose specific duties upon the Director. Still, neither overrides the fundamental operation of §1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) obligates the program Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children solely when such responsibility is “necessary.” This section additionally empowers the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Even where the word “shall” appears, the provision is steeped in executive branch discretion and does not set forth an unequivocal mandate. Moreover, the record does not clarify whether any unaccompanied refugee children are implicated in this matter. Likewise, subsection (e)(7)(A) specifies the “Secretary shall develop and implement” assistance projects for cash and medical support to refugees “as needed.” The parameters and substance of these programs are left to the executive’s judgment—signaling congressional flexibility rather than strict requirement.

From my perspective, Section 1522 confers upon the executive the authority to design refugee programs at its discretion contingent upon the existence of appropriations. It does not command the creation or operation of such programs. Given that the statute does not compel the executive to supply these services, it is not within this court’s jurisdiction to require their provision. For these reasons, I respectfully dissent in part.
