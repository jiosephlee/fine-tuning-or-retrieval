The Court conducted oral argument in this matter on September 3, 2025, under an expedited schedule. A formal opinion will follow in due course. Pending further action by this Court, this Order shall serve as a stay during the pendency of the appeal. The administrative stay previously entered on July 18, 2025, is hereby set aside. Additionally, the earlier stay dated March 25, 2025, as subsequently modified on April 21 and May 9, 2025, is also vacated. The district court’s preliminary injunctions entered on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45), and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79), are stayed in full except as expressly indicated in this Order. The government’s emergency motion filed on July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108), is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under the authority granted by 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and accordingly ordered the “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until further assessment could be completed. Exec. Order. No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (“Whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all financial support for the USRAP program, ostensibly in accordance with a separate executive order called “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This pause on funding, which remained in place pending additional review, encompassed resources allocated for domestic resettlement services to refugees who had already been admitted to the United States.

The plaintiffs consist of refugees who had previously been admitted to the United States or had received approval for resettlement in the United States but remained abroad, along with three organizations that had entered into cooperative agreements with the State Department to facilitate overseas processing for refugee applicants and to deliver resettlement services to refugees admitted into the country. In February 2025, these plaintiffs initiated an action in the Western District of Washington, contending that Executive Order 14163’s suspension of the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified in various sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and further alleged that the withdrawal of funding from USRAP violated various provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (characterizing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court delivered an oral ruling granting a preliminary injunction that barred the enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The following day, February 26, the government terminated all cooperative agreements related to providing reception and placement services to refugees in the United States, as well as all but one cooperative agreement supporting USRAP processing abroad. On February 28, the district court issued a written order clarifying the February 25 preliminary injunction, indicating that it also prohibited the cessation of funding for USRAP. Subsequently, on March 24, the district court entered a second preliminary injunction requiring the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements that had been terminated subsequent to the February 25 decision. The government submitted notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay pending appeal of the district court’s February 28 order, which a motions panel of this court partially granted on March 25. In referencing Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the authority underpinning the President’s issuance of Executive Order No. 14163, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nevertheless, we denied the stay motion “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Two further orders were issued clarifying this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel ordered comprehensive briefing and instructed the Clerk of Court to assign the appeal to the next available calendar.

After briefing was completed but prior to the argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court entered an order on July 14 establishing a structure to implement the limited carveout and appointed a magistrate judge to supervise this process. The government responded on July 17 by submitting an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order, which plaintiffs opposed. On July 18, 2025, we granted an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order. Oral argument was set to proceed on an expedited schedule.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties’ briefs and the arguments presented orally, the matter is now deemed submitted for a decision. A merits opinion will be released in due time. Meanwhile, we hereby lift our administrative stay dated July 18, along with our March 25 stay, as modified on April 21 and May 9. In their place, we impose a stay that will remain effective until this appeal is finally resolved. Accordingly, the district court’s orders of February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed in full, except as specifically provided below.

The criteria for granting a stay are well settled. Our analysis involves four elements: (1) whether the applicant for the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant would suffer irreparable harm without a stay; (3) whether granting the stay would cause significant harm to other parties involved in the litigation; and (4) where the public interest is best served. See Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Of these, the most pivotal are the applicant’s likelihood of success and the prospect of irreparable harm. Id. The government is poised to succeed in contesting plaintiffs’ objections to the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions, and it is not within our purview to conduct a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Id. at 685. “[T]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, to bar the “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The remaining elements, which implicate both domestic and foreign policy considerations as informed by the President’s “broad discretion,” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84, likewise weigh in favor of the United States.

The government, for reasons that will be set forth in detail in a forthcoming opinion, is unlikely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ claims brought under the APA. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, certain reception and placement services are mandated to be provided to refugees following their admission into the United States. Specifically, § 1522(a)(1)(A) obligates the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—a component of the Department of Health and Human Services—“to the extent of available appropriations”—to: (i) allocate sufficient resources for employment training and job placement so that refugees can attain economic self-sufficiency as rapidly as possible, (ii) offer refugees the chance to receive enough English language instruction to enable their effective resettlement expeditiously, (iii) ensure that cash assistance to refugees is distributed in a way that does not undermine their pursuit of economic self-sufficiency, in accordance with subsection (e)(2), and (iv) guarantee that women have equal opportunities with men to access training and educational programs. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (which outlines the federal funding available for refugee services). Likewise, regarding unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Regarding the provision of cash and medical assistance to newly resettled refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) mandates that the Secretary of Health and Human Services “shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” It is also noted that Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance for Fiscal Year 2025, as reflected in Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (adopting the funding levels specified in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), with an $800 million reduction imposed by the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). In addition, § 1523 requires that the Secretary provide accountability by submitting a comprehensive report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

Pursuant to statute, these resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be delivered by means of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). The United States has customarily formed cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations for the provision of these services. The organizational plaintiffs are among those entities with which the Department of State has previously maintained cooperative agreements.

As previously noted, in February 2024, the State Department terminated all cooperative agreements relating to these services. Recognizing the cessation of funding for resettlement services, the government notified the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, during oral argument before the panel, the government stated it was not aware of any progress toward engaging a new agency or agencies to supply such reception and placement services.

Given the government’s lack of certainty about its capacity to provide the reception and placement services required by 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is therefore ordered to restore such cooperative agreements as are necessary to offer the reception and placement services delineated in § 1522 to refugees admitted into the United States. These services must include the ordinary and customary services previously provided to such refugees under the former cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President possesses broad authority and receives considerable deference in immigration policy, the district court erred by issuing an injunction preventing the executive branch from executing the President’s chosen policy to restrict refugee admissions and related services. Consequently, I would grant a full stay of the district court’s injunctions.

My fellow panel members and I concur that the President is empowered to halt refugee admissions, justifying a stay of the injunction pertaining to that matter. However, we disagree as to whether the federal government is legally compelled to furnish services to refugees who were admitted prior to the implementation of the suspension. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as obligating the government to offer specific services to these individuals. In my view, this statute is best understood as a grant of authority, not as an imposition of a duty.

To begin, the heading of Section 1522 reads: “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis added). The word “authorization” denotes discretion, rather than compulsion. AUTHORIZATION, Black's Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining it as “permission to do something” or the “official document granting such permission”). Thus, § 1522 permits action by Congress; it does not impose a requirement.

Moreover, the four individual statutory subsections that allocate funding for various refugee services—such as “Program of initial resettlement” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—all employ the language “Director is authorized” to provide such aid. U.S.C. § 1522(b)-(e) (emphasis added). This phrasing further indicates that the executive branch retains discretion in determining funding for these services.

In contending that Section 1522 mandates funding, my colleagues reference §1522(a)—labeled “Conditions and consideration”—which employs the term “shall” as well as the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this language is limited by the phrase that comes immediately before it: “In providing assistance under this section [i.e., the discretionary funding provisions outlined in §1522(b)-(e)]. . .” Thus, §1522(a) sets forth specific mandatory “conditions and considerations” only if the government opts to provide funding for refugee programs. For instance, the Director “shall. . . make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [pursuant to §1522(c)’s discretionary funding for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible”—that is, this is a mandatory condition to be considered if the government elects to allocate such funding.

To reinforce their argument that spending is mandatory, my colleagues refer to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which may appear to impose certain duties on the Director. Yet, neither provision convincingly undermines the broader function of §1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) obligates the program’s Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children only when such responsibility is deemed “necessary.” The section further authorizes the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Despite the inclusion of “shall,” the subsection clearly defers to the executive branch and contains no absolute mandate. Moreover, the record does not establish whether any unaccompanied refugee children are actually implicated in this matter. Likewise, subsection (e)(7)(A) provides that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” programs offering cash and medical assistance to refugees “as needed.” The scope and implementation of such programs remain within the discretion of the executive, making this provision far from a binding legislative directive.

In my assessment, Section 1522 permits the executive branch to design and administer programs at its discretion, provided appropriations exist. It does not oblige the continued operation of such programs. Because the statute does not impose a requirement on the executive branch to deliver these services, it is beyond our court’s authority to order their provision. Accordingly, I respectfully dissent in part.
