Oral argument in this matter was conducted on September 3, 2025, under expedited procedures. An opinion will be forthcoming. Pending further direction from this court, the present Order shall serve as a stay during the pendency of the appeal. The administrative stay previously granted on July 18, 2025, is hereby vacated. In addition, we vacate the stay entered on March 25, 2025, including the amendments made on April 21 and May 9, 2025. The district court’s preliminary injunctions dated February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. The emergency motion submitted by the government on July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108), is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order labeled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that allowing refugees to enter the United States through the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Consequently, he ordered that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until further evaluation. Exec. Order No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (stating in part that “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). On January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all financial support for the USRAP program, supposedly relying on a separate executive order titled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This freeze, while under review, covered funding for resettlement assistance provided to refugees admitted to the United States.

The plaintiffs consist of refugees who were either previously admitted into the United States or have been granted approval for resettlement in the United States but currently remain abroad, as well as three organizations with cooperative agreements with the State Department to facilitate overseas processing of refugee applicants and to offer resettlement services for refugees who have entered the United States. In February 2025, these plaintiffs initiated an action in the Western District of Washington, alleging that Executive Order 14163's suspension of the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified in various portions of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and further asserting that the discontinuation of funding for USRAP violated provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (noting that the Refugee Act is “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court delivered an oral preliminary injunction preventing the enforcement or execution of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The following day, February 26, the government rescinded all cooperative agreements to provide reception and placement services for refugees inside the United States, along with all but one of the agreements providing USRAP processing support internationally. On February 28, the district court entered a written order clarifying the boundaries of its February 25 preliminary injunction, expressly barring the suspension of USRAP funding as well. On March 24, the court granted a second preliminary injunction, this time instructing the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that were canceled after the February 25 order. The government lodged notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s February 28 order while its appeal was pending, and a motions panel of this court partially granted that request on March 25. Citing Trump v. Hawaii, the panel noted that 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the statutory authority behind the President’s issuance of Executive Order No. 14163, “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nonetheless, the panel denied the stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Two further orders were later issued to clarify the “limited carveout from the stay.” See Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel also instructed that the case proceed to full briefing and ordered the Clerk of Court to set the appeal on the next available argument calendar.

After briefing concluded, but prior to the scheduled oral argument on September 19, 2025, the district court issued an order on July 14 setting out a structure for implementing the limited carveout and designating a magistrate judge to supervise this framework. Subsequently, on July 17, the government submitted an emergency motion requesting enforcement, clarification, or modification of the stay order, to which the plaintiffs objected. On July 18, 2025, this court granted an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and arranged for expedited oral argument.

DISCUSSION

Having reviewed the parties’ briefs and heard oral argument, we submit this matter for determination. A substantive opinion will be issued in due course. Meanwhile, we hereby vacate our prior administrative stay dated July 18, along with the stay entered on March 25 as subsequently modified on April 21 and May 9. We now grant a stay pending the ultimate disposition of this appeal. The district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, are fully stayed, except as specified herein.

The criteria governing the issuance of a stay are well settled. We weigh four considerations: (1) whether the movant has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of prevailing on the merits; (2) whether the movant will suffer irreparable harm in the absence of a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will cause significant injury to the opposing parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. See Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Among these, the showing of probable success on the merits and of irreparable injury—“the most critical” elements—carry the greatest weight. Id. The government is apt to succeed in its defense against the plaintiffs’ challenge to the legality of Executive Order No. 14163’s refugee admissions suspension, and we are precluded from conducting “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. Section 1182(f) requires only that the President “find[]” the entry of particular aliens would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and that prerequisite has plainly been met in this instance. Id. at 685. The “plain language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not surpass any textual limitation on presidential authority,” id. at 688, to suspend “the entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other considerations, including the impact on both domestic and international concerns as identified through the “broad discretion” afforded to the President, Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84, also support the position of the United States.

The government, however, is unlikely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act claims, as will be addressed in a forthcoming opinion. According to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, following their admission to the United States, refugees are entitled to certain reception and placement services provided by the government. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) directs the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, which operates within the Department of Health and Human Services, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to: (i) ensure sufficient resources for employment training and job placement so that refugees can achieve economic independence promptly; (ii) give refugees opportunities to receive adequate English language instruction for effective resettlement as swiftly as possible; (iii) guarantee that cash assistance is furnished to refugees in a manner that does not impede their economic self-sufficiency, in line with subsection (e)(2); and (iv) ensure that women have equal access as men to participate in training and instruction. Refer also to 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq., which outlines federal funds for refugee services. In relation to unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) stipulates: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Regarding cash and medical assistance for newly arrived refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) instructs the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” Notably, for fiscal year 2025, Congress allocated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance per Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating funds in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), an amount subsequently decreased by $800 million via the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Section 1523 further mandates that the Secretary submit a comprehensive report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

Pursuant to statute, resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be delivered by means of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Historically, the United States has utilized cooperative agreements with several resettlement organizations to provide these services. The organizational plaintiffs comprise three such entities that have entered into cooperative agreements with the Department of State.

As previously noted, the Department of State terminated all cooperative agreements related to these services in February 2024. Recognizing the cessation of resettlement services funding, on March 10, 2025, the government informed the district court that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, at oral argument before the panel, the government stated it was not aware of any progress in efforts to retain a new agency or agencies to furnish reception and placement services.

Given the government’s admitted uncertainty about providing the reception and placement services required by 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby ordered to restore those cooperative agreements essential for delivering the reception and placement services outlined in § 1522 to admitted refugees. The reinstated services must encompass the customary and standard assistance previously provided to such refugees under prior cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is granted substantial discretion and deference with respect to immigration policies, the district court erroneously blocked the executive branch from enforcing the President’s decision to restrict the admission of refugees and the provision of related services. Accordingly, I would grant a stay of the district court’s injunctions in full.

While my fellow judges and I concur that the President holds the authority to suspend refugee admissions—therefore, we would stay the district court’s injunction as it relates to that matter—we disagree as to whether federal law obligates the government to continue furnishing services to individuals recently admitted prior to the implementation of the suspension. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing an affirmative obligation on the government to supply certain services to refugees. In my view, however, this statute is more appropriately interpreted as a grant of power, rather than a compulsory directive.

To begin, Section 1522 is captioned “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis in original). “Authorization” implies discretion rather than obligation. As defined by Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024), AUTHORIZATION means “permission to do something” or refers to the “official document granting such permission.” Therefore, under § 1522, Congress permits the specified actions, but does not mandate them.

Moreover, the four particular statutory subsections that allocate resources for various refugee services—for example, “Program of initial resettlement,” or “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—all stipulate that the “Director is authorized” to render such assistance. See U.S.C. § 1522(b)-(e) (emphasis in original). This further indicates congressional intent to bestow discretion upon the executive branch concerning the allocation of such funding.

In asserting that Section 1522 creates a duty to fund, my colleagues point to §1522(a), entitled “Conditions and consideration,” which employs the term “shall” along with the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this is limited by the phrase directly before it: “In providing assistance under this section [i.e., referencing the discretionary funding specified in §1522(b)-(e)]. . .” Put differently, §1522(a) establishes certain required “conditions and considerations” only in the event that the government decides to allocate funds to refugee programs. For instance, the Director is instructed to “make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [referring to the discretionary allocations in §1522(c) for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible [meaning this is the required consideration that applies if funding occurs].”

To further support their argument for compulsory spending, my colleagues refer to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which can be read as placing obligations upon the Director. Yet neither of these subsections effectively undermines the prevailing structure of §1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) obliges the program’s Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children only when such responsibility is “necessary,” and it authorizes the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Although the term “shall” is present, the subsection grants considerable discretion to the executive branch and does not set forth an unequivocal requirement. Furthermore, the record does not make it clear whether any unaccompanied refugee children are involved in this case. Likewise, subsection (e)(7)(A) directs that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” programs for cash and medical assistance to refugees “as needed.” The scope and details of these programs remain under executive judgment, falling short of a definite congressional command.

I believe that Section 1522 empowers the executive to design programs within its own discretion, contingent upon the availability of appropriated funds, without making the operation of such programs obligatory. Because the law does not compel the executive branch to offer these services, our court is not empowered to require their provision. Accordingly, I respectfully dissent in part.
