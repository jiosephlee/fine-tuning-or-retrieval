The court conducted oral argument on an expedited schedule on September 3, 2025. A written opinion will follow in due course. Meanwhile, this Order serves as a stay pending appeal and until further directive from this court. The administrative stay granted on July 18, 2025, is hereby vacated. In addition, our earlier stay, entered on March 25, 2025, and modified on April 21 and May 9, 2025, is also vacated. The preliminary injunctions issued by the district court on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45), and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79), are stayed in full, except to the extent otherwise provided in this Order. The emergency motion filed by the government on July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108), is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump issued Executive Order No. 14163, titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program,” and published at 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under the authority of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President made a finding that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and ordered that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until further determinations are made. Exec. Order No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (stating in pertinent part: “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all USRAP funding, purportedly based on a separate executive order titled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid,” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This suspension, subject to further review, encompassed funding for domestic resettlement assistance for refugees who had been admitted to the United States.

The plaintiffs in this case consist of refugees who have either already been granted admission to the United States or have received approval for resettlement but currently reside outside the country, along with three organizations that maintained cooperative agreements with the State Department to conduct overseas processing for refugee applicants and provide resettlement services to admitted refugees. In February 2025, these plaintiffs initiated a lawsuit in the Western District of Washington, contending that Executive Order 14163’s suspension of the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (which is codified in different sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and asserting that the withdrawal of funding from USRAP violated various parts of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. Cf. Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (referring to the Refugee Act as setting forth “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court, by oral order, granted a preliminary injunction preventing enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The following day, February 26, the government proceeded to terminate all cooperative agreements concerning the provision of reception and placement services for refugees within the United States, as well as all but one cooperative agreement for USRAP processing assistance overseas. On February 28, the district court issued a written order clarifying and delineating the reach of the February 25 preliminary injunction, including a prohibition against the suspension of USRAP funding. Subsequently, on March 24, the district court granted a second preliminary injunction directing the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements ended after the initial February 25 decision. The government submitted notices of appeal on February 28 and again on March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s order from February 28 pending appeal, which a motions panel of this court partially granted on March 25. In that decision, referencing Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the authority for the President’s issuance of Executive Order No. 14163 under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nevertheless, we declined to grant the stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Subsequently, we issued two further orders to clarify this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel called for complete briefing and instructed the Clerk of Court to assign the appeal to the next available calendar.

After the briefing and before the argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court issued an order on July 14 to set up a mechanism for implementing the limited carveout, including appointing a magistrate judge to manage the process. On July 17, the government filed an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay, which plaintiffs contested. On July 18, 2025, we granted an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order. The court ordered oral argument to proceed on an expedited schedule.

DISCUSSION

Having considered the parties' written submissions and oral presentations, the matter is hereby submitted for adjudication. An opinion addressing the substantive issues will be forthcoming. Pending that, we hereby dissolve our administrative stay dated July 18 and our stay from March 25, including its subsequent modifications on April 21 and May 9. A new stay is granted, to remain effective until this appeal is finally resolved. The district court's orders issued on February 28 and March 24, 2025, are entirely stayed, with exceptions as specifically stated below.

The criteria for granting a stay are well-defined. We assess four considerations: (1) whether the party requesting the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of prevailing on the merits; (2) whether that party will face irreparable harm if a stay is denied; (3) whether issuance of the stay will cause substantial harm to other interested parties; and (4) the public interest implicated by the stay. See Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Among these, the first two—the probability of success on the merits and the threat of irreparable harm—are “the most critical.” Id. The government is likely to succeed in defending Executive Order No. 14163’s pause on refugee admissions, and judicial review must not “probe deeply into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. The only statutory prerequisite in § 1182(f) is that the President “find[]” that allowing entry for the specified aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The President has clearly satisfied this standard in the current situation. Id. at 685. “[T]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, regarding suspension of “the entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The remaining considerations—implicating U.S. interests both domestically and internationally and recognized within the scope of the President’s “broad discretion,” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84—also support the government’s position.

However, as will be discussed more extensively in a subsequent opinion, the government appears unlikely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ APA claims. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government has a statutory obligation to deliver specified reception and placement services to refugees upon their entry into the United States. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) mandates that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, part of the Department of Health and Human Services, “to the extent of available appropriations,” must: (i) allocate adequate resources to facilitate employment training and job placement with the aim of achieving the swiftest possible economic self-sufficiency for refugees; (ii) offer refugees adequate opportunities for English language training to support prompt and effective resettlement; (iii) ensure, consistent with subsection (e)(2), that the disbursement of cash assistance does not discourage economic self-sufficiency among refugees; and (iv) guarantee women equal access to participate in training and instructional programs as men. Refer also to 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq., which outlines federal funding provisions for refugee services. Additionally, regarding unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Concerning cash and medical support for newly resettled refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) instructs the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For the fiscal year 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, as set forth in Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating funding provided by division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), which amount was reduced by $800 million in the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Moreover, § 1523 requires that the Secretary submit a comprehensive report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

Pursuant to statute, these resettlement services, commonly known as “reception and placement” services, may be delivered via contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Historically, the United States has used cooperative agreements with a range of resettlement organizations to facilitate these services. The organizational plaintiffs are three such entities that have entered into cooperative agreements with the Department of State.

As previously noted, the State Department terminated all cooperative agreements for the provision of these services in February 2024. Recognizing the termination of resettlement service funding, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, at oral argument, the government advised the panel that it could not confirm whether there had been any advancement toward engaging a new agency or agencies capable of supplying reception and placement services.

Given the government’s current uncertainty regarding its ability to deliver the reception and placement services required by 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is ordered to restore such cooperative agreements as may be necessary to supply the reception and placement services outlined in § 1522 for refugees admitted to the United States. These services shall encompass those standard and customary services that refugees previously received under the former cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President possesses considerable discretion and is afforded deference in immigration affairs, the district court improperly issued an injunction restraining the executive branch from enacting the President's policy to restrict the admission of refugees and the provision of services to them. Therefore, I would grant a complete stay of the district court’s injunctions.

There is consensus between myself and my colleagues that the President is empowered to place a temporary halt on refugee admissions, and accordingly, we would stay the district court’s injunction regarding that aspect. However, we differ as to whether the federal government is legally compelled to provide services to individuals who had already been admitted prior to the suspension taking effect. My colleagues read 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a requirement upon the government to render certain services to refugees. In contrast, I find that the statute is most properly understood as conferring authority, not establishing an obligation.

Let us begin with the statute’s text: The heading for section 1522 reads, “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis supplied). The term “authorization” is indicative of permission rather than imposition. See AUTHORIZATION, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining the term as “permission to do something” or the “official document granting such permission”). In §1522, Congress provides the executive branch with the authority to act, but does not impose a mandatory duty to do so.

Moreover, the four particular statutory subsections that appropriate funds for various refugee services—such as “Program of initial resettlement,” “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—specify that the “Director is authorized” to deliver such assistance. U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis supplied). These textual cues once again indicate that Congress vested the executive with the discretion to determine funding for refugee assistance.

My colleagues contend that Section 1522 requires mandatory funding by referencing §1522(a), labeled “Conditions and consideration,” which includes the term “shall” along with the clause “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this language is limited by the phrase that comes immediately before: “In providing assistance under this section [that is, the discretionary funding provisions found in §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ” Essentially, §1522(a) imposes mandatory “conditions and considerations” only if the government opts to provide funding for refugee programs. For instance, the Director “shall... make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [under the discretionary funding granted by §1522(c) for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’] to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible [that is, a required condition or consideration but only once the government has chosen to fund the program].”

To support their position that spending is required, my colleagues also cite subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which arguably place some duties upon the Director. Neither, in my view, overrides the general operation of §1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) obligates the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee minors solely when such responsibility is “necessary.” This subsection also empowers the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Even with its use of “shall,” this section displays a pronounced deference to executive judgment, lacking any categorical command. Additionally, the record does not clarify whether any unaccompanied refugee minors are involved in this matter. In a comparable manner, subsection (e)(7)(A) instructs that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” projects offering cash and medical assistance to refugees “as needed.” Determining the scope and scale of these programs is left to the executive, revealing no absolute directive from Congress.

I conclude that Section 1522 allows the executive branch to design and administer programs at its discretion, contingent upon available appropriations, but does not require the establishment or operation of such programs. As the statute imposes no firm obligation on the executive to provide these services, it would be inappropriate for this court to compel them. For these reasons, I respectfully dissent in part.
