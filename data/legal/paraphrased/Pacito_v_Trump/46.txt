Title: Pacito v. Trump
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
Date: September 12, 2025
Docket: Nos. 25-1313 and 25-1939
Author: Panel order; partial concurrence and partial dissent by Lee, Circuit Judge

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order named “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Invoking the authority granted by 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and instructed that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” while further determinations are made. Exec. Order. No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (stating in relevant part: “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). On January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all USRAP funding, citing a separate executive order called “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This freeze, pending further evaluation, extended to funds for services supporting refugee resettlement within the United States.

The plaintiffs consist of refugees who were either previously granted entry into the United States or have received approval for resettlement but continue to remain abroad, along with three organizations that maintained cooperative agreements with the State Department for processing overseas refugee applicants and delivering resettlement services within the United States. In February 2025, these plaintiffs initiated a lawsuit in the Western District of Washington, asserting that Executive Order 14163’s halt of the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified in scattered sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and contending that cutting off funding to USRAP breached multiple provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (characterizing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court delivered an oral preliminary injunction that barred enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The next day, February 26, the government ended all cooperative agreements for providing reception and placement services to refugees in the United States and all but one cooperative agreement for USRAP processing abroad. On February 28, the district court released a written order clarifying the reach of its February 25 preliminary injunction, specifically indicating that the prohibition also encompassed any suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, the district court issued a second preliminary injunction, directing the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements terminated following the February 25 order. The government subsequently filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s order issued on February 28, pending appeal. On March 25, a motions panel from this court partially granted that request. Citing Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the authority invoked by the President to issue Executive Order No. 14163, under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). However, we denied the request for a stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Subsequently, we issued two further orders to clarify this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel requested full briefing and directed the Clerk of Court to set the appeal for the next available calendar date.

After the completion of briefing, but before the oral argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court entered an order on July 14 establishing procedures to enforce the limited carveout and appointed a magistrate judge to administer the framework. On July 17, the government filed an emergency motion seeking enforcement, clarification, or modification of the stay order, opposed by plaintiffs. On July 18, 2025, we imposed an administrative stay of the district court’s enforcement order from July 14 and scheduled oral argument to occur on an expedited timeline.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties’ briefing and hearing oral argument, the matter is now taken under submission for decision. The Court will release a merits opinion in due time. Meanwhile, we hereby lift the administrative stay entered on July 18 as well as our stay of March 25, as subsequently modified on April 21 and May 9. We now impose this stay to remain effective until this appeal is fully resolved. The district court’s orders of February 28 and March 24, 2025 are stayed in full, save as otherwise indicated below.

The legal standards governing issuance of a stay are settled. Our analysis is guided by four criteria: (1) whether the party seeking the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable harm without the stay; (3) whether granting the stay will cause substantial harm to other parties involved; and (4) where the public interest is situated. Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Of these, the likelihood of success on the merits and the possibility of irreparable injury are regarded as “the most critical.” Id. The government has shown a probability of prevailing in its defense of the validity of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions, and we are precluded from conducting “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Id. at 685. “[T]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, to suspend the “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The remaining criteria—which consider domestic and foreign policy concerns as identified within the “broad discretion” granted to the President, Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84—also favor the government.

As will be discussed in detail in an ensuing opinion, the government is unlikely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act claims. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is obligated to furnish specified reception and placement assistance to refugees after they are admitted into the country. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) obliges the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, which is part of the Department of Health and Human Services, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to: (i) allocate sufficient resources for employment training and placement with the goal of attaining refugees’ economic self-sufficiency as rapidly as possible, (ii) offer refugees access to adequate English language instruction to facilitate prompt and effective resettlement, (iii) ensure the provision of cash assistance to refugees in a manner that does not undermine their pursuit of self-sufficiency in accordance with subsection (e)(2), and (iv) guarantee that women enjoy equivalent opportunities as men to participate in such training and instruction. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (detailing federal funding for refugee services). In regard to unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) specifies: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Concerning cash and medical benefits for newly arrived refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) mandates that the Secretary of Health and Human Services “shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For the fiscal year 2025, Congress has appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance. See Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating funds appropriated in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), which was subsequently decreased by $800 million in the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Section 1523 further requires the Secretary to provide a comprehensive report detailing activities conducted under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of both the House of Representatives and the Senate no later than January 31 following the conclusion of each fiscal year.

By statute, these resettlement services—commonly called “reception and placement” services—are permitted to be delivered through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Traditionally, the United States has established cooperative agreements with numerous resettlement entities to furnish these services. The organizational plaintiffs in this matter are three such entities that have held cooperative agreements with the Department of State.

As previously noted, in February 2024, the State Department ended all cooperative agreements related to these services. Recognizing the cessation of financial support for resettlement services, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, despite that assertion, the government informed the panel at oral argument that it did not know whether any steps had been taken to find a new agency or agencies to provide reception and placement services.

Given the government’s inability to confirm its capacity to deliver the reception and placement services required by 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby ordered to reestablish those cooperative agreements necessary to offer the reception and placement services set forth in § 1522 to refugees admitted to the United States. Such services must encompass the standard services customarily provided to such refugees under the previous cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is typically afforded substantial discretion and judicial deference in immigration affairs, the district court erred by enjoining the executive branch from executing the President’s chosen policy restricting the admission of refugees and the provision of related services. Therefore, I would grant a stay of the district court’s injunctions in full.

There is consensus among my colleagues and myself that the President possesses the authority to halt refugee admissions; accordingly, we would stay the district court’s injunction relating to that issue. However, we diverge as to whether the federal government bears a legal obligation to continue providing services to refugees who were recently admitted prior to the implementation of the suspension. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a requirement on the government to deliver specified services to such refugees. In contrast, I view the provision as best interpreted as permissive authority, rather than an affirmative mandate.

To analyze this question, we consider the language at the outset: the heading of Section 1522 states, “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis added). The use of "authorization" implies permission, not obligation. According to Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024), AUTHORIZATION is defined as “permission to do something” or “official document granting such permission.” Thus, by enacting §1522, Congress empowered action, but did not compel it.

Moreover, the four separate statutory subsections funding specific refugee services—such as the “Program of initial resettlement,” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—uniformly state that the “Director is authorized” to provide such assistance. See U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis added). These provisions once more signal that Congress vested discretionary authority, rather than imposing mandatory duties, upon the executive branch concerning the allocation of funding.

When asserting that Section 1522 mandates funding, my colleagues refer to §1522(a)—labeled “Conditions and consideration”—which uses the terms “shall” and “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this is limited by the immediately preceding language: “In providing assistance under this section [meaning the discretionary funding provisions contained in §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ” Thus, §1522(a) sets forth required “conditions and considerations” that apply only if the government opts to allocate funds to refugee programs. For instance, the Director is required to “make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [under §1522(c)’s discretionary funding for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugees’] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible”—that is, this obligation arises only if the government chooses to fund these initiatives.

To further support their position that the spending is mandatory, my colleagues point to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), claiming they place certain duties upon the Director. Yet, neither provision effectively challenges the broader framework of §1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) obligates the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee minors only when it is “necessary.” The provision additionally authorizes the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Although this subsection also uses “shall,” the language grants broad leeway to the executive and does not create a concrete requirement. Moreover, the record does not indicate whether any unaccompanied refugee minors are at issue here. Likewise, subsection (e)(7)(A) provides that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” cash and medical assistance projects for refugees “as needed.” The extent and parameters of these programs are subject to executive determination, reflecting administrative discretion rather than a direct mandate from Congress.

From my perspective, Section 1522 gives authority to the executive branch to develop such programs at its discretion while appropriated funds are available, but it does not obligate the government to operate these programs. Because the statute does not compel the executive branch to provide these services, the court lacks grounds to require their implementation. Accordingly, I respectfully dissent in part.
