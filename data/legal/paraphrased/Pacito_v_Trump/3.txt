BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under the authority of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President found that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and instructed that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until further determinations are made. Exec. Order. No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (which states in pertinent part: “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Then, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all financial support for the USRAP program, ostensibly in accordance with a separate executive order entitled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This freeze, which was to remain until further review, also encompassed funding for services supporting the domestic resettlement of refugees accepted into the United States.

The plaintiffs consist of refugees who were either previously granted admission to the United States or had received approval for resettlement in the United States but have not yet entered the country, along with three organizations holding cooperative agreements with the State Department to facilitate overseas processing for refugee applicants and to deliver resettlement services to refugees upon admission to the United States. In February 2025, these plaintiffs initiated an action in the Western District of Washington, contending that the suspension of the refugee program under Executive Order 14163 contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (as codified in the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and further alleging that the cessation of funding for USRAP breached various provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (referring to the Refugee Act as establishing “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court issued an oral preliminary injunction barring the enforcement or execution of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The following day, February 26, the government terminated every cooperative agreement for the provision of reception and placement services to refugees within the United States, as well as all but one of the cooperative agreements for USRAP processing support abroad. On February 28, the district court entered a written order delineating the breadth of the preliminary injunction from February 25, clarifying that it also barred the suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, the district court granted a second preliminary injunction, directing the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 order. The government lodged notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s February 28 order while the appeal was pending, which a motions panel of this court partially granted on March 25. In quoting Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the authority the President relied on to issue Executive Order No. 14163, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nevertheless, we denied the request for a stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. We subsequently entered two additional orders to clarify this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel ordered full briefing and instructed the Clerk of Court to place the appeal on the next available calendar.

After briefing was completed but prior to the argument scheduled for September 19, 2025, the district court issued an order on July 14 that established a framework to implement the limited carveout and designated a magistrate judge to supervise the process. On July 17, the government filed an emergency motion seeking enforcement, clarification, or modification of the stay order, which plaintiffs opposed. On July 18, 2025, we granted an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and scheduled expedited oral argument.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties’ briefs and the arguments presented orally, the case is now under submission for adjudication. A decision addressing the merits will follow in due course. Meanwhile, we hereby dissolve our administrative stay entered on July 18, as well as our previous stay of March 25, which was amended on April 21 and May 9. We now impose this stay pending the ultimate resolution of this appeal. The district court’s orders from February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed in full, except as specified below.

The criteria for granting a stay are well settled. Our analysis is guided by four considerations: (1) whether the party seeking the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of prevailing on the merits; (2) whether, absent a stay, the applicant would sustain irreparable harm; (3) whether issuance of the stay would cause significant harm to other parties involved in the litigation; and (4) the public interest. See Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Of these, the likelihood of success and the prospect of irreparable harm are regarded as “the most critical.” Id. The government appears poised to succeed against the plaintiffs’ challenge to the lawfulness of Executive Order No. 14163’s temporary halt on refugee admissions, and our review cannot extend to “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Id. at 685. “[T]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, to suspend the “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The remaining considerations, which pertain to both the internal and external interests of the United States as recognized under the President’s “broad discretion,” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84, also weigh in favor of the United States.

As will be set out in detail in a forthcoming opinion, the government is unlikely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ claims brought under the Administrative Procedure Act. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is obligated to furnish specific reception and placement services to refugees upon their admission to the United States. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) mandates that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, which operates within the Department of Health and Human Services, “to the extent of available appropriations,” must: (i) allocate adequate resources for employment training and placement with the goal of attaining refugees’ economic self-sufficiency as promptly as possible; (ii) offer refugees sufficient English language instruction to ensure their rapid and effective resettlement; (iii) guarantee that cash assistance provided to refugees is administered so as not to undermine their economic self-sufficiency, as outlined in subsection (e)(2); and (iv) ensure equal participation opportunities in training and instruction for both women and men. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (detailing federal funding for refugee services). Regarding unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Concerning cash and medical aid for newly resettled refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For fiscal year 2025, Congress has allotted $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, see Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating funding appropriated in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), though this amount was reduced by $800 million pursuant to the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Section 1523 further requires that the Secretary submit a comprehensive report on activities conducted under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of both the House of Representatives and the Senate no later than January 31 following the conclusion of each fiscal year.

Pursuant to statute, these resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—can be delivered via contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Historically, the United States has utilized cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations to administer these services. The organizational plaintiffs are three of the organizations with which the Department of State has entered into such cooperative agreements.

As noted previously, the State Department, in February 2024, terminated all cooperative agreements concerning the provision of these services. Following this cessation of resettlement funding, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. However, despite this assertion, the government stated during oral argument before the panel that it could not confirm whether any steps had actually been taken toward retaining a new agency or agencies to furnish reception and placement services.

Given the government’s uncertainty as to its ability to fulfill the statutory obligation to provide reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby ordered to reinstate those cooperative agreements necessary for the provision of the reception and placement services set forth in § 1522 to refugees admitted to the United States. These services shall comprise the standard and customary services previously provided to such refugees under the former cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is granted broad discretion and is generally accorded deference in immigration policy, the district court erred by prohibiting the executive branch from enacting the President’s decision to restrict the admission of refugees and the provision of services to them. Accordingly, I would grant a complete stay of the district court’s injunctions.

While my fellow panel members and I concur that the President possesses the authority to halt refugee admissions, justifying a stay of the district court’s injunction as to that aspect, we disagree on whether the federal government is legally compelled to continue providing services to refugees admitted before the suspension took effect. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a requirement on the government to furnish particular services to refugees. In my view, the statute is more appropriately interpreted as granting permission rather than imposing an obligation.

To begin: The heading of Section 1522 is “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis in original). “Authorization” implies a grant of permission, not a directive. AUTHORIZATION, Black's Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining it as “permission to do something” or the “official document granting such permission”). Section 1522 therefore empowers action, but does not mandate it.

Moreover, each of the four separate statutory subsections that allocate funds for distinct refugee services—such as “Program of initial resettlement” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—consistently provides that the “Director is authorized” to extend such assistance. U.S.C. § 1522(b)-(e) (emphasis in original). These statutory formulations further indicate that the executive branch retains discretion regarding the allocation of such funds.

In contending that Section 1522 requires mandatory funding, my colleagues focus on §1522(a)—entitled “Conditions and consideration”—which contains both the term “shall” and the clause “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this directive is modified by the phrase that immediately precedes it: “In providing assistance under this section [referring to the discretionary funding mechanisms in §1522(b)-(e)]…” Thus, §1522(a) establishes certain mandatory “conditions and considerations” only if the government elects to provide funding for refugee programs. For instance, the Director is instructed to “make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [under § 1522(c)’s discretionary provisions for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible”—meaning, this is a required condition or factor only if the government chooses to allocate such funding.

To further support their position of compelled spending, my colleagues refer to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which might be interpreted as imposing obligations upon the Director. Nonetheless, neither provision successfully overcomes the fundamental discretionary structure of § 1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) stipulates that the program’s Director assumes legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee minors only when such responsibility is deemed “necessary.” It also grants the Director authority “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Although the subsection employs “shall,” it overwhelmingly defers to executive discretion and does not establish an unequivocal directive. Moreover, it remains unclear from the record whether any unaccompanied refugee children are implicated in this matter. Similarly, subsection (e)(7)(A) provides that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” projects for cash and medical assistance for refugees “as needed.” The specific contours and limits of these programs are left to executive judgment, which falls short of a clear congressional command.

From my perspective, Section 1522 permits the executive to establish programs within its own discretion so long as funds are appropriated. It does not obligate the government to implement those programs. Because the statute does not compel the executive branch to provide these services, it is not within our court’s authority to require their operation. I therefore respectfully dissent in part.
