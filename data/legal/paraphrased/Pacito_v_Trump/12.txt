Title: Pacito v. Trump
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
Date: September 12, 2025
Docket: Nos. 25-1313 and 25-1939
Author: Panel order; partial concurrence and partial dissent by Lee, Circuit Judge

Following expedited oral argument conducted on September 3, 2025, an opinion from the court will follow in due course. Meanwhile, this Order serves to effectuate a stay pending appeal and any further directives from this court. The administrative stay previously entered on July 18, 2025, is now vacated, as is the stay issued on March 25, 2025, as subsequently amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025. The preliminary injunctions entered by the district court on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45), and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79), are hereby stayed in full, save for any exceptions expressly delineated within this Order. Additionally, the government’s emergency motion filed on July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108), is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order named “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under authority granted by 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that permitting refugees to enter through the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and ordered that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until further determinations are made. Exec. Order. No. 14163, § 3(a). Refer to 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (stating that “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all financial support for the USRAP program, citing as authority a separate executive order called “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). As part of this temporary suspension pending further assessment, funding was also withheld from domestic resettlement services intended for refugees who had already been admitted into the United States.

The plaintiffs consist of individuals who are refugees either already admitted to the United States or approved for resettlement but still outside the country, as well as three organizations that had entered into cooperative agreements with the State Department to conduct overseas processing for refugee applicants and to provide resettlement assistance to admitted refugees. In February 2025, these plaintiffs brought an action in the Western District of Washington, contending that the suspension of the refugee program under Executive Order 14163 contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (which is codified in multiple sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and further alleging that the withdrawal of funding for USRAP violated multiple provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (referring to the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court verbally issued a preliminary injunction barring enforcement or execution of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. On the following day, February 26, the government ended all cooperative agreements for the reception and placement of refugees in the United States and terminated all but one agreement for USRAP processing abroad. Subsequently, on February 28, the district court filed a written order clarifying the boundaries of the February 25 preliminary injunction, explicitly stating that it includes a prohibition on the suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, the court granted a second preliminary injunction, this time directing the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 order. The government filed notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay pending appeal of the district court’s February 28 order, which was partially granted by a motions panel of this court on March 25. Citing Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the power pursuant to which the President issued Executive Order No. 14163, namely 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nevertheless, we denied the stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Subsequently, we issued two further orders providing clarification regarding this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel ordered that briefing proceed and instructed the Clerk of Court to place the appeal on the next available calendar.

After briefing but prior to the argument scheduled for September 19, 2025, the district court issued an order on July 14 establishing a framework to enforce the limited carveout and designating a magistrate judge to supervise its implementation. On July 17, the government submitted an emergency motion seeking to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order, which the plaintiffs opposed. On July 18, 2025, we entered an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and ordered expedited oral argument.

DISCUSSION

After reviewing the parties' submissions and hearing oral argument, the case is now deemed ready for a decision. A substantive opinion will follow in due course. Meanwhile, we hereby lift the administrative stay imposed on July 18 as well as the stay entered on March 25 and modified on April 21 and May 9. In their place, we grant this stay to remain effective until the resolution of the present appeal. All portions of the district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed, with the exception of the instances specifically identified below.

The criteria for granting a stay are firmly established. We evaluate four considerations: (1) whether the applicant for the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether, in the absence of a stay, the applicant would suffer irreparable harm; (3) whether the granting of a stay would cause significant injury to other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Among these, the first two—a likelihood of success on the merits and the risk of irreparable harm—are identified as “the most critical.” Id. The government stands a strong chance of prevailing in response to plaintiffs’ challenge concerning the legality of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions. We may not subject the President’s stated grounds for the suspension to an in-depth assessment of their persuasiveness. Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. The only express requirement of § 1182(f) is that the President “find[]” that permitting entry for the specified aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The record reflects the President has met this obligation. Id. at 685. “[T]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, to bar “the entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). With respect to the remaining factors—which implicate significant domestic and international interests, and fall within the “broad discretion” Congress has conferred on the President, Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84—these too weigh in favor of the United States.

As will be elaborated upon in a forthcoming opinion, the government appears unlikely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act claims. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is obligated to provide specified reception and placement services to refugees after they are admitted into the United States. According to § 1522(a)(1)(A), the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—part of the Department of Health and Human Services—“to the extent of available appropriations,” must: (i) allocate adequate resources for job training and placement so that refugees may attain economic self-sufficiency as rapidly as possible; (ii) ensure access to sufficient English language training for refugees to enable expeditious resettlement; (iii) guarantee that cash assistance for refugees is distributed in a way that does not undermine their path to economic independence, as set forth in subsection (e)(2); and (iv) ensure that women are afforded the same training and instructional opportunities as men. Reference is also made to 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq., which outlines the federal financial support available for refugee services. Regarding unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) mandates: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). As for cash and medical assistance offered to newly arrived refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) instructs the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For fiscal year 2025, it is observed that Congress allocated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, see Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating appropriations in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), with this sum subsequently reduced by $800 million under the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Additionally, § 1523 imposes a reporting duty on the Secretary, requiring a comprehensive report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

Pursuant to statute, these resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—are authorized to be delivered by means of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). The United States has traditionally utilized cooperative agreements with a range of resettlement organizations for the provision of such services. The organizational plaintiffs are three such entities that have maintained cooperative agreements with the Department of State.

As previously noted, the State Department ended all such cooperative agreements for the provision of these services in February 2024. Recognizing this cessation of resettlement funding, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nonetheless, during oral argument before the panel, the government was unable to confirm whether any advancement had been made in identifying a new agency or agencies to deliver these services.

Given the government’s lack of clarity concerning the continued provision of the reception and placement services mandated by 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is ordered to restore those cooperative agreements necessary for furnishing the services outlined in § 1522 to refugees admitted to the United States. These services must include the standard services previously provided to such refugees under the former cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is granted considerable latitude and receives substantial deference concerning immigration issues, the district court erred by prohibiting the executive branch from carrying out the President’s chosen policy to restrict both the admission of refugees and the allocation of services to them. Consequently, I would stay the entirety of the district court’s injunctions.

While my fellow panelists concur that the President possesses the legal authority to institute a suspension on refugee admissions, and accordingly would stay the injunction related to that policy, we disagree as to whether the federal government has a binding legal obligation to render services to refugees who were recently admitted prior to the initiation of the suspension. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a statutory requirement that the government provide certain services to these individuals. In my view, this provision is better interpreted as merely granting permission, rather than establishing a duty.

We begin with the text: The heading of § 1522 is “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis supplied). “Authorization” conveys a permissive stance, not a compulsory one. According to Black’s Law Dictionary, AUTHORIZATION is defined as “permission to do something” or an “official document granting such permission.” (12th ed. 2024). Congress, therefore, provides for action in § 1522; it does not mandate it.

Additionally, the four particular statutory subsections addressing funding for distinct refugee services—for instance, “Program of initial resettlement,” “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—all stipulate that the “Director is authorized” to furnish such assistance. See U.S.C. § 1522(b)-(e) (emphasis supplied). Again, this language indicates that the executive branch retains discretion with respect to the provision of funding.

In asserting that Section 1522 creates an obligation for mandatory funding, my colleagues refer to §1522(a)—entitled “Conditions and consideration”—which includes the terms “shall” as well as “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this language is limited by the immediately preceding clause: “In providing assistance under this section [that is, the discretionary funding described in §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ” Therefore, §1522(a) establishes certain required “conditions and considerations” only if the government elects to provide funding to refugee programs. For instance, the Director “shall. . . make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [from the discretionary funding allocated under § 1522(c) for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible [meaning, this is the required consideration to be addressed only if the government provides funding for it].”

To strengthen their argument for mandatory expenditures, my colleagues rely on subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which might be read as creating certain responsibilities for the Director. Nevertheless, these provisions do not persuasively challenge the overall structure of § 1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) obligates the Director to take legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children solely if such a responsibility is deemed “necessary,” and empowers the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Although the subsection contains the directive “shall,” it clearly affords significant deference to executive judgment, and lacks any categorical directive. Moreover, the current record does not establish whether unaccompanied refugee children are even involved. By the same token, subsection (e)(7)(A) requires that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” programs offering cash and medical aid to refugees “as needed.” The scope and substance of those programs remain at the discretion of the executive, falling short of a directive imposed by Congress.

In my assessment, Section 1522 grants the executive branch the authority to design and implement programs as it sees fit, conditional on available appropriations. The statute does not impose a duty for those programs to be administered. Since there is no statutory requirement obligating the executive to provide these services, it is not within the court’s purview to command their provision. I therefore respectfully dissent in part.
