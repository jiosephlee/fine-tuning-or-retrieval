Title: Pacito v. Trump
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
Date: September 12, 2025
Docket: Nos. 25-1313 and 25-1939
Author: Panel order; partial concurrence and partial dissent by Lee, Circuit Judge

The court conducted oral argument in this matter on an expedited schedule on September 3, 2025. A formal opinion will be issued in due course. Meanwhile, this Order serves as a stay pending appeal and any further directive from this court. The administrative stay previously entered on July 18, 2025, is vacated. In addition, the court vacates its earlier stay from March 25, 2025, including the amendments made on April 21 and May 9, 2025. The preliminary injunctions issued by the district court on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45), and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79), are stayed in full, with the exception of any provisions otherwise addressed in this Order. The government’s emergency motion filed July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108), is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under the authority of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President found that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and ordered that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until further determinations are made. Exec. Order No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (stating in relevant part: “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all funding for the USRAP program, allegedly acting pursuant to another executive order titled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This funding freeze, pending additional assessment, encompassed support for domestic resettlement services provided to refugees admitted into the United States.

The plaintiffs consist of refugees who have either previously been admitted to the United States or have received approval for resettlement but are still located abroad, as well as three organizations holding cooperative agreements with the State Department to conduct overseas processing for refugee applicants and to deliver resettlement services to refugees once admitted to the United States. In February 2025, these plaintiffs initiated an action in the Western District of Washington, contending that Executive Order 14163’s suspension of the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified in various sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and further alleging that the withdrawal of USRAP funding breached multiple sections of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (describing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court delivered an oral preliminary injunction barring enforcement or execution of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The following day, February 26, the government terminated every cooperative agreement for providing reception and placement services to refugees within the United States and all but one agreement supporting USRAP processing overseas. On February 28, the district court issued a written order clarifying the February 25 preliminary injunction’s parameters, indicating it also blocked the suspension of funding for USRAP. On March 24, the district court entered a second preliminary injunction directing the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that had been terminated following the February 25 decision. The government submitted notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s February 28 order while its appeal was pending, and a motions panel of this court partially granted that request on March 25. In referencing Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the President’s authority to issue Executive Order No. 14163 under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). However, we denied the stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Subsequently, we issued two more orders clarifying this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel also called for comprehensive briefing and instructed the Clerk of Court to schedule the appeal for the next available calendar.

After the completion of briefing but prior to the oral argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court issued an order on July 14 creating a framework for implementation of the limited carveout and appointing a magistrate judge to supervise its enforcement. In response, on July 17, the government filed an emergency motion seeking enforcement, clarification, or modification of the stay order, which the plaintiffs opposed. On July 18, 2025, we imposed an administrative stay on the district court’s July 14 enforcement order. We arranged for oral argument to be heard on an expedited timeline.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties’ written submissions and oral presentations, the matter is now submitted for a decision. The Court’s opinion on the merits will be released in due time. Meanwhile, we hereby lift our administrative stay entered on July 18, as well as the stay issued on March 25, as modified on April 21 and May 9. We now impose this stay pending the ultimate disposition of this appeal. The district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed in full, except as specifically provided below.

The criteria governing the grant of a stay are firmly established. The Court examines four considerations: (1) whether the party requesting the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether that party will suffer irreparable harm if the stay is not granted; (3) whether issuing the stay will cause significant injury to other interested parties; and (4) the location of the public interest. Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Among these, the first and second—showing probable success on the merits and the risk of irreparable injury—are considered “the most critical.” Id. The government is likely to succeed regarding the plaintiffs’ challenge to the lawfulness of Executive Order No. 14163’s halt to refugee admissions, and we are not permitted to undertake “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. Section 1182(f) requires only that the President “find[]” that admitting the affected aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and “The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Id. at 685. “[T]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, to bar the “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other relevant factors, involving both the domestic and foreign policy interests of the United States, which are subject to the “broad discretion” vested in the President, Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84, likewise support the position of the United States.

As will be detailed in a subsequent opinion, the government does not appear likely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ claims brought under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is obligated to provide specified reception and placement services to refugees once they are admitted to the United States. Specifically, § 1522(a)(1)(A) mandates that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, located within the Department of Health and Human Services, “to the extent of available appropriations,” must: (i) allocate sufficient resources for employment training and job placement to facilitate refugees’ attainment of economic self-sufficiency as swiftly as possible; (ii) offer refugees adequate opportunities to receive English language instruction to support their rapid and effective resettlement; (iii) ensure that cash assistance to refugees is administered so as not to impede their economic self-sufficiency, consistent with subsection (e)(2); and (iv) guarantee equal access for women and men to training and instructional programs. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (regarding the federal funding provided for refugee services). With regard to unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Regarding the provision of cash and medical assistance for refugees recently resettled, § 1522(e)(7)(A) specifies that the Secretary of Health and Human Services “shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For fiscal year 2025, Congress allocated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, as set forth in Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating appropriations from division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), with an $800 million reduction imposed by the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Additionally, § 1523 imposes a reporting requirement on the Secretary, mandating submission of a detailed account “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

Pursuant to statute, these resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be delivered via contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Historically, the United States has utilized cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations to offer these services. The organizational plaintiffs are three such entities with which the Department of State previously maintained cooperative agreements.

As previously noted, the State Department terminated all such cooperative agreements providing these services in February 2024. Following the cessation of funding for resettlement services, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, at oral argument before the panel, the government indicated that it did not know whether any steps had been taken to obtain a new agency or agencies to provide reception and placement services.

Given the government’s lack of clarity regarding its ability to fulfill its statutory obligation to provide reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is ordered to reinstate those cooperative agreements necessary to furnish the services specified in § 1522 to refugees admitted into the United States. These services must encompass the standard services previously provided to such refugees under earlier cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is granted broad discretion and deference regarding immigration policies, the district court erred in enjoining the executive branch from carrying out the President’s policy to restrict refugee admissions and related services. Accordingly, I would grant a full stay of the district court’s injunctions.

While my fellow panel members and I concur that the President possesses the authority to suspend refugee admissions—and would therefore stay the district court’s injunction on that issue—we disagree on the question of whether the federal government is legally compelled to offer services to refugees who were recently admitted prior to the suspension’s commencement. My colleagues interpret 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a duty on the government to deliver specified services to refugees. In my view, this statute is best construed as permissive authority rather than an obligatory directive.

To begin, the heading of Section 1522 is “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis in original). The term “authorization” conveys discretion, not compulsion. See AUTHORIZATION, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining the term as “permission to do something” or the “official document granting such permission”). Therefore, §1522 grants the government the option to act, rather than imposing an obligation.

Additionally, each of the four specific statutory provisions that allocate funding for various refugee services—for example, “Program of initial resettlement,” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—employs the phrase that the “Director is authorized” to provide aid. See U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis added). These provisions again reflect Congress’s intent to vest discretion in the executive branch with respect to funding.

In contending that Section 1522 creates an obligation for mandatory funding, my colleagues rely on §1522(a)—captioned “Conditions and consideration”—which contains both the directive “shall” and the qualification “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, that phrasing is circumscribed by the phrase that comes immediately before it: “In providing assistance under this section [meaning the discretionary funding provisions contained in §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ” Thus, §1522(a) sets out mandatory “conditions and considerations” only when the government elects to allocate funds for refugee initiatives. For instance, the Director is required to “make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [in accordance with § 1522(c)’s discretionary funding mechanism for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible”—in other words, this constitutes a required consideration that applies only if funding is actually provided by the government.

To further support their assertion of compulsory expenditures, my colleagues point to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which could be construed as placing certain duties on the Director. Nevertheless, neither provision is sufficient to overcome the fundamental structure of § 1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) stipulates that the Director must assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee minors solely if such responsibility is “necessary.” This part also grants the Director the authority “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Although the subsection employs “shall,” it remains laden with executive discretion and does not establish an unequivocal requirement. Moreover, the record is unclear as to whether unaccompanied refugee children are present in the current context. Likewise, subsection (e)(7)(A) provides that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” programs for cash and medical assistance to refugees “as needed.” The extent and specifics of these programs rest with executive judgment, not congressional compulsion.

I am therefore persuaded that Section 1522 merely empowers the executive branch to develop such programs at its discretion and contingent on the availability of appropriations; it does not impose a duty to operate them. Given that the law does not compel the executive branch to offer these services, our court lacks the authority to require their provision. Accordingly, I must respectfully dissent in part.
