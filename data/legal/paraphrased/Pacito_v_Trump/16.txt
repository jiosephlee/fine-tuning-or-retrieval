Title: Pacito v. Trump  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit  
Date: September 12, 2025  
Docket: Nos. 25-1313 and 25-1939  
Author: Panel order; partial concurrence and partial dissent by Lee, Circuit Judge

Following the expedited oral argument held on September 3, 2025, the panel will issue an opinion in due course. In the meantime, this Order serves as a stay pending appeal and until the court provides further instructions. The administrative stay that was implemented on July 18, 2025, is hereby set aside. Likewise, the stay previously granted on March 25, 2025, as revised on April 21 and May 9, 2025, is also lifted. The preliminary injunctions entered by the district court on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45), and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79), are stayed in full, with the exception of those elements expressly preserved in this Order. The emergency motion filed by the government on July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108), is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Invoking authority under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that permitting “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and accordingly ordered that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until additional determinations could be made. Exec. Order No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (stating that “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all USRAP funding, purportedly based on a separate executive order captioned “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This funding suspension, subject to ongoing review, encompassed support for domestic resettlement services for refugees already admitted into the United States.

The plaintiffs consist of refugees who were either previously admitted to the United States or have been cleared for resettlement in the United States but are still located outside the country, along with three organizations that maintained cooperative agreements with the State Department to carry out overseas processing for refugee applicants and to deliver resettlement assistance for refugees upon arrival in the United States. In February 2025, these plaintiffs initiated legal action in the Western District of Washington, asserting that Executive Order 14163’s suspension of the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified at various sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and that the withdrawal of funding from USRAP was inconsistent with certain provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. Cf. Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (characterizing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court delivered an oral preliminary injunction, preventing enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. One day later, on February 26, the government canceled all cooperative agreements for the provision of reception and placement services for refugees within the United States and all but one agreement providing overseas support for USRAP processing. On February 28, the district court issued a written order clarifying the February 25 preliminary injunction to include the prohibition of USRAP funding suspension. On March 24, the district court granted a second preliminary injunction compelling the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that were ended after the February 25 ruling. The government filed notices of appeal regarding these rulings on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay pending appeal of the district court’s February 28 order, which was partially granted by a motions panel of this court on March 25. Referring to Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the President’s authority in issuing Executive Order No. 14163 under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). However, the stay motion was denied “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. We later issued two more orders providing clarification of this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel called for comprehensive briefing and instructed the Clerk of Court to schedule the appeal for the next available sitting.

After briefing was submitted and prior to oral argument, which was set for September 19, 2025, the district court entered an order on July 14 establishing a mechanism for enforcing the limited carveout, including the appointment of a magistrate judge to supervise implementation. On July 17, the government submitted an emergency motion seeking enforcement, clarification, or modification of the stay order, opposed by the plaintiffs. On July 18, 2025, we imposed an administrative stay on the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and set oral argument to proceed on an expedited timeline.

DISCUSSION

After considering both the written briefs and oral arguments presented by the parties, the matter is now under submission for decision. The court will issue a decision on the merits in due course. Meanwhile, the administrative stay entered on July 18 and the stay imposed on March 25—together with amendments to that stay dated April 21 and May 9—are hereby vacated. We now impose a stay pending the final outcome of this appeal. Accordingly, all portions of the district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed, except as otherwise specified below.

The criteria for granting a stay are well-settled. Four elements are weighed: (1) Whether the applicant for the stay has demonstrated a strong likelihood of prevailing on the merits; (2) whether, without a stay, the applicant will suffer irreparable harm; (3) whether the stay will cause significant injury to other interested parties; and (4) the position of the public interest. Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). The first two factors—likelihood of success on the merits and the risk of irreparable injury—are regarded as “the most critical.” Id. The government is likely to succeed against plaintiffs’ claims contesting Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions, as the judiciary is not authorized to conduct “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. The only requirement in § 1182(f) is that the President “find[]” that allowing the entry of covered aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” a standard the President has clearly met here. Id. at 685. “[T]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, authorizing the suspension of the “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other considerations, which pertain to both the domestic and foreign concerns of the United States—as recognized by the “broad discretion” the statute confers on the President, Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84—also support the position of the United States.

The government, for reasons that will be detailed more fully in a forthcoming opinion, does not appear likely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act claims. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, certain reception and placement services must be provided by the government to refugees upon their entry into the United States. Specifically, under § 1522(a)(1)(A), the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—a component of the Department of Health and Human Services—is obligated “to the extent of available appropriations” to: (i) allocate adequate resources for employment training and placement aimed at ensuring refugees become economically self-sufficient as soon as feasible; (ii) afford refugees opportunities for sufficient English language instruction to promote prompt and effective resettlement; (iii) guarantee cash assistance is delivered to refugees in a manner that does not hinder their economic self-sufficiency, consistent with subsection (e)(2); and (iv) ensure that female refugees have equal access as males to training and educational opportunities. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (addressing federal funding provisions for refugee services). Concerning unaccompanied refugee minors, § 1522(d)(2)(B) stipulates: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Regarding cash and medical support for recently arrived refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) mandates the Secretary of Health and Human Services “shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For fiscal year 2025, Congress allocated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance pursuant to Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating funding as set forth in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), which was subsequently decreased by $800 million pursuant to the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Additionally, § 1523 requires the Secretary to provide accountability through a detailed report on activities under this subchapter, to be submitted to the Committees on the Judiciary of both the House of Representatives and the Senate by January 31 following the conclusion of each fiscal year.

Pursuant to statute, these resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be delivered via contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Traditionally, the United States has utilized cooperative agreements with different resettlement organizations to offer these services. The organizational plaintiffs are three such entities with which the Department of State has maintained cooperative agreements.

As previously noted, in February 2024, the State Department ended all cooperative agreements concerning these services. Recognizing the cessation of funding for resettlement services, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, at oral argument before the panel, the government indicated it did not know whether any steps had been taken to identify a new agency or agencies capable of offering these reception and placement services.

Given the government’s lack of certainty about fulfilling its statutory obligation to provide reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is hereby ordered to restore such cooperative agreements as are required to supply the reception and placement services specified in § 1522 to refugees admitted to the United States. These services must include the standard and customary services that refugees previously received under earlier cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is granted significant discretion and is afforded deference in the realm of immigration, the district court erred by enjoining the executive branch from carrying out the President’s policy choice to restrict the admission of refugees and the provision of services to them. Accordingly, I would grant a full stay of the district court’s injunctions.

While my colleagues and I concur that the President possesses the authority to institute a temporary suspension on refugee admissions and would therefore stay the district court's injunction as to that aspect, we diverge on the question of whether the federal government is legally obligated to continue providing services to those refugees who were recently admitted prior to the implementation of the suspension. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a requirement on the government to furnish specific services to refugees. However, in my view, this section is better interpreted as a provision of authority—rather than a mandate.

To begin, § 1522 is titled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis in original). The concept of authorization confers discretion rather than imposes an obligation. AUTHORIZATION, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining it as “permission to do something” or the “official document granting such permission”). Thus, § 1522 permits government action but does not compel it.

Moreover, the four distinct statutory subsections that appropriate funds for various refugee-related services—such as “Program of initial resettlement” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—consistently use the phrase “Director is authorized” to provide assistance. See 8 U.S.C. § 1522(b)-(e) (emphasis in original). Once more, these statutory formulations indicate that the executive branch possesses discretion regarding the allocation of funding.

In asserting that Section 1522 mandates funding, my colleagues refer to §1522(a), labeled “Conditions and consideration,” which contains both the directive term “shall” and the qualifier “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this language is limited by the phrase that directly precedes it: “In providing assistance under this section [i.e., the permissive funding elements of §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ” Thus, §1522(a) establishes obligatory “conditions and considerations” only in the event the government opts to fund refugee initiatives. For instance, the Director “shall . . . make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [from the discretionary funding authorized by §1522(c) for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugees’] to ensure refugees achieve economic self-sufficiency as expeditiously as possible [that is, this mandatory standard must be applied should the government choose to allocate funding for this purpose].”

In support of their argument for mandatory expenditures, my colleagues point to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), contending they place definite duties upon the Director. Nonetheless, these provisions do not convincingly undermine the fundamental structure of §1522. Under subsection (d)(2)(B), the program Director is obligated to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee minors only if “necessary.” The same subsection grants the Director authority “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Even though “shall” appears in the subsection, it is heavily deferential to executive discretion and lacks a definite command. Furthermore, there is no indication in the current record of any unaccompanied refugee children relevant to this dispute. In the same vein, subsection (e)(7)(A) provides that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” assistance programs for cash and medical benefits for refugees “as needed.” The scope and specifics of these programs are committed to executive determination and discretion, evidencing a lack of strict congressional directive.

From my perspective, Section 1522 grants the executive branch authority to devise such programs at its discretion, conditioned on the availability of appropriated funds. It does not mandate the operation of these programs. Since the statute does not compel the executive to deliver these services, it falls beyond the judiciary’s purview to require their provision. Accordingly, I respectfully dissent in part.
