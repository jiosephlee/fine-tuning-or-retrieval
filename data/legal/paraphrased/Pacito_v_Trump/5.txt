Oral argument for this matter was conducted on an expedited schedule on September 3, 2025. The court will issue its opinion in due time. Meanwhile, this Order serves as a stay during the pendency of the appeal and until further directive from this court. The administrative stay imposed on July 18, 2025, is hereby vacated. Additionally, the prior stay entered on March 25, 2025, and amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025, is also vacated. The district court’s preliminary injunctions issued on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45) and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79) are stayed in full, except to the extent otherwise provided in this Order. The government’s emergency motion dated July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108), is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under the authority of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President found that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and accordingly ordered that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until further determinations are made. Exec. Order. No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (authorizing the President to “suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate,” whenever he concludes such entry “would be detrimental to the interest of the United States”). On January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all financial support for the USRAP program, purportedly relying on a separate executive order named “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This suspension, effective pending additional review, encompassed funds for domestic resettlement assistance for refugees who had been admitted into the United States.

The plaintiffs consist of refugees who were either previously granted admission to the United States or who have been approved for resettlement in the United States but remain abroad, as well as three organizations that had entered into cooperative agreements with the State Department to facilitate overseas processing for individuals seeking refugee status and to provide resettlement services to refugees admitted into the United States. In February 2025, the plaintiffs initiated an action in the Western District of Washington, contending that Executive Order 14163’s suspension of the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified in various sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and asserting that the withdrawal of funding from USRAP violated multiple provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (characterizing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court, in an oral ruling, issued a preliminary injunction forbidding the enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The following day, February 26, the government terminated all cooperative agreements for reception and placement services for refugees in the United States, as well as all but one agreement concerning overseas USRAP processing support. Subsequently, on February 28, the district court released a written order clarifying the reach of the February 25 preliminary injunction, making explicit that it also enjoins the suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, the district court granted a second preliminary injunction, directing the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 order. The government submitted notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay pending its appeal of the district court’s February 28 order; a motions panel of this court partially granted that request on March 25. In referencing Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the President’s authority to issue Executive Order No. 14163, under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). However, we denied the request for a stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Subsequently, we issued two more orders to clarify this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel ordered full briefing on the appeal and directed the Clerk of Court to schedule it on the next available calendar.

After briefing, but before the scheduled oral argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court on July 14 entered an order establishing a framework for enforcement of the limited carveout, including the appointment of a magistrate judge to supervise the process. The government responded on July 17 by filing an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the stay order, which plaintiffs opposed. On July 18, 2025, we entered an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and set the case for oral argument on an expedited schedule.

DISCUSSION

After consideration of the parties’ briefing and oral argument, the case is now taken under submission for decision. An opinion addressing the merits will be issued in due time. Meanwhile, we hereby dissolve our administrative stay dated July 18 and our stay from March 25, as modified on April 21 and May 9. We now grant a stay pending the ultimate disposition of this appeal. The district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed in full, with the exception of any provisions specified below.

The criteria for granting a stay are settled law. Four considerations guide our determination: (1) whether the party seeking the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable harm without a stay; (3) whether granting the stay will cause substantial injury to other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Of these, the first two—probability of success on the merits and risk of irreparable injury—are “the most critical.” Id. The government appears likely to succeed in defending Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions against plaintiffs’ challenge, and we are constrained from conducting “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Id. at 685. “[T]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, regarding the suspension of “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other factors—which involve both domestic and international concerns of the United States, subject to the “broad discretion” entrusted to the President, Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84—also support the United States.

The government, however, is unlikely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ claims brought under the APA, for reasons that will be detailed in a forthcoming opinion. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, certain reception and placement services must be furnished by the government to refugees once they have entered the United States. Specifically, § 1522(a)(1)(A) obligates the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, an entity within the Department of Health and Human Services, “to the extent of available appropriations,” to: (i) allocate adequate resources for employment training and placement to facilitate refugees’ rapid attainment of economic self-sufficiency; (ii) ensure refugees are given opportunities for sufficient English language instruction to support their prompt resettlement; (iii) guarantee that the provision of cash assistance to refugees does not undermine their economic self-sufficiency, consistent with subsection (e)(2); and (iv) ensure women are afforded equal opportunities with men in training and instruction programs. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (setting forth the federal funding mechanisms for refugee services). In addition, regarding unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) stipulates: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). For cash and medical assistance relating to newly resettled refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For fiscal year 2025, Congress allocated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, as set out in Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating appropriations from division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), although this sum was subsequently reduced by $800 million under the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Under § 1523, the Secretary is further required to submit a comprehensive report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

Pursuant to statute, resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be furnished via contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Traditionally, the United States has executed cooperative agreements with different resettlement organizations to supply these services. The organizational plaintiffs are three entities that have held such cooperative agreements with the Department of State.

As previously noted, the State Department ended all cooperative agreements for the provision of these services in February 2024. Recognizing the termination of resettlement services funding, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, during oral argument before the panel, the government stated that it did not know whether any steps had been taken to obtain a new agency or agencies to deliver these reception and placement services.

Given the government’s lack of certainty about its capacity to provide the reception and placement services required under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is ordered to restore the necessary cooperative agreements to deliver the reception and placement services outlined in § 1522 to refugees admitted into the United States. These services must encompass the ordinary and typical services that refugees previously received under earlier cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is afforded considerable discretion and judicial deference in immigration affairs, the district court erroneously barred the executive branch from carrying out the President’s policy restricting the admission of refugees and the provision of services to them. Therefore, I would grant a full stay of the district court’s injunctions.

My fellow panel members and I concur that the President possesses the power to suspend refugee admissions, and for that reason, we would stay the district court’s injunction relating to this matter. However, we differ in our interpretation regarding whether the federal government is legally obligated to continue furnishing services to individuals who were admitted as refugees before the suspension was implemented. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 to require the government to supply specified services to refugees. In my view, the language of the statute is more appropriately read as conferring permission, not imposing a mandate.

To begin, Section 1522 is entitled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis added). The use of “authorization” denotes a grant of permission rather than an obligation. See AUTHORIZATION, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining the term as “permission to do something” or the “official document granting such permission”). Accordingly, Congress grants authority under §1522, but does not command action.

Moreover, each of the four specific statutory provisions allocating funds for various refugee programs—for instance, “Program of initial resettlement,” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—expressly states that the “Director is authorized” to extend such assistance. U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis added). This repeated use of authorization language further indicates that the executive retains discretion in administering funding.

In contending that Section 1522 requires mandatory funding, my colleagues focus on §1522(a), entitled “Conditions and consideration,” which contains the term “shall” as well as the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this language is limited by the immediately preceding clause: “In providing assistance under this section [that is, the discretionary funding provisions in §1522(b)-(e)]. . .” Thus, §1522(a) establishes certain mandatory “conditions and considerations” only if the government elects to finance refugee programs. For instance, the Director “shall. . . make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [pursuant to the discretionary funding for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’ under §1522(c)] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible [that is, this constitutes the mandatory condition or consideration the government must address should it choose to provide such funding].”

To support their assertion of mandatory funding, my colleagues refer to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which arguably impose certain duties on the Director. Nonetheless, neither subsection convincingly rebuts the general framework of §1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) obligates the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children only when such responsibility is “necessary.” This provision further empowers the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Although the subsection employs the word “shall,” its language is highly deferential to executive discretion and does not establish a strict mandate. Furthermore, the record does not clearly indicate whether any unaccompanied refugee children are involved in the present matter. In a similar vein, subsection (e)(7)(A) provides that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” programs for cash and medical support for refugees “as needed.” The specific scope and implementation of these programs are left to executive judgment, rather than being dictated by Congress.

I believe that Section 1522 grants authority to the executive branch to establish programs at its discretion, provided appropriations are available. It does not impose an obligation to operate those programs. Since the statute does not require the executive branch to provide these services, the court lacks authority to compel their provision. Accordingly, I respectfully dissent in part.
