BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order No. 14163, titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under the authority of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that allowing refugees to enter the United States through the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and therefore ordered the suspension of refugee admissions under the USRAP “pending further findings.” Exec. Order No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (authorizing the President, when he determines that entry by any aliens or classes of aliens would harm the interests of the United States, to “suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate” for such period as he considers necessary). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all financial support for the USRAP program, allegedly pursuant to another executive order, “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This pause, which will remain in effect pending further assessment, also encompassed funding for resettlement assistance within the United States for refugees who had been admitted.

The plaintiffs consist of refugees who have either previously been admitted into the United States or have been approved for resettlement but currently remain abroad, as well as three organizations that maintained cooperative agreements with the State Department to conduct overseas processing for individuals seeking refugee status and to provide resettlement services to admitted refugees. In February 2025, the plaintiffs initiated legal action in the Western District of Washington, asserting that the suspension of the refugee program under Executive Order 14163 contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (as codified in various sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and that the cessation of funding for USRAP was in violation of certain provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (characterizing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court delivered an oral preliminary injunction that barred the government from enforcing or carrying out §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. Subsequently, on February 26, the government terminated all cooperative agreements for reception and placement services for refugees within the United States and, with one exception, terminated all overseas cooperative agreements supporting USRAP processing. On February 28, the district court issued a written order further clarifying the preliminary injunction from February 25, making clear that it also enjoined the suspension of USRAP funding. Then, on March 24, the court granted a second preliminary injunction compelling the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 order. The government submitted notices of appeal on both February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay pending appeal from the district court's February 28 order, which a motions panel of this court partially granted on March 25. Referencing Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the statutory authority for the President's issuance of Executive Order No. 14163, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nevertheless, we denied the request for a stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Subsequently, we entered two further orders to clarify this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel ordered that the appeal proceed with full briefing and directed the Clerk of Court to set the case on the next available calendar.

After briefing but prior to the scheduled September 19, 2025 argument, the district court issued an order on July 14 to create a framework for implementing the limited carveout and designated a magistrate judge to oversee the process. On July 17, the government submitted an emergency motion seeking to enforce, clarify, or modify the stay order, which plaintiffs opposed. On July 18, 2025, we entered an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order. Oral argument was set on an expedited schedule.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties’ written submissions and the oral argument, the matter is now submitted for a decision. An opinion addressing the merits will be forthcoming. Meanwhile, we hereby lift the administrative stay issued on July 18, as well as the stay entered on March 25 and its subsequent amendments on April 21 and May 9. In their place, we grant a stay pending the ultimate resolution of this appeal. Accordingly, the district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed in full, with the exceptions specified below.

The legal principles governing the issuance of a stay are settled. Our analysis looks to four elements: (1) whether the party seeking a stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant faces a risk of irreparable harm if the stay is denied; (3) whether granting the stay would significantly injure other parties with an interest in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest resides. Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Among these considerations, the first two—likelihood of success and the prospect of irreparable harm—are “the most critical.” Id. The government appears likely to succeed regarding the plaintiffs’ challenge to the legality of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions, and the courts are not to conduct “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. The statute, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), requires only that the President “find[]” the entry of certain aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” That standard has plainly been met in this case. Id. at 685. Further, “[t]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, with respect to suspending “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The remaining considerations, involving both domestic and foreign policy interests of the United States, also support the United States’ position due to the “broad discretion” the President possesses in these matters. Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84.

As will be set forth more comprehensively in a subsequent opinion, the government does not appear likely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ claims brought under the APA. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is obligated to deliver certain reception and placement services to refugees once they are admitted to the United States. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) mandates that, “to the extent of available appropriations,” the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—a component of the Department of Health and Human Services—must: (i) allocate adequate resources for employment training and job placement to enable refugees to achieve economic self-sufficiency at the earliest possible time, (ii) furnish refugees with access to English language instruction to facilitate prompt and effective resettlement, (iii) ensure that the provision of cash assistance to refugees does not act as a disincentive to their economic independence, consistent with subsection (e)(2), and (iv) guarantee that women have equal access with men to training and educational opportunities. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (detailing the scope of federal funding for refugee services). With regard to unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Regarding cash and medical assistance for recently resettled refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For fiscal year 2025, Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, see Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (adopting the appropriations set forth in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), which amount was reduced by $800 million under the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Under § 1523, the Secretary is required to provide a comprehensive report detailing activities conducted under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of both the House of Representatives and the Senate no later than January 31 following the close of each fiscal year.

According to statute, these resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—can be furnished by means of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). The United States has customarily utilized cooperative agreements with different resettlement organizations for the provision of these services. The plaintiff organizations in this case are among those with which the Department of State has entered into such cooperative agreements.

As previously noted, in February 2024, the State Department terminated all cooperative agreements relating to these services. Recognizing the cessation of funding for resettlement services, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nevertheless, at oral argument before the panel, the government indicated that it did not know whether any advancements had occurred in identifying a new agency or agencies to supply reception and placement services.

Given the government’s lack of clarity about its capacity to fulfill the reception and placement services mandated by 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is ordered to reinstate those cooperative agreements that are required to deliver the reception and placement services outlined in § 1522 to refugees admitted to the United States. These services must encompass those that have customarily been provided to such refugees under previous cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is afforded considerable latitude and judicial deference in the realm of immigration policy, the district court erred in enjoining the executive from carrying out the President’s chosen approach to restrict the admission of refugees and the attendant services provided to them. Accordingly, I would grant a complete stay of the district court’s injunctions.

While my fellow judges and I concur that the President possesses the authority to suspend refugee admissions—leading us to support staying the district court’s injunction with respect to that matter—we differ on the question of whether the federal government is legally obliged to continue providing services to individuals who were admitted prior to the effective date of the suspension. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing an obligation on the government to furnish certain services to refugees. In my view, however, the statute is best interpreted as conferring discretionary authority rather than establishing a compulsory duty.

Let us begin with the statutory language: Section 1522 is entitled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis in original). The term “authorization” connotes permission, not compulsion. See AUTHORIZATION, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining the term as “permission to do something” or an “official document granting such permission”). Section 1522 therefore grants permission for government action but does not impose a mandate.

Moreover, the four particular statutory subsections providing for funding of various refugee-related services—such as “Program of initial resettlement,” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—uniformly provide that the “Director is authorized” to deliver assistance. U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis added). Once more, this language signals that the executive branch retains discretion over the allocation of funding.

In contending that Section 1522 creates an obligation for mandatory funding, my colleagues refer to §1522(a), labeled “Conditions and consideration,” which contains the term “shall” as well as the expression “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this wording is constrained by the phrase that comes directly before it: “In providing assistance under this section [referring to the discretionary funding authorizations in §1522(b)-(e)]. . .” In effect, §1522(a) imposes certain mandatory “conditions and considerations” only if the government opts to allocate funding for refugee programs. For instance, the Director “shall...make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [pursuant to § 1522(c)’s discretionary authority regarding ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible”—that is, this sets forth a required condition or consideration that must be honored if the government elects to fund such initiatives.

To reinforce their assertion of obligatory spending, my colleagues rely on subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which could be construed as imposing specific requirements on the Director. Yet neither provision convincingly alters the general framework of § 1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) obligates the program’s Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee minors only when such responsibility is deemed “necessary.” This part further authorizes the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Despite the inclusion of “shall,” this language substantially defers to executive judgment and lacks an unequivocal mandate. Furthermore, the record does not establish whether any unaccompanied refugee children are presently implicated. In the same vein, subsection (e)(7)(A) provides that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” initiatives for cash and medical assistance to refugees “as needed.” The scope and content of these programs are left to the executive’s discretion, falling short of a directive from Congress.

From my perspective, Section 1522 permits the executive to design programs as it sees fit, contingent upon the existence of appropriations, but does not impose a requirement to implement such programs. Since the statute does not obligate the executive branch to furnish these services, our court lacks authority to require their provision. I therefore respectfully dissent in part.
