Title: Pacito v. Trump
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
Date: September 12, 2025
Docket: Nos. 25-1313 and 25-1939
Author: Panel order; partial concurrence and partial dissent by Lee, Circuit Judge

This matter was argued before the court on an expedited schedule on September 3, 2025. A formal opinion will be forthcoming. Meanwhile, this Order will serve as a stay during the appeal and until further order of the court. The administrative stay imposed on July 18, 2025, is hereby set aside. Additionally, our earlier stay from March 25, 2025, as modified on April 21 and May 9, 2025, is also vacated. The district court’s preliminary injunctions dated February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45), and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79), are stayed in full with the exception of any provisions noted in this Order. The government’s emergency motion filed July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108), is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order No. 14163, titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program,” published at 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 on January 30, 2025. Acting under the authority of 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and ordered the suspension of “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP” until further determinations are made. Exec. Order. No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (stating in part, “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all USRAP program funding, purportedly in reliance on a separate order titled “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid,” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This suspension also applied, pending additional review, to financial support for resettlement services in the United States for refugees who had been admitted.

The plaintiffs in this case are refugees who were either previously admitted into the United States or have received approval for resettlement in the United States but are still located outside of the country, as well as three organizations that had entered into cooperative agreements with the State Department to carry out overseas processing for refugee status applicants and to provide resettlement services for refugees who had entered the United States. In February 2025, these plaintiffs initiated litigation in the Western District of Washington, contending that Executive Order 14163’s pause on the refugee program contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified in sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and further asserting that the cessation of funding for USRAP breached several sections of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (noting that the Refugee Act sets out “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). The district court, on February 25, delivered an oral preliminary injunction that restrained the enforcement or execution of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. Following this, on February 26, the government canceled every cooperative agreement related to providing reception and placement assistance for refugees within the United States and all but one agreement relating to overseas USRAP processing services. Then, on February 28, the district court entered a written order to clarify and delimit the February 25 injunction, stating that it also barred suspension of USRAP funding. Subsequently, on March 24, the district court granted a second preliminary injunction directing the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that had been ended after the February 25 ruling. The government submitted notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s order dated February 28, which was partially granted by a motions panel of this court on March 25. Citing Trump v. Hawaii, we recognized that the authority the President relied upon to issue Executive Order No. 14163, namely 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nevertheless, we denied the request for a stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Subsequently, we released two further orders to clarify this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel also ordered full briefing and directed the Clerk of Court to schedule the appeal on the next available calendar.

After the briefing period but before the argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court entered an order on July 14 to set up a structure for implementing the limited carveout and to assign a magistrate judge to supervise it. On July 17, the government filed an emergency motion seeking enforcement, clarification, or modification of the stay order, which plaintiffs contested. On July 18, 2025, we imposed an administrative stay of the district court’s enforcement order of July 14. We ordered expedited oral argument on the matter.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties' briefs and oral arguments, the matter is now deemed submitted for a decision. A substantive opinion will follow in due time. Meanwhile, we hereby lift both our administrative stay entered on July 18 and our previously issued stay from March 25, as modified on April 21 and May 9. A new stay shall be in place pending the ultimate resolution of this appeal. Accordingly, the district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed in full, with the exceptions set forth below.

The criteria for granting a stay are well-settled. There are four pertinent considerations: (1) whether the party requesting a stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will face irreparable harm if the stay is denied; (3) the extent to which other parties interested in the proceeding will suffer significant injury from issuance of the stay; and (4) the public interest. Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Of these, the first two—the likelihood of prevailing on the merits and the risk of irreparable harm—are deemed the most crucial. Id. In this instance, the government is likely to succeed in defending against plaintiffs’ claims regarding the legality of Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions, and the judiciary may not “engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. Section 1182(f) imposes only one requirement: that the President “find[]” the entry of the relevant aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” This standard has plainly been met here. Id. at 685. Moreover, the statute’s text is explicit, and the Executive Order does not go beyond any limitation specified therein. Id. at 688; see also 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (permitting suspension of “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States”). The other factors—which implicate domestic and international interests of the nation, subject to the President’s “broad discretion,” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84—likewise weigh in the United States’ favor.

As will be discussed in detail in a forthcoming opinion, the Court finds that the government does not appear likely to succeed on at least one of plaintiffs’ claims brought under the APA. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, following their admission to the United States, refugees are entitled to specified reception and placement services provided by the government. Section 1522(a)(1)(A) obligates the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—which operates within the Department of Health and Human Services—“to the extent of available appropriations”—to: (i) allocate adequate resources to support employment training and placement to facilitate rapid economic self-sufficiency for refugees, (ii) ensure that refugees have opportunities to receive English language instruction sufficient to support efficient resettlement, (iii) guarantee that cash assistance is distributed in a way that does not undermine refugees’ efforts to achieve economic independence, in compliance with subsection (e)(2), and (iv) ensure that female refugees receive the same access as male refugees to available training and instructional programs. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (which delineate available federal funding for refugee services). In a parallel provision concerning unaccompanied refugee children, § 1522(d)(2)(B) stipulates: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). For newly arrived refugees eligible for cash and medical assistance, § 1522(e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” The record further shows that for fiscal year 2025, Congress has allocated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, see Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (referencing the appropriations from division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), though this amount was diminished by $800 million pursuant to the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Additionally, under § 1523, the Secretary must provide a comprehensive report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

Pursuant to statute, these resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be delivered through contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Traditionally, the United States has utilized cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations to carry out these services. The organizational plaintiffs are three such entities with which the Department of State has established cooperative agreements.

As previously stated, in February 2024, the State Department discontinued all cooperative agreements related to these services. Acknowledging this cessation of funding, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. However, during oral argument before the panel, the government indicated that it did not know whether any steps had been taken towards identifying or engaging a new agency or agencies to deliver reception and placement services.

Given the government’s uncertainty regarding its capacity to provide the reception and placement services required by 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is ordered to restore such cooperative agreements as are needed to deliver the reception and placement services outlined in § 1522 to refugees admitted into the United States. These services must include the typical and customary support previously made available to such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is granted substantial discretion and is accorded deference in the realm of immigration, the district court erred when it restrained the executive branch from carrying out the President’s policy choice to restrict the admission of refugees and the provision of services to them. Accordingly, I would grant a stay of the district court’s injunctions in full.

While my fellow judges and I concur that the President is empowered to halt refugee admissions and would therefore stay the district court’s injunction pertaining to that subject, we diverge concerning whether the federal government is legally obligated to render services to refugees recently admitted before the moratorium commenced. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a requirement that the government provide specific services to refugees. In my view, however, this provision is best understood as authorizing, rather than compelling, such action.

Let us begin with the statute itself: Section 1522 is captioned “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis in original). The word “authorization” denotes permission rather than obligation. See AUTHORIZATION, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (“permission to do something” or the “official document granting such permission”). Through § 1522, Congress grants authority for action, but does not mandate it.

Moreover, the four particular statutory subsections that allocate funding for various refugee services—such as those labeled “Program of initial resettlement” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—all specify that the “Director is authorized” to offer assistance. U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis in original). Once again, these statutory formulations confer discretion on the executive branch with respect to funding.

In support of the assertion that Section 1522 mandates funding, my colleagues refer to §1522(a)—titled “Conditions and consideration”—which employs the term “shall” as well as the clause “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this phrasing is limited by the phrase that immediately precedes it: “In providing assistance under this section [i.e., referring to the discretionary funding provisions found in §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ”. This indicates that §1522(a) prescribes certain compulsory “conditions and considerations” only in cases where the government elects to fund refugee initiatives. For instance, the Director “shall…make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [under §1522(c)’s discretionary grants and contracts for refugee services] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible [meaning this is a condition or consideration that becomes binding only if funding is provided].”

To further substantiate their position regarding obligatory expenditures, my colleagues refer to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which they contend may place duties upon the Director. Nevertheless, neither subsection convincingly challenges the overall framework of §1522. Specifically, subsection (d)(2)(B) obligates the Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children only when that responsibility is deemed “necessary.” This provision also empowers the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Even where the word “shall” appears, the subsection is replete with deference to executive authority and does not express a categorical directive. Additionally, it remains unclear from the available record whether unaccompanied refugee children are at issue in the current matter. Along similar lines, subsection (e)(7)(A) provides that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” cash and medical support projects for refugees “as needed,” leaving the design and scope of such programs to executive judgment—far from constituting a mandatory directive from Congress.

Based on my analysis, Section 1522 enables the executive branch to create programs at its discretion, provided that appropriations are made available, but it does not compel the operation of such programs. Since the statutory scheme does not obligate the executive to provide these services, it is not within this court’s authority to require their provision. Accordingly, I respectfully dissent in part.
