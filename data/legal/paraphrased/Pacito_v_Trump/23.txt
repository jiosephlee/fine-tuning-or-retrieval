Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit
Date: September 12, 2025

Author: Panel order; partial concurrence and partial dissent by Lee, Circuit Judge

Following expedited oral argument conducted on September 3, 2025, the Court will issue its opinion in due course. In the meantime, this Order shall serve as a stay pending the outcome of the appeal or further instruction from this Court. The administrative stay entered on July 18, 2025, is hereby set aside. Additionally, we lift the earlier stay dated March 25, 2025, and its amendments from April 21 and May 9, 2025. The preliminary injunctions granted by the district court on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45) and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79) are stayed in full except where otherwise indicated within this Order. The government’s emergency motion dated July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108) is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order called “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President found that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and consequently ordered the suspension of “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP” until further evaluation. Exec. Order No. 14163, § 3(a). See 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (authorizing the President to “suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants” if such entry “would be detrimental to the interest of the United States,” or to impose restrictions deemed appropriate). Four days later, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all funding for the USRAP, allegedly relying on another executive order, “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This suspension, pending additional review, extended to funding for resettlement services within the United States for refugees who had already been admitted.

The plaintiffs comprise individuals who are refugees either previously admitted into the United States or approved for resettlement but still located abroad, along with three organizations that held cooperative agreements with the State Department to conduct overseas processing for refugee status applicants and to provide resettlement assistance within the United States to admitted refugees. In February 2025, these plaintiffs initiated an action in the Western District of Washington, asserting that the suspension of the refugee program under Executive Order 14163 contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (with codification throughout the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and alleging that halting USRAP funding breached multiple sections of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (characterizing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court granted a preliminary injunction from the bench barring enforcement or execution of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The next day, February 26, the government ended every cooperative agreement for providing reception and placement services to refugees in the United States, in addition to terminating all but one cooperative agreement supporting USRAP processing overseas. On February 28, the district court issued a written order to clarify the reach of the February 25 injunction, explicitly barring suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, the court entered a second preliminary injunction directing the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements cancelled following the February 25 decision. The government lodged notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s February 28 order while its appeal was pending, and a motions panel from this court partially granted that request on March 25. Relying on Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the authority granted by 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the basis for Executive Order No. 14163, “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nonetheless, the stay motion was denied “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. We subsequently issued two additional orders clarifying the parameters of this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel ordered comprehensive briefing and instructed the Clerk of Court to place the case on the next available calendar for appeal.

After the briefing but before the argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court entered an order on July 14 establishing a framework to implement the limited carveout and designating a magistrate judge to supervise the process. On July 17, the government submitted an emergency motion seeking enforcement, clarification, or modification of the stay order, which was opposed by the plaintiffs. On July 18, 2025, we entered an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 order of enforcement and set oral argument to proceed on an expedited schedule.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties’ briefs and oral argument, we take the matter under submission. A decision on the merits will be issued in due course. In the meantime, we hereby lift both our administrative stay dated July 18 and the stay entered on March 25, as modified on April 21 and May 9. A new stay is imposed pending the ultimate resolution of this appeal. The district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed in full, save as otherwise specified below.

The criteria for granting a stay are settled. We weigh four considerations: (1) whether the party seeking the stay has demonstrated a strong likelihood of prevailing on the merits; (2) whether, in the absence of a stay, the applicant will suffer irreparable harm; (3) whether issuance of the stay would cause substantial injury to the other parties; and (4) the public interest. See Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Of these, the likelihood of success on the merits and potential for irreparable harm are “the most critical.” Id. The government is likely to succeed regarding the plaintiffs’ challenge to Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions, and the court is not permitted to conduct “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ that the entry of the covered aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” Id. at 685. “[T]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, to block the “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The remaining considerations, affecting both the domestic and international interests of the United States and as assessed within the scope of the President’s “broad discretion,” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84, likewise support the United States.

The government, however, is unlikely to succeed on at least one of the plaintiffs’ claims brought under the Administrative Procedure Act, for reasons that will be detailed in a forthcoming opinion. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, following a refugee’s admission to the United States, the government is obligated to provide specific reception and placement services. Under § 1522(a)(1)(A), the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement, which is housed within the Department of Health and Human Services, must “to the extent of available appropriations”: (i) allocate adequate resources for employment training and placement to promote economic self-sufficiency among refugees expeditiously; (ii) afford refugees access to sufficient English language instruction so that they can resettle effectively and swiftly; (iii) ensure the provision of cash assistance in a manner that does not hinder the economic self-sufficiency of refugees, in accordance with subsection (e)(2); and (iv) guarantee that women have the same opportunities as men to participate in training and instructional activities. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq., which sets forth the scope of federal funding provided for refugee services. In addition, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states regarding unaccompanied refugee minors: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Further, § 1522(e)(7)(A) directs the Secretary of Health and Human Services to “develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed,” with regard to cash and medical aid for newly arrived refugees. For fiscal year 2025, Congress allocated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, as outlined in Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating the funds appropriated in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), though the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6), later reduced this by $800 million. Additionally, § 1523 mandates that the Secretary provide a comprehensive report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

Pursuant to statute, resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be administered by means of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Historically, the United States has utilized cooperative agreements with various resettlement organizations for the provision of these services. The organizational plaintiffs in this matter are three organizations with which the Department of State has previously entered into such cooperative agreements.

As noted earlier, the State Department, in February 2024, ended all cooperative agreements related to the provision of these services. Recognizing this cessation of funding for resettlement services, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nonetheless, at oral argument before the panel, the government indicated that it was not aware of any advancement toward identifying or securing a new agency or agencies capable of offering reception and placement services.

Given the government’s lack of clarity about its ability to fulfill its statutory obligation under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 regarding reception and placement services, the government is hereby ordered to reinstate the necessary cooperative agreements to ensure provision of the reception and placement services specified in § 1522 to refugees admitted into the United States. These services are to encompass the regular and customary assistance previously afforded to such refugees under prior cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President is afforded significant latitude and respect concerning immigration policies, the district court erred by prohibiting the executive branch from enacting the President’s chosen policy restricting the admission of refugees and curtailing services provided to them. Accordingly, I would grant a full stay of the district court’s injunctions.

While my colleagues and I concur that the President possesses the power to pause refugee admissions—warranting a stay of the district court's injunction on that issue—we differ on whether the federal government is obligated by law to continue providing services to recently admitted refugees prior to the commencement of the suspension. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing such a requirement on the government. In my view, however, the language of this provision is better interpreted as a permissive grant of authority rather than a compulsory directive.

To begin, the heading of § 1522 is “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” (emphasis added). The term “authorization” connotes discretion rather than obligation. As defined in Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024), “authorization” is “permission to do something” or an “official document granting such permission.” Therefore, Congress is simply enabling actions through §1522; it is not mandating them.

Moreover, the four distinct subsections within the statute that allocate resources for various refugee services—such as the “Program of initial resettlement” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—consistently state that the “Director is authorized” to provide aid. U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis added). These textual cues again indicate that the executive branch retains discretion over such funding decisions.

In contending that Section 1522 mandates funding, my colleagues refer to §1522(a), titled “Conditions and consideration,” which employs the term “shall” along with the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” Yet, this directive is tempered by the phrase immediately before it: “In providing assistance under this section [i.e., the discretionary funding provisions of §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ” Thus, §1522(a) imposes obligatory “conditions and considerations” only if the government elects to allocate funds for refugee programs. For instance, the Director is directed to “make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [pursuant to §1522(c)’s discretionary funding for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible,” meaning this is a compulsory condition or consideration solely if the government chooses to provide such funding.

To strengthen their assertion of obligatory expenditures, my colleagues rely on subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which can arguably be interpreted as placing certain duties on the Director. However, neither provision convincingly undermines the broader structure of §1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) obligates the program’s Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee children only where this is deemed “necessary.” It further authorizes the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Despite using “shall,” the language confers broad deference upon the executive and does not constitute an unequivocal mandate. Furthermore, the record is silent regarding the presence of unaccompanied refugee children in this particular matter. Likewise, subsection (e)(7)(A) declares that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” cash and medical support projects for refugees “as needed.” The scope and parameters of these projects remain at the discretion of the executive, falling short of a clear congressional requirement.

From my perspective, Section 1522 empowers the executive branch to design programs at its discretion, provided appropriations are available, but does not obligate those programs to be carried out. Since the statute does not mandate the executive to furnish these services, it is not within this court’s purview to compel them. I must therefore respectfully dissent in part.
