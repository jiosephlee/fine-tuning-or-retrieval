Title: Pacito v. Trump  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit  
Date: September 12, 2025  
Docket: Nos. 25-1313 and 25-1939  
Author: Panel order; partial concurrence and partial dissent by Lee, Circuit Judge

This matter was argued before the court on an expedited schedule on September 3, 2025. A formal opinion will be forthcoming. Until then, this Order will serve to stay the proceedings pending resolution of the appeal and further directive from this court. The administrative stay entered on July 18, 2025, is hereby lifted. Our earlier stay granted on March 25, 2025, and later modified on April 21 and May 9, 2025, is also dissolved. The district court’s preliminary injunctions entered on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45) and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 79) are fully stayed except where expressly stated otherwise in this Order. The government’s emergency motion filed July 17, 2025 (Dkt. 108) is denied as moot.

BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed an executive order named “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program.” Exec. Order No. 14163, 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Citing authority under 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States” and instructed that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” until further determinations are made. Exec. Order No. 14163, § 3(a). Refer to 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (“[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Four days later, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all funding for the USRAP program, allegedly under the authority of another executive order, “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This suspension, which was stated to be temporary pending further evaluation, also withheld funding for domestic resettlement services provided to refugees admitted to the United States.

The plaintiffs consist of refugees who were either previously admitted to the United States or who have received approval for resettlement but remain abroad, as well as three organizations holding cooperative agreements with the State Department to conduct overseas processing for refugee applicants and to deliver resettlement services for refugees entering the United States. In February 2025, these plaintiffs initiated legal action in the Western District of Washington, asserting that the suspension of the refugee program under Executive Order 14163 contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified within various sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and that cutting off USRAP funding breached several provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (referring to the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court delivered an oral preliminary injunction preventing the government from enforcing or implementing §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. On February 26, the government ended all cooperative agreements concerning reception and placement services for refugees within the United States, and terminated all but one cooperative agreement related to USRAP processing overseas. On February 28, the district court provided a written order clarifying that the February 25 preliminary injunction also enjoined the suspension of USRAP funding. On March 24, a second preliminary injunction was issued by the district court, directing the State Department to restore any cooperative agreements that had been ended subsequent to the February 25 decision. The government submitted notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s February 28 order pending appeal, and a motions panel of this court partially granted that request on March 25. In doing so, the panel cited Trump v. Hawaii, stating that the power under which the President issued Executive Order No. 14163, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). However, the court denied the stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. Two further orders were issued clarifying this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel then called for full briefing and instructed the Clerk of Court to schedule the appeal for the next available calendar.

After the briefing concluded but before the scheduled argument on September 19, 2025, the district court issued a July 14 order setting forth procedures for enforcing the limited carveout and assigning a magistrate judge to oversee its implementation. On July 17, the government submitted an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or modify the stay order, which the plaintiffs opposed. On July 18, 2025, this court imposed an administrative stay on the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and ordered that oral argument proceed on an expedited timetable.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties’ written submissions and the arguments presented, the matter is now under advisement and will be resolved with an opinion addressing the merits in due time. Meanwhile, we hereby lift both our administrative stay entered on July 18 and the prior stay from March 25, as subsequently modified on April 21 and May 9. A new stay is granted, effective until the conclusion of this appeal. Accordingly, the district court’s orders dated February 28 and March 24, 2025, are stayed in full, with specified exceptions set forth below.

The criteria governing the issuance of a stay are well-settled. The court weighs four considerations: (1) whether the applicant has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of prevailing on the merits; (2) whether the applicant would suffer irreparable harm if a stay were denied; (3) whether issuance of the stay would cause substantial injury to the other interested parties; and (4) the public interest. See Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). Of these, the likelihood of success on the merits and the prospect of irreparable harm are “the most critical.” Id. The government appears poised to succeed against the plaintiffs’ contention that Executive Order No. 14163’s refugee admission suspension is invalid, and judicial review of the President’s underlying justification must be limited. See Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686 (“a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications” is precluded). The only requirement set by § 1182(f) is that the President make a finding that admission of the specified noncitizens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” a standard that is clearly satisfied here. Id. at 685. Furthermore, “[t]he language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority,” id. at 688, including the power to bar “the entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The additional factors, which relate to both national and foreign interests as shaped by the President’s “broad discretion,” likewise weigh in the government’s favor. Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84.

As will be discussed more thoroughly in a forthcoming opinion, the government appears unlikely to succeed with respect to at least one of the plaintiffs’ Administrative Procedure Act claims. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is obligated to deliver certain reception and placement services to refugees once they have entered the United States. Specifically, § 1522(a)(1)(A) mandates that the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—a division of the Department of Health and Human Services—“to the extent of available appropriations”—(i) allocate adequate resources for job training and job placement in order to facilitate refugees’ rapid attainment of economic self-sufficiency, (ii) offer refugees access to sufficient English language instruction to support prompt resettlement, (iii) ensure cash assistance is structured so that it does not act as a deterrent to economic self-reliance, pursuant to subsection (e)(2), and (iv) guarantee equal participation opportunities in training and instruction for women and men. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (outlining the federal funding allotted for refugee-related services). Additionally, regarding unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) states: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Regarding cash and medical support for refugees recently settled in the United States, § 1522(e)(7)(A) provides that the Secretary of Health and Human Services “shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” It should be noted that for fiscal year 2025, Congress allocated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, see Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating funds appropriated in division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), which amount was subsequently reduced by $800 million by the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Under § 1523, the Secretary must also provide a comprehensive report “on activities under this subchapter to the Committees on the Judiciary of the House of Representatives and of the Senate not later than January 31 following the end of each fiscal year.”

Pursuant to statute, these resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be delivered via contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Historically, the United States has utilized cooperative agreements with several resettlement organizations for the provision of these services. The organizational plaintiffs are three entities with which the Department of State has maintained cooperative agreements.

As previously noted, in February 2024, the Department of State terminated all existing cooperative agreements for such services. Upon acknowledging this termination of resettlement funding, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. Nonetheless, at oral argument before the panel, the government indicated that it did not know whether any steps had been taken to procure a new agency or agencies to provide reception and placement services.

Given the government’s uncertainty concerning its current capacity to provide the statutorily required reception and placement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the government is therefore ordered to reinstate the cooperative agreements necessary for delivery of the reception and placement services referenced in § 1522 to refugees who have been granted admission to the United States. These services must comprise the standard and customary support previously provided to such refugees under the now-terminated cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President possesses broad discretion and receives significant judicial deference regarding immigration issues, the district court improperly prohibited the executive branch from carrying out the President's chosen course to restrict the admission of refugees and limit related services. Accordingly, I would suspend the district court’s injunctions in full.

My fellow panel members and I concur that the President is vested with the authority to halt refugee admissions and would therefore stay the district court’s injunction as to that aspect. However, we diverge on whether federal officials are legally obliged to continue providing services to those recently admitted prior to the suspension's enactment. My colleagues construe 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as imposing a statutory requirement on the government to render specified services to refugees. In my judgment, that provision is more appropriately understood as a grant of authority rather than an obligation.

To analyze this, we look at the outset: The heading of § 1522 is “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis in original). The word “Authorization” connotes discretion rather than compulsion. AUTHORIZATION, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining the term as “permission to do something” or the “official document granting such permission”). In enacting § 1522, Congress permits such action; it does not direct it.

Additionally, each of the four distinct statutory sections appropriating funds for refugee services—for example, the “Program of initial resettlement” and the “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees”—specifically provides that the “Director is authorized” to furnish assistance. See U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis in original). These formulations again demonstrate Congress’s intent to vest discretion in the executive branch regarding funding decisions.

In contending that Section 1522 establishes compulsory funding, my colleagues rely on §1522(a)—captioned “Conditions and consideration”—which contains the term “shall” alongside the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this language is modified by the preceding clause: “In providing assistance under this section [referring to the discretionary appropriations set forth in §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ” Thus, §1522(a) sets forth certain mandatory “conditions and considerations” if, and only if, the government elects to allocate funds to refugee programs. By way of illustration, the Director “shall...make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [within the discretionary funding structure of § 1522(c) for ‘Project grants and contracts for services for refugee’] to facilitate economic self-sufficiency for refugees as expeditiously as possible [meaning this requirement is mandatory only upon the government’s decision to provide funding].”

To reinforce their assertion of obligatory expenditures, my colleagues reference subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which arguably set forth certain requirements for the Director. Neither, though, is sufficient to contradict the fundamental structure of §1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) stipulates that the program’s Director must assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee minors solely when such responsibility is “necessary.” This provision also permits the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Even with the command “shall,” the subsection largely defers to executive judgment and lacks any unequivocal directive. Furthermore, it remains uncertain from the existing record whether unaccompanied refugee children are even implicated in this case. In like fashion, subsection (e)(7)(A) provides that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” cash and medical assistance projects for refugees “as needed.” The contours of these programs rest on executive branch determination—scarcely the sort of direct congressional directive at issue.

From my perspective, Section 1522 grants authority to the executive branch to create programs at its discretion, subject to available appropriations. It does not impose any duty for those programs to be maintained. Since the law does not obligate the executive to deliver these services, it is not within our court’s purview to order them. Accordingly, I must respectfully dissent in part.
