The court convened for oral argument in this matter on an accelerated schedule on September 3, 2025. A formal opinion will be issued at a later date. Meanwhile, this Order serves as a stay pending appeal and until further direction from this court. The administrative stay entered on July 18, 2025, is hereby set aside. In addition, the prior stay granted on March 25, 2025, as later amended on April 21 and May 9, 2025, is also vacated. The preliminary injunctions entered by the district court on February 28, 2025 (Dist. Ct. Dkt. 45), and March 24, 2025 (Dist. Ct. BACKGROUND

On January 20, 2025, President Trump signed Executive Order No. 14163, entitled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program,” which was published at 90 Fed. Reg. 8459 (Jan. 30, 2025). Acting under the authority granted by 8 U.S.C. §§ 1182(f) and 1185(a), the President concluded that “entry into the United States of refugees under the [U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP)] would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and ordered that “entry into the United States of refugees under the USRAP be suspended” pending additional findings. Exec. Order. No. 14163, § 3(a). See also 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (“[W]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interest of the United States, he may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate”). Subsequently, on January 24, 2025, the Department of State halted all funding for the USRAP program, referencing a separate executive order, “Reevaluating and Realigning United States Foreign Aid.” Exec. Order No. 14169, 90 Fed. Reg. 8619 (Jan. 20, 2025). This funding pause, pending further evaluation, encompassed support for domestic resettlement services provided to refugees already admitted into the United States.

The plaintiffs in this case consist of refugees who had either already been admitted to the United States or who had obtained approval for resettlement but remained outside the country, as well as three organizations that held cooperative agreements with the State Department to facilitate overseas processing for individuals seeking refugee status and to deliver resettlement services to refugees admitted to the United States. In February 2025, these plaintiffs initiated a lawsuit in the Western District of Washington, contending that the suspension of the refugee program under Executive Order 14163 contravened the Refugee Act of 1980, Pub. L. No. 96-212, 94 Stat. 102 (codified in multiple sections of the Immigration and Nationality Act, 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101 et seq.), and that the termination of funding for USRAP violated various sections of the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C. § 551 et seq. See Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511, 520 (2009) (characterizing the Refugee Act as “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons”). On February 25, the district court issued an oral preliminary injunction that barred enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of Executive Order No. 14163. The next day, February 26, the government ended all cooperative agreements for providing reception and placement assistance to refugees in the United States, along with all but one agreement for supporting USRAP processing overseas. On February 28, the district court released a written order clarifying the parameters of the February 25 preliminary injunction, explicitly stating it also enjoined the suspension of USRAP funding. Subsequently, on March 24, the district court granted a second preliminary injunction directing the State Department to restore all cooperative agreements that had been terminated after the February 25 decision. The government lodged notices of appeal on February 28 and March 25, 2025.

The government requested a stay of the district court’s February 28 order while its appeal was pending; on March 25, a motions panel from this court partially granted that request. Relying on Trump v. Hawaii, we noted that the President’s authority under Executive Order No. 14163, pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), “‘exudes deference’ to the President and ‘vests [him] with ample power to impose entry restrictions in addition to those elsewhere enumerated in the [Immigration and Nationality Act].’” Order of March 25, 2025, at 2 (quoting 585 U.S. 667, 684 (2018)). Nonetheless, we denied the stay “to the extent the district court’s preliminary injunction order applies to individuals who were conditionally approved for refugee status by the United States Citizenship and Immigration Services before January 20, 2025.” Id. at 1. We later issued two orders to clarify this “limited carveout from the stay.” Order of May 9, 2025, at 1; Order of April 21, 2025. The motions panel directed the parties to submit full briefing and ordered the Clerk of Court to set the appeal on the next available calendar.

After briefing but prior to the oral argument set for September 19, 2025, the district court entered a July 14 order establishing procedures to implement the limited carveout and appointed a magistrate judge to supervise this process. On July 17, the government submitted an emergency motion to enforce, clarify, or amend the prior stay order, which plaintiffs opposed. On July 18, 2025, we entered an administrative stay of the district court’s July 14 enforcement order and expedited the scheduling of oral argument.

DISCUSSION

After considering the parties’ submissions and oral arguments, the matter is now under advisement. A substantive opinion will be forthcoming. Meanwhile, we hereby lift our administrative stay from July 18, as well as our previous stay from March 25, as modified on April 21 and May 9. A new stay is issued, effective until the appeal is ultimately resolved. All provisions of the district court’s February 28 and March 24, 2025 orders are stayed in full, except where specified otherwise below.

The criteria for granting a stay are long established. We assess four considerations: (1) whether the party requesting the stay has demonstrated a substantial likelihood of prevailing on the merits; (2) whether, without a stay, the applicant would sustain irreparable harm; (3) the extent to which granting the stay would cause significant injury to other interested parties; and (4) the public interest. Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). The most significant considerations are the first two—likelihood of success on the merits and risk of irreparable injury. Id. The government appears likely to succeed in defending Executive Order No. 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions against plaintiffs’ challenge. Further, the judiciary is not to “undertake a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686. “Section 1182(f) requires only that the President ‘find[]’ the entry of certain aliens ‘would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.’ That standard has plainly been met in this instance.” Id. at 685. “[T]he language of § 1182(f) is unequivocal, and the [Executive Order] remains within the express textual limits of the President’s authority,” id. at 688, to bar “entry of . . . any class of aliens into the United States,” U.S.C. § 1182(f). The other relevant considerations—addressing both domestic and international United States interests, informed by the President’s “broad discretion,” Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 683–84—also weigh in favor of the United States.

The reasons for this determination will be addressed thoroughly in a forthcoming opinion; nonetheless, the government does not appear likely to succeed on at least one of plaintiffs’ claims brought under the APA. Pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1522, following refugee admission to the United States, the government is obligated to provide certain reception and placement services. According to § 1522(a)(1)(A), the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement—a unit within the Department of Health and Human Services—must, "to the extent of available appropriations": (i) allocate adequate resources toward employment training and placement, aimed at fostering economic self-sufficiency among refugees as quickly as possible; (ii) ensure access for refugees to adequate English language instruction to promote effective and prompt resettlement; (iii) guarantee the provision of cash assistance in a manner that does not deter refugees’ economic self-sufficiency, consistent with subsection (e)(2); and (iv) ensure that women receive equal opportunities alongside men to participate in training and instruction. See also 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq. (regarding federal funding for refugee programs). Regarding unaccompanied minor refugees, § 1522(d)(2)(B) stipulates: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care” (emphasis in original). Concerning cash and medical assistance for newly resettled refugees, § 1522(e)(7)(A) mandates that the Secretary of Health and Human Services “shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty-six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” For fiscal year 2025, Congress allocated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, see Pub. L. No. 119-4 § 1101(a)(11) (incorporating appropriations from division F of Pub. L. No. 118-147), which was subsequently reduced by $800 million pursuant to the Rescissions Act of 2025, Pub. L. No. 119-28, § 2(b)(6). Additionally, § 1523 imposes a reporting requirement on the Secretary, obligating a comprehensive report detailing activities under this subchapter to be submitted to the House and Senate Judiciary Committees no later than January 31 after the close of each fiscal year.

According to statute, these resettlement services—commonly known as “reception and placement” services—may be administered via contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with “public or private nonprofit agencies.” U.S.C. § 1522(b)(1)(A)(ii). Historically, the United States has entered into cooperative agreements with numerous resettlement organizations to deliver these services. The plaintiff organizations in this action are three such entities with which the Department of State maintained cooperative agreements.

As previously noted, in February 2024, the State Department terminated all such cooperative agreements for providing these services. In recognition of this cessation of funding, the government informed the district court on March 10, 2025, that it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency that could provide reception and placement services.” Dist. Ct. Dkt. 62, at 5. However, at oral argument, the government indicated to the panel that it could not confirm whether any progress had occurred towards engaging a new agency or agencies to furnish reception and placement services.

Given the government's lack of clarity concerning its ability to deliver the reception and placement services that 8 U.S.C. § 1522 mandates, the government is ordered to reestablish the cooperative agreements required to supply the reception and placement services set forth in § 1522 for refugees admitted to the United States. These services must include the standard services that had previously been provided to such refugees pursuant to earlier cooperative agreements.

LEE, Circuit Judge, concurring-in-part and dissenting-in-part.

Although the President holds broad authority and is generally granted significant deference regarding immigration issues, the district court erred when it prohibited the executive branch from carrying out the President’s policy choices to restrict the admission of refugees and the provision of services to them. Accordingly, I would grant a stay of the district court's injunctions in full.

My fellow panel members and I concur that the President is empowered to temporarily halt refugee admissions and therefore agree to stay the portion of the district court's injunction addressing that subject. However, we disagree as to whether federal law obligates the government to provide services to refugees admitted prior to the suspension's effective date. My colleagues read 8 U.S.C. § 1522 as establishing a requirement for the government to extend certain services to refugees. In my view, the statute is best understood as a grant of authority, not as an imposition of duty.

To begin, the heading of § 1522 is “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” (emphasis added). The term “authorization” indicates a permissive, rather than mandatory, scope. See AUTHORIZATION, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) (defining it as “permission to do something” or the “official document granting such permission”). Therefore, Congress, through § 1522, permits such action; it does not mandate it.

Moreover, the statute contains four subsections addressing funding for various types of refugee services—such as “Program of initial resettlement” and “Cash assistance and medical assistance to refugees.” Each states that the “Director is authorized” to provide such assistance. See U.S.C. §1522(b)-(e) (emphasis added). Once again, this language in the statute signals that the executive branch retains discretion regarding funding.

In support of their assertion that Section 1522 compels funding, my colleagues refer to §1522(a)—captioned “Conditions and consideration”—which employs the term “shall” and includes the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” However, this language is restricted by the phrase immediately preceding it: “In providing assistance under this section [i.e., referring to the discretionary funding mechanisms of §1522(b)-(e)]. . . ”. Essentially, §1522(a) imposes certain mandatory “conditions and considerations” only in the event that the government opts to finance refugee programs. For instance, the Director “shall. . . make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement [according to § 1522(c)’s discretionary grants for “Project grants and contracts for services for refugee”] in order to achieve economic self-sufficiency among refugees as expeditiously as possible [i.e., this is the requisite condition or factor that becomes operative if the program receives funding].”

To further support the contention of compulsory expenditures, my colleagues point to subsections (d)(2)(B) and (e)(7)(A), which might be interpreted as placing certain requirements upon the Director. Nevertheless, neither provision compellingly challenges the predominant discretionary scheme of § 1522. Subsection (d)(2)(B) obligates the program’s Director to assume legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied refugee minors solely if such responsibility is “necessary.” The section likewise empowers the Director “to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Even with the directive word “shall,” the subsection conveys substantial deference to executive discretion and imposes no strict directive. Furthermore, the record does not clarify whether any unaccompanied refugee children are involved in the present case. In like fashion, subsection (e)(7)(A) provides that the “Secretary shall develop and implement” initiatives offering cash and medical support to refugees “as needed.” The structure and scope of such initiatives remain subject to the executive’s judgment—far from a definitive legislative command.

From my perspective, Section 1522 permits the executive to design and establish programs as it deems appropriate so long as appropriated funds exist. The statute does not impose an obligation that such programs actually be run. Because the executive is not statutorily compelled to offer these services, this court lacks grounds to direct their provision. Accordingly, I respectfully dissent in part.
