Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana statute regarding the termination of insurance coverage for failure to pay an entity that financed the premiums. The appellants, who are former policyholders, assert that the insurer’s process for cancellation failed to adhere to the statute, which allegedly led to misrepresentations and violated the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no breach of the statute had occurred and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, were previously insured through GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto—who is not a party to this suit—was involved in a collision with plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the GoAuto insured implicated in Jenkins’s accident each used American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”) to finance their GoAuto insurance premiums. Under the financing arrangement, APAC paid the entire premium to GoAuto and the insured parties repaid APAC through monthly installments. Each of these finance agreements included a provision granting APAC a power of attorney, which permitted APAC to cancel the insurance policy if the insured failed to make premium payments. All three policies were ultimately cancelled for nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins brought a class action in a Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, contending that GoAuto had not validly cancelled their policies and had breached its duty of good faith. When the complaint was later amended, Wright joined as an additional plaintiff. The action was then removed by GoAuto to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides filed motions for summary judgment limited to the question of whether GoAuto’s cancellation process for the financed policies met the requirements of Louisiana law. The district court ruled in GoAuto’s favor on summary judgment, determining that GoAuto had followed the procedures mandated by state law, and accordingly dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently appealed that decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by: (1) determining that the statutory requirements regarding delivery of the cancellation notice were satisfied; (2) concluding that GoAuto properly received the requests for cancellation; and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. In addressing these contentions, we will first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes governing cancellation of financed insurance policies, and then assess whether GoAuto’s procedures conformed to those statutes. Louisiana law provides that an insurance-premium finance company may initiate cancellation of an insurance policy after an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following conditions: (i) The premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as set out in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) The premium finance agreement is in default and that default remains uncured; (iii) Upon default, a notice of cancellation was provided to the insured in accordance with Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, with the date of such transmission specified by the premium finance company; and (iv) Copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all individuals or entities with an interest in the covered property as indicated by the premium finance agreement, specifying the names and addresses of any governmental bodies, parties with a security interest in the insured property, or other third parties to whom the insurance premium finance company sent the notice of cancellation. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement has gone into default, and that default has not been remedied within the allowed period. (3) Following default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured. Copies of these notices are attached, and the affidavit verifying mailing is available upon request. (4) All parties listed in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement also received copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

When that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system processes the cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later the same morning after cancellation, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation papers. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually deliver the documents to a filing cabinet used jointly by both GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the executed cancellation document is instead given to a GoAuto employee, who marks the document as “received” before placing it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We must now assess whether these procedures satisfy the requirements of Louisiana law as previously examined. Central to Plaintiffs’ contention is the assertion that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until shortly after, but definitively following, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs interpret the statute’s mandate that the premium insurer “certify” as necessitating a signature prior to any effective cancellation. However, La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a) does not explicitly mention a signature requirement. If such a requirement exists, it would have to be derived from the meaning of “certifying.” Except as otherwise noted below, no Louisiana case reviewing the practices of insurance-premium finance companies under the statute has specifically addressed the necessity of a signature. One appellate decision summarized the statutory directive as calling for “a statement certifying compliance with” the law. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. In another case, the court permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional cases provide little further guidance on the issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, the absence of a signature was noted as a deficiency in the notice, observing that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court there did not rule that lacking a signature would automatically render the cancellation invalid if the other statutory requirements were satisfied. Here, as in Benitez, the notices lack both a printed name and a signature identifying a specific employee. Unlike Benitez, though, the notices in this case do “certify” the required information. In the absence of more specific guidance from Louisiana courts, we perceive no impediment to a company certifying information without indicating a particular employee’s identity. The relevant statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a), directs that “the insurance premium finance company” must effect cancellation by “certifying” specified details in a notice. The call for “strict adherence” to the cancellation process does not authorize courts to supplement statutory requirements. Therefore, we hold that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on a premium finance company’s notice to the insurer. 

Plaintiffs also argue that GoAuto’s process falls short of the statutory command because GoAuto never physically receives a cancellation request. The law requires, “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, the physical repository for printed requests was a filing area belonging to GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself. Since 2017, Plaintiffs assert, GoAuto lacks possession of the requests because the task of affixing the date stamp is performed by an APAC employee. They maintain that the use of “receipt” by the legislature, rather than only requiring that the request be transmitted, necessitates that GoAuto actually possess the notice before policy cancellation. Neither the Louisiana Supreme Court nor the statute defines “receipt.” Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Relying on these definitions, we determine that GoAuto is authorized to cancel a policy after obtaining possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As discussed previously, when GoAuto’s computer system receives the e-mailed notice in the early morning, that system proceeds to cancel the policy. A GoAuto deponent detailed the interaction between the APAC computer system and a GoAuto web service as follows: during cancellation, “the APAC process . . . makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system processes the cancellation. Subsequently, at that time, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents. To recap our earlier citation of the statute: prior to cancellation, it is required that the insurer has “receipt” of a cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute, being contemporary, reflects modern practices where company computers perform many tasks formerly done by people. The approach used by GoAuto to receive the notice and act upon it fulfills the statutory requirements. Additionally, the district court determined that GoAuto’s procedures adhered to the statute for a separate reason, stating that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed a lack of Louisiana case law interpreting “private carrier” and instead found persuasive the reasoning of a panel from this court regarding circumstances under which a railroad constitutes a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was inventive and outside the conventional framework. Since we hold that the email meets the statutory requirement, we need not address the merits of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we submit these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court for certification. They argue that determining the correct meaning of these statutory terms is significant for Louisiana's insurance regulatory framework and industry. However, “as a general rule, we are reluctant to certify legal questions unless there is a strong justification; certification is such a valuable tool that we hesitate to overuse it and thereby jeopardize future access.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a term has yet to be construed by the state's highest court “does not in itself justify certification.” Id. Therefore, we refuse to certify this question. Since GoAuto’s processes adhere to the statutory requirements, the district court properly denied summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
