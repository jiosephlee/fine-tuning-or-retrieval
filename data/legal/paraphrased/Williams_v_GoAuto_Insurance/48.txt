Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we must interpret a Louisiana statute related to the termination of insurance coverage when insured individuals fail to pay their premium finance company. The appellants, who are former policyholders, assert that the insurance provider’s process for cancelling their policies contravened the statutory requirements, and allege this resulted in both misrepresentations and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that there was no breach of the statute and dismissed all of the plaintiffs’ claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another GoAuto policyholder, not a litigant in this action, was involved in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, a plaintiff. Williams, Wright, and the driver from Jenkins’s accident all used American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”) to finance their GoAuto insurance premiums. Under these arrangements, APAC paid the full premium to GoAuto while the insureds agreed to repay APAC in monthly installments. The agreements with APAC included a provision granting APAC a power of attorney, enabling APAC to cancel the insurance policies in the event of nonpayment. Each of the relevant policies was ultimately cancelled due to nonpayment of premiums. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action lawsuit against GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, asserting that GoAuto had ineffectively cancelled their policies and breached its duty of good faith. After the complaint was amended, Wright was added as a plaintiff. The case was removed to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana by GoAuto. Both sides moved for summary judgment on the narrow issue of whether GoAuto’s process for cancelling policies financed through APAC complied with Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in GoAuto’s favor, determining that its cancellation procedures were lawful under state law, and dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by: (1) concluding that the statutory requirements for delivering a notice of cancellation were satisfied; (2) determining that GoAuto appropriately received the cancellation requests; and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. In order to consider these claims, we first outline the relevant provisions of Louisiana law governing the cancellation of financed insurance policies, followed by an examination of the procedures used by GoAuto to comply with these statutes. According to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company is authorized to request the cancellation of an insurance policy when a default has not been remedied, by either sending a copy of the notice of cancellation and a certification statement to the insurer through mail, private carrier, or electronic mail. The certification must state the following: (i) The premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as described in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) The agreement is in default, and this default has not been timely resolved; (iii) After default, the insured received a notice of cancellation as described in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, specifying the date the premium finance company sent this notice to the insured; and (iv) All persons listed as having an interest under the premium finance agreement were sent copies of the cancellation notice, identifying the names and addresses of governmental agencies, security interest holders in the insured property, or other third parties to whom such notice was sent by the insurance premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) There exists a default under the terms of the Premium Finance Agreement, which remains uncured within the prescribed time. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued and sent to the insured; copies of these notices are enclosed, and the affidavit of mailing can be provided upon request. (4) The Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was also delivered to all parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss arising under it.

Upon transmission of the notice, GoAuto’s computer system processes the cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later that morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation notice. Before 2017, an APAC employee would personally deliver these documents to a filing cabinet jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the signed cancellation notice is instead hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who stamps the document as “received” and then files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We now examine whether these procedures align with the Louisiana law previously discussed. Central to the Plaintiffs’ position is the absence of a signature on APAC’s certification until shortly after, but definitely following, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs contend that the statutory mandate for “certifying” by the premium insurer requires a signature to be present before cancellation may occur. The statute, however, does not expressly mention a signature. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be necessary, that requirement must be derived from the interpretation of “certifying.” With one exception that will be addressed, no Louisiana court considering the practices of insurance-premium finance companies under the statute has decided whether a signature is required. One court characterized the statutory obligation as needing “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another court permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional cases provide equally little guidance on this point. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, the lack of a signature was noted among deficiencies in the notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court in Benitez did not conclude that merely omitting a signature would invalidate the cancellation notice if other requirements were met. Similar to the facts in Benitez, the notices found in this record likewise fail to name a particular employee, either by printed name or signature. However, distinct from Benitez, each notice here “certifies” the necessary information. In the absence of more explicit Louisiana case law, we discern no basis to find that a business entity may not certify such information even if an employee is not individually identified. The statutory language itself specifies that it is “the insurance premium finance company” that carries out cancellation by “certifying” certain details in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Insistence on “strict adherence” to the cancellation procedures does not authorize courts to supplement statutory requirements. Accordingly, we find that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the cancellation notice transmitted by the premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s process fails to comply strictly with the statute because GoAuto does not physically receive a cancellation request. The relevant statutory provision states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, the filing space where paper requests were kept belonged to GoAuto Management Services, rather than GoAuto itself. Since 2017, Plaintiffs assert, GoAuto does not obtain the requests because the employee affixing the relevant stamp works for APAC. Plaintiffs contend that the legislature’s selection of the word “receipt”—rather than requiring only transmission of the request—imposes an obligation on GoAuto to actually possess the notice prior to effecting cancellation.

The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted “receipt” as it appears in the statute, nor is there a statutory definition provided. According to Black’s Law Dictionary, “receipt” means “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” is defined as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Using these definitions, we determine that GoAuto is authorized to cancel a policy upon acquiring possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As previously discussed, after the notice is e-mailed and received by GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning, the system effectuates the cancellation. A GoAuto deponent described the manner in which the APAC system interfaces with a GoAuto web service: for example, throughout the cancellation procedure, the APAC system “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date.” The deponent further explained that “a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

Human involvement occurs only later in the morning, once the GoAuto computer system has processed the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. We recapitulate our earlier quotation of the statutory language: to effect cancellation, the insurer must first have “receipt” of the cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The statute reflects contemporary circumstances, where tasks formerly performed by individuals are now often handled by corporate computer systems. GoAuto’s process for receiving and responding to cancellation notices fulfills the statutory requirements. It is worth noting that the district court determined GoAuto’s cancellation procedures met the statutory standard for a different reason, stating, “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged there was no Louisiana case law interpreting “private carrier,” and drew an analogy from this court’s panel’s description of when a railroad qualified as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s construction of “private carrier” was both creative and atypical. Since we conclude that the e-mail meets the requirements of the statute, we do not address whether the district court’s definition was correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we submit these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. They argue that correctly interpreting the statutory language has significant implications for both Louisiana’s insurance industry and its statutory framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a state supreme court has not yet interpreted a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Consequently, we deny certification of the question. Since GoAuto’s methods are consistent with the statute, the district court correctly denied the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
