Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

This case calls for interpretation of a Louisiana statute related to the cancellation of insurance policies for nonpayment to the premium finance company. The former policyholders contend that the insurance company’s cancellation process did not meet the statute’s requirements, and that this led to misrepresentations as well as a breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no statutory infraction occurred and dismissed every claim with prejudice. We AFFIRM the judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this suit, was involved in a collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the driver from the Jenkins accident each arranged to finance their GoAuto insurance premiums through contracts with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these agreements, APAC would pay the full insurance premium to GoAuto, and the insureds, in turn, made monthly installment payments to APAC. Each financing contract also appointed APAC as attorney-in-fact, thereby granting APAC authority to cancel the insurance policy if premiums were not paid as agreed. Nonpayment led to the cancellation of each policy in question. Williams and Jenkins commenced a class action lawsuit in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto failed to effectively terminate their insurance coverage and acted in bad faith. Wright was subsequently added as a plaintiff via an amended pleading. GoAuto removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides submitted motions for summary judgment, focusing exclusively on whether GoAuto’s method of cancelling financed policies was compliant with Louisiana statutes. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, finding that its cancellation procedures conformed with state requirements, and accordingly dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright then filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court committed errors by (1) concluding that the statutory requirements for providing notice of cancellation were met, (2) determining that GoAuto correctly received the cancellation requests, and (3) denying their motion for partial summary judgment. In order to evaluate these claims, we first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes that govern the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium finance agreements, and then consider the steps GoAuto took to satisfy those statutory mandates. Pursuant to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company is authorized to initiate the cancellation of a policy following an uncured default by “sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” several elements: (i) the premium finance agreement incorporates a valid power of attorney under Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) the agreement is in default and such default remains uncured; (iii) upon default, notice of cancellation was dispatched to the insured according to the process in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the specific date it was sent to the insured by the finance company; and (iv) copies of the notice of cancellation were transmitted to all individuals with an interest in any potential loss as identified in the finance agreement, listing the names and addresses of any relevant governmental agencies, security interest holders, or third parties notified by the finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes a lawful power of attorney. (2) There exists a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and this default has not been cured within the designated period. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit evidencing mailing is available upon request. (4) All parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as holding an interest in potential losses have been sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

When that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system effectuates cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later on the same morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the cancellation documents after they have been printed. Prior to 2017, an APAC employee would manually place these documents into a filing cabinet jointly used by both GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the signed cancellation document is instead physically delivered to a GoAuto employee, who then stamps it as “received” and files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We must now determine whether these procedures align with the Louisiana statute previously analyzed. Central to Plaintiffs’ position is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until after, and indisputably subsequent to, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs construe the statutory mandate that the premium insurer “certify” as necessitating a signature prior to effectuating cancellation. However, the statute itself does not expressly demand a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be required, such an obligation must be inferred from the term “certifying.” Except for a forthcoming exception, no Louisiana case reviewing insurance-premium finance company practices under the statute has directly confronted whether a signature is obligatory. One appellate court summarized the statutory obligation as necessitating “a statement certifying compliance with” the relevant provision. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another permitted cancellation to occur “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional cases are similarly unilluminating with respect to the signature issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate court opinion, the lack of a signature was addressed among the deficiencies of a particular notice: the court observed that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court did not determine that a missing signature, in isolation, invalidates a cancellation so long as the other statutory components are satisfied. In this matter, similar to Benitez, the cancellation notices in evidence also fail to list any specific employee by printed name or by signature. Distinctly, though, every notice in the present case does “certify” the necessary information. Given the absence of more definitive authority in Louisiana, there appears to be no prohibition against a company certifying information without naming a specific employee. The statute in question expressly provides that “the insurance premium finance company” effects cancellation by “certifying” certain information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The mandate for “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not authorize courts to impose requirements not set out in the statute itself. Therefore, we determine that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the notice sent by a premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto failed to strictly comply with statutory procedures since it never received a cancellation request. The statute specifies: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, the joint filing room where printed requests were kept belonged to GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto. Plaintiffs claim that, since 2017, GoAuto itself has not received such requests because the individual applying the stamp is an APAC employee. They interpret the legislature's use of “receipt,” rather than mere transmittal, to mean that GoAuto must actually take physical possession of the notice prior to canceling the policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted the meaning of “receipt” as used in this statutory context, and no definition is provided within the statute. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Relying on these definitions, we hold that GoAuto may effectuate cancellation upon obtaining possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation. As previously discussed, when the e-mailed notice reaches GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning hours, that system cancels the policy. A deponent from GoAuto explained the interface between the APAC computer system and a GoAuto web service as follows: During the cancellation process, the APAC system “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system processes the cancellation. At that point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. To restate the statute previously quoted: before a cancellation can occur, the insurer must have received notice of cancellation from the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is modern and reflects contemporary practices, wherein tasks once performed by individuals are now frequently carried out by corporate computer systems. GoAuto’s process of receiving and responding to the notice aligns with the statute’s requirements. It should be noted that the district court determined GoAuto’s cancellation processes conformed to the statutory mandate on an alternative basis, stating that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed that there is no Louisiana case law interpreting “private carrier,” and analogized from a Fifth Circuit panel’s discussion concerning when a railroad is considered a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s characterization of “private carrier” was notably creative and nontraditional. Since our conclusion rests on the sufficiency of the e-mail under the statute, we decline to address the correctness of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we submit these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. They argue that resolving the correct meaning of these statutory terms has significant implications for Louisiana’s insurance regulatory framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). Merely because a state supreme court has not previously construed a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore decline to certify the question. Since GoAuto followed the requirements of the statute, the district court properly denied the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
