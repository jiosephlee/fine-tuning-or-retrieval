Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are asked to interpret a Louisiana statute relating to the cancellation of insurance policies for the nonpayment of premiums to the premium finance company. The appellants, who are former policyholders, allege that the insurer failed to adhere to the statutory requirements when cancelling their policies, and that this led to misrepresentations as well as violations of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that there was no breach of the statute and dismissed all causes of action with prejudice. We AFFIRM the district court’s judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this litigation, was involved in an automobile accident with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the individual involved in Jenkins’s collision each entered into a premium financing agreement with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”) to finance their GoAuto insurance policies. Under these agreements, APAC paid the full insurance premium directly to GoAuto and, in exchange, the insureds were required to make monthly installment payments to APAC. Each financing contract included a provision granting APAC power of attorney to cancel the respective insurance policy should the insured default on payment. Ultimately, all three insurance policies were cancelled for failure to pay premiums.

Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action suit in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, contending that GoAuto had not properly cancelled their insurance coverage and had breached its duty of good faith. Subsequently, after the complaint was amended, Wright joined as an additional plaintiff. GoAuto then removed the lawsuit to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both parties proceeded to submit summary judgment motions, addressing only whether the manner in which GoAuto cancelled financed policies was consistent with the requirements of Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in GoAuto’s favor, finding its cancellation procedures adhered to state law. Accordingly, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by (1) concluding that the statutory requirements for delivering the cancellation notice were met, (2) determining that GoAuto correctly received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To resolve these points, we first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes governing cancellation of financed insurance policies before reviewing the procedures GoAuto followed to comply. Louisiana law provides that an insurance-premium finance company may initiate cancellation of an insurance policy following an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following: (i) the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as outlined in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection, (ii) the agreement is in default and such default has not been cured in a timely manner, (iii) following default, a notice of cancellation was issued to the insured in compliance with Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, with the date of mailing by the finance company to the insured specified, (iv) copies of the notice of cancellation were distributed to every individual identified in the premium finance agreement as holding an interest in any potential loss, specifying the names and addresses of any government agencies, holders of a security interest in the insured property, or third parties to whom the finance company has dispatched the notice of cancellation. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement is currently in default, and that default has not been remedied within the required time period. (3) After the occurrence of a default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was mailed to the insured. Copies of these notices are attached, and affidavit evidence of mailing can be provided upon request. (4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were also mailed to every individual identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss covered by the agreement.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto's computer system processes a cancellation of the policy. After cancellation is complete that morning, an APAC employee uses a signature stamp on the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would physically deliver the paperwork to a filing cabinet that both GoAuto and APAC utilized. Beginning in 2017, the signed cancellation document is instead hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who stamps it as “received” and subsequently stores it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We now turn to the issue of whether these procedures are consistent with the requirements of the Louisiana statute previously discussed. Plaintiffs focus their argument on the absence of a signature on APAC’s certification until after, and indeed only after, the policy has already been automatically canceled. Plaintiffs interpret the statutory mandate of “certifying” by the premium insurer as necessitating a signature prior to effecting cancellation. However, the statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a), does not specifically mention a signature. If a signature were mandated, this requirement would need to be inferred from the use of the word “certifying.” Except as noted in one instance which will be discussed, Louisiana courts examining premium finance company procedures under this statute have not squarely addressed whether a signature is necessary. One appellate court summarized the statute’s requirement as “a statement certifying compliance with” its provisions. See KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision allowed cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional cases provide little further clarification. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

A court of appeal once referenced the absence of a signature when reviewing defects in a notice, stating that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court stopped short of ruling that a missing signature would automatically invalidate a cancellation so long as other requirements were met. Similarly to Benitez, the notices in the present case do not include the printed name or signature of any employee. In contrast with Benitez, the current notices do “certify” the necessary information. Absent more direct Louisiana precedent, we find no justification to prevent a company from certifying facts without specifying or naming the employee responsible. The statute expressly provides that it is “the insurance premium finance company” itself that accomplishes cancellation by “certifying” specific details in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Interpreting “strict adherence” to statutory cancellation requirements does not allow courts to impose additional mandates. Thus, we determine that Louisiana law does not require a signature from the premium finance company on its notice to the insurer.

Plaintiffs also argue that GoAuto’s process is not in strict compliance with the statute because GoAuto never actually receives a request for cancellation. The statute states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Prior to 2017, the location where printed cancellation requests were stored was operated by GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself. From 2017 forward, according to Plaintiffs, GoAuto does not receive such requests because it is an APAC employee who places the stamp. Plaintiffs point to the legislature’s use of the word “receipt”—rather than merely requiring the request to be sent—as evidence that GoAuto must take actual possession of the notice before cancelling coverage. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted “receipt” under this statute, and there is no statutory definition provided. According to Black’s Law Dictionary, “receipt” means “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” means “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Applying these definitions, we hold that GoAuto becomes authorized to cancel a policy once it takes possession of, is delivered, or otherwise gains custody of a cancellation request. As previously discussed, GoAuto’s computer system receives the e-mailed notice early in the morning and executes the policy cancellation. One GoAuto deponent described the interaction between an APAC computer system and GoAuto’s web service, explaining: During the cancellation process, APAC’s process sends a request to a GoAuto web service to either request or effectuate cancellation for a policy slated for cancellation on that date. In practical terms, the web service acts as a listener: it remains open, listening for calls, and when called upon, it asks for further instruction, and if it understands the command, it executes the desired task.

No human intervention occurs until later in the day, after the GoAuto computer system has processed the cancellation. At that subsequent stage, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the hard copies of the cancellation notices. To restate our prior summary of the statutory provision, what must happen before cancellation is that the insurer has received a notice of cancellation sent by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary in nature, reflecting an era when company computer systems perform many functions that once required personal involvement. The method employed by GoAuto to receive and respond to the notice complies with the statutory requirement. We note that the district court determined that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures satisfied the statute by a different rationale: it concluded that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged that there is no Louisiana precedent interpreting the term “private carrier” and looked to analogous reasoning from this court’s panel regarding when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s construction of “private carrier” was notably inventive and atypical. Since we conclude that the e-mail communication fulfills the statutory requirement, we do not address the soundness of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask us to refer these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that a proper reading of the statutory terms holds significant implications for Louisiana’s insurance regulatory framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). Merely because a state supreme court has not previously addressed or defined a given term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. For these reasons, we decline the request to certify. Since GoAuto’s methods align with statutory requirements, the district court correctly denied the Plaintiffs’ summary judgment motion. AFFIRMED.
