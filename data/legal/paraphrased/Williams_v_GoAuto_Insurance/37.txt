Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

This case presents the issue of interpreting a Louisiana statute that addresses policy cancellation due to nonpayment to the entity that provided premium financing. The appellants, who were previously insured, argue that the insurance company failed to follow the statutory procedures for cancellation, thereby making false representations and breaching the duties of good faith and fair dealing. The district court found that there had been no violation of the statute and dismissed all of the plaintiffs’ claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, both previously insured by GoAuto Insurance Company, bring this action as former insureds. Another GoAuto policyholder, who is not involved in this lawsuit, was in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, another plaintiff. Williams, Wright, and the individual connected to Jenkins’s accident all obtained their GoAuto policies through premium financing agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these arrangements, APAC paid GoAuto the full premium, while the insureds agreed to make monthly payments to APAC. Each agreement included a power of attorney provision permitting APAC to cancel the related insurance policy if premiums were not paid. Ultimately, each of these insurance policies was cancelled due to nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action against GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, asserting that GoAuto failed to properly cancel their policies and did not act in good faith. Wright was later joined as a plaintiff by an amended complaint. The case was then removed by GoAuto to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Subsequently, both sides moved for summary judgment on the narrow issue of whether GoAuto’s method for cancelling these financed policies complied with Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, concluding that the procedures used met the requirements of Louisiana law. Consequently, the court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright have appealed that decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by (1) concluding that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) determining that GoAuto properly received the cancellation requests, and (3) refusing to grant their motion for partial summary judgment. To evaluate these claims, we first summarize Louisiana's statutory framework governing the cancellation of financed insurance policies, and then assess GoAuto’s methods of complying with those statutes. In Louisiana, an insurance-premium finance company is permitted to initiate cancellation of an insurance policy following an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following: (i) the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as required by Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) the agreement is in default, and the default has not been cured within the prescribed period; (iii) after default, a notice of cancellation was dispatched to the insured as described in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, including the date it was sent by the premium finance company; and (iv) all interested parties listed in the premium finance agreement, including governmental agencies, security interest holders, or third parties, received copies of the notice of cancellation, with their names and addresses specified, as required. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement is presently in default, and that default has not been remedied within the required time frame. (3) Upon default, the insured was provided with a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit attesting to their mailing is available upon request. (4) All parties listed in the Premium Finance Agreement as having a stake in any potential loss were also sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system processes the policy cancellation. Later on the same morning the cancellation occurs, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually place the documents in a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. Starting in 2017, a GoAuto employee, after receiving the signed cancellation document, stamps it as “received” and then files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn now to the issue of whether these procedures are consistent with the Louisiana statute previously addressed. The Plaintiffs’ main contention is that APAC’s certification does not bear a signature until shortly after, but distinctly following, the point at which the policy is automatically canceled. Plaintiffs argue that the statutory mandate for "certifying" by the premium insurer necessitates a signature prior to cancellation taking effect. However, the statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a), does not expressly mandate a signature. If such a requirement exists, it must be implied solely from the term "certifying." Aside from an exception that will be analyzed further, Louisiana courts examining premium finance companies’ practices in light of this statute have generally not ruled on whether a signature is necessary. One decision summarized the statutory obligation as requiring "a statement certifying compliance with" the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another opinion permitted cancellation "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company." Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Further decisions have similarly failed to directly resolve this specific issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, the absence of a signature was noted among that notice’s defects: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not rule that lack of a signature, by itself, would invalidate a cancellation notice provided other requirements were fulfilled. Much like in Benitez, the notices at issue here fail to identify any particular employee either through a printed name or signature. Differently from Benitez, every notice here does “certify” the pertinent details. In the absence of clearer Louisiana precedent, we do not find any basis to preclude a company from certifying information even if no specific employee is identified. The statutory provision expressly designates “the insurance premium finance company” as the party effectuating cancellation by “certifying” specific content within a notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The requirement for “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not authorize courts to augment the statutory protocol. Accordingly, we find that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on notices issued by premium finance companies to insurers.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s processes do not comply strictly with the statute, asserting that GoAuto never actually received a cancellation request. The statute specifies: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Prior to 2017, printed requests were kept in a shared filing room that belonged to GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto. Since 2017, Plaintiffs maintain, GoAuto does not receive the requests because an APAC employee places the stamp on them. Plaintiffs assert that the legislature’s selection of “receipt”—as distinct from mere sending—requires that GoAuto actually take possession of the notice before proceeding with policy cancellation.

The Louisiana Supreme Court has yet to construe “receipt” for purposes of this statute, nor does the statute define the term. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). We adopt these definitions and determine that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it obtains possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request. As previously described, when the e-mailed notice reaches GoAuto’s computer system during the early morning hours, the policy is canceled by the system. A GoAuto witness described how the APAC computer system connects to a GoAuto web service: For example, during cancellation processing, the APAC system . . . makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request or execute a cancellation for a policy scheduled to cancel that day. . . . A web service functions as a listener—it constantly listens, waiting for requests, and once it receives one, it processes the instructions if it understands them.

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system has processed the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee utilizes a signature stamp on the printed cancellation forms. To restate our prior summary of the statute: before cancellation occurs, the insurer must have had “receipt” of notice of cancellation transmitted by the premium finance company, as required under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The statute, which reflects present-day conditions, recognizes that company computers now perform many tasks previously handled by humans. GoAuto’s approach to receiving and processing the notice meets the statute’s requirements. We also note that the district court concluded that GoAuto’s cancellation practices met the statutory demands on a different ground: it found that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged that there is no Louisiana precedent interpreting “private carrier,” and analogized to this court’s decision explaining when a railroad acts as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was both inventive and unconventional. However, as we hold that the e-mail complies with the statutory requirements, we need not address the correctness of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to submit these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that correctly interpreting the statutory language is significant for both Louisiana's insurance sector and legal framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that the state supreme court has not yet construed a term does not “warrant certification.” Id. Accordingly, we decline certification in this case. Given that GoAuto’s procedures are consistent with the statute, the district court correctly rejected their motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
