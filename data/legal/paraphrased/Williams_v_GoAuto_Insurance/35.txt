Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana law related to the cancellation of insurance policies for failure to pay the company that provided premium financing. The appellants, who were previously insured, contend that the insurer’s cancellation process failed to comply with the requirements of the statute, allegedly leading to false representations and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that there was no breach of the statute and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies issued by GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not involved in this action, was in a motor vehicle collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. All three individuals—Williams, Wright, and the driver connected to Jenkins’s accident—used American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”) to finance the payment of their GoAuto insurance premiums. Under the financing arrangement, APAC would cover the entire premium amount, and the insured parties would repay APAC through monthly installments. The agreements conferred upon APAC a power of attorney empowering it to cancel the insurance policies in the event that any installments were not paid. Each of these policies was terminated for nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins subsequently initiated a class action against both GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, asserting that GoAuto had failed to validly cancel their insurance and had not acted in good faith. Upon the filing of an amended complaint, Wright was added as a plaintiff. GoAuto removed the matter to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties proceeded to file motions for summary judgment, focused exclusively on whether GoAuto’s method for cancelling the financed insurance contracts adhered to Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in GoAuto’s favor, finding that its cancellation practices complied with state law requirements, and dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright appealed that decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by: (1) concluding that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) determining that GoAuto correctly received the requests for cancellation, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To evaluate these contentions, we first outline the applicable Louisiana law regarding the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium finance agreements, and then consider the procedures GoAuto utilized to adhere to those legal requirements. Louisiana law permits an insurance-premium finance company to initiate cancellation of an insurance policy after an unremedied default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” these matters: (i) That the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as specified in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) That the agreement is in default and such default has not been remedied in a timely manner; (iii) That after the default, notice of cancellation was provided to the insured as detailed in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, identifying the date on which the premium finance company sent this notice to the insured; and (iv) That copies of the notice of cancellation were issued to all individuals or entities indicated by the premium finance agreement as having an interest in possible losses, listing the names and addresses of any relevant government agencies, security interest holders, or other third parties to whom the company dispatched the cancellation notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes a legally effective power of attorney. (2) There exists a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and such default has not been rectified within the prescribed period. (3) Following default, the insured was sent a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation; included herewith are copies of the notices, and an affidavit confirming mailing is available upon request. (4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were provided to all parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system processes a cancellation of the policy. After cancellation, later that morning, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, APAC personnel physically placed these documents into a filing cabinet jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. From 2017 onwards, the signed cancellation paperwork is handed to a GoAuto employee, who stamps it as “received” and then stores it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn now to the issue of whether these procedures adhere to the requirements set out in Louisiana law as previously discussed. Central to the Plaintiffs’ claim is their assertion that APAC’s certification does not bear a signature until immediately after—but definitively not before—the policy automatically terminates. The Plaintiffs read the statute’s command that the premium insurer provide “certification” as meaning a signature must exist before cancellation may occur. However, the statutory text contains no explicit mention of any signature requirement. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If such a requirement exists, it would have to be implied from the use of the term “certifying.” Except for one case to be examined separately, no Louisiana court opinion addressing insurance-premium finance companies’ compliance with this statute has ruled on the necessity of a signature. One court summarized the statutory demand as a need for “a statement certifying compliance with” the law. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision permitted cancellation “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other court opinions provide no greater clarity on this matter. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate opinion, the absence of a signature was referenced as part of a notice’s deficiencies: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court did not determine that merely lacking a signature would render the cancellation ineffective provided the other requirements were met. Here, as in Benitez, the notices in this case do not show a specific employee’s name either in print or by signature. Unlike the Benitez notices, though, each of these notices “certifies” the appropriate information. Given the absence of clearer guidance from Louisiana courts, we find no basis for concluding that certification must come from a specifically named employee. The statutory provision explicitly states that “the insurance premium finance company” effects cancellation by “certifying” particular information in a notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The directive for “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not empower courts to impose additional requirements. We therefore hold that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on cancellation notices provided by a premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s process does not comply strictly with the statute because GoAuto never actually receives a cancellation request. The statute states that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). According to Plaintiffs, prior to 2017, the physical storage room holding the cancellation requests belonged to GoAuto Management Services, rather than GoAuto. Since 2017, Plaintiffs allege GoAuto does not receive the requests since the person applying the stamp is an employee of APAC. Plaintiffs claim the use of the word “receipt” in the statute, instead of just requiring the request to be sent, obligates GoAuto to actually take physical possession of the cancellation notice before it can cancel the policy.

The Louisiana Supreme Court has not construed the meaning of “receipt” in this context, and the statute itself does not define the term. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Based on these definitions, we determine that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it obtains possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request.

As previously explained, when an e-mailed notice reaches GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning, the system executes the policy cancellation. A GoAuto witness detailed how an APAC computer system interfaces with a GoAuto web service: During the cancellation process, “the APAC process . . . makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

Human intervention does not occur until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system has carried out the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. To reiterate our previous discussion of the statute: the requirement before cancellation is that the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company, pursuant to La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute reflects modern realities, where tasks formerly performed by people are now handled by company computer systems. GoAuto’s approach to receiving and processing the notice is compliant with the statutory requirements. Additionally, the district court found GoAuto’s cancellation process to be statutorily compliant for another reason: it concluded that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” Noting the absence of Louisiana precedent defining “private carrier,” the district court identified a Fifth Circuit panel’s analysis, in which a railroad’s status as a private carrier was explained as an analogous case. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The lower court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was inventive and unorthodox. As our decision rests on the sufficiency of the e-mail under the statute, we decline to address whether this definition is correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask us to certify these questions concerning statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court. They argue that correctly construing these statutory terms is significant to Louisiana’s regulatory scheme and insurance sector. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a term lacks definition from the state supreme court “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Accordingly, we refuse to certify the issue. As GoAuto’s practices conform to the statute, the district court properly denied their summary judgment motion. AFFIRMED.
