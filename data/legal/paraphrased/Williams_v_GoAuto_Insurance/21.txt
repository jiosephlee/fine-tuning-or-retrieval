Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who are no longer policyholders of GoAuto Insurance Company, filed suit as plaintiffs. A third policyholder insured by GoAuto, not named as a party in this case, was in an accident with plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Each of Williams, Wright, and the policyholder involved in Jenkins’s accident financed their GoAuto insurance coverage through an arrangement with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these agreements, APAC paid the entire policy premium directly to GoAuto, and in return, the insureds repaid APAC with monthly installments. Each agreement also provided APAC with a power of attorney, authorizing APAC to cancel the insurance if the insured failed to make premium payments. All of the policies were cancelled following nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action against GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, alleging that GoAuto did not validly cancel the insurance policies and breached its duty of good faith. After an amended complaint was filed, Wright was joined as an additional plaintiff. GoAuto then removed the litigation to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides moved for summary judgment on the narrow issue of whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures for financed policies complied with Louisiana statutory requirements. The district court ruled in GoAuto’s favor, granting summary judgment and determining that its cancellation process satisfied Louisiana law. Accordingly, all claims against GoAuto were dismissed with prejudice by the district court. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently appealed.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors in three respects: (1) in determining that the statutory process for delivering notice of cancellation was observed, (2) in concluding that GoAuto correctly received the requests for cancellation, and (3) in rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. In order to resolve these issues, we first outline Louisiana’s statutory framework regarding the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium finance companies, and then consider GoAuto’s practices for adhering to those requirements. Louisiana statutes permit an insurance-premium finance company to initiate cancellation of a policy after an uncured default by “sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” these items: (i) That the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as specified in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) That the premium finance agreement is in default and that default remains uncured; (iii) That after default, the company sent a notice of cancellation to the insured in compliance with Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, stating the date on which the premium finance company sent notice to the insured; (iv) That copies of the cancellation notice were provided to every individual listed in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any loss under the policy, including identification of the names and addresses of any relevant governmental entities, holders of a security interest in the insured property, or other third parties who were notified of the cancellation by the insurance premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes a valid authorization granting power of attorney. (2) There exists a default under the terms of the Premium Finance Agreement, which remains uncured beyond the allowed period. (3) Following the default, the insured was sent a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation. Enclosed herewith are copies of such notices, and an affidavit establishing proof of mailing may be provided upon request. (4) All parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any loss potentially covered thereby also received copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computerized system executes cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later that same morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed documents effecting cancellation. Before 2017, an APAC employee would physically place these documents in a filing cabinet that was jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the executed cancellation document is instead delivered in person to a GoAuto employee, who stamps it “received” before filing it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We must now determine whether these procedures align with the relevant Louisiana statutes previously discussed. The Plaintiffs principally contend that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until immediately following—and definitively after—the automatic cancellation of the policy. According to the Plaintiffs, the statutory mandate for "certifying" by the premium insurer necessitates that a signature be affixed prior to any cancellation taking effect. However, the statute itself does not specifically mention a signature requirement. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If such a requirement were to exist, it would have to be implied by the statutory term "certifying." Except as noted below, no Louisiana court decisions analyzing insurance-premium finance company procedures pursuant to this statute have addressed whether a signature is mandatory. For instance, one court summarized the statutory requirement as needing "a statement certifying compliance with" the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another opinion upheld cancellation "[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company." Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional judicial decisions offer no further clarity on the matter. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, pp. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, the absence of a signature was addressed among the deficiencies of a particular notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court there did not determine that merely lacking a signature would invalidate a cancellation if the other statutory requirements were satisfied. The notices at issue here, similar to those in Benitez, do not display the name or signature of a specific employee. In contrast to Benitez, each of these notices does include a “certification” of the pertinent information. Absent definitive Louisiana precedent to the contrary, we discern no basis to conclude that a company is prohibited from certifying information simply because the notice does not specify an individual employee’s identity. The statute expressly states that “the insurance premium finance company” effects cancellation by “certifying” certain items in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The concept of “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not authorize courts to impose additional procedural requirements. We therefore hold that Louisiana law does not demand a signature on cancellation notices issued by the premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s practices fall short of the statutory mandate because GoAuto never actually receives a cancellation request. The relevant statute provides: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Prior to 2017, the location where cancellation requests were physically stored was owned by GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto. Since 2017, according to Plaintiffs, GoAuto does not receive the requests because the APAC employee applies the stamp. Plaintiffs emphasize that by using the word “receipt” rather than merely stating the request must be sent, the legislature intended for GoAuto to have physical possession of the notice before canceling the policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted the meaning of “receipt” within this statutory context, nor does the statute define the term. Black’s Law Dictionary provides that “receipt” means “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” is defined as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Applying these definitions, we find that GoAuto is authorized to cancel a policy when it has obtained possession, delivery, or control of a cancellation request. As previously discussed, once GoAuto’s computer system receives the emailed notice in the early morning, it processes and cancels the policy. A deponent from GoAuto described how the APAC computer interacts with the GoAuto web service: During cancellation, the APAC process calls the GoAuto web service to request or execute cancellation for a policy scheduled to cancel on that date. The web service operates like a listener—constantly awaiting a call and, upon recognizing its prompt, responds and carries out the appropriate activity if the request is understood.

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, following the time when the GoAuto computer system carries out the cancellation. At that point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation papers. To reiterate our previous summary of the statute, the requirement before cancellation occurs is that the insurer must have "receipt" of the cancellation notice from the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary, reflecting the present era where much of what was formerly done by people is now accomplished by company computer systems. GoAuto’s process for receiving and responding to the notice is compliant with the statute. It is worth noting that the district court ruled that GoAuto’s procedures for cancellation aligned with the statutory requirement for a different reason: it found “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged that there is no Louisiana jurisprudence construing the term “private carrier” and instead looked to an analogous interpretation by this court regarding when a railroad is considered a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s approach to defining “private carrier” was both creative and unorthodox. As our determination is that the e-mail fulfills the statutory requirement, we do not reach the question of whether that definition is valid.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs have asked us to certify these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that the correct reading of the statutory language carries significant consequences for Louisiana’s insurance sector and its regulatory framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that the Louisiana Supreme Court has yet to define a term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Therefore, we deny the request for certification. Given that GoAuto’s actions are consistent with the statute, the district court’s refusal to grant Plaintiffs’ summary judgment motion was not in error. AFFIRMED.
