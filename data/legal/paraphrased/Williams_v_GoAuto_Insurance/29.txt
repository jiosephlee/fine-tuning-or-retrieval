Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are tasked with interpreting a Louisiana law regarding the cancellation of insurance policies due to failure to pay a premium finance company. The appellants, who are former policyholders, argue that the insurance company’s cancellation process did not adhere to the statutory requirements, leading to both misrepresentations and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that the statute had not been violated and dismissed the plaintiffs’ claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto—who is not involved in this suit—had a collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the GoAuto policyholder engaged in Jenkins’s incident all financed their insurance premiums via agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these arrangements, APAC paid GoAuto the full insurance premium upfront, while the policyholders were required to make monthly installment payments to APAC. Each of the financing contracts gave APAC a power of attorney, empowering APAC to cancel the respective insurance policies if the insured failed to make premium payments. Ultimately, each policy was terminated because of nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action suit against GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, alleging, among other things, that GoAuto failed to properly cancel their insurance policies and did not act in good faith. Wright joined the lawsuit as an additional plaintiff after an amended complaint was submitted. GoAuto removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties submitted motions for summary judgment, focusing solely on whether GoAuto’s process for cancelling these financed insurance policies conformed to Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, determining that its cancellation practices were consistent with state law. Consequently, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright then sought review by filing an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made mistakes by (1) concluding that the statutory process for delivering the notice of cancellation had been followed, (2) determining that GoAuto appropriately received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their request for partial summary judgment. To evaluate these claims, we first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes governing the cancellation of insurance policies subject to premium financing, and then review how GoAuto’s practices align with those legal requirements. According to Louisiana law, a company providing insurance-premium financing can seek to cancel an insurance policy after a default remains uncured by “sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following conditions: (i) the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as outlined in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) the premium finance agreement is in default and such default has not been cured in a timely manner; (iii) after default, the premium finance company provided the insured with a notice of cancellation as prescribed by Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, stating when the notice was sent to the insured; and (iv) copies of the cancellation notice were distributed to all individuals identified in the agreement as having an interest in potential losses under the policy, detailing the names and addresses of any government agencies, lienholders on the insured property, or third parties who received notice of cancellation from the premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement is presently in default, and the default has not been corrected within the required period. (3) Following this default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued and sent to the insured. Copies of these notices are attached, and an affidavit proving mailing is available upon request. (4) The Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was also provided to all parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under its terms.

Once this notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system processes the cancellation of the policy. After the cancellation has been completed later that morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Prior to 2017, an APAC employee would physically place these documents in a filing cabinet jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. From 2017 onward, the completed cancellation document is instead hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who stamps it “received” and then files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn next to the question of whether the procedures in question are consistent with Louisiana law as previously examined. Central to Plaintiffs' position is the assertion that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until immediately after, but indisputably following, the policy’s automatic cancellation. Plaintiffs read the statutory mandate of “certifying” by the premium insurer to mean a signature must precede any cancellation. However, the statutory language does not expressly mention a requirement for a signature. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If the statute necessitates a signature, such an obligation would have to be implied within the meaning of “certifying.” Except for one decision to be discussed, Louisiana courts interpreting insurance-premium finance company procedures under this provision have not resolved whether a signature is essential. In one case, a court summarized the statute as obligating “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another case permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional case law offers little further clarification on this issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, the absence of a signature was noted among the deficiencies of a notice, observing: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court in Benitez did not find that the lack of a signature alone would invalidate the cancellation so long as the other statutory components were satisfied. Like in Benitez, the notices present in this case file similarly do not provide the printed name or signature of any specific employee. In contrast to Benitez, though, the notices here expressly “certify” the relevant information. Given the absence of clearer precedent in Louisiana, we find no basis to hold that a company cannot certify required information simply because a specific employee’s name is not indicated. The relevant statutory provision provides that “the insurance premium finance company” itself completes cancellation by “certifying” particular information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The requirement of “strict adherence” to the cancellation procedure does not empower courts to modify or add requirements to those procedures. We determine Louisiana law does not mandate that a notice sent by a premium finance company to an insurer be signed.

Plaintiffs additionally argue that GoAuto’s processes are noncompliant with the statute as GoAuto never actually received a cancellation request. The statute states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Prior to 2017, storage for printed requests was located in the shared filing room of GoAuto Management Services, rather than GoAuto. Since 2017, Plaintiffs assert that GoAuto fails to receive the requests because an APAC employee is responsible for applying the stamp. Plaintiffs maintain that because the legislature used the term “receipt” instead of requiring mere dispatch, GoAuto must actually take possession of the notice before it may effect policy cancellation. The Louisiana Supreme Court has yet to interpret the meaning of “receipt” in the context of this statute, and no statutory definition exists. Black’s Law Dictionary provides that “receipt” means “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and to “receive” is “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Relying on these definitions, we hold that GoAuto is permitted to cancel a policy when it comes into possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As outlined previously, when GoAuto’s computer system receives an emailed notice during the early morning, that system processes the policy cancellation. One GoAuto deponent described how the APAC computer network interacts with a GoAuto web service, explaining: During cancellation, the APAC process “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until later in the day, after the GoAuto computer system processes the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. To reiterate our previous summary of the statute: prior to cancellation, the insurer must have had "receipt" of the notice of cancellation transmitted by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This is a contemporary statute reflecting present-day practices, in which computers at the company perform tasks that were once carried out by people. GoAuto’s process of receiving and responding to the notice fulfills the statutory requirement. The district court determined that GoAuto’s method for effecting cancellation complied with the statute on different grounds, stating that "the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier." The district court noted that Louisiana jurisprudence does not address the interpretation of "private carrier," and turned for guidance to an analogous Fifth Circuit panel decision concerning when a railroad constitutes a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The lower court’s characterization of "private carrier" was both creative and unconventional. Since we determine that the e-mail satisfies the statutory requirement, we do not address the correctness of this definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we certify these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. They argue that a definitive construction of these statutory terms holds significant relevance for both the state's insurance industry and its legislative framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). Merely because a state supreme court has not defined a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. For these reasons, we decline to certify the questions presented. Since GoAuto’s procedures align with the statutory requirements, the district court properly denied summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
