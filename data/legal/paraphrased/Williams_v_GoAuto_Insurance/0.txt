Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana statute addressing the cancellation of insurance policies due to nonpayment to the premium finance company. The former policyholders allege that the insurer’s methods for canceling policies were not consistent with the statutory requirements, leading to alleged misrepresentations and a breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no violation of the statute occurred and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, both former policyholders with GoAuto Insurance Company, serve as plaintiffs in this case. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this litigation, was in a motor vehicle collision involving Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the GoAuto policyholder involved in Jenkins’s incident all utilized financing arrangements for their GoAuto insurance through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these arrangements, APAC remitted the entire insurance premium directly to GoAuto, and the insureds agreed to repay APAC in monthly installments. Each agreement included a power of attorney provision allowing APAC to terminate the relevant insurance policy should the insured default on payment. Due to nonpayment, each policy was cancelled. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action suit in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, contending that GoAuto failed to properly cancel their insurance policies and did not uphold its obligation of good faith. Following the filing of an amended complaint, Wright joined as an additional plaintiff. GoAuto subsequently removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides then submitted motions for summary judgment limited to the issue of whether GoAuto’s policy cancellation process for financed insurance satisfied Louisiana statutory requirements. The district court granted GoAuto’s motion for summary judgment, determining that its procedures conformed to Louisiana law. Consequently, all claims against GoAuto were dismissed with prejudice by the district court. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright then filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by (1) concluding that the statutory procedures regarding delivery of the cancellation notice were properly adhered to, (2) determining that GoAuto appropriately received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To resolve these issues, we will first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes governing the cancellation of insurance policies that are financed, followed by an analysis of GoAuto’s methods for fulfilling these statutory requirements. According to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company is authorized to seek cancellation of an insurance policy when a default remains uncured “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” all of the following: (i) That the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as specified in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) That the premium finance agreement is in default and this default has not been remedied within the allotted time; (iii) That upon default, the premium finance company sent a notice of cancellation to the insured as described in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, identifying the date it was sent to the insured; (iv) That copies of the cancellation notice were also sent to all individuals identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the policy, including specifying the names and addresses of government agencies, holders of security interests in the insured property, or any third parties to whom the premium finance company dispatched the cancellation notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate exact compliance with statutory provisions when a finance company utilizes its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). Furthermore, “any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). The requirement for strict statutory compliance “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto implements the following process to cancel a policy: upon the insured’s failure to make a timely payment, APAC sends the insured notice that the policy will be cancelled unless payment is received within ten days. Should payment not be made by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically transmits to GoAuto an e-mail containing the notice of cancellation and a request for cancellation. This cancellation request is on APAC’s letterhead and includes the following certification: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) There is a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and this default has not been remedied within the required period. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured. Enclosed are copies of the notices, and an affidavit attesting to their mailing can be provided upon request. (4) All individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss thereunder were sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

When that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system terminates the policy. Subsequently, on the same morning following cancellation, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC worker would manually deliver the documents to a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, however, the signed cancellation document is instead brought directly to a GoAuto employee, who marks it as “received” with a stamp and then stores it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next examine whether these procedures align with the relevant Louisiana law previously discussed. The Plaintiffs’ main contention is that APAC’s certification does not bear a signature until shortly after—and indisputably after—the policy has been canceled automatically. Plaintiffs interpret the statute’s requirement for the premium insurer to “certify” as necessitating a signature prior to any effective cancellation. However, the statute itself, La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a), contains no explicit language mandating a signature. If a signature were indeed necessary, that requirement would have to be implied from the term “certifying.” Except for an exception we address separately, Louisiana courts examining insurance-premium finance companies’ processes under this statute have not directly ruled on whether a signature is needed. One court described the statutory requirement as necessitating “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. See KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another opinion upheld cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional cases offer no greater guidance on this specific issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

A court of appeal previously discussed the lack of a signature in relation to deficiencies in a cancellation notice, stating that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not determine that the absence of a signature alone would invalidate the cancellation if other requirements were satisfied. Similarly to Benitez, the notices present in this case do not specify a particular employee by printed name or signature. In contrast to Benitez, each of the current notices does “certify” the necessary information. In the absence of more definitive authority from Louisiana, we see no basis to conclude that a company cannot certify information without identifying a specific employee. The statute itself provides that it is “the insurance premium finance company” that accomplishes cancellation by “certifying” certain facts in a notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The mandate for “strict adherence” to the cancellation procedures does not authorize courts to supplement those procedures. We therefore hold that Louisiana law does not mandate that the premium finance company’s notice to the insurer be signed. Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s procedures fail to strictly comply with the statute because GoAuto allegedly never actually receives a cancellation request. According to the statute, “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs claim that, prior to 2017, the shared filing room where printed requests were kept was under GoAuto Management Services’ control, not GoAuto’s. Since 2017, they assert, GoAuto does not receive the requests because the employee responsible for stamping is employed by APAC. Plaintiffs assert that by using the term “receipt,” rather than simply requiring the request to be sent, the legislature intended for GoAuto to take actual possession of the notice before cancelling a policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted “receipt” as used in this statute, nor does the statute define the term. Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and defines “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). We adopt these definitions and determine that GoAuto may proceed with policy cancellation once it has possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As described, when the e-mailed notice is received by GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning, that system cancels the policy. A GoAuto witness detailed the interaction between the APAC and GoAuto systems: Specifically, during the cancellation process, APAC’s system initiates a call to a GoAuto web service to request or effectuate the cancellation of a policy scheduled for cancellation on a particular date. The web service functions as a listener—it is constantly awaiting calls, and when prompted, it asks for further instructions; if it recognizes the request, it executes the corresponding action.

No individual becomes involved until some time later in the morning, after GoAuto’s computer system has processed and implemented the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. To reiterate our earlier summary of the statutory language, before cancellation may occur, the insurer must have “receipt” of the notice of cancellation sent by the premium finance company, as required by La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This is a contemporary statute reflective of present-day operations, where corporate computers now perform functions formerly handled by individuals. GoAuto’s approach to receiving and acting upon notice fulfills the statute’s requirements. We also note the district court found GoAuto’s cancellation process complied with the statute for an additional reason: “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged there is no Louisiana case law construing “private carrier,” and looked to an analogous situation where this court analyzed whether a railroad qualified as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was notably creative and atypical. However, since we conclude that the e-mail complies with the statutory mandate, we need not address whether the definition adopted by the district court is valid.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we submit these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court for certification. They argue that the correct reading of these statutory terms carries significant implications for Louisiana's insurance sector and regulatory framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The fact that a state supreme court has yet to construe a term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Therefore, we refuse to certify the question. Since GoAuto’s practices are consistent with the statute, the district court properly denied the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
