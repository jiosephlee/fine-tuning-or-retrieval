Southwick: This case calls for interpretation of a Louisiana law concerning the termination of insurance policies due to nonpayment by the party that provided premium financing. The appellants, who were once policyholders, argue that the insurer failed to follow the statutory requirements for cancellation, which they contend amounted to misrepresentation and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that the insurer did not violate the statute and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the district court’s decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company, serve as the plaintiffs in this matter. In a separate incident, another individual insured by GoAuto—who is not a party to this action—was involved in a collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Each of the plaintiffs, as well as the driver involved in Jenkins’s accident, financed their GoAuto insurance premiums through agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these agreements, APAC advanced the full premium payment to GoAuto and, in exchange, collected monthly payments from the policyholders. The financing contracts provided APAC with a power of attorney, which authorized it to terminate the insurance coverage if the policyholders defaulted on their payments. Ultimately, nonpayment led to the cancellation of each respective policy. Subsequently, Williams and Jenkins commenced a class action suit in a Louisiana state court against both GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto had not lawfully cancelled their policies and had failed to exercise good faith. Wright was added as a plaintiff through an amended complaint. GoAuto then removed the lawsuit to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides moved for summary judgment, focusing exclusively on whether GoAuto’s cancellation process for financed policies conformed to Louisiana statutory requirements. The district court awarded summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, determining that its cancellation practices were consistent with state law, and dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made several errors: (1) in determining that the statutory procedures governing notice of cancellation delivery were satisfied, (2) in concluding that GoAuto duly received the cancellation requests, and (3) in rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To address these contentions, we begin by outlining the relevant Louisiana statutes regarding the cancellation of insurance policies obtained through financing, followed by an evaluation of GoAuto’s processes in relation to those legal requirements. According to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company may initiate cancellation of an insurance policy following an unresolved default by transmitting to the insurer—either through postal mail, private carrier, or electronic mail—a copy of the cancellation notice, accompanied by a certification affirming: (i) the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney in accordance with Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) the agreement is in default and such default remains uncured; (iii) upon default, a cancellation notice was sent to the insured as required under Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, including the date on which the premium finance company dispatched it to the insured; and (iv) duplicate notices of cancellation were sent to all parties with a recorded interest in potential losses under the agreement, with specification of the identities and addresses of any government agencies, secured interest holders, or other third parties to whom the notice was provided. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate exact compliance with statutory provisions when a finance company invokes its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). If there is any flaw in adhering to the required procedure, the cancellation is rendered invalid. See Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). The necessity for strict compliance fulfills two important objectives: it establishes a precise point in time when cancellation becomes effective and ensures a period in which the policyholder can remedy the default. See Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). To effectuate cancellation of a policy, GoAuto implements the following protocol. Upon a policyholder’s failure to submit timely payment, APAC dispatches a notice advising that the policy will be cancelled unless payment is received within ten days. Should payment not be made by midnight of the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically transmits an e-mail to GoAuto containing both the cancellation notice and a formal request for cancellation. This request, printed on APAC’s letterhead, states as follows: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney provision. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement currently remains in default, with the default not having been remedied within the required timeframe. (3) Following the occurrence of default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued and delivered to the insured party. Enclosed are copies of these notices; an affidavit providing proof of mailing can be furnished upon request. (4) The Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was also distributed to every party identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss covered by the agreement.

Once the notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system terminates the policy. Subsequently, later that morning, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the hard-copy cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC staff member would manually file these documents in a cabinet that was jointly used by both GoAuto and APAC. From 2017 onward, however, the signed cancellation document is hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who then marks it as “received” and files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next examine whether these actions conform to the Louisiana statute previously analyzed. Central to the Plaintiffs’ position is the fact that APAC’s certification does not bear a signature until a point that is distinctly after the policy has already lapsed automatically. The Plaintiffs construe the statutory mandate for “certifying” by the premium insurer as necessitating a signature prior to any policy cancellation taking effect. However, the statute itself—La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a)—contains no explicit stipulation for a signature. If a signature is indeed required, such an obligation would have to be inferred from the statute’s use of the term “certifying.” With one notable exception, Louisiana courts reviewing procedures of insurance-premium finance companies under this provision have not directly addressed whether a signature is compulsory. In one decision, the statutory duty was summarized as necessitating “a statement certifying compliance with” the provision. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. In a separate case, a cancellation was authorized “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial business office. Employees performing roles for both companies are under the employment of GoAuto Management Services, LLC. For purposes of this appeal, we consider personnel assigned to either entity as employees of the respective company.

Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. Cir. 6/20/89). Other case law does not offer meaningful guidance on the issue. See, for example, Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50. In one appellate decision, the absence of a signature was only cited as one of several deficiencies in a notice of cancellation: the notice “does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court did not rule that merely lacking a signature would invalidate a cancellation notice if the remaining statutory requirements were otherwise satisfied. Consistent with Benitez, the cancellation notices in the present record do not include the printed name or signature of a specific employee. Distinct from Benitez, however, each notice here “certifies” the pertinent information. In the absence of authoritative Louisiana case law to the contrary, there appears to be no legal barrier preventing a company from certifying information in a notice without specifying an individual employee. Indeed, the relevant statutory provision declares it is “the insurance premium finance company” that accomplishes cancellation through “certifying” the necessary information within the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The requirement of “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not permit courts to supplement the statutory framework with new requirements. Accordingly, we hold that Louisiana law does not mandate that a notice sent by a premium finance company to an insurer bear a signature.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto failed to strictly comply with statutory procedures because it allegedly never received a cancellation request. The statutory text states that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs note that before 2017, a shared filing space for printed requests was owned by GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself. Post-2017, they contend that GoAuto never obtains the requests, since an APAC employee is responsible for stamping them. Plaintiffs interpret the statute’s use of “receipt”—as opposed to merely requiring that the request be sent—to mean that GoAuto must take physical possession of the notice before effectuating cancellation.

The Louisiana Supreme Court has not specifically construed the meaning of “receipt” under this statute, nor does the statute define it. According to Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024), “receipt” is “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” means “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Relying on these definitions, we find that GoAuto may cancel a policy as soon as it assumes possession, delivery, or control over the cancellation request. As described, when the cancellation notice arrives electronically in GoAuto’s system during the early morning hours, the policy is automatically canceled by the computer. A GoAuto deponent further elaborated on the technical process, stating that APAC’s computer system interacts with a GoAuto web service as follows: During cancellation, the APAC process contacts the GoAuto web service to request or execute a cancellation for the specific policy scheduled to be canceled on that date. In this context, the web service acts as a perpetual “listener,” awaiting calls, and when invoked, it seeks instructions—if the command is recognized, the corresponding action is carried out.

Human involvement does not occur until some time after the GoAuto computer system processes the cancellation. Subsequently, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. To reiterate our earlier exposition of the statutory language, prior to cancellation, the statute requires that the insurer have “receipt” of a cancellation notice issued by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary, reflecting modern business practices, where many tasks traditionally performed by individuals are now handled by computer systems. The mechanism GoAuto uses to receive and process the notice is consistent with the statute’s requirements. The district court concluded GoAuto’s procedures complied with the statute for a different reason, specifically stating that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” While noting the absence of Louisiana judicial interpretation of “private carrier,” the district court looked to a Fifth Circuit panel’s analysis in a railroad context as a useful comparison. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The lower court’s construction of “private carrier” was both resourceful and somewhat unorthodox. However, since we determine that the email notification fulfills the statute’s requirements, we need not assess the accuracy of that particular interpretation.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that these statutory interpretation issues be certified to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that clarifying the statutory terms is significant to Louisiana’s insurance regulations and legal framework. However, because we find that GoAuto’s procedures are consistent with the statute, the district court’s decision to deny their motion for summary judgment was not in error.

Typically, we exercise caution when considering the certification of legal questions unless there exists a strong justification for such action; because certification serves as a critical tool for this court, we are mindful not to overuse it and potentially jeopardize its future availability. Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere absence of a definition for a particular term by a state supreme court “does not, by itself, provide adequate grounds for certification.” Id. Accordingly, we choose not to certify the question. AFFIRMED.
