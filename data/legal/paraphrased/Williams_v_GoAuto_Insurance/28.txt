This case presents an examination of a Louisiana statute that addresses the cancellation of insurance coverage when premiums have not been paid to the financing entity. The former policyholders argue that the insurer failed to adhere to statutory requirements in its cancellation process, leading to alleged misrepresentations and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that no statutory breach had occurred and dismissed every claim with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not involved in this matter, was engaged in a motor vehicle collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the driver associated with Jenkins’s accident each financed their insurance premiums with GoAuto through financing agreements made with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these agreements, APAC paid GoAuto the entire premium amount and, in exchange, received monthly installment payments from each insured party. Each of these agreements assigned APAC a power of attorney that permitted it to cancel the respective insurance policy upon the insured’s failure to remit payment. All of the relevant policies were terminated due to nonpayment of premiums. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action lawsuit in Louisiana state court against both GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto did not properly cancel their policies and failed to act in good faith. Wright was later added as a plaintiff through an amended complaint. GoAuto removed the matter to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides filed motions for summary judgment, disputing only whether GoAuto’s method of cancelling the financed insurance contracts adhered to the requirements of Louisiana law. The district court found in GoAuto’s favor, concluding that its cancellation procedures met state legal standards, and therefore dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright then appealed.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court committed error in three respects: (1) by determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the cancellation notice were satisfied, (2) by concluding that GoAuto properly received the cancellation requests, and (3) by rejecting their motion seeking partial summary judgment. In order to address these contentions, we will first review the relevant Louisiana statutes governing the cancellation of insurance policies that are financed, and then evaluate the procedures utilized by GoAuto in attempting to comply with these statutory mandates. Pursuant to Louisiana law, a company that finances insurance premiums may initiate the cancellation of an insurance policy after an uncured default by “sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following elements: (i) That the premium finance agreement contains a legally valid power of attorney in accordance with Paragraph (1) of the Subsection; (ii) That the agreement is in default and the default remains unremedied; (iii) That, once default occurred, the finance company sent a notice of cancellation to the insured as required by Paragraph (2) of the Subsection, indicating the date that the premium finance company sent the notice to the insured; and (iv) That copies of the notice of cancellation were also sent to all individuals or entities identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in potential losses under the policy, including specifying the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, secured parties, or third parties to whom the premium finance company sent the cancellation notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney provision. (2) There has been a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and such default has not been remedied within the applicable cure period. (3) Following this default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured; enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit of mailing can be provided if requested. (4) All parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having a potential interest in any resulting loss received copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Upon issuance of this notice, GoAuto’s computer system processes a cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later the same morning, an employee of APAC applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents. Before 2017, an APAC staff member would deliver the documents to a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. Starting in 2017, the executed cancellation document is instead taken directly to a GoAuto employee, who stamps it “received” and then places it into the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn next to whether these procedures adhere to the Louisiana statute previously discussed. The central element of Plaintiffs’ claim is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until a time soon after, but definitely following, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs argue that the statutory mandate of “certifying” by the premium insurer means a signature is required before cancellation can become effective. However, the statute itself does not explicitly mention a requirement for a signature. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be necessary, that requirement would have to be inferred from the term “certifying.” Aside from an exception that will be addressed, Louisiana courts reviewing the practices of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute have not considered whether a signature is required. In paraphrasing the statutory language, one court stated only that there must be “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another case permitted cancellation “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Similar judicial opinions offer no additional guidance regarding this issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

A court of appeal opinion addressed the deficiencies in a notice, noting its lack of a signature: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court there did not find that merely lacking a signature would invalidate the cancellation if the other required aspects were present. In the present matter, like Benitez, the notices do not identify any individual employee either by signature or printed name. But here, each notice includes a “certification” of the necessary information. Given the absence of explicit Louisiana authority, we perceive no prohibition on a company certifying such information without naming an employee. The statute itself describes that “the insurance premium finance company” effects cancellation by “certifying” specific facts in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The mandate for “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not empower courts to supplement these procedures. Accordingly, we determine that Louisiana law does not impose a requirement that the notice provided by the premium finance company to the insurer be signed. Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s practices fall short of statutory strict compliance because GoAuto never actually “received” a cancellation request. The statutory language provides that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, the physical storage area for the printed cancellation requests was owned by GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto itself. Plaintiffs assert that since 2017, GoAuto does not receive the requests, because the staff member who applies the relevant stamp is employed by APAC. Plaintiffs interpret the legislature’s use of the word “receipt”—as opposed to only mandating that a notice be sent—as obligating GoAuto to actually possess the notice before effectuating cancellation. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not yet interpreted “receipt” in this statutory context, nor does the statute contain its own definition. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). We apply these definitions and conclude that GoAuto can effectuate a cancellation upon taking possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request. As previously explained, once the cancellation notice arrives to GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning hours, the system proceeds to cancel the policy. A GoAuto witness described the interaction between APAC’s computer system and a GoAuto web service: During cancellation processing, the APAC system “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

Human involvement only occurs later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system has processed the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents. To restate our earlier summary of the statute: before a cancellation occurs, the insurer must have had “receipt” of the cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary, recognizing that company computers now perform many functions previously handled by people. The way GoAuto receives and responds to such notice meets the statute’s requirements. It is worth noting that the district court found GoAuto’s cancellation procedures compliant with the statute based on a different rationale: namely, that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged the lack of Louisiana precedent interpreting “private carrier,” and relied on an analogous Fifth Circuit panel’s discussion of when a railroad qualified as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s construction of “private carrier” was original and nontraditional. Since we conclude that the e-mail satisfies the statutory requirements, we decline to address whether that definition is correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to submit these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court for resolution, arguing that the clarification of the statutory language is significant to Louisiana's insurance sector and legislative framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). Furthermore, the mere absence of a definitive interpretation of a term by a state supreme court “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Accordingly, we refuse to certify this issue. Given that GoAuto’s practices are in accordance with the statute, the district court properly denied their motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
