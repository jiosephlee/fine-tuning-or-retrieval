Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana law relating to the termination of insurance policies due to nonpayment to the entity that provided premium financing. The former policyholders contend that the insurer failed to follow the statutory cancellation requirements, leading to alleged misrepresentations and violations of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that no statutory infraction had occurred and dismissed all of the plaintiffs’ claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, formerly held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto—who is not a party to this case—was in a collision involving Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the insured driver from Jenkins’s accident all obtained their GoAuto policies using financing arrangements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these arrangements, APAC paid the total insurance premiums to GoAuto and the insureds made monthly payments to APAC. These financing contracts provided APAC with a power of attorney, which empowered APAC to cancel the insurance policies if the insureds failed to pay. Cancellation of each plaintiff’s policy occurred due to nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins commenced a class action in a Louisiana state court against both GoAuto and APAC, contending that GoAuto did not properly cancel their insurance and failed to adhere to its duty of good faith. Wright joined the suit as an additional plaintiff following the filing of an amended complaint. The matter was subsequently removed by GoAuto to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides then moved for summary judgment, addressing only whether the procedures utilized by GoAuto to cancel financed insurance policies complied with Louisiana statutory law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, determining its cancellation process conformed to state requirements. Accordingly, all claims against GoAuto were dismissed with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made mistakes by (1) concluding that the statutory procedures for delivering the notice of cancellation had been followed, (2) determining that GoAuto properly received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. In order to address these points, we begin by outlining the relevant provisions of Louisiana law governing the cancellation of financed insurance policies and then analyze how GoAuto’s practices align with those legal requirements. According to Louisiana law, a premium finance company can seek cancellation of an insurance policy after a default that is not cured by “sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” all of the following: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as detailed in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection. (ii) The premium finance agreement is in default, and the default remains uncured. (iii) After default, the insured received a notice of cancellation pursuant to Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the date when the premium finance company sent the notice to the insured. (iv) Copies of the cancellation notice were also sent to all individuals listed in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in potential loss under the agreement, including names and addresses of government agencies, secured parties, or third parties to whom the premium finance company directed cancellation notices. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts demand exact compliance with the statutory provisions when a finance company uses its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “Any deviation from these procedures leads to an invalid cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). The requirement of strict statutory compliance “serves two purposes: first, it provides a precise method for determining the specific time and date a cancellation is effective, and second, it ensures that a minimum period exists during which the policyholder can remedy any default.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). In order to carry out cancellations, GoAuto implements the following process: If a policyholder does not pay on time, APAC issues a notice informing the insured that their policy will be canceled if payment is not received within ten days. Should payment remain outstanding by the end of the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically forwards GoAuto an email that includes both the notice of cancellation and a formal request to cancel. This cancellation request appears on APAC’s letterhead and includes the following certification: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement has been breached, and this breach has not been remedied within the required time. (3) After default occurred, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured. Enclosed are copies of such notices; affidavit of mailing can be supplied if needed. (4) The Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was also sent to every individual identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in potential losses under the agreement.

When that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system automatically terminates the policy. After the cancellation occurs later that morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee manually placed these documents into a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, however, the signed cancellation document is brought to a GoAuto employee, who stamps it as “received” before placing it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next evaluate whether these procedures adhere to the Louisiana statute previously analyzed. The Plaintiffs’ main contention is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until shortly after, but still subsequent to, the policy’s automatic cancellation. Plaintiffs construe the statutory mandate of “certifying” by the premium insurer as imposing an obligation to obtain a signature prior to cancellation. However, the statute itself does not expressly mention a signature requirement. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be required, such necessity would have to arise from the meaning of “certifying.” Apart from one exception, Louisiana appellate decisions that have examined insurance-premium finance companies’ practices under this statute have not decided whether a signature is essential. In one instance, a court summarized the statutory duty as requiring “a statement certifying compliance with” the law. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another appellate opinion permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other decisions similarly fail to clarify this specific issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

An opinion from a court of appeal referenced the absence of a signature while discussing flaws in that notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not find that simply lacking a signature would alone make the cancellation ineffective if other elements were satisfied. Similarly to Benitez, the notices in the present record do not indicate any specific employee, either by printed name or signature. Differing from Benitez, though, each notice here “certifies” the applicable information. Given the lack of more explicit Louisiana authority, we find no basis to conclude that a company must name an employee in order to certify such information. The statutory provision itself clarifies that “the insurance premium finance company” is responsible for effectuating cancellation by “certifying” particular information within the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Interpreting “strict adherence” to the cancellation procedure requirements does not justify courts augmenting the statute’s procedures. We, therefore, hold that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the notice the premium finance company sends to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s procedures do not strictly comply with the statute, asserting that GoAuto never receives a request for cancellation. The relevant statute states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs assert that before 2017, the shared filing area that housed the physical requests was managed by GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself. Since 2017, they state, GoAuto does not directly receive the requests because the APAC employee, not a GoAuto employee, applies the relevant stamp. They contend that the legislature’s selection of the term “receipt,” rather than merely requiring transmission of the request, means GoAuto must physically obtain the notice prior to cancelling a policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted the meaning of “receipt” in this statute, and there is no statutory definition provided. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and defines “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). We adopt these definitions and determine that GoAuto becomes authorized to cancel a policy once it takes delivery, possession, or custody of a cancellation request. As previously discussed, when GoAuto’s computer system receives an e-mailed notice in the early morning, the policy is then cancelled by that system. A GoAuto deponent described the way the APAC system interacts with a GoAuto web service: For instance, during cancellation, the APAC process “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

There is no human involvement until later in the day after the GoAuto computer system initiates the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an employee of APAC applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. We reiterate our prior summary of the statutory provision. To effect cancellation, what is required is that the insurer receive notification of cancellation from the premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary, reflecting technological advancements, and tasks once handled manually are now performed by company computers. GoAuto’s process for receiving and responding to such notice complies with statutory requirements. Additionally, we observe that the district court determined that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures were statutorily compliant on a different basis: it concluded that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged there is no Louisiana case law directly interpreting the term “private carrier,” and considered persuasive a Fifth Circuit panel’s discussion concerning when a railroad constituted a private carrier, referencing Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was innovative and unconventional. Since we conclude that the e-mail notification complies with the statute, we do not address the merits of the district court’s definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to certify these questions involving statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court. They argue that the correct reading of these statutory terms is significant for Louisiana’s insurance sector and overall statutory framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a state’s highest court has not yet provided a definition for a term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Consequently, we refuse to certify the issue. Given that GoAuto’s procedures are consistent with the statute, the district court correctly denied Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
