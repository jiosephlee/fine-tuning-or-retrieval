Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we must interpret a Louisiana law that addresses the cancellation of insurance policies for failure to pay the premium finance company. The former policyholders argue that the insurer failed to follow the statutory cancellation procedures, thereby committing misrepresentation and violating the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court found no breach of the statute and dismissed the plaintiffs' claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the district court's decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this action, was in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, also a plaintiff. Williams, Wright, and the driver involved in Jenkins’s accident each obtained their GoAuto insurance policies by entering into premium finance agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these arrangements, APAC paid GoAuto the full premium upfront and, in exchange, the insureds made monthly payments to APAC. Each agreement conveyed to APAC a power of attorney that authorized APAC to cancel the respective insurance policy if the insured defaulted on payment. The insurance policies for each of these individuals were terminated due to nonpayment of premiums. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action lawsuit against GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, contending that GoAuto failed to validly cancel their insurance and did not fulfill its duty of good faith. After an amended complaint was filed, Wright was added as a plaintiff. GoAuto subsequently removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides then sought summary judgment on the narrow issue of whether GoAuto’s method for cancelling these financed insurance policies was in compliance with Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in GoAuto’s favor, concluding that its cancellation process met the requirements of state law, and therefore dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright appealed this decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made mistakes by (1) concluding that the statutory methods for delivering notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) determining that GoAuto duly received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To evaluate these claims, we will first outline the relevant Louisiana statutory framework governing the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium finance agreements, and then assess GoAuto’s practices for adhering to those statutory requirements. Louisiana statutes permit an insurance-premium finance company to request cancellation of an insurance policy following an uncured default by sending the insurer, either by mailing, private carrier, or electronic mail, both a copy of the notice of cancellation and a certification statement that includes the following: (i) That the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as described in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) That the agreement is in default and that default has not been timely remedied; (iii) That, following the default, a notice of cancellation was issued to the insured in accordance with Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, with the date of such notice specified by the premium finance company; and (iv) That copies of the cancellation notice were sent to all individuals or entities indicated in the finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss, specifying the names and addresses of government agencies, security interest holders in the insured property, or other third parties to whom notice was provided. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate precise compliance with the statutory provisions governing the exercise of a power of attorney by a finance company to cancel an insurance policy. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). Any irregularity in following these procedures renders the cancellation invalid. Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Rigid compliance with the statute fulfills two purposes: it establishes an unambiguous method to determine the precise time and date of cancellation and ensures the insured has a minimum period to remedy the default. Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). In its policy cancellation process, GoAuto implements the following steps. When a premium payment is not made on time, APAC sends a notice informing the insured that the policy will be cancelled unless payment is received within ten days. Should payment not be received by the deadline—midnight on the tenth day—APAC’s computer system automatically transmits an email to GoAuto containing a copy of the cancellation notice and a cancellation request. The cancellation request, printed on APAC’s letterhead, includes the following certification from APAC:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement has been breached, and such breach remains uncured within the designated timeframe. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit of mailing can be provided upon request. (4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were also sent to all parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss thereunder.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system effectuates the policy cancellation. Subsequently, later the same morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. Before 2017, an APAC staff member would manually file these documents in a cabinet that was jointly used by both GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, however, the signed cancellation form is hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who marks the document as “received” with a stamp and then files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next evaluate whether these procedures are consistent with the Louisiana law previously discussed. Central to the Plaintiffs’ contention is the fact that APAC’s certification does not bear a signature until shortly after, but unquestionably following, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs construe the statutory mandate that the premium insurer "certify" as imposing an obligation that a signature must be present before cancellation can occur. However, the statute itself does not specifically mention any signature requirement. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If a signature is necessary, that requirement would have to be implied by the term “certifying.” With one exception, which will be addressed separately, Louisiana courts evaluating the procedures of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute have not considered whether a signature is required. In one case, the court characterized the statutory requirement as the necessity for “a statement certifying compliance with” the law. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional opinions do not address this issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

An appellate court addressed the issue of an unsigned notice in evaluating the deficiencies of such a notice, observing that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. The court did not rule that the absence of a signature, on its own, invalidates the cancellation if the other statutory requirements are met. Similarly to Benitez, the notices at issue here also fail to provide either a printed name or a signature of any specific employee. However, in contrast to Benitez, these notices do “certify” the pertinent information. In the absence of more definitive Louisiana precedent, we do not find any basis to prohibit a company from certifying information without identifying an individual employee by name. The relevant statutory provision expressly indicates that “the insurance premium finance company” is responsible for effecting cancellation by “certifying” specified information within the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The requirement of “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not empower courts to expand those procedures beyond the statute’s text. We therefore hold that Louisiana law does not mandate the notice from a premium finance company to an insurer be signed.

Plaintiffs also assert that GoAuto's process fails to comply strictly with the statute because GoAuto allegedly never received a request for cancellation. The statute provides: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs argue that before 2017, the shared filing room in which printed requests were maintained was under the control of GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto. Since 2017, Plaintiffs claim GoAuto does not directly receive the requests because the individual affixing the stamp is employed by APAC. Plaintiffs maintain that the legislative use of the word “receipt,” rather than simply requiring the notice to be sent, obligates GoAuto to actually take possession of the cancellation notice prior to cancelling the policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not yet construed the term “receipt” as used in this context, nor is the term defined in the statute. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and defines “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Applying these definitions, we find that GoAuto is entitled to cancel a policy once it obtains possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request.

As described previously, when the e-mailed notice reaches GoAuto’s computer system during the early morning, that system then effectuates the cancellation. A GoAuto representative detailed the interaction between an APAC computer system and a GoAuto web service: Specifically, during the cancellation workflow, the APAC system initiates a call to a GoAuto web service in order to request or effectuate the cancellation for a policy scheduled for cancellation on that date. The web service, essentially functioning as a listener, remains constantly available; upon receiving the appropriate call, it prompts for further instruction and, if it comprehends the request, proceeds to execute the necessary action.

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system completes the cancellation process. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. To reiterate our prior summary of the statute, the prerequisite for cancellation is that the insurer must have "receipt" of the cancellation notice issued by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute reflects modern realities, where computers handle functions previously performed by humans. GoAuto’s process for receiving and responding to the notice meets the statute’s requirements. We note that the district court determined GoAuto’s cancellation procedures were statutorily compliant on another basis: “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged that there is no Louisiana precedent interpreting “private carrier,” and instead relied on a panel of this court that analyzed whether a railroad qualified as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s construction of “private carrier” was both creative and unorthodox. Since we conclude the e-mail meets the statutory requirements, we decline to address the validity of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we submit these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court for certification. They argue that determining the correct meaning of these statutory terms is significant for both the insurance industry and the legislative framework in Louisiana. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that the state supreme court has not yet construed a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. For these reasons, we decline to certify the question. Since GoAuto’s actions are in accordance with the statute, the district court’s decision to deny Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment was not in error. AFFIRMED.
