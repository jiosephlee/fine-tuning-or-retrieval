Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana law concerning the termination of insurance policies due to the policyholder’s failure to pay the company that provided the premium financing. The appellants, who were previously insured, argue that the insurer’s procedures for cancellation did not conform to statutory requirements and that this noncompliance amounted to both misrepresentation and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that no violation of the statute had occurred and dismissed every claim with prejudice. We AFFIRM the judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, both formerly insured by GoAuto Insurance Company, are plaintiffs in this case. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this matter, was involved in a motor vehicle collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the driver engaged in the incident with Jenkins all financed their respective GoAuto insurance premiums through a contract with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under the terms of these agreements, APAC advanced the full premium payments to GoAuto, and the insureds were required to reimburse APAC via monthly installments. These financing agreements each provided APAC with a power of attorney enabling APAC to cancel the related insurance policy in the event of nonpayment of the premium. Due to missed payments, all three policies in question were terminated for nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a putative class action against GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, claiming GoAuto failed to properly effectuate the cancellation of their insurance policies and did not fulfill its obligations of good faith. Wright was later added as a plaintiff via an amended pleading. GoAuto subsequently removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides moved for summary judgment, focusing exclusively on whether GoAuto’s method for cancelling the financed policies was in accordance with Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, concluding that the company’s cancellation procedures met statutory requirements. Consequently, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright have appealed that decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made mistakes by (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation were met, (2) concluding that GoAuto appropriately received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. In order to address these claims, we first summarize Louisiana's legal framework regarding the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium finance agreements, and then we review GoAuto’s methods for adhering to those legal requirements. According to Louisiana statutes, an insurance-premium finance company may initiate cancellation of an insurance policy after a default that has not been remedied, by transmitting to the insurer—whether through regular mail, private carrier, or electronic mail—a copy of the cancellation notice along with a certificate verifying the following: (i) The premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney in accordance with Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) the agreement is in default and that default remains uncured; (iii) following default, the premium finance company sent a cancellation notice to the insured as directed in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, stating the date the notice was sent; and (iv) duplicate copies of the cancellation notice were sent to all individuals with an interest in any possible loss as indicated in the finance agreement, and the certification must specify the identities and addresses of relevant governmental bodies, security interest holders in the insured property, or other third parties to whom the cancellation notice was delivered by the premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement has entered default status and such default has not been remedied within the prescribed period. (3) Following default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured. Copies of these notices are attached, and an affidavit proving their mailing can be provided upon request. (4) All parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement also received copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system proceeds to cancel the policy. Sometime after the cancellation occurs that same morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the hard copy cancellation documents. Before 2017, an APAC employee would physically deposit the documents into a filing cabinet that both GoAuto and APAC used. After 2017, the signed cancellation document is instead delivered directly to a GoAuto employee, who marks it as “received” with a stamp and then files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next address whether these procedures satisfy the requirements of Louisiana law previously discussed. The Plaintiffs’ main contention is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until after, and indeed only shortly after, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs interpret the statutory mandate of “certifying” by the premium insurer as necessitating that a signature precede any cancellation. However, the statute does not explicitly demand a signature. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If such a requirement exists, it would have to be derived solely from the meaning of “certifying.” Except as otherwise discussed below, no Louisiana court opinion concerning the procedures of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute has directly addressed whether a signature is required. For instance, one court rephrased the statute’s demand as the need for “a statement certifying compliance with” its terms. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another court permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other decisions are likewise unilluminating regarding this specific issue. See, for example, Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

An appellate court opinion referenced the absence of a signature while discussing the deficiencies of a notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not determine that a missing signature alone would render the cancellation invalid if other requirements were satisfied. Here, as in Benitez, the notices lack identification of any specific employee either by name or signature. Unlike Benitez, though, each of these notices does "certify" the relevant details. In the absence of clearer Louisiana precedent, we perceive no basis for concluding that a company may not certify information without naming an individual employee. The statute itself provides that “the insurance premium finance company” effects cancellation by “certifying” certain information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Demanding “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not grant courts authority to supplement those procedures. Therefore, we determine Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs also argue that GoAuto’s procedures do not strictly comply with the statute because GoAuto never actually receives the cancellation request. The statute states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, printed requests were stored in a shared filing room owned by GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself. Since 2017, Plaintiffs assert that GoAuto does not receive requests since the APAC employee, not a GoAuto employee, affixes the stamp. Plaintiffs maintain the legislature’s choice of the term “receipt”—instead of merely requiring notice to be sent—means GoAuto must take physical possession of the notice prior to cancellation. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not construed the meaning of “receipt” in this context, nor is it defined by statute. Black’s Law Dictionary provides that “receipt” means "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession," and "receive" means "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). We adopt these definitions and find that GoAuto is permitted to cancel a policy once it obtains possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request.

As previously noted, once GoAuto’s computer system receives the emailed notice in the early morning, the system executes the policy cancellation. According to a GoAuto deponent, the APAC computer system communicates with a GoAuto web service as follows: During cancellation, the APAC system “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, once the GoAuto computer system initiates the cancellation process. At that point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the hard copies of the cancellation documents. To reiterate our earlier citation of the statutory language: prior to cancellation, the insurer must have “receipt” of the notice of cancellation that the premium finance company sends. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The statute is contemporary and takes into account the roles that company computers now perform, functions that were previously managed by people. GoAuto’s approach to receiving and acting on notice meets the statutory requirements. We also note that the district court concluded GoAuto’s cancellation process complied with the statute on an alternative ground: specifically, it reasoned that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” Noting the lack of Louisiana jurisprudence construing the term “private carrier,” the district court found persuasive a Fifth Circuit panel’s discussion on when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was inventive and not typical. However, since we conclude the e-mail satisfies statutory requirements, we do not reach the question of whether the district court’s definition is correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we submit these questions of statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that the correct understanding of these statutory terms is significant for Louisiana’s insurance sector and regulatory framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The absence of a definition by the state supreme court of a term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Thus, we choose not to certify the question. Since GoAuto’s practices are in accordance with the statutory requirements, the district court’s denial of summary judgment was not in error. AFFIRMED.
