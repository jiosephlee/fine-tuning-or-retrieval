Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana law concerning the cancellation of insurance coverage due to failure to pay premiums that had been financed. The appellants, who were previously insured, assert that the insurer failed to follow the statutory cancellation requirements, leading to allegations of false statements and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court found no breach of the statute and dismissed the case with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another GoAuto policyholder, who is not involved in this case, was in a collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the individual from Jenkins’s accident all arranged financing for their GoAuto insurance premiums via American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under the terms of these agreements, APAC paid GoAuto the total premium upfront and, in turn, the insureds made monthly installment payments to APAC. Each agreement conferred upon APAC a power of attorney, permitting APAC to cancel the respective insurance policy should there be a failure to pay premiums. The cancellation of each policy followed instances of nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins subsequently initiated a class action suit in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, contending that GoAuto did not properly cancel their insurance policies and failed to discharge its obligation of good faith. Wright was later added as a plaintiff through an amended complaint. GoAuto subsequently removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties presented cross-motions for summary judgment confined exclusively to the question of whether GoAuto’s process for canceling financed policies adhered to Louisiana law. The district court found in favor of GoAuto on summary judgment, ruling that GoAuto’s cancellation practices conformed to state law. Consequently, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright appealed this decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs assert that the district court made mistakes by (1) determining that the statutory requirements for issuing the notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) concluding that GoAuto duly received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. In order to resolve these points, we will first outline the relevant Louisiana law concerning the cancellation of insurance policies purchased with financing, and then review how GoAuto sought to adhere to that legal framework. Louisiana law permits an insurance-premium finance company to initiate the cancellation of an insurance policy when a default remains unremedied, "by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" the following conditions: (i) That the premium finance agreement includes a legitimate power of attorney as described in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) That a default has occurred under the premium finance agreement and remains unresolved; (iii) That upon default, the premium finance company sent a cancellation notice to the insured pursuant to Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating when the notice was sent; and (iv) That duplicates of the cancellation notice were sent to every individual listed in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any possible loss, with the notice specifying the names and addresses of relevant government agencies, security interest holders, or other third parties who also received notice from the insurance premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) There is currently a default on the Premium Finance Agreement, and this default has not been remedied within the required time frame. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured party. Attached are copies of these notices; an affidavit verifying mailing can be provided upon request. (4) All individuals or entities identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss have been sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

When that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system processes the cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, on the same morning, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the cancellation documents that have been printed. Before 2017, an APAC employee would physically deliver these documents to a filing cabinet jointly used by both GoAuto and APAC. Since 2017, however, the signed cancellation document is instead physically delivered to a GoAuto employee, who then stamps the document as “received” and stores it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next examine whether these procedures are consistent with the Louisiana statute previously discussed. Central to the Plaintiffs’ position is the assertion that APAC does not place a signature on the certification until shortly after, yet definitively following, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs maintain that the statutory mandate for “certifying” by the premium insurer requires that a signature be affixed prior to any cancellation taking effect. The statute, however, does not specifically mention a signature requirement. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Any signature prerequisite would have to be implied from the term “certifying.” Except for a particular exception to be addressed below, no Louisiana court decision analyzing the procedures of insurance-premium finance companies under the statute has resolved whether a signature is necessary. For instance, one court rephrased the statutory obligation as necessitating “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another opinion upheld cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). The remaining case law provides little guidance on this particular issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

A court of appeal referenced the absence of a signature as one of the deficiencies in a notice, observing that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court did not hold that the mere lack of a signature would itself invalidate the cancellation if the notice otherwise satisfied necessary requirements. In the current matter, similar to Benitez, the notices do not indicate which employee prepared or signed them. Unlike in Benitez, though, each notice here expressly “certifies” the pertinent information. With no clearer authority from Louisiana courts, we find no justification to conclude a company may not certify information without designating a specific employee. The statute expressly provides that it is “the insurance premium finance company” that accomplishes cancellation by “certifying” specific information in its notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Courts are not authorized to supplement the procedural requirements for cancellation under the guise of “strict adherence.” We therefore hold that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on notices issued by the premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs additionally argue that GoAuto’s procedures do not fully comply with the statute because GoAuto itself did not receive a request for cancellation. The statute directs that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs point out that, prior to 2017, the room where cancellation requests were kept belonged to GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto. After 2017, they claim, GoAuto does not receive these requests because an employee of APAC is responsible for placing the stamp. Plaintiffs interpret the statutory term “receipt,” instead of merely requiring the request to be sent, as necessitating that GoAuto actually physically obtains the notice before cancelling the policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined “receipt” for purposes of this statute, nor does the statute itself supply a definition. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Relying on these definitions, we find that GoAuto is entitled to cancel a policy after it comes into possession, custody, or has taken delivery of a cancellation request.

As we discussed previously, when GoAuto’s computer system receives the e-mailed notice in the early morning, the policy is cancelled by that system. A GoAuto representative described the way in which the APAC computer system communicates with a GoAuto web service, stating: For example, in the course of the cancellation process, the APAC process . . . contacts a GoAuto web service to request or perform a cancellation for a policy scheduled to cancel on that specific date. . . . Essentially, a web service functions as a listener; it continuously awaits a call and, upon receiving it, asks for instructions and, if able to process the instructions, executes the requested action.

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, after GoAuto’s computer system completes the cancellation process. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. To reiterate our prior summary of the relevant statute: before cancellation can occur, the insurer must have had “receipt” of the cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute reflects modern realities, wherein tasks formerly performed by people are now managed by corporate computer systems. GoAuto’s process for receiving and acting upon notice meets the statutory requirement. We also note that the district court determined GoAuto’s cancellation process satisfied the statute for a different reason, stating that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed that there is no Louisiana case law clarifying “private carrier,” and it relied by analogy on a panel from this court describing when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was both novel and unusual. However, since we conclude that the e-mail satisfies the statutory requirement, we need not address the soundness of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to submit these questions regarding statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that resolving the meaning of the statutory language is significant for both Louisiana’s insurance sector and its legislative framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that the state supreme court has yet to construe a given term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore decline to certify these issues. Because GoAuto’s practices conform to the statutory requirements, the district court correctly rejected the motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
