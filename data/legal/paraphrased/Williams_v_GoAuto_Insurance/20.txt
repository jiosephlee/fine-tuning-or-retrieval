Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana statute regulating the cancellation of insurance policies for nonpayment to premium finance companies. The former policyholders assert that the insurance company's cancellation procedures failed to conform to statutory requirements, thereby constituting misrepresentations and violating duties of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no statutory infraction had occurred and dismissed every claim with prejudice. We AFFIRM.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, are individuals who previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this litigation, was in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, also a plaintiff. Williams, Wright, and the driver who collided with Jenkins all obtained their GoAuto policies using premium financing agreements entered into with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under those agreements, APAC would pay GoAuto the full insurance premium upfront, while the insureds would make monthly installment payments to APAC. Each of these finance agreements included a power of attorney that empowered APAC to cancel the corresponding insurance policy if the insured failed to pay the required premiums. All the relevant policies were ultimately cancelled for nonpayment. Subsequently, Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, contending that GoAuto did not effectively cancel their policies and failed to act in good faith. Felita Wright was added as a plaintiff following the filing of an amended complaint. GoAuto removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides then moved for summary judgment on the single issue of whether GoAuto’s method for cancelling the financed policies conformed to Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, finding that the cancellation procedures were in compliance with state law, and accordingly dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently appealed this decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors in three respects: (1) in determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation had been satisfied, (2) in concluding that GoAuto had appropriately received the requests for cancellation, and (3) in denying their motion seeking partial summary judgment. To respond to these contentions, we first outline the relevant Louisiana law that governs cancellation of insurance policies that have been financed, and then analyze the methods GoAuto used to comply with these legal requirements. According to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company is permitted to request cancellation of an insurance policy after a default has not been remedied, “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following elements: (i) that the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as outlined in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) that the agreement is in default and that the default remains uncured; (iii) that upon default, the notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as specified in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, including the date the notice was sent by the premium finance company to the insured; and (iv) that copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all persons indicated by the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any possible loss, including specification of the names and addresses of governmental entities, security interest holders in the insured property, or other third parties to whom notice was provided by the insurance premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement has been breached, and this breach has not been remedied within the required time frame. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and the affidavit of mailing can be provided upon request. (4) All individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having a potential interest in any loss under the agreement were also sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system processes the policy for cancellation. Later on the same morning after the cancellation is effective, an employee of APAC applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation documents. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually place these documents into a filing cabinet shared by APAC and GoAuto. Starting in 2017, after the document has been signed, it is physically delivered to a GoAuto employee who stamps the document to indicate receipt, and then places it in the joint filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We now turn to the issue of whether the procedures at issue are consistent with the relevant Louisiana statute. Central to the Plaintiffs’ position is the fact that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until shortly after—though distinctly following—the automatic policy cancellation. Plaintiffs construe the statute’s “certifying” requirement by the premium insurer as demanding that a signature be present prior to effectuating cancellation. The statute itself contains no explicit mention of a signature requirement. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Any necessity for a signature would have to be inferred solely from the term “certifying.” Barring an exception which we will address, no Louisiana court analyzing the procedures of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute has considered whether a signature is necessary. In one case, the court restated the statutory command as requiring “a statement certifying compliance with” the law. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another opinion held that cancellation is permitted “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional decisions offer no further clarity on this particular point. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

A court of appeal in one case referenced the absence of a signature as one among several deficiencies with a cancellation notice, stating: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court in Benitez did not find that lacking a signature alone would invalidate a cancellation if the other statutory requirements were met. In the present matter, as in Benitez, the notices do not specify any particular employee by printed name or signature; yet, unlike in Benitez, each notice here includes a “certification” of the relevant facts. Given the absence of more specific Louisiana precedent, we find no basis to conclude that certification must be tied to an identified individual employee. The relevant statutory provision expressly assigns the act of certification to “the insurance premium finance company,” authorizing cancellation through the company “certifying” specified information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). “Strict adherence” to the cancellation procedures does not permit courts to impose requirements not present in the statute. We therefore determine that Louisiana law does not make a signature mandatory on cancellation notices sent by the premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs also argue that GoAuto’s process fails to comply strictly with the statute because GoAuto never actually receives a cancellation request. The statutory language states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, the shared document storage space for such requests was operated by GoAuto Management Services, rather than GoAuto itself. Since 2017, according to Plaintiffs, GoAuto does not obtain the requests directly, as an APAC employee is responsible for affixing the stamp. Plaintiffs maintain that the statutory use of “receipt”—rather than merely requiring that the request be sent—implies that GoAuto must physically possess the notice before it cancels the policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted “receipt” in this context, nor does the statute define the term. Black’s Law Dictionary provides that “receipt” means “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” while “receive” is defined as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Applying these definitions, we hold that GoAuto is authorized to cancel a policy upon obtaining possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As previously discussed, when the notice is received via email by GoAuto’s computer system in the early hours, the system executes the cancellation. A GoAuto deponent described the interface between the APAC computer system and a GoAuto web service as follows: for example, in the cancellation sequence, the APAC system “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual participates until later in the morning after GoAuto’s computer system completes the cancellation process. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation paperwork. Previously, we summarized the language of the statute: before cancellation, the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice issued by the premium finance company, according to La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute reflects contemporary circumstances, and company computers now perform tasks formerly carried out by people. GoAuto’s approach to both receiving the notice and acting upon it complies with the statutory requirements. We note the district court concluded that GoAuto’s procedures for cancellation satisfied the statute by a different rationale: it determined that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged the lack of Louisiana case law interpreting “private carrier” and relied on a panel decision from this court that discussed when a railroad qualified as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s characterization of “private carrier” was notably creative and outside conventional definitions. Since we conclude that the e-mail meets the statutory requirements, we do not address the correctness of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge that we certify these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. They argue that accurately construing these statutory provisions has significant implications for Louisiana's insurance framework and regulatory system. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). Furthermore, the mere fact that a state supreme court has not yet addressed the meaning of a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Accordingly, we decline to certify these questions. Since GoAuto’s procedures satisfy the statutory requirements, the district court properly denied Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
