Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana statute pertaining to the termination of insurance policies for nonpayment by the party that financed the premium. The former insured parties allege that the insurer's cancellation process failed to adhere to the statute, resulting in both misrepresentations and a breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court found no violation of the statute and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, were previously insured by GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not involved in this matter, was in an accident with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the driver in Jenkins’s accident each entered into premium financing agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”), in order to finance their GoAuto insurance policies. Under these agreements, APAC paid GoAuto the total insurance premium, while the insureds were required to make monthly installment payments to APAC. Each agreement provided APAC with a power of attorney authorizing it to cancel the insurance policy if the insureds failed to pay premiums. Nonpayment ultimately led to the cancellation of each policy. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action lawsuit against GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, contending that GoAuto had not properly cancelled their policies and had not acted in good faith. Wright was added as an additional plaintiff via an amended complaint. GoAuto removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties subsequently filed cross-motions for summary judgment addressing only the sufficiency of GoAuto’s policy cancellation procedure under Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, concluding that GoAuto’s process complied with applicable state law. Accordingly, all claims against GoAuto were dismissed with prejudice by the district court. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright appealed this decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made mistakes by (1) determining that the statutory requirements regarding delivery of the cancellation notice were satisfied, (2) concluding that GoAuto properly received the requests for cancellation, and (3) denying the Plaintiffs’ motion for partial summary judgment. To evaluate these contentions, our analysis first outlines the relevant Louisiana statutory framework for canceling insurance policies that have been financed, followed by a review of GoAuto's methods of adhering to those requirements. Louisiana law allows an insurance-premium finance company to initiate cancellation of a policy if a default is not timely cured, by providing the insurer with a copy of the cancellation notice and a statement certifying specific information—either by mail, private carrier, or electronic mail. These certifications must include: (i) Confirmation that the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney in compliance with Paragraph (1) of the Subsection, (ii) affirmation that the agreement is in default and such default remains uncured, (iii) assurance that upon default, a cancellation notice was delivered to the insured as prescribed by Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, along with the date the company sent such notice, and (iv) affirmation that copies of the cancellation notice were sent to all parties listed in the finance agreement as having a vested interest in potential losses under the policy, specifying the names and addresses of government agencies, security interest holders, or third parties who were provided notice by the premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes a lawful power of attorney. (2) There exists a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, which has not been timely remedied. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit verifying mailing can be provided upon request. (4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were also sent to all individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement.

Upon receipt of this notice, GoAuto’s computerized system processes the cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later that morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually place these documents in a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the signed cancellation documents are instead delivered directly to a GoAuto employee, who stamps them as “received” and then stores them in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn next to the question of whether these procedures align with the relevant Louisiana law previously outlined. Central to the Plaintiffs’ contention is the absence of a signature on APAC’s certification until after the policy has been automatically canceled. Plaintiffs maintain that the statute’s requirement of the premium insurer “certifying” necessitates the inclusion of a signature prior to any cancellation. The text of the statute does not specifically mention a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be mandated, such a requirement would have to be implied by the term “certifying.” Except for one instance to be examined further, Louisiana courts analyzing insurance-premium finance company processes under this statute have not addressed whether a signature is essential. One appellate court reworded the statutory requirement as a need for “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another appellate court permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional decisions do not clarify the issue any further. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, the absence of a signature was noted among the deficiencies of the notice at issue, stating the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not decide that the lack of a signature, standing alone, would render a cancellation ineffective if all other requirements are met. Consistent with Benitez, the notices in this matter also fail to list a specific employee by name, whether printed or signed. Unlike in Benitez, each notice here does include a certification of the pertinent information. Given the absence of a more definitive Louisiana precedent, we find no basis to conclude that certification by a company must be accompanied by the identification of an individual employee. The statute itself prescribes that “the insurance premium finance company” effects cancellation by “certifying” information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Demanding “strict adherence” to statutory procedures does not authorize the courts to supplement those procedures with additional requirements. Thus, we determine that Louisiana law does not require a notice from a premium finance company to the insurer to be signed.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto's cancellation processes are deficient because GoAuto did not receive the actual cancellation request. The statute states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs point out that, before 2017, the shared file room where physical requests were kept belonged to GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto. Since 2017, Plaintiffs assert that GoAuto has not received requests directly, as the stamping is performed by an APAC employee. Plaintiffs suggest that the statutory term “receipt”—rather than merely requiring the request to be sent—obliges GoAuto to physically obtain the notice prior to effectuating cancellation.

The Louisiana Supreme Court has not yet interpreted the meaning of “receipt” in this statutory context, nor does the statute provide a definition. According to a leading legal dictionary, “receipt” is defined as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” while “receive” means “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Adopting these definitions, we find that GoAuto is entitled to cancel a policy when it obtains possession, custody, or delivery of a cancellation request. As already discussed, when GoAuto’s computer system receives the e-mailed notice in the early morning, it processes the policy cancellation. A GoAuto representative described the process by which an APAC computer communicates with a GoAuto web service: during cancellation, the APAC process “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date.” The witness further explained, “a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

Human involvement occurs only later in the day, following the cancellation performed by the GoAuto computer system. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation documents. To briefly restate our earlier citation of the statute, prior to any cancellation, the insurer must have had "receipt" of a notice of cancellation provided by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary in nature and recognizes that tasks formerly undertaken by individuals are now commonly handled by company computers. GoAuto’s method of receiving and processing cancellation notices meets the requirements set by the statute. We also note that the district court found GoAuto’s procedures to be in compliance with the statute for a separate reason: “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed there was a lack of Louisiana precedent interpreting “private carrier” and considered a Fifth Circuit panel’s discussion of when a railroad qualified as a private carrier to be analogous. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s characterization of “private carrier” was both inventive and nontraditional. Since we conclude that the e-mail procedure fulfills the statutory mandate, we do not reach the question of whether this definition is valid.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to certify these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that clarifying the statutory language has significant implications for the state's insurance sector and legislative framework. However, as noted in Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997), “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Merely because a state supreme court has not previously interpreted a statutory term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Accordingly, we decline to certify the issue. Since GoAuto’s practices meet the statute’s requirements, the district court properly rejected Plaintiffs’ summary judgment motion. AFFIRMED.
