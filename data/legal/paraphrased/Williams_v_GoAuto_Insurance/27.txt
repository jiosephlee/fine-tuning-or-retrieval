Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana statute that regulates the cancellation of insurance policies due to nonpayment to a premium finance company. The appellants, who are former policyholders, assert that the insurer failed to follow the statutory requirements during the cancellation process, thereby engaging in misrepresentation and violating the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined there had been no violation of the statute and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, previously insured by GoAuto Insurance Company, serve as plaintiffs in this matter. Nicholas Jenkins, another plaintiff, was involved in a collision with a third GoAuto policyholder who is not part of this litigation. Williams, Wright, and the driver in Jenkins’s incident all purchased their insurance coverage from GoAuto by entering financing agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these agreements, APAC covered the full premium amount, while the insureds repaid APAC through monthly installments. The financing agreements included a power of attorney clause empowering APAC to terminate the insurance policies if premiums were not paid. Due to nonpayment, all the relevant insurance policies were cancelled. Subsequently, Williams and Jenkins commenced a class action lawsuit in Louisiana state court against both GoAuto and APAC, contending that GoAuto had failed to properly cancel their policies and had not fulfilled its good faith obligations. Wright was joined as a plaintiff when the complaint was amended. The action was removed by GoAuto to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The case proceeded on cross-motions for summary judgment limited to the question of whether GoAuto’s cancellation process for financed policies was in compliance with Louisiana statutory requirements. The district court granted GoAuto’s summary judgment motion, finding its cancellation practices accorded with state law, and dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright sought appellate review of that decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court erred in three respects: (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering notice of cancellation were satisfied; (2) concluding that GoAuto correctly received the cancellation requests; and (3) denying the Plaintiffs’ motion for partial summary judgment. In order to resolve these points, we first outline the Louisiana statutory framework governing the cancellation of financed insurance policies, and subsequently analyze GoAuto’s compliance with those legal mandates. Pursuant to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company may request the cancellation of an insurance policy after an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following conditions: (i) the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as required by Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) there has been a default on the premium finance agreement, which remains uncured; (iii) after the default, a notice of cancellation was issued to the insured pursuant to Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the date it was sent by the premium finance company to the insured; and (iv) copies of the notice of cancellation were provided to all parties listed in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any resulting loss, specifying the names and addresses of governmental agencies, security interest holders, or third parties to whom such notice was sent by the insurance premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes a legally effective power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement has entered into default and such default has not been cured within the required time frame. (3) Following default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured; copies of these notices are attached, and affidavit proof of mailing can be provided upon request. (4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were also mailed to each individual identified by the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss arising under it.

When that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system effectuates cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, on the same morning and after cancellation, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the generated cancellation documents. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually place these documents into a filing cabinet shared by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the signed cancellation document is physically handed to a GoAuto employee, who marks the document as “received” and then files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn next to the question of whether these procedures meet the requirements of the Louisiana statute previously addressed. Plaintiffs’ main contention centers on the absence of a signature on APAC’s certification until shortly after—though definitively subsequent to—the policy’s automatic cancellation. Plaintiffs read the statute’s mandate that the premium insurer must “certify” as requiring a signature prior to effecting cancellation. Notably, the statutory language itself does not explicitly mandate a signature. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be necessary, it would have to be implied through the meaning of “certifying.” With one notable exception, Louisiana courts addressing premium finance company procedures in connection with this statute have not confronted the specific question of whether a signature is compulsory. One court described the statute as requiring “a statement certifying compliance with” its provisions. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision permitted cancellation “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Further relevant authority offers little additional guidance. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, pp. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

An appellate court, in examining deficiencies in a notice, referenced the absence of a signature, stating: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that opinion did not find that a lack of a signature alone would invalidate the cancellation so long as the remaining statutory requirements were met. In the present record, as in Benitez, the notices do not indicate any specific employee by either printed name or signature. However, contrary to Benitez, each notice here contains a “certification” of the pertinent facts. In the absence of more explicit Louisiana precedent, we see no basis to prohibit a corporation from certifying facts without associating the certification to a named employee. The statute itself specifies that “the insurance premium finance company” executes cancellation by “certifying” information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Courts’ requirement for “strict adherence” to cancellation procedure does not authorize them to supplement statutory procedures. We therefore find no provision in Louisiana law requiring a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s process does not strictly follow the statute because GoAuto did not physically receive a request for cancellation. According to the statute, “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, the physical storage location for printed requests belonged to GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto. Plaintiffs maintain that after 2017, GoAuto does not receive requests because the stamping employee works for APAC. They assert that the legislature’s selection of the word “receipt,” rather than merely requiring transmission, obligates GoAuto to have actual possession of the notice before proceeding with cancellation.

The Louisiana Supreme Court has not clarified the meaning of “receipt” under the statute, nor is there a statutory definition. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession," and “receive” as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). We apply these definitions and determine that GoAuto can effect cancellation upon acquiring possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request. As previously discussed, after an e-mailed notice is received in the early morning by GoAuto’s computer, cancellation is executed by that computer system. A GoAuto deponent described how an APAC computer system communicates with a GoAuto web service: For example, in the cancellation process, “the APAC process . . . makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual participates until later in the morning, following the GoAuto computer system's execution of the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. To restate our prior recitation of the statute: what must occur before cancellation is that the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary, and tasks once performed by people are now often managed by company computer systems. The procedure employed by GoAuto to receive notice and process it fulfills the statutory requirement. Additionally, we note that the district court concluded GoAuto’s cancellation procedures met the statute by different reasoning: it stated, “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed that there is no Louisiana jurisprudence on “private carrier” and found persuasive an opinion from this court panel analyzing when a railroad functions as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was creative and unusual. As we determine the e-mail satisfies the statute, we need not address whether that definition was correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to certify these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that resolving the meaning of these statutory terms holds significant implications for Louisiana’s insurance framework and statutory structure. However, “As a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The fact that a term lacks definition by a state supreme court “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Accordingly, we decline the request for certification. Since GoAuto’s procedures adhere to the statutory requirements, the district court did not commit error in rejecting the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
