Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana statute that addresses the cancellation of insurance policies due to the insureds' failure to pay the premium finance company. The former insureds contend that the insurer’s methods of cancellation did not conform to statutory requirements, and as a result, allege misrepresentation as well as a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that no violation of the statute occurred and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this litigation, was in a collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the driver from Jenkins’s incident each arranged to finance their GoAuto insurance premiums through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under this arrangement, APAC would remit the full premium amount to GoAuto, and the insureds were responsible for making monthly installment payments to APAC. Each financing contract conveyed a power of attorney to APAC, enabling it to cancel the respective insurance policies should the insureds fail to make the required payments. Due to nonpayment, all the relevant policies were cancelled. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action in Louisiana state court against both GoAuto and APAC, alleging that GoAuto did not properly cancel their insurance policies and failed to act in good faith. Wright was later added as a plaintiff through an amended complaint. The case was subsequently removed by GoAuto to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties then submitted cross-motions for summary judgment, which addressed only whether GoAuto’s method of cancelling the financed insurance policies complied with Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, concluding that its cancellation procedures met the requirements of state law. Consequently, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright then filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made mistakes by (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) concluding that GoAuto appropriately received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. In order to respond to these contentions, we first outline the Louisiana statutory framework governing the cancellation of financed insurance policies, and then review GoAuto’s methods for compliance with those legal requirements. Pursuant to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company may initiate cancellation of an insurance policy following an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” several conditions: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as outlined in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection. (ii) The premium finance agreement has gone into default and the default has not been cured within the permitted time. (iii) After the default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as required in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, identifying the date the premium finance company sent the notice to the insured. (iv) Copies of the cancellation notice were distributed to every party identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in potential losses under the policy, listing the names and addresses of any governmental entities, security interest holders in the insured property, or third parties who received a cancellation notice from the insurance premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate exact compliance with the statutory provisions whenever a finance company utilizes its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). Any irregularity in following this procedure leads to the cancellation being invalid. Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). The requirement for strict compliance fulfills two purposes: first, it establishes a definitive procedure for determining the precise moment of cancellation; and second, it guarantees a minimum period in which the insured can remedy the default. Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). In accordance with these requirements, GoAuto has established certain procedures to effectuate a policy cancellation. When an insured does not pay on time, APAC issues a notice warning the insured that the policy will be cancelled unless payment is made within ten days. Should payment not be received by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s automated system generates an e-mail to GoAuto attaching the notice of cancellation and a formal request for cancellation. This cancellation request is presented on APAC’s letterhead and contains the following certification from APAC:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) There has been a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and such default remains uncured within the prescribed period. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit attesting to mailing can be produced if required. (4) All parties indicated by the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss thereunder received copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

When that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system terminates the policy. Following the cancellation, later that same morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually deliver the documents to a filing cabinet jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. Starting in 2017, the signed cancellation document is instead handed directly to a GoAuto employee, who then stamps it as “received” and stores it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn to the question of whether these procedures satisfy the requirements of the Louisiana statute previously discussed. The Plaintiffs’ central contention is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until shortly after, but definitively subsequent to, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs read the statutory mandate for the premium insurer to “certify” as imposing a prerequisite that a signature must be present before any cancellation can occur. However, the statutory text itself does not expressly demand a signature. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be required, it would have to be derived from the meaning of “certifying.” With one exception addressed below, Louisiana case law interpreting the procedures of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute has not confronted the issue of whether a signature is necessary. In one case, the court restated the statutory obligation as requiring “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another court permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional decisions are likewise unilluminating on this particular issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, the absence of a signature was noted among the deficiencies of a cancellation notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not determine that a missing signature alone would render a cancellation ineffective if other statutory requirements were met. Similar to Benitez, the notices present in this matter also lack identification of any individual employee, whether by printed name or signature. In contrast to Benitez, though, each of these notices does “certify” the relevant information. Given the absence of clearer precedent in Louisiana, we find no basis for concluding that a company is unable to certify information solely because it does not identify an employee by name. The statute expressly provides that it is “the insurance premium finance company” that effects cancellation by “certifying” specific information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Requiring “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not mean courts may add further requirements beyond those set forth in the statute. Therefore, we hold that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the cancellation notice that a premium finance company sends to an insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s procedures fall short of the statute’s requirements because GoAuto never actually receives a cancellation request. The statutory language specifies: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, the shared room where printed cancellation requests were kept was controlled by GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself. Since 2017, Plaintiffs assert, GoAuto does not take delivery of these requests, as the employee applying the stamp works for APAC. Plaintiffs maintain that the legislature’s use of the word “receipt,” rather than merely requiring a request be sent, means that GoAuto must physically receive the notice before cancelling the policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not provided an interpretation of “receipt” within this statute, nor does the statute supply its own definition. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Relying on these definitions, we determine that GoAuto is authorized to cancel a policy once it comes into possession, obtains delivery, or otherwise takes custody of a cancellation request. As previously described, once the e-mailed notice is received by GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning, that system carries out the cancellation. A GoAuto representative detailed the interaction between APAC’s computer system and a GoAuto web service, explaining: For example, during the cancellation workflow, the APAC system calls a GoAuto web service to request or process a cancellation for a policy scheduled to cancel on a given date. Essentially, the web service functions as a listener, continually waiting to be called; when it receives the appropriate signal, it asks what action is needed, and if it recognizes the request, it carries out the activity.

Human involvement occurs only after the GoAuto computer system processes the cancellation later in the morning. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation paperwork. To reiterate our previous discussion of the statutory language: before cancellation, the insurer must have “receipt” of the notice of cancellation provided by the premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute reflects modern circumstances in which corporate computer systems handle many tasks formerly performed by people. GoAuto’s approach to receiving and acting upon the notice meets the statutory requirements. Additionally, the district court concluded that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with the statute by different reasoning: it determined that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed that there was no Louisiana precedent construing the term “private carrier” and regarded as analogous a panel decision from this court discussing when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was creative and atypical. Since we hold that the e-mail fulfills the statutory requirements, we do not address whether this definition is proper.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to refer these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court for certification. They argue that resolving the meaning of these statutory terms holds significant consequences for Louisiana’s insurance sector and legislative framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a state supreme court has yet to define a term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore refuse to certify the question. Since GoAuto’s procedures adhere to the statutory requirements, the district court properly denied Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
