Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana statute concerning the cancellation of insurance policies due to nonpayment to the premium finance company. The former policyholders allege that the insurer’s method of cancellation failed to adhere to the statutory requirements, amounting to misrepresentation and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no statutory breach occurred and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company, serve as the plaintiffs in this case. Another individual, also insured by GoAuto but not a party to this action, was involved in a collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the driver implicated in Jenkins’s incident all obtained financing for their GoAuto insurance policies through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under the arrangement, APAC paid the full policy premium directly to GoAuto, while the insureds agreed to make monthly installment payments to APAC. The terms of each financing agreement conferred a power of attorney upon APAC, allowing it to cancel the relevant insurance policies in the event of nonpayment. Ultimately, all of the policies were cancelled due to failure to pay. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, alleging that GoAuto had failed to properly cancel their insurance policies and had not acted in good faith. Wright was added as a plaintiff following the filing of an amended complaint. GoAuto removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides submitted motions for summary judgment limited to the issue of whether GoAuto’s method of cancelling financed insurance policies conformed to Louisiana statutory requirements. The district court ruled in favor of GoAuto by granting summary judgment, finding that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures were in accordance with state law. Consequently, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright then filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made mistakes in three respects: (1) concluding that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation were satisfied; (2) determining that GoAuto appropriately received the requests for cancellation; and (3) denying their motion for partial summary judgment. In considering these claims, we first outline the relevant provisions of Louisiana law concerning the cancellation of insurance policies that have been financed, followed by a review of GoAuto’s methods for complying with those statutory provisions. According to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company may initiate the cancellation of an insurance policy due to an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following points: (i) that the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as described in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) that the premium finance agreement is in default and the default remains uncured; (iii) that after the default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured in accordance with Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the date it was sent by the premium finance company; and (iv) that copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all individuals identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in potential losses under the policy, listing the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, secured parties with an interest in the insured property, or other third parties to whom the insurance premium finance company provided the cancellation notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate exact compliance with statutory provisions when a finance company utilizes its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). If there is any flaw in this procedure, it leads to an invalid cancellation of the policy. See Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). The requirement of strict compliance with the statute fulfills two objectives: first, it establishes a definitive means to determine the precise date and time of cancellation, and second, it guarantees a minimum period within which the default may be remedied. Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). The procedures GoAuto implements for cancelling a policy are as follows. Should an insured fail to remit payment on time, APAC issues a notice informing the insured that cancellation will occur if payment is not made within ten days. If payment remains outstanding as of midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system sends an e-mail to GoAuto that includes a copy of the cancellation notice and a request for cancellation. This request, sent on APAC letterhead, contains the following certification from APAC:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney provision. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement has been breached and the breach remains uncured within the required time period. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit attesting to mailing can be provided upon request. (4) All individuals or entities identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement were also sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system terminates the policy. Subsequently, later the same morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC staff member would manually deliver these documents to a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the signed cancellation document is instead physically delivered to a GoAuto employee, who marks it as “received” with a stamp before placing it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We now turn to the question of whether these procedures satisfy the requirements of Louisiana law previously addressed. The Plaintiffs’ central contention is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until shortly after, but definitely subsequent to, the automatic cancellation of the policy. According to the Plaintiffs, the statutory mandate that the premium insurer “certify” necessitates a signature prior to effectuating a cancellation. However, the statute itself contains no explicit mention of a signature requirement. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If such a requirement exists, it must be inferred solely from the use of the word “certifying.” With one exception to be discussed, Louisiana courts considering insurance-premium finance company practices under this statute have not squarely addressed whether a signature is necessary. For example, one court restated the statutory obligation as needing “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional decisions provide no greater clarity on the issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate case, the opinion discussed the absence of a signature as one of the deficiencies of a notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not determine that a missing signature alone would render a cancellation invalid if the other statutory requirements were satisfied. Similarly, in the present matter, the notices do not include the printed name or signature of any employee. Unlike in Benitez, however, each notice here “certifies” the pertinent information. In the absence of more definitive guidance from Louisiana courts, we see no basis to conclude that a company must identify an employee by name in order to certify the information. The statutory language specifies that “the insurance premium finance company” effects cancellation by “certifying” certain facts in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The call for “strict adherence” to the statutory procedure does not empower courts to impose additional requirements. We therefore find that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the cancellation notice provided by the premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s procedures fall short of strict statutory compliance because GoAuto allegedly never receives the cancellation request. According to the statute, “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs point out that, before 2017, printed requests were stored in a shared filing room owned by GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto. Since 2017, they contend GoAuto does not “receive” the requests because an APAC employee is responsible for stamping them. Plaintiffs assert that the legislature’s use of “receipt,” rather than merely requiring that the request be sent, obligates GoAuto to obtain possession of the notice before effecting cancellation. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not clarified the meaning of “receipt” in this context, nor does the statute define it. Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Relying on these definitions, we conclude GoAuto may cancel a policy upon acquiring possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request.

As previously detailed, once GoAuto’s computer system receives the e-mailed notice in the early morning hours, that system initiates cancellation of the policy. A GoAuto deponent described the interaction between the APAC computer system and a GoAuto web service as follows: during the cancellation process, the APAC process “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until sometime later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system has already processed the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee affixes a signature stamp to the printed cancellation documents. To reiterate our earlier summary of the statutory provision: before a cancellation can occur, the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company, pursuant to La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary and acknowledges that tasks formerly performed by people are now frequently handled by computers. GoAuto’s procedure for receiving and acting upon the notice meets the requirements of the statute. Additionally, we note the district court determined that GoAuto’s cancellation process complied with the statute for a separate reason: it found that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed that there is no Louisiana precedent interpreting “private carrier” and considered a panel decision from this court that discussed when a railroad constituted a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was creative and atypical. Because we hold that the e-mail transmission meets the statutory requirement, we decline to address whether that definition was proper.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to certify these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that the correct reading of these statutory terms carries significant implications for Louisiana’s insurance regulatory framework. However, as we have stated, “As a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The fact that a state supreme court has yet to construe a term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Therefore, we decline to certify the question. Since GoAuto’s practices satisfy the statutory requirements, the district court correctly denied the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
