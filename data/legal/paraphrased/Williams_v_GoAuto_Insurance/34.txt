Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

This case presents a question regarding interpretation of a Louisiana law related to the cancellation of insurance policies due to nonpayment by the premium finance company. The former policyholders allege that the insurer's methods of cancellation failed to satisfy the statute’s requirements, which, according to them, amounted to misrepresentation and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no violation of the statute had occurred and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company, brought this action as plaintiffs. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this litigation, was in a collision involving Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the driver from Jenkins’s incident had each arranged for their GoAuto policy premiums to be financed through agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these agreements, APAC paid the full premium amount to GoAuto upfront and, in exchange, the insureds paid APAC back in monthly installments. As part of each agreement, the insureds granted APAC a power of attorney enabling it to cancel their respective policies if they defaulted on payments. Due to nonpayment, each policy was cancelled. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, claiming that GoAuto had not validly cancelled their coverage and had breached its duty of good faith. Wright joined as an additional plaintiff following the filing of an amended complaint. GoAuto subsequently removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides submitted motions for summary judgment focusing exclusively on the question of whether GoAuto’s methods for cancelling the financed policies were consistent with Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, determining that GoAuto’s procedures were in accordance with the relevant state law. Consequently, the court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright appealed from this judgment.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made mistakes by (1) determining that the statutory methods for delivering notice of cancellation were adhered to, (2) concluding that GoAuto appropriately received the cancellation requests, and (3) denying their motion for partial summary judgment. To evaluate these assertions, we first outline the relevant Louisiana statutory framework concerning the cancellation of financed insurance policies, before reviewing GoAuto’s compliance procedures. Pursuant to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company has the authority to request the cancellation of an insurance policy following an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following points: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as stated in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection. (ii) The premium finance agreement is in default, and the default has not been cured in a timely fashion. (iii) Upon default, the insured was provided with a notice of cancellation as described in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, including the date when the notice was sent to the insured by the premium finance company. (iv) Copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all individuals indicated in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss, and those copies specified the names and addresses of any government agencies, holders of security interests in the insured property, or other third parties who were sent notice of cancellation by the insurance premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) There is a current default on the Premium Finance Agreement which has not been remedied within the required period. (3) Following default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued and sent to the insured; enclosures include copies of these notices, and the affidavit evidencing mailing can be provided upon request. (4) The Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was also sent to every person identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement.

When this notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system effectuates cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later the same morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. Before 2017, an APAC employee would physically transport the documents to a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. From 2017 onward, the signed cancellation document is delivered in person to a GoAuto employee, who then stamps the document as “received” and places it in a shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We now turn to the issue of whether these procedures satisfy the Louisiana statute previously examined. Central to the Plaintiffs’ position is the fact that APAC’s certification bears no signature until just after, but distinctly following, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs contend that the statutory mandate of certification by the premium insurer necessitates the presence of a signature prior to cancellation. However, the statute does not explicitly require a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If such a requirement exists, it would have to arise solely from the meaning of the term “certifying.” Except for an instance to be addressed, Louisiana courts considering the practices of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute have not decided whether a signature is essential. One appellate court summarized the statute as requiring “a statement certifying compliance with” its provisions. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision upheld cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other cases likewise fail to shed light on the signature issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate case, the absence of a signature was cited as one of several deficiencies in a cancellation notice: the notice “does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court in Benitez did not conclude that merely lacking a signature would render the cancellation ineffective if other statutory requirements were satisfied. In this case, similar to Benitez, the notices do not indicate the identity of any employee by name or signature. Yet, unlike Benitez, the notices here do include a certification of the pertinent information. In the absence of more definitive Louisiana authority, we discern no basis for requiring the information to be certified exclusively by a named individual. The relevant statutory language specifies that “the insurance premium finance company” is to effectuate cancellation by “certifying” certain facts in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Demanding “strict adherence” to cancellation requirements does not empower the judiciary to impose additional requirements not found in the statute. Thus, we determine that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer. 

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto failed to strictly comply with the statutory procedure because GoAuto allegedly never received a cancellation request. The statute in question provides that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Prior to 2017, printed cancellation requests were kept in a shared filing room managed by GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto itself. Since 2017, plaintiffs contend, GoAuto has not received requests because the APAC employee is the one who applies the stamp. Plaintiffs assert that the legislature’s use of the word “receipt,” rather than merely requiring the request be sent, means GoAuto must physically receive the notice to lawfully cancel the policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted “receipt” for this statute, nor is there a statutory definition. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and defines “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Adopting these definitions, we find that GoAuto is authorized to cancel a policy when it comes into possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As described previously, the GoAuto computer system cancels the policy upon receiving the e-mailed notice in the early morning. A GoAuto witness explained the interaction of an APAC computer system with a GoAuto web service: During the cancellation process, APAC’s system “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until later in the day, after the GoAuto computer system completes the cancellation process. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. We reiterate our earlier summary of the statutory requirements. Before cancellation can occur, the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice that is sent by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This is a contemporary statute, reflective of present-day practices, in which company computers now perform many tasks previously carried out by people. The process by which GoAuto receives and processes the notice fulfills the statutory requirements. We note the district court determined GoAuto’s cancellation procedures were in accordance with the statute for a different reason: it reasoned that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged that there is no Louisiana case law interpreting “private carrier,” and instead found persuasive a decision by another panel of this court addressing when a railroad constituted a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court's construction of “private carrier” was both creative and novel. Since we conclude that the email procedure satisfies the statutory requirement, we need not address the soundness of the district court’s interpretation.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we certify these questions regarding statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court. They argue that accurately construing these statutory terms holds significance for both Louisiana’s insurance industry and its statutory framework. However, “As a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a state supreme court has not yet defined a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore refuse to certify this issue. Since GoAuto’s procedures adhere to the relevant statutory provisions, the district court’s denial of their motion for summary judgment was not erroneous. The judgment is AFFIRMED.
