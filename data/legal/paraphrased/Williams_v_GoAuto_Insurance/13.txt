Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are tasked with interpreting a Louisiana law that regulates the termination of insurance policies due to the insured’s failure to pay the premium finance company. The former policyholders argue that the insurer's process for canceling their policies violated this law, leading to alleged misrepresentation and breaches of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no violation of the statute had occurred and dismissed all of the plaintiffs' claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not involved in this case, was in an accident with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Each of Williams, Wright, and the driver from Jenkins’s accident used financing agreements through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”) to pay for their GoAuto insurance policies. Under these agreements, APAC paid the full premium amount to GoAuto and, in exchange, the insureds agreed to make monthly payments to APAC. The agreements also conferred a power of attorney on APAC, empowering it to cancel the insurance policies if the insureds defaulted on payments. Each of their insurance policies was cancelled due to nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins brought a class action suit in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto did not properly effectuate cancellation of their policies and did not fulfill its duty of good faith. Wright was added as a plaintiff through an amended complaint. GoAuto removed the lawsuit to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties submitted cross-motions for summary judgment focused exclusively on the adequacy of GoAuto's procedure for cancelling financed policies under Louisiana law. The district court entered summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, concluding that its cancellation methods were consistent with state requirements. Consequently, the court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently appealed.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by (1) concluding that the statutory process for delivering the notice of cancellation was adhered to, (2) determining that GoAuto appropriately received the requests for cancellation, and (3) denying the Plaintiffs’ motion for partial summary judgment. In order to assess these assertions, we first outline the relevant Louisiana law concerning the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium finance agreements, before analyzing GoAuto’s compliance procedures. Louisiana statutes provide that an insurance-premium finance company is authorized to request policy cancellation due to an unresolved default "by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail... a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a statement certifying" the following conditions: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney in accordance with Paragraph (1) of this Subsection. (ii) The premium finance agreement is in default, and the default has not been timely remedied. (iii) After default, a cancellation notice was sent to the insured consistent with Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the date the notice was sent by the premium finance company to the insured. (iv) Copies of the cancellation notice were sent to every individual identified by the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the policy, specifying the names and addresses of governmental agencies, security interest holders, or third parties who were provided with the cancellation notice by the insurance premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate strict compliance with statutory procedures when a finance company employs its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). Furthermore, “any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). The purpose of such strict statutory compliance is twofold: “(1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). In practice, GoAuto implements the following process for policy cancellation. When an insured does not make a timely premium payment, APAC issues a notice to the insured stating that the policy will be cancelled unless payment is received within ten days. Should the payment not be received by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically generates an e-mail to GoAuto, which includes both a copy of the cancellation notice and a cancellation request. The cancellation request appears on APAC’s letterhead and contains the following certification: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) There exists a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and this default has not been remedied within the designated time period. (3) Following the occurrence of the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was dispatched to the insured; enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit attesting to the mailing can be provided if requested. (4) All individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss covered thereby also received copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system terminates the policy. Following the cancellation, later the same morning, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually deliver these documents to a filing cabinet used by both GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the signed cancellation document has instead been delivered to a GoAuto employee, who marks it as “received” with a stamp and then files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next examine whether these procedures align with the Louisiana statute previously analyzed. The Plaintiffs’ principal contention is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until immediately after, but undeniably subsequent to, the automatic policy cancellation. According to the Plaintiffs, the statutory mandate that the premium insurer “certify” requires the existence of a signature before a cancellation can lawfully occur. The statutory text itself does not specifically mention any signature requirement. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If such a requirement exists, it must arise solely from the statute’s use of the word “certifying.” Apart from an exception addressed later in this opinion, Louisiana case law reviewing the practices of insurance-premium finance companies under this provision has not squarely decided whether a signature is necessary. One court has paraphrased the requirement as the need for “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision found cancellation permissible “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional authorities similarly provide no clear answer to this issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, pp. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate case, the court referred to the lack of a signature while discussing the deficiencies of that notice, stating the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. The court, however, did not rule that merely lacking a signature would invalidate a cancellation notice, provided all other requirements are satisfied. In this matter, similar to Benitez, the notices lack both a specific employee’s name and a signature. In contrast to Benitez, the present notices do include a “certification” of the pertinent information. Given the absence of authoritative Louisiana case law addressing this exact issue, there is no clear justification to require that such certification only be done by an identified employee. The statutory language specifies that “the insurance premium finance company” accomplishes cancellation by “certifying” particular facts in its notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The mandate for “strict adherence” to cancellation protocols does not empower courts to impose additional procedural steps not set forth in the statute. Accordingly, we determine Louisiana law does not make a signature mandatory on cancellation notices from premium finance companies to insurers.

The Plaintiffs further argue GoAuto did not strictly comply with the statutory procedures, asserting that GoAuto never actually received a cancellation request. The statute in question states that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs note that, prior to 2017, cancellation requests were maintained in a shared filing area belonging to GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto. Plaintiffs assert that since 2017, GoAuto itself does not “receive” cancellation requests because an APAC employee is responsible for affixing the stamp. Plaintiffs contend the legislative use of the word “receipt,” rather than requiring that notices merely be sent, obliges GoAuto to take physical possession of a cancellation request prior to effectuating cancellation. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted “receipt” as used in this statute, and the statute provides no definition. Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Relying on these definitions, we hold that GoAuto is authorized to cancel a policy once it comes into possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As previously outlined, once the notice is emailed to GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning, that system effectuates the cancellation. A GoAuto deponent described the integration between APAC’s computer process and GoAuto’s web service by explaining: during the cancellation workflow, APAC’s process will “make a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual participates until after GoAuto’s computer system processes the cancellation later in the morning. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. To restate our previous summary of the statute: before cancellation, the insurer must have “receipt” of notice of cancellation provided by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary, and in the current era, many tasks formerly performed by people are now handled by company computers. The approach GoAuto uses to receive the notice and respond complies with statutory requirements. We note the district court held that GoAuto’s cancellation process met statutory mandates for a different reason: “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged a lack of Louisiana precedent regarding the interpretation of “private carrier,” and found instructive a panel of this court discussing when a railroad constitutes a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s understanding of “private carrier” was creative and unusual. As we hold that the e-mail meets the statutory requirement, we do not reach the question of whether that definition is valid.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to certify these questions concerning statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court, asserting that determining the correct meaning of the statutory terms holds significance for the state’s insurance regulatory framework. However, “[a]s a general rule, we are reluctant to certify legal questions unless there is a compelling justification; the option to certify is a valuable tool for this court, and overreliance on it could jeopardize its continued use.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that the state’s highest court has not previously construed a term “does not, by itself, justify certification.” Id. Accordingly, we choose not to certify this issue. Since GoAuto’s practices are in accordance with the statutory requirements, the district court properly denied the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
