Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

This case calls for an examination of a Louisiana statute that relates to the termination of insurance policies due to the nonpayment of premiums to the company providing premium financing. The plaintiffs, who are former policyholders, contend that the insurer’s process for canceling their policies failed to adhere to statutory requirements, thereby amounting to false representations and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court found no infraction of the statute and dismissed all the plaintiffs’ claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company, are the plaintiffs in this case. Nicholas Jenkins, another plaintiff, was in an accident with a third individual who also held a GoAuto policy but is not a party to this action. Williams, Wright, and the individual involved in Jenkins’s accident each entered into premium finance agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”) to finance their GoAuto insurance premiums. Through these arrangements, APAC paid the premiums up front to GoAuto, and the insured parties repaid APAC in monthly installments. The financing agreements gave APAC a power of attorney empowering it to cancel the relevant insurance policies if the insured failed to make payments. All three policies were eventually cancelled for nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a putative class action in a Louisiana state court against both GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto had improperly cancelled their policies and failed to meet its obligations of good faith. Wright was later added as a plaintiff through an amended complaint. GoAuto subsequently removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties then cross-moved for summary judgment on the narrow issue of whether GoAuto’s method of cancelling these premium-financed policies conformed to Louisiana law. The district court ruled in favor of GoAuto, concluding that its cancellation process met the requirements of state law, and therefore entered summary judgment in GoAuto’s favor, dismissing all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright appealed the judgment.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made three errors: first, in determining that the statutory procedures for providing notice of cancellation were observed; second, in finding that GoAuto appropriately received the requests to cancel; and third, in refusing to grant their motion for partial summary judgment. To resolve these issues, we first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes governing the cancellation of insurance policies that are financed, and then review the steps GoAuto took to comply with those statutes. According to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company is authorized to request policy cancellation in the event of an unremedied default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following requirements: (i) the agreement to finance the premium includes a valid power of attorney as detailed in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) there is a default on the premium finance agreement, and that default has not been timely cured; (iii) following default, a notice of cancellation was provided to the insured in accordance with Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, and the date when the premium finance company sent the notice to the insured is specified; and (iv) notices of cancellation were distributed to every person shown in the agreement as having an interest in potential losses under the policy, identifying by name and address all governmental agencies, security interest holders in the insured property, or other third parties to whom the finance company sent notice of cancellation. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement has entered into default and said default remains uncured within the applicable timeframe. (3) Following the occurrence of a default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was dispatched to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and the affidavit of proof of mailing may be provided upon request. (4) All individuals listed in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement also received copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is issued, GoAuto’s computerized system processes the policy cancellation. Subsequently, on the same morning following the cancellation, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the cancellation documents that have been printed. Before 2017, an APAC employee would physically store the documents in a filing cabinet jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. Commencing in 2017, the procedure changed so that the executed cancellation document is delivered by hand to a GoAuto employee, who then stamps the document as “received” and files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next address whether these procedures adhere to the provisions of Louisiana law previously outlined. Central to the Plaintiffs’ position is the fact that APAC’s certification does not bear a signature until shortly after, though definitively following, the point at which the policy is automatically canceled. Plaintiffs contend that the statute’s mandate for “certifying” by the premium insurer necessitates that a signature be present prior to any cancellation taking place. However, the statute itself does not specifically mention a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If a signature were indeed necessary, such a mandate would have to be read into the meaning of “certifying.” Except for one decision to be discussed subsequently, Louisiana courts examining insurance-premium finance companies’ practices under this statute have not tackled the issue of whether a signature is required. One court summarized the statutory mandate as requiring “a statement certifying compliance with” the law. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another court permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other cases are similarly uninformative regarding this particular issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

An appellate court once referenced the lack of a signature as one of a notice’s deficiencies, stating the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not determine that missing a signature alone would invalidate a cancellation when all other statutory requirements were met. Here, as in Benitez, the notices at issue do not list any specific employee’s name, printed or signed. Unlike Benitez, though, each notice in this matter “certifies” the necessary facts. Given the absence of more specific authority from Louisiana courts, we discern no basis to prevent a company from certifying information without identifying a particular employee. The statute expressly provides that “the insurance premium finance company” carries out cancellation by “certifying” the required statements in the notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). “Strict adherence” to the cancellation procedures does not authorize the courts to impose additional requirements. Therefore, we hold Louisiana law does not mandate that a premium finance company’s notice to the insurer include a signature.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s process fails to strictly follow the statute because GoAuto never “received” a cancellation request. According to the statutory language, “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs state that before 2017, the physical filing area for storing printed requests belonged to GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself. Since 2017, Plaintiffs contend, GoAuto does not come into possession of cancellation requests because an APAC employee affixes the timestamp. Plaintiffs assert that because the statute uses “receipt,” rather than only requiring the request be sent, GoAuto must have physical possession of the notice before it may cancel a policy.

The Louisiana Supreme Court has not specifically interpreted the term “receipt” within this statutory context, nor is there a definition in the statute itself. Black’s Law Dictionary offers that “receipt” is "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession," and defines "receive" as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). We adopt these meanings, and therefore find that GoAuto may proceed to cancel a policy once it possesses, has delivery of, or custody over a cancellation request.

As previously described, upon the e-mailed notice being received by GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning, the system executes the cancellation. A GoAuto witness described the interaction between APAC and GoAuto computer systems: during cancellation, the APAC process communicates with a GoAuto web service to request or perform cancellation for a policy scheduled for cancellation on a given date. The witness further explained, a web service functions essentially as a listener—constantly waiting for a request, and, when a proper command is received, it executes the relevant action.

No human becomes involved until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system has completed the cancellation process. At that subsequent point, an employee at APAC applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation documents. To restate our earlier summary of the statute: before a policy is canceled, the insurer must have "receipt" of a notice of cancellation issued by the premium finance company, in accordance with La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary and recognizes that tasks once performed manually are now handled by computers within companies. GoAuto’s process for receiving and acting on the notice meets the statute’s requirements. Additionally, we note the district court determined that GoAuto’s cancellation protocol complied with the statute for another reason: it concluded that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court pointed out that Louisiana precedent interpreting the term “private carrier” is lacking and instead drew comparison to a panel of this court analyzing when a railroad functioned as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was both creative and atypical. Since we hold the e-mail meets the statutory requirement, we decline to address whether this definition is valid.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to refer these questions of statutory construction to the Louisiana Supreme Court. According to them, correctly construing these statutory provisions holds significant consequences for both Louisiana’s insurance sector and its statutory framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The fact that the state supreme court has yet to interpret a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore decline to certify the issue. Since GoAuto’s practices are consistent with the statute, the district court properly denied the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
