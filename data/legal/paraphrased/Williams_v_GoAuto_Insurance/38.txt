Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are tasked with interpreting a Louisiana statute addressing the cancellation of insurance policies following nonpayment to the premium finance company. The appellants, who are former policyholders, allege that the insurer failed to follow the statutory procedures for cancellation, thereby committing misrepresentations and breaching the duties of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that there was no statutory violation and dismissed all of the claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this case, was in a collision involving Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. All three—the driver from Jenkins's accident, Williams, and Wright—used American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”) to finance their respective GoAuto policies. Under this arrangement, APAC paid GoAuto the full insurance premium upfront, while the insureds repaid APAC through monthly installments. The financing agreements all gave APAC a power of attorney that allowed it to terminate the insurance coverage if the insureds defaulted on their payments. Each of the policies was ultimately cancelled for failure to pay. Williams and Jenkins initiated a putative class action in Louisiana state court against both GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto did not validly cancel their policies and that it breached its duty of good faith. Subsequently, Wright was added as a plaintiff through an amended complaint. GoAuto then removed the matter to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides submitted motions for summary judgment addressing exclusively whether GoAuto’s method for terminating financed insurance policies met the requirements of Louisiana law. The district court sided with GoAuto, granting summary judgment in its favor after finding its cancellation practices consistent with state law. Consequently, the court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright have appealed this decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors in (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) concluding that GoAuto appropriately received the cancellation requests, and (3) refusing to grant their motion for partial summary judgment. In order to resolve these claims, we first outline Louisiana statutes that govern the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium finance agreements, and then evaluate the steps GoAuto took to comply with those statutes. According to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company may seek to cancel an insurance policy after an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” these points: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as stipulated in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) There is a default on the premium finance agreement, and that default remains uncured; (iii) Following the default, the insured was issued a notice of cancellation as specified in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, with the date the premium finance company sent it to the insured being identified; and (iv) Copies of the notice of cancellation were also sent to all parties identified by the premium finance agreement as holding an interest in any potential loss, with the certification naming the addresses of any governmental agencies, lienholders on the insured property, or third parties to whom the insurance premium finance company provided notice of cancellation. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) There exists a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and this default remains uncured within the requisite time. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and affidavit proof of mailing can be provided upon request. (4) The Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was also distributed to all parties listed in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in potential losses thereunder.

When such notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system effectuates the cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later that morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would physically file the documents in a filing cabinet utilized by both GoAuto and APAC. From 2017 onwards, the signed cancellation document is physically delivered to a GoAuto employee, who stamps it as “received” and places it into the jointly accessed filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We now turn to the issue of whether these procedures are consistent with the Louisiana statute previously analyzed. The Plaintiffs’ main contention is that APAC’s certification does not bear a signature until shortly after—and definitively after—the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs argue that the statutory mandate requiring “certifying” by the premium insurer necessitates a signature before cancellation can be effectuated. However, the statute itself is silent regarding any signature requirement. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be compulsory, that requirement would have to be implied by the term “certifying.” Except for a case we will address later, no Louisiana case law reviewing the practices of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute has ruled on whether a signature is required. In one decision, the court restated the statutory directive as necessitating “a statement certifying compliance with” the law. See KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. In another, a cancellation was permitted “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other authorities similarly fail to provide guidance on the issue. See, for example, Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, pp. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate court decision, the absence of a signature was noted as one of the deficiencies with the notice at issue: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court in Benitez did not find that a missing signature alone would be fatal to a cancellation where the other necessary elements were satisfied. Similar to Benitez, the notices in the present record also fail to specify any particular employee by printed name or signature. Unlike Benitez, though, each of these notices does “certify” the necessary information. In light of the absence of controlling Louisiana precedent, we find no justification to require that only a specifically-named employee may certify information. The statutory text explicitly provides that “the insurance premium finance company” certifies information to effectuate cancellation in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The requirement of “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not empower courts to introduce additional procedural steps. We therefore determine that Louisiana law does not impose a signature requirement on the premium finance company’s notice to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further assert that GoAuto’s practices fail to comply strictly with the statutory procedure, alleging that GoAuto never actually receives a cancellation request. The statute declares: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, Plaintiffs state that the physical location where requests were filed was managed by GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself. Since 2017, Plaintiffs argue GoAuto has not received requests, since an APAC employee is responsible for affixing the required stamp. They maintain that by using “receipt” rather than merely requiring transmission, the statute demands GoAuto to physically obtain the notice prior to cancelling the policy. Neither the Louisiana Supreme Court nor the statutory language defines “receipt” in this context. A leading legal dictionary gives the definition of “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and of “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Adopting these definitions, we hold that GoAuto may proceed with cancellation once it has acquired possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As previously discussed, the GoAuto computer system receives the e-mailed notice during the early morning hours and then effectuates the policy cancellation. A GoAuto witness detailed the process by which the APAC computer system communicates with a GoAuto web service: Specifically, during the cancellation process, APAC’s system initiates a request to a GoAuto web service to either request or execute cancellation on a policy scheduled for cancellation that day. Essentially, the web service operates like a perpetual listener, standing by for instructions; upon receiving the appropriate request, it determines the requested action, and, if able, carries it out.

No individual becomes involved until later in the day, after the GoAuto computer system processes the cancellation. At that point, an employee of APAC affixes a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. To reiterate our previous discussion of the statute: prior to cancellation, what is required is that the insurer has received notice of cancellation transmitted by the premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The statute is contemporary and acknowledges that many functions previously handled by people are now performed by computers. GoAuto’s process for receiving the notice and acting upon it is in compliance with the statutory requirements. We further note that the district court determined that GoAuto’s cancellation practices met the statutory mandate in a different manner: namely, that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged there was no Louisiana precedent interpreting “private carrier” and found instructive a panel decision from this court explaining when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was both innovative and atypical. However, since we conclude that the email complies with the statutory requirement, we do not reach the issue of whether that definition is correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs seek certification of these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that the correct reading of the statutory language holds significant impact for Louisiana's insurance regulatory framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The absence of a definition of a statutory term by the state supreme court “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Accordingly, we decline the request for certification. Since GoAuto’s processes are consistent with the statutory requirements, the district court properly denied their motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
