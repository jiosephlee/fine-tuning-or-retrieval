Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana statute relating to the cancellation of insurance coverage due to nonpayment to a premium finance company. The former policyholders contend that the insurer’s cancellation methods were contrary to statutory requirements and led to misrepresentations as well as a breach of the duties of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no violation of the statute had occurred and dismissed the plaintiffs’ claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the district court’s judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company, initiated this action as plaintiffs. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this matter, was involved in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, also a plaintiff. All three—Williams, Wright, and the driver associated with Jenkins's accident—used American Premium Assistance Company, LLC ("APAC") to finance their GoAuto insurance policies. Under this arrangement, APAC advanced the full premium amount to GoAuto in exchange for monthly installment payments from the insured parties. Each financing contract conferred upon APAC a power of attorney, enabling it to cancel the insurance coverage in the event the insured defaulted on payment. Nonpayment resulted in cancellation of all the relevant policies. Subsequently, Williams and Jenkins commenced a class action in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto had failed to properly cancel their insurance policies and did not fulfill its obligation of good faith. Wright joined as a plaintiff when the complaint was amended. GoAuto removed the lawsuit to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties then submitted cross-motions for summary judgment limited to the issue of whether GoAuto’s cancellation practices for financed policies conformed to Louisiana law. The district court granted GoAuto summary judgment, determining that its cancellation procedures met state legal requirements. Accordingly, the court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently appealed that decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made mistakes by (1) determining that the statutory process for delivering cancellation notices was observed, (2) concluding that GoAuto appropriately received the requests for cancellation, and (3) rejecting their request for partial summary judgment. In order to evaluate these points, we will first summarize the relevant Louisiana statutes regarding the cancellation of financed insurance policies, and then review GoAuto's methods for meeting these requirements. Louisiana law authorizes an insurance-premium finance company to seek the cancellation of an insurance policy after an uncured default by “sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney under Paragraph (1) of this Subsection. (ii) The premium finance agreement is in default and that default remains uncured. (iii) Upon default, the premium finance company sent a cancellation notice to the insured as described in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, specifying when the notice was sent to the insured. (iv) Copies of the cancellation notice were provided to any parties named in the premium finance agreement with an interest in potential loss, indicating the names and addresses of relevant government entities, lienholders, or third parties who received such notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement is in a state of default, and this default has not been remedied within the required time frame. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured; enclosures contain copies of these notices, and the affidavit confirming mailing is available upon request. (4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were also sent to every individual identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any loss that could arise under the agreement.

After that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system effectuates the policy cancellation. Subsequently, later the same morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the hard copies of the cancellation documents. Before 2017, an APAC staff member would manually deliver the documents to a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. Since 2017, the signed cancellation document is delivered in person to a GoAuto staff member, who stamps it as “received” and then stores it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next examine whether these procedures are consistent with the Louisiana law previously addressed. Central to Plaintiffs' contention is the fact that APAC’s certification does not bear a signature until shortly after, but definitively after, the policy is automatically canceled. Plaintiffs interpret the statutory mandate of “certifying” by the premium insurer to necessitate that a signature must be provided before a cancellation can occur. However, the statute does not expressly mention a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be required, such a mandate would have to be inferred from the use of the word “certifying.” With one exception, which we shall address, no Louisiana court decision evaluating insurance-premium finance companies’ practices under the statute has directly considered whether a signature is necessary. One court summarized the statutory requirement as the need for “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. In another instance, cancellation was permitted “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other cases likewise do not directly address this issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate court opinion, the absence of a signature was referenced as one of that notice's deficiencies: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not rule that a lack of a signature alone would invalidate the cancellation, provided other requirements were satisfied. Similar to Benitez, the current notices in evidence do not list any employee by name or with a signature. However, each of these notices “certifies” the necessary information, which sets them apart from the situation in Benitez. Given the absence of explicit guidance from Louisiana courts, we find no basis to conclude a company cannot certify information absent the identification of a specific employee. The applicable statute indicates it is “the insurance premium finance company” itself that brings about cancellation by “certifying” specified information in a notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). “Strict adherence” to the cancellation procedures as outlined in the statute does not authorize courts to impose additional requirements. Therefore, we determine that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the cancellation notice sent from the premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s practices do not strictly comply with the statute because GoAuto allegedly did not receive a cancellation request. The relevant statute says that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, Plaintiffs assert that the joint filing room where paper requests were held was maintained by GoAuto Management Services, rather than GoAuto itself. Post-2017, Plaintiffs allege, GoAuto does not obtain the requests because an APAC employee handles the stamping process. Plaintiffs interpret the statutory term “receipt,” rather than the mere act of sending the request, to require that GoAuto actually take control or possession of the notice before a policy can be cancelled. The Louisiana Supreme Court has yet to clarify the meaning of “receipt” as used in the statute, and there is no statutory definition provided. According to a major legal dictionary, “receipt” means “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” is defined as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Relying on these definitions, we conclude that GoAuto may lawfully effect cancellation upon assuming possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request.

As previously noted, once an emailed notice is received by GoAuto’s computer systems in the early morning, the policy is automatically cancelled. A GoAuto deponent described the process by which an APAC computer system interacts with a GoAuto web service as follows: During the cancellation process, APAC's system initiates a call to a GoAuto web service to request or execute the cancellation of a policy scheduled to terminate on that day. The web service essentially operates as a listener—constantly on standby to respond to calls, upon which, if it recognizes and understands the request, it carries out the specified activity.

No individual is engaged until sometime later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system completes the cancellation process. At that subsequent stage, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation papers. We reiterate our prior summary of the statutory requirement: before cancellation occurs, the insurer must have had “receipt” of the cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company as required under La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary and acknowledges that tasks formerly done by people are now managed by company computers. GoAuto’s approach to receiving and processing the notice fulfills the requirements of the statute. Additionally, we note that the district court determined GoAuto’s cancellation process met the statutory mandate for another reason: it stated that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court pointed out the lack of Louisiana precedent on interpreting “private carrier” and considered relevant a decision from this court explaining when a railroad qualified as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The lower court's understanding of “private carrier” was both creative and nontraditional. Since we conclude that the email transmission meets the statute’s requirements, we do not address whether this interpretation is correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs have asked that we certify these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. They argue that interpreting these statutory terms correctly is significant for both Louisiana’s insurance sector and its regulatory framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). Moreover, the fact that a particular term has yet to be defined by a state supreme court “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Therefore, we refuse the request for certification. Since GoAuto’s procedures conform to the statutory requirements, the district court’s denial of Plaintiffs’ summary judgment motion was proper. AFFIRMED.
