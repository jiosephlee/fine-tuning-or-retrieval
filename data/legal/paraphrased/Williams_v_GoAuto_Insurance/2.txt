Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are tasked with interpreting a Louisiana law that addresses the cancellation of insurance policies due to failure to pay a premium finance company. The appellants, who were previously insured, argue that the insurer's process for cancellation failed to adhere to the statute, which they contend led to both misrepresentation and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that the statute had not been violated and dismissed every claim with prejudice. We AFFIRM the district court’s decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, were previously insured by GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual, also insured by GoAuto but not involved in this suit, was in a collision with plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. All three—Williams, Wright, and the driver in Jenkins’s accident—had financed their GoAuto insurance policies through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”), pursuant to agreements in which APAC paid GoAuto the full premium upfront in exchange for monthly payments from the insureds. These finance agreements also provided APAC with a power of attorney to cancel the insurance policies should the insureds fail to make payments. The insurance policies were each cancelled due to nonpayment. Subsequently, Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto had not properly cancelled their insurance policies and had breached its duty of good faith. Wright was added as a plaintiff after the complaint was amended. GoAuto then removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides submitted motions for summary judgment limited to the question of whether GoAuto’s process for cancelling the financed policies complied with Louisiana law. The district court ruled in favor of GoAuto, granting its motion for summary judgment and finding that its cancellation procedures conformed to state requirements. Consequently, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made mistakes by (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering a notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) concluding that GoAuto properly received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To resolve these issues, we first outline Louisiana's legal framework regarding the cancellation of insurance policies financed by premium finance agreements, and then consider GoAuto’s methods for adhering to that framework. Pursuant to Louisiana law, a premium finance company may seek cancellation of an insurance policy following an uncured default by transmitting to the insurer—whether through mail, private carrier, or electronic mail—a copy of the cancellation notice along with a statement certifying the following elements: (i) that the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as specified in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) that the premium finance agreement is in default, and the default has not been cured within the designated time; (iii) that upon default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured according to Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the date the premium finance company sent the notice to the insured; and (iv) that copies of the notice of cancellation were provided to all parties indicated by the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement, with names and addresses listed for any governmental bodies, holders of security interests in the insured property, or third parties to whom the insurance premium finance company sent such notice of cancellation. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts demand exact compliance with statutory provisions when a finance company invokes its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “Any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Such strict compliance fulfills two purposes: first, it establishes an unequivocal mechanism for determining the precise time and date of cancellation; second, it ensures a minimum period in which the default may be remedied. Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto utilizes the following process to cancel a policy. When a policyholder fails to make a timely payment, APAC issues a notice to the insured warning that the policy will be cancelled if payment is not received within ten days. Should the insured fail to pay by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically transmits an email to GoAuto that contains both a copy of the cancellation notice and a cancellation request. This cancellation request, which is prepared on APAC’s letterhead, contains the following certification: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) There has been a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and such default remains uncured within the applicable timeframe. (3) Following the default, the insured was issued a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and the affidavit verifying mailing can be provided if requested. (4) All individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss covered by the agreement were also sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system processes the policy cancellation. Subsequently, later that morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually deliver the documents to a filing cabinet that both GoAuto and APAC used. Beginning in 2017, however, the signed cancellation document is handed directly to a GoAuto employee, who stamps it as “received” and files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn to the question of whether these procedures are consistent with the Louisiana statute previously discussed. The Plaintiffs’ central contention is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until shortly after, but still subsequent to, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs read the statutory mandate that the premium insurer must "certify" as necessitating a signature before a cancellation can take effect. However, the statute does not explicitly mention a signature. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be required, that requirement would have to arise from the term “certifying.” Except for an exception we will address later, Louisiana courts evaluating premium finance company practices under this provision have not ruled on whether a signature is necessary. For instance, one court described the statutory requirement as the need for “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. See KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision upheld cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other decisions offer no further guidance on this specific issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate court decision, the absence of a signature was cited among the deficiencies of a cancellation notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not determine that the lack of a signature alone would render a cancellation invalid if all other requirements were met. Similarly, the notices at issue here also fail to name any specific employee either by printed name or signature. In contrast to Benitez, however, each notice here does “certify” the relevant details. Given the absence of more definitive authority from Louisiana, we find no basis to conclude that a company is prohibited from certifying information in a notice simply because no individual employee is named. The statute in question expressly states that “the insurance premium finance company” effects cancellation by “certifying” specific information in a notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The mandate for “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not authorize courts to create additional procedural requirements. Therefore, we hold that Louisiana law does not mandate that a cancellation notice from a premium finance company to the insurer bear a signature.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s procedures fall short of the statutory requirements because GoAuto allegedly never actually receives a cancellation request. The statute directs that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, the shared records room containing printed requests was maintained by GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto. Plaintiffs assert that since 2017, GoAuto does not receive the requests because the person applying the stamp is employed by APAC. They argue that the legislature’s choice of the term “receipt,” as opposed to merely requiring notice to be sent, obliges GoAuto to obtain possession of the notice prior to cancelling the policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted “receipt” as it is used in the statute, and the statute provides no definition. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Adopting these definitions, we determine that GoAuto may cancel a policy when it obtains possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As previously explained, once an emailed notice is received by GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning, the system proceeds to cancel the policy. A GoAuto deponent described how the APAC computer system communicates with a GoAuto web service, stating: For example, during the cancellation procedure, the APAC process “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual participates until later in the day, after GoAuto’s computer system processes the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation forms. To recapitulate our earlier summary of the statute: prior to cancellation, the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice transmitted by the premium finance company, in accordance with La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary in nature, reflecting modern practices where computers perform functions that people previously did. GoAuto’s process of receiving and responding to the notice fulfills the statute’s requirements. Additionally, the district court concluded that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures satisfied the statute for a different reason: “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court noted there is a lack of Louisiana case law interpreting the term “private carrier” and relied on a Fifth Circuit panel’s discussion regarding when a railroad is considered a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was creative and unusual. Since we determine that the e-mail meets the statutory requirements, we need not address the appropriateness of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to certify these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that how these statutory terms are construed significantly impacts Louisiana’s insurance sector and statutory framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). Merely because a state supreme court has not previously construed a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore choose not to certify the question. Since GoAuto’s practices are in accordance with the statute, the district court’s denial of the Plaintiffs’ summary judgment motion was not erroneous. AFFIRMED.
