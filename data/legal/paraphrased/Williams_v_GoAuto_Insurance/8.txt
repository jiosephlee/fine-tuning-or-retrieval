Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana law that addresses the termination of insurance policies for nonpayment to the premium finance company. The former policyholders allege that the insurer failed to follow the statutory cancellation requirements, which they argue led to misrepresentations and a breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court found no violation of the statute and dismissed the claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company, are the plaintiffs in this matter. Another GoAuto policyholder, who is not a party to this case, was involved in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, one of the plaintiffs. Williams, Wright, and the policyholder implicated in Jenkins’s accident all entered into premium finance agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”) to finance their GoAuto policies. Under these agreements, APAC paid the full premium amount to GoAuto, and the insureds were required to make monthly installment payments to APAC. Each agreement included a power of attorney clause empowering APAC to terminate the insurance policies if the insured failed to make premium payments. The policies in question were all cancelled due to nonpayment. Subsequently, Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, alleging that GoAuto failed to properly cancel their policies and did not act in good faith. Wright joined as a plaintiff after an amended complaint was filed. GoAuto removed the matter to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties then submitted cross-motions for summary judgment on the narrow issue of whether GoAuto’s method of cancelling financed policies complied with Louisiana law. The district court granted GoAuto’s motion, finding that its cancellation procedures were consistent with state requirements, and dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright appealed this decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors in three respects: (1) by determining that the statutory process for delivering a notice of cancellation had been adhered to, (2) by concluding that GoAuto correctly received the requests for cancellation, and (3) by rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. In order to address these issues, we will first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes governing the cancellation of financed insurance policies, followed by an analysis of GoAuto’s methods for meeting those legal requirements. Louisiana law provides that an insurance-premium finance company is authorized to seek cancellation of an insurance policy following an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” certain criteria: (i) the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as outlined in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) the agreement is in default and the default remains uncured; (iii) a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured after default, as set out in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, stating the date it was sent by the premium finance company to the insured; (iv) copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all parties identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in potential losses under the policy, specifying the names and addresses of any government agencies, security interest holders in the insured property, or other third parties to whom notice of cancellation was sent by the insurance premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts demand exact compliance with these statutory provisions when a finance company invokes its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). Should there be any flaw in this process, “an ineffective cancellation of the policy” will result. Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). The insistence on strict statutory compliance “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto employs specific steps when cancelling a policy. Upon an insured’s failure to pay on time, APAC issues notice to the insured advising that their policy will be cancelled if payment is not made within ten days. If no payment is received by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically transmits an email to GoAuto with both the notice of cancellation and a request for cancellation attached. This cancellation request, sent on APAC’s letterhead, includes the following certification: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) There is a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and such default remains uncured within the required time frame. (3) Following the occurrence of default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit of proof of mailing can be produced upon request. (4) All individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss have been sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

When that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system terminates the policy. Subsequently, later that same morning following the cancellation, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually place the documents into a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the signed cancellation documents are instead hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who marks the document as “received” and then files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next address whether these procedures align with the relevant Louisiana law previously discussed. Central to the Plaintiffs’ position is the fact that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until shortly after, but definitively after, the policy has been automatically canceled. Plaintiffs construe the statutory mandate that the premium insurer “certify” as imposing a requirement for a signature prior to any cancellation taking effect. However, the statute itself does not expressly mention the need for a signature. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should such a requirement exist, it would have to arise solely from the meaning of the term “certifying.” Except for an instance we will address, Louisiana court decisions reviewing the practices of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute have not opined on whether a signature is necessary. One court summarized the statute’s demand as requiring “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another court permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other relevant decisions are likewise not illuminating on this issue. See, for example, Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, pp. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, the absence of a signature was noted among other deficiencies of the notice at issue: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court did not determine that a missing signature alone would render the cancellation invalid if the other statutory requirements were satisfied. Here, as was true in Benitez, the notices in question do not display the name of any specific employee, either in print or by signature. In contrast to Benitez, each of these notices does “certify” the pertinent facts. In the absence of clearer authority from Louisiana courts, we find no basis for concluding that certification of information by a company necessitates the identification of a particular employee. The relevant statute expressly indicates that “the insurance premium finance company” is responsible for effecting cancellation by “certifying” specific details in a notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). A requirement of “strict adherence” to statutory procedures for cancellation does not authorize the judiciary to expand those procedures. Accordingly, we determine that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on a notice sent by a premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s procedures fail to comply strictly with the statute because GoAuto did not actually receive a cancellation request. The statutory text states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs note that, before 2017, the shared filing room where physical requests were kept was owned by GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto itself. Since 2017, they assert, GoAuto has not received requests directly because the employee who stamps them is employed by APAC. Plaintiffs assert that by using the term “receipt” instead of simply requiring transmission, the legislature intended for GoAuto to actually take possession of the notice before cancelling a policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted “receipt” as used in the statute, nor does the statute provide a definition. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and defines “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Relying on these definitions, we find that GoAuto may effect cancellation of a policy when it comes into possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As previously described, once GoAuto’s computer system receives the emailed notice in the early morning hours, the system cancels the policy. A GoAuto witness described the interaction between APAC’s computer system and a GoAuto web service as follows: During cancellation, the APAC system “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until after the GoAuto computer system has processed the cancellation later in the morning. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the cancellation documents that have been printed. To restate our prior summary of the statute, before a cancellation occurs, it is required that the insurer has “receipt” of a cancellation notice from the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute reflects current times, in which computers within companies perform tasks that were formerly completed by people. GoAuto’s approach to receiving and processing the notice fulfills the statute’s requirements. We note that the district court concluded that GoAuto’s cancellation process adhered to the statute on different grounds: it determined that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged that Louisiana precedent interpreting “private carrier” is lacking and found a panel decision from this court, discussing when a railroad qualified as a private carrier, to be instructive. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s understanding of “private carrier” was creative and unconventional. Since we conclude that the e-mail meets the statutory requirements, we need not address the appropriateness of this definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we submit these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that the correct reading of these statutory terms holds significant relevance for both the state’s insurance sector and legislative framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The fact that a state supreme court has not previously defined a term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore refuse to certify the question. Because GoAuto’s practices satisfy the statutory requirements, the district court properly denied the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
