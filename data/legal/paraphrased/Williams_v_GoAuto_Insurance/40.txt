Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we are tasked with interpreting a Louisiana statute relating to the termination of insurance policies due to nonpayment by the party who financed the insurance premiums. The former policyholders allege that the insurance company's cancellation process was not in accordance with the statutory requirements, leading to both misrepresentation and a breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that the statute was not violated and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who were previously insured by GoAuto Insurance Company, brought this action as plaintiffs. Another GoAuto policyholder, who is not a party to this case, was involved in a motor vehicle collision with plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the driver from Jenkins’s accident each secured financing for their GoAuto insurance premiums through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”), pursuant to agreements where APAC paid the premiums in full to GoAuto and the insureds repaid APAC through monthly installments. The financing contracts granted APAC a power of attorney, enabling APAC to cancel the insurance policies if premiums were not paid. Due to nonpayment, each policy at issue was cancelled. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action lawsuit against GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, contending that GoAuto had not validly cancelled the policies and had breached the duty of good faith. Wright was added as a plaintiff via an amended pleading. GoAuto subsequently removed the matter to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides submitted motions for summary judgment focused exclusively on whether GoAuto’s cancellation practices for the financed policies complied with Louisiana statutory requirements. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, concluding that its procedures met the standards imposed by Louisiana law. Consequently, all claims against GoAuto were dismissed with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright then filed this appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court committed error by: (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation were adhered to, (2) finding that GoAuto adequately received the cancellation requests, and (3) denying their motion seeking partial summary judgment. To resolve these claims, we first outline the relevant provisions of Louisiana law regarding the cancellation of financed insurance policies, and then review the procedures GoAuto followed to comply with those statutes. Louisiana law permits an insurance-premium finance company to request cancellation of an insurance policy in the event of an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following elements: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as specified in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) The premium finance agreement is in default and the default has not been cured in a timely manner; (iii) Upon default, the premium finance company sent a notice of cancellation to the insured as required in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, including the date it was sent; (iv) Copies of the cancellation notice were also provided to any persons identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in potential losses, with the certification stating the names and addresses of government agencies, security interest holders in the insured property, or other third parties to whom the cancellation notice was provided by the insurance premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement is in a state of default, and this default has not been timely remedied. (3) When default occurred, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and affidavit evidence of mailing can be provided if requested. (4) All individuals identified by the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement were also sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto's computer system processes the cancellation of the policy. Sometime after the cancellation is processed that morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC staff member would manually place the documents into a filing cabinet used by both GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, however, a GoAuto employee receives the signed cancellation document, marks it as “received” with a stamp, and files it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We now examine whether these processes satisfy the requirements of the Louisiana statute previously discussed. Central to the Plaintiffs' position is their observation that APAC’s certification does not bear a signature until shortly after, yet undeniably after, the policy is automatically canceled. Plaintiffs assert that the statutory mandate for the premium insurer to “certify” necessitates that a signature be affixed prior to effectuating a cancellation. The statute itself, however, does not expressly mention any requirement for a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should such a requirement exist, it would need to be inferred from the term “certifying.” Apart from an exception that will be addressed, no Louisiana court opinion considering the procedures of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute has considered whether a signature is necessary. In one instance, a court summarized the statutory directive as necessitating “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. In another case, cancellation was permitted “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional cases provide little additional guidance on this issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

A court of appeal in one opinion observed a missing signature while evaluating the notice’s deficiencies, noting that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court did not decide that the absence of a signature alone would invalidate a cancellation, provided that other necessary elements were present. In this case, similar to Benitez, the record’s notices fail to name a specific employee either in print or by signature; yet, unlike Benitez, these notices each “certify” the essential information. Given the lack of explicit Louisiana precedent, we find no basis for concluding that a company cannot certify information unless a particular employee is identified. The statutory provision at issue states that “the insurance premium finance company” effects cancellation by “certifying” required information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Demanding “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not grant courts leeway to impose additional procedural requirements. We therefore hold that Louisiana law does not mandate that the notice sent by a premium finance company to the insurer bear a signature.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures are deficient under the statute because GoAuto purportedly never receives a cancellation request. The statute specifies that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, Plaintiffs assert that a shared filing room housing the printed requests belonged to GoAuto Management Services, not to GoAuto. Since 2017, they contend GoAuto does not actually receive the requests, because the employee who applies the stamp is employed by APAC. Plaintiffs claim that the legislature’s choice of the word “receipt,” as opposed to a simple requirement that the request be sent, obliges GoAuto to actually acquire the notice before effectuating cancellation.

Neither the Louisiana Supreme Court nor the statute defines “receipt” in this context. According to a prominent legal dictionary, “receipt” means “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” means “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Relying on these definitions, we conclude GoAuto may cancel a policy once it obtains possession, delivery, or control of a request for cancellation. As previously described, once GoAuto’s computer system receives the e-mailed notice during the early morning, it processes the policy cancellation. According to deposition testimony from a GoAuto representative, the APAC computer system interacts with a GoAuto web service as follows: In the course of cancellation, “the APAC process . . . makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

Human involvement does not occur until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system initiates the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. To recap the statute previously quoted: before a cancellation, the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company, pursuant to La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This law operates in a modern context, where computers perform many tasks once carried out by individuals. The process used by GoAuto to receive and respond to such notice complies with the statute. The district court alternatively determined that GoAuto’s method conformed with the law by concluding that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” Recognizing that there was no Louisiana jurisprudence interpreting “private carrier,” the district court referred to a panel decision from this court that had addressed the question in the context of a railroad’s status as a private carrier, specifically citing Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was described as creative and non-traditional. Because this court finds that the email method fulfills the statutory requirements, there is no need to address the soundness of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we certify these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that the correct reading of these statutory terms holds significance for Louisiana’s insurance framework and statutory regulations. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that the state’s highest court has not yet defined a term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore refuse to certify the question. Since GoAuto’s methods adhere to the statutory requirements, the district court correctly rejected their motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
