This case presents an issue regarding a Louisiana law concerning the cancellation of insurance policies due to nonpayment to the entity that provided premium financing. The appellants, who are former policyholders, argue that the insurer failed to follow the procedures required by the statute, thereby making false representations and violating the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded there had been no breach of the statute and dismissed all of the plaintiffs’ claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this case, was involved in a collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the driver from Jenkins's accident each entered into financing arrangements for their GoAuto insurance policies through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these arrangements, APAC advanced the full insurance premium to GoAuto, and the insureds were to repay APAC through monthly installments. The financing agreements included a power of attorney granting APAC authority to cancel the respective insurance policies if premium payments were missed. All relevant policies were cancelled due to nonpayment of premiums. Subsequently, Williams and Jenkins brought a class action suit against GoAuto and APAC in a Louisiana state court, asserting that GoAuto had ineffectively cancelled their policies and failed to act in good faith. Upon the filing of an amended complaint, Wright was added as an additional plaintiff. GoAuto removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides then moved for summary judgment, with their motions limited to the issue of whether GoAuto’s process for cancelling these financed policies was in compliance with Louisiana law. The district court ruled in favor of GoAuto, granting its summary judgment motion and determining that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures met state legal requirements. This resulted in a dismissal, with prejudice, of all claims against GoAuto. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently appealed.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by (1) determining that the statutory methods for delivering the notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) concluding that GoAuto duly received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion seeking partial summary judgment. To evaluate these claims, we first outline the applicable Louisiana statutes governing the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium finance agreements, followed by a review of GoAuto’s process for complying with these requirements. According to Louisiana law, a company that finances insurance premiums may initiate cancellation of an insurance policy after a default that has not been remedied, “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” each of the following points: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as described in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection. (ii) The premium finance agreement is in default and the default remains uncorrected. (iii) After default, the insured was sent a notice of cancellation as set forth in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, which included the date that the premium finance company sent the notice to the insured. (iv) Copies of the notice of cancellation were also sent to every individual identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in potential losses, with identification of the names and addresses of any relevant governmental entities, security interest holders in the property insured, or third parties to whom notice was sent by the insurance premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes a legally enforceable power of attorney. (2) There has been a default under the Premium Finance Agreement which has not been rectified within the prescribed time. (3) When default occurred, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit attesting to their mailing can be provided upon request. (4) The Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was also provided to each party identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement.

Upon the issuance of that notice, GoAuto's computer system processes the policy cancellation. Subsequently, later that morning, an APAC employee places a signature stamp on the printed cancellation notices. Before 2017, an APAC staff member would manually store the documents in a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, after being stamped as “received” by a GoAuto employee, the signed cancellation document is placed in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn now to the question of whether these procedures adhere to the Louisiana law previously addressed. Central to the Plaintiffs’ claim is their assertion that APAC’s certification remains unsigned until a point after, but not before, the policy is subject to automatic cancellation. Plaintiffs contend that the statutory mandate requiring the premium insurer to "certify" necessitates the existence of a signature before cancellation can occur. The relevant statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a), does not specifically mention any requirement of a signature. Should a signature be required, such an obligation would have to be implied from the statutory use of “certifying.” Except for a situation we will address further on, no Louisiana case examining insurance-premium finance company practices under this statute has determined whether a signature is essential. One court summarized the statute as demanding “a statement certifying compliance with” its provisions. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another case permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other judicial opinions do not provide further clarification on this matter. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

An appellate court once referenced the lack of a signature when analyzing deficiencies in a cancellation notice: the "'notice of cancellation' does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein." Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that decision did not determine that a missing signature alone would render a cancellation invalid if other statutory elements were met. In the current case, as in Benitez, the cancellation notices do not indicate by name or signature any individual employee. In contrast to Benitez, these notices do “certify” the relevant contents. Because there is no definitive guidance from Louisiana courts, we find no basis for requiring that only a named employee can certify such information. The statutory language itself states it is “the insurance premium finance company” that effects cancellation by “certifying” designated information within the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The mandate for “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures should not be construed as a license for courts to impose procedural requirements beyond those outlined by statute. We therefore determine that Louisiana law does not obligate the premium finance company’s notice to the insurer to bear a signature.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s procedures lack strict statutory compliance because GoAuto allegedly never physically received a request for cancellation. The governing provision reads: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs allege that, prior to 2017, requests were retained in a shared filing area that belonged to GoAuto Management Services—not GoAuto itself. From 2017 onward, Plaintiffs claim GoAuto has not directly obtained the requests because an APAC employee now affixes the necessary stamp. They assert that, by choosing the term "receipt," rather than merely requiring the request to be sent, the legislature intended that GoAuto must actually gain possession of the notice before terminating a policy.

Neither the Louisiana Supreme Court nor the relevant statute define “receipt” within this context. Black’s Law Dictionary provides the following definitions: “receipt” is "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession," and "receive" means "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Adopting these definitions, we find that GoAuto is authorized to cancel a policy once it acquires possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request.

As previously outlined, upon the arrival of an e-mailed cancellation notice in GoAuto’s computer system during early morning hours, that system effectuates the policy cancellation. A GoAuto witness described the electronic interaction with an APAC computer system as follows: For example, in the cancellation process, the APAC system “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date.” In effect, the web service functions as a listener, persistently awaiting a call; when it recognizes it is being invoked, it queries the caller’s instructions and, if those instructions are clear, completes the requested task.

Human involvement does not occur until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system has executed the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation paperwork. To restate our previous summarization of the statute: the requirement before cancellation is that the insurer must have "receipt" of the cancellation notice from the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary, crafted for modern conditions, and current business operations delegate many tasks once performed manually to computers. The procedure by which GoAuto’s computer system receives and processes the notice fulfills the requirements of the statute. Additionally, we note that the district court determined that GoAuto’s cancellation process complied with the statutory mandate on an alternative basis: “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged the lack of Louisiana jurisprudence interpreting “private carrier” and found a panel opinion from this court regarding when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier to be analogous. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court's interpretation of “private carrier” was notably creative and unorthodox. As we conclude that the e-mail process satisfies the statute, we do not address the soundness of that interpretation.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we refer these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that the correct understanding of the statutory language significantly affects both Louisiana’s insurance laws and industry. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The absence of a specific definition for a term from a state supreme court, alone, “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Therefore, we refuse to certify the issue. Since GoAuto’s processes align with the statutory requirements, the district court properly denied the Plaintiffs' summary judgment motion. AFFIRMED.
