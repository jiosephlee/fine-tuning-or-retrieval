Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana law pertaining to the termination of insurance coverage due to nonpayment to the premium finance company. The appellants, who are former policyholders, allege that the insurer failed to follow the statute's cancellation requirements, thereby committing misrepresentation and violating the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that the insurer's conduct did not breach the statute and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies issued by GoAuto Insurance Company. Another GoAuto policyholder—who is not a party to this litigation—was involved in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, also a plaintiff. Williams, Wright, and the policyholder involved in Jenkins's accident each used American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”) to finance their insurance policies with GoAuto. Under these arrangements, APAC would remit the entire insurance premium to GoAuto and, in exchange, the insureds agreed to make monthly payments to APAC. Each finance agreement assigned APAC a power of attorney permitting it to terminate the insurance policy if premiums were not paid. Each policy was indeed terminated for nonpayment. Subsequently, Williams and Jenkins brought a class action suit in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, alleging that GoAuto had failed to properly cancel their insurance policies and had not fulfilled its duty of good faith. Wright was added as a plaintiff through an amended complaint. GoAuto removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties then filed motions for summary judgment limited to the question of whether GoAuto’s cancellation process for financed policies conformed to Louisiana law. The district court ruled in favor of GoAuto on summary judgment, finding GoAuto's procedures to be in accordance with state law. Consequently, the court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright appealed that decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court committed error by (1) concluding that the statutory procedures for delivering notice of cancellation were observed, (2) determining that GoAuto correctly received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. In order to respond to these claims, we will first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes concerning the cancellation of insurance policies that are financed, followed by a review of the methods utilized by GoAuto to satisfy those statutory requirements. Louisiana law permits an insurance-premium finance company to initiate the cancellation of an insurance policy when there is an uncured default, which may be done "by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" the following: (i) the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as referenced in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) the agreement is in default and the default remains uncured; (iii) once the default occurred, notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as outlined in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, with the date of mailing by the premium finance company specified; and (iv) copies of the notice of cancellation were delivered to all persons identified by the premium finance agreement as holding an interest in any potential loss, including the identification of any governmental entities, security interest holders in the insured property, or third parties to whom such notice was provided. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate exact compliance with these statutory mandates when a finance company utilizes its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Precisely following the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto utilizes the following process to terminate a policy. In situations where an insured does not pay promptly, APAC issues a notification warning the insured that the policy will be canceled absent payment within ten days. Should payment not be received by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically generates an e-mail to GoAuto containing both the notice of cancellation and a cancellation request. The cancellation request appears on APAC’s letterhead and includes the certification as follows: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney provision. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement is currently in a state of default, and such default has not been remedied within the required period. (3) Following the occurrence of default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured party. Enclosed are copies of these notices; the affidavit of mailing can be provided upon request. (4) Parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having any interest in potential losses were also sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

When that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer automatically terminates the policy. After the policy has been cancelled, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork later that morning. Before 2017, an APAC staff member would manually deliver these documents to a filing cabinet used by both GoAuto and APAC. Starting in 2017, after the cancellation document has been signed, it is hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee who marks it as “received” with a stamp before placing it into the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn next to the issue of whether these procedures are consistent with the Louisiana law previously examined. Central to the Plaintiffs’ position is the absence of a signature on APAC’s certification until immediately following, yet certainly after, the policy is automatically canceled. Plaintiffs contend that the statutory mandate for the premium insurer to “certify” implies that a signature must precede cancellation. However, the statute itself does not explicitly mention a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be required, such necessity would have to derive from the meaning of the term “certifying.” Aside from an exception discussed below, no Louisiana court decisions evaluating the practices of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute have directly addressed the requirement of a signature. In one case, the court restated the statutory provision to mean that there should be “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision upheld cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other cases are likewise not instructive regarding this issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

A court of appeal addressed the issue of an unsigned notice while evaluating that notice’s deficiencies: it observed that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any specific employee of [the premium finance company] who prepared it, bears no individual’s signature, and does not claim to ‘certify’ any of the information it contains.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court in Benitez did not determine that a missing signature alone would make the cancellation ineffective if the other necessary requirements were satisfied. Similar to the situation in Benitez, the notices present in this record likewise lack both a printed name and a signature of any particular employee. Distinct from Benitez, though, each notice here includes language “certifying” the pertinent contents. In the absence of clearer guidance from Louisiana authorities, we discern no basis to prevent a company from certifying facts within a notice without identifying a particular employee. The statute itself directs that “the insurance premium finance company” must effectuate cancellation by “certifying” requisite information in the notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The requirement for “strict adherence” to the cancellation procedures should not be construed as a license for the courts to impose additional steps not stated in the statute. Thus, we find no requirement under Louisiana law that a notice from a premium finance company to an insurer must bear a signature. Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s processes do not conform to statutory demands because GoAuto does not actually receive a cancellation request. The statute states that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs note that before 2017, GoAuto Management Services—and not GoAuto—controlled the filing area for the printed cancellation requests. They allege that from 2017 onward, GoAuto itself does not receive the requests, since the employee who applies the stamp works for APAC. Plaintiffs maintain that the statutory term “receipt,” rather than a requirement that the request merely be sent, obligates GoAuto to obtain actual possession of the notice before executing the cancellation. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted the term “receipt” within this statutory context, and no statutory definition is given. Black’s Law Dictionary provides the meaning of “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” while “receive” is defined as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Relying on these definitions, we hold that GoAuto is permitted to cancel an insurance policy once it acquires possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As described earlier, when the e-mailed notice is received by GoAuto’s computer system during the early morning hours, the system initiates the cancellation of the policy. A GoAuto witness explained the interaction between APAC and GoAuto’s systems: Specifically, during the cancellation operation, the APAC system “calls” a GoAuto web service to either request or execute a cancellation for a policy set to cancel on that date. The web service essentially acts as a listener, always on standby for an incoming request, and upon receiving such a request and understanding its content, the system performs the corresponding task.

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system processes the cancellation. At that subsequent stage, an APAC employee affixes a signature stamp to the physical cancellation documents. To recapitulate our earlier summary of the statutory requirement: prior to cancellation, the insurer must have had "receipt" of the cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is of recent vintage and reflects modern practices, wherein company computers perform tasks historically managed by people. GoAuto's process of receiving and responding to cancellation notices through its computer system fulfills the statutory requirement. Additionally, the district court found that GoAuto’s cancellation process satisfied the statute in another respect: it determined that "the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier." The district court acknowledged there is no Louisiana precedent construing “private carrier” and identified as analogous a Fifth Circuit panel's discussion of a railroad's status as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s understanding of “private carrier” was innovative and nontraditional. Since we hold that the email meets the requirements of the statute, we do not address the soundness of the district court’s definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to refer these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that correctly construing these statutory provisions has significant implications for Louisiana’s insurance sector and legal framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere absence of a state supreme court definition for a term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore refuse to certify the issue. As GoAuto’s methods conform to the statutory requirements, the district court did not abuse its discretion in denying their summary judgment motion. AFFIRMED.
