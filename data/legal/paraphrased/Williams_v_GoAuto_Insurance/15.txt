Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are asked to examine a Louisiana law regulating the cancellation of insurance policies due to nonpayment to the entity that provided premium financing. The former policyholders contend that the insurer failed to follow the statutory cancellation procedures, leading to alleged misrepresentations and violations of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that there was no breach of the statute and dismissed every claim with prejudice. We AFFIRM that decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who were formerly insured by GoAuto Insurance Company, brought this action as plaintiffs. Another individual, also insured by GoAuto but not involved in this case, was in an accident with Nicholas Jenkins, who is also a plaintiff. All three—Williams, Wright, and the driver from Jenkins’s incident—entered into premium finance agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”) to pay for their GoAuto insurance. Through these agreements, APAC paid the full insurance premium directly to GoAuto and, in exchange, the insureds made monthly payments to APAC. Each agreement included a power of attorney authorizing APAC to cancel the corresponding insurance policy if the insured failed to pay the premiums. The insurance policies for each plaintiff were ultimately cancelled due to nonpayment. Subsequently, Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action against GoAuto and APAC in Louisiana state court, contending that GoAuto had not properly cancelled their policies and had breached its duty of good faith. Wright was added as a plaintiff through an amended complaint. The action was then removed by GoAuto to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties submitted cross-motions for summary judgment focused exclusively on whether GoAuto’s cancellation practices for financed policies conformed to the requirements of Louisiana law. The district court ruled in favor of GoAuto, concluding its procedures met statutory standards, and dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright then brought this appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by: (1) concluding that the statutory process for delivering a notice of cancellation was correctly followed, (2) determining that GoAuto properly received the requests for cancellation, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To resolve these issues, we first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes governing the cancellation of insurance policies that are financed, and then analyze GoAuto’s methods for fulfilling these legal requirements. Louisiana statutes provide that an insurance premium finance company is permitted to seek cancellation of a policy following an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail... a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a statement certifying” that: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney in accordance with Paragraph (1) of this Subsection, (ii) the premium finance agreement is in default and that default remains uncured within the specified timeframe, (iii) after the default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as described in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, including the date the notice was sent to the insured by the premium finance company, and (iv) copies of the cancellation notice were provided to all persons identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss, specifying the names and addresses of relevant government agencies, security interest holders in the insured property, or third parties to whom the finance company has sent the cancellation notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate meticulous compliance with statutory requirements when a finance company acts under its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). Any procedural irregularity during this cancellation process leads to an invalid cancellation of the insurance policy. See Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Such strict statutory compliance fulfills two primary purposes: first, to provide an unambiguous means for determining the specific date and time when cancellation is effective; and second, to afford a minimum period within which the default may be remedied. Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto utilizes the following steps when cancelling a policy: upon an insured’s failure to make a timely payment, APAC issues a notice to the insured stating the policy will be cancelled if payment is not received within ten days. Should payment not be received by midnight of the tenth day, APAC’s automated computer system emails GoAuto with a cancellation notice and a formal cancellation request. This cancellation request, printed on APAC’s letterhead, includes the following certification by APAC:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes a legally effective power of attorney. (2) There exists a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and such default has not been remedied within the required time period. (3) Following the default, the insured was provided with a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit verifying mailing can be supplied upon request. (4) All parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any loss that may arise thereunder were provided with copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

When the notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system processes the cancellation of the policy. Sometime after this cancellation occurs in the morning, an APAC employee stamps the printed cancellation papers with a signature stamp. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually deliver the documents to a filing cabinet that both GoAuto and APAC used. From 2017 onward, the signed cancellation documents are hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who marks the documents as “received” with a stamp before storing them in the same shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We now turn to the issue of whether these procedures satisfy the requirements set forth in the Louisiana statute previously discussed. Central to the Plaintiffs’ position is the assertion that APAC’s certification is not signed until immediately after, yet still subsequent to, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs construe the statute’s requirement that the premium insurer provide “certification” as meaning a signature must exist prior to effectuating cancellation. Notably, the statute does not expressly call for a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If such a signature were required, it would have to be inferred from the term “certifying” itself. Except for one instance to be addressed separately, Louisiana case law analyzing insurance-premium finance company practices under this statute has not squarely confronted whether a signature is necessary. One appellate court summarized the statutory mandate as necessitating “a statement certifying compliance with” the relevant provisions. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision permitted cancellation to occur “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other decisions offer no additional clarity on this issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, the absence of a signature was noted among the deficiencies of a particular notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court in that case did not find that a missing signature, in and of itself, would prevent cancellation so long as other required components were present. Similar to Benitez, the notices at issue in this case fail to identify a specific employee by name or signature. However, these notices differ from those in Benitez because each expressly “certifies” the pertinent information. In the absence of more definitive Louisiana precedent, we find no basis for concluding a company is unable to certify required information without naming an individual employee. The statute itself assigns the responsibility of effecting cancellation through “certifying” specific details in a notice to “the insurance premium finance company.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Demanding “strict adherence” to statutory cancellation procedures does not grant courts license to impose requirements not found in the text. Accordingly, we hold that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the cancellation notice from the premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s process is noncompliant with the statute because GoAuto allegedly never received a cancellation request. The statutory language provides, “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs note that before 2017, requests were physically stored in a common filing room owned by GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself. They claim that, since 2017, GoAuto no longer obtains these requests because the APAC employee applies the required stamp, not a GoAuto employee. Plaintiffs further assert that, by selecting the term “receipt,” rather than merely requiring a request be sent, the legislature intended GoAuto must actually obtain and possess the notice before policy cancellation can occur. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not provided an interpretation of the statutory term “receipt,” nor is it defined within the statute itself. According to Black’s Law Dictionary, “receipt” means "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession," while “receive” is defined as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Accepting these definitions, we find that GoAuto is permitted to cancel a policy once it has possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request. As previously discussed, upon e-mail receipt in the early morning by GoAuto’s computer infrastructure, that system automatically cancels the relevant policy. A GoAuto representative described the process by which the APAC computer system interfaces with a GoAuto web service: During cancellation, the APAC system initiates a call to a GoAuto web service to request or carry out the cancellation for a policy scheduled for cancellation on a given date. The web service operates as a continuous listener, responding to requests by processing and executing the specified cancellation activity upon recognition and understanding.

Human intervention does not occur until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system processes the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee affixes a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. To reiterate our earlier summary of the statute: prior to effecting cancellation, it is required that the insurer have received a notice of cancellation from the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). As a contemporary statute designed for present conditions, it allows for computers within companies to perform tasks that were once manual. GoAuto’s procedure of electronically receiving and acting on the cancellation notice fulfills the statutory requirements. It is notable that the district court determined GoAuto’s cancellation process complied with the statute for a different reason: that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged there is a lack of Louisiana jurisprudence interpreting “private carrier” and, to resolve this, cited a panel from this court regarding when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court's interpretation of “private carrier” was both creative and atypical. Because we conclude that receipt of the notice by e-mail is sufficient under the statute, we need not evaluate the correctness of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we submit these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that clarifying the relevant statutory language holds significant implications for Louisiana’s insurance sector and legal framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere absence of a state supreme court interpretation for a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Therefore, we refuse to certify the issue. Since GoAuto’s practices align with statutory requirements, the district court’s denial of summary judgment was not in error. AFFIRMED.
