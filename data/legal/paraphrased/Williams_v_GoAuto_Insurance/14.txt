Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are tasked with interpreting a Louisiana law that addresses how insurance policies may be canceled for failure to pay the premium finance company. The appellants, who are former policyholders, argue that the insurer failed to follow the mandated cancellation process, leading to alleged misrepresentations and a breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that the insurer did not violate the statute and dismissed all of the claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the district court’s judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, both previously insured through GoAuto Insurance Company, are the plaintiffs in this matter. Nicholas Jenkins, also a plaintiff, was in a motor vehicle accident with another individual who had insurance coverage from GoAuto; this other GoAuto insured is not a party to the present action. All three—Williams, Wright, and the insured involved in Jenkins’s accident—entered into premium finance agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these agreements, APAC advanced full payment of the insurance premiums to GoAuto and, in exchange, collected monthly installment payments from the insureds. Each such financing agreement assigned APAC a power of attorney that enabled it to cancel the corresponding insurance policy in the event of nonpayment. The respective insurance policies were in fact cancelled due to nonpayment of premiums. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action against GoAuto and APAC in a Louisiana state court, asserting claims that GoAuto failed to properly cancel their policies and breached the duty of good faith. Following an amended complaint, Wright was added as an additional plaintiff. GoAuto subsequently removed the lawsuit to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both parties sought summary judgment exclusively on the issue of whether GoAuto’s methods for cancelling financed policies were consistent with Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, concluding its cancellation practices conformed with state statutory requirements. Consequently, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright have appealed this judgment.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made three errors: (1) in concluding that the statutory procedures for delivering the notice of cancellation were satisfied; (2) in determining that GoAuto properly received the requests for cancellation; and (3) in denying Plaintiffs’ motion for partial summary judgment. To evaluate these claims, we first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes governing cancellation of financed insurance policies and then assess GoAuto’s compliance with those statutory requirements. Pursuant to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company is permitted to seek cancellation of an insurance policy due to an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following criteria: (i) That the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as described in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) That the agreement is in default and that the default has not been cured within the relevant timeframe; (iii) That following default, a notice of cancellation was provided to the insured as required by Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, specifying the date that notice was sent by the finance company to the insured; and (iv) That copies of the notice of cancellation were provided to all persons indicated in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss, listing the names and addresses of all governmental agencies, secured parties, or third parties to whom notice of cancellation was sent by the insurance premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate exact compliance with these statutory provisions when a finance company uses its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). Any irregularity in carrying out this procedure leads to an invalid policy cancellation. Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Rigid observance of the statute accomplishes two objectives: (1) it supplies an explicit procedure for determining the precise moment of cancellation, and (2) it allows a minimum period during which the default can be remedied. Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). In practice, GoAuto implements the following steps for cancelling a policy. When an insured neglects to pay by the due date, APAC issues a notice to the insured warning that failure to submit payment within ten days will result in cancellation. If the payment is not made by midnight of the tenth day, APAC’s automated system sends an email to GoAuto attaching the cancellation notice and a cancellation request. This cancellation request is drafted on APAC’s letterhead and provides the following certification by APAC:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) There has been a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and the default remains uncured within the required time frame. (3) Following the occurrence of default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and the affidavit evidencing proof of mailing can be provided if requested. (4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were also sent to every individual indicated by the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss covered by the agreement.

Once the notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system processes the policy cancellation. After the cancellation occurs later that morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually transport these documents to a filing cabinet jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, however, the stamped cancellation document is hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who marks the document as "received" and then files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We now turn to whether these procedures satisfy the requirements of Louisiana law as previously discussed. A central point in the Plaintiffs’ argument is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until after, and not before, the automatic cancellation of the policy. According to the Plaintiffs, the statute’s directive that the premium insurer “certify” necessitates that a signature be present before a cancellation can take effect. However, the statute itself does not expressly mention a requirement for a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If a signature is mandatory, this obligation would have to be found in the meaning of “certifying.” Except for one instance addressed below, Louisiana appellate decisions evaluating the practices of insurance-premium finance companies under the statute have not ruled on the necessity of a signature. In one case, the court summarized the statute as requiring “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other decisions likewise do not offer further guidance on the issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate court opinion, the absence of a signature was noted among the deficiencies of the notice: the "'notice of cancellation' does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein." Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court did not determine that lacking a signature alone would invalidate a cancellation so long as other requirements were satisfied. Similar to Benitez, the notices at issue here also do not specify an individual employee’s name or provide a signature. In contrast to Benitez, each notice in this case “certifies” the relevant statements. In the absence of more definitive guidance from Louisiana courts, we find no basis to conclude that certification of information cannot be performed by the company without attributing it to a particular employee. The statute explicitly provides that it is “the insurance premium finance company” that effects cancellation through “certifying” specified information in its notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). "Strict adherence" to statutory cancellation procedures does not allow courts to impose additional requirements not stated in the statute. We therefore hold that Louisiana law does not mandate that the premium finance company’s notice to the insurer be signed.

Plaintiffs also argue GoAuto’s cancellation procedure does not strictly comply with the statutory requirements because GoAuto did not directly receive a cancellation request. The statute states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, according to the record, the shared filing space storing printed requests belonged to GoAuto Management Services and not GoAuto. Since 2017, Plaintiffs assert, GoAuto has not been in actual receipt because the employee placing the stamp works for APAC. Plaintiffs claim that the statute’s use of “receipt,” rather than merely requiring that the request be sent, obliges GoAuto to physically possess the notice prior to cancelling the policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted the meaning of “receipt” in this context, and the statute gives no definition. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Accepting these definitions, we find that GoAuto may proceed with cancellation once it has taken possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request. As previously stated, after the notice is e-mailed and received by GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning, the policy is then cancelled by that system. A GoAuto deponent described the manner in which APAC’s computer system communicates with GoAuto’s web service: for example, during the cancellation process, “the APAC process . . . makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual participates in the process until later in the morning, after GoAuto's computer system completes the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. To restate our previous summary of the statute, what is required before cancellation is that the insurer receives “notice of cancellation sent by the premium finance company.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute reflects contemporary conditions, as corporate computers now perform many tasks previously handled by humans. The way GoAuto receives and responds to these notices via its computer system meets the statute’s requirements. Additionally, we note that the district court concluded GoAuto’s cancellation process complied with the statute on another basis, reasoning that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed that there is no Louisiana precedent on interpreting “private carrier,” and looked for guidance to a panel from this court that discussed when a railroad qualified as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was novel and unconventional. As our decision rests on the sufficiency of the email under the statute, we do not address whether this definition was correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to refer these matters of statutory construction to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that determining the correct meaning of the statutory language is significant for Louisiana’s insurance laws and regulatory framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere absence of a prior definition of a term by the state supreme court “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Accordingly, we refuse the request to certify. Since GoAuto’s procedures adhere to the statutory requirements, the district court correctly denied Plaintiffs’ summary judgment motion. AFFIRMED.
