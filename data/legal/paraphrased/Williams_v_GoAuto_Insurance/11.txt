Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana statute that regulates the cancellation of insurance policies for failure to pay the premium finance company. The former policyholders contend that the insurer’s methods of cancellation did not adhere to the statutory requirements, leading to alleged misrepresentations and violations of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court found no violation of the statute and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. A separate individual insured by GoAuto, who is not involved in this case, was in an accident with plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. All three—Williams, Wright, and the driver who collided with Jenkins—financed their GoAuto insurance through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”), pursuant to agreements under which APAC paid GoAuto the full insurance premium upfront, and the insureds agreed to repay APAC in monthly installments. Each of these finance agreements included a power of attorney provision that permitted APAC to cancel the corresponding insurance policy if the insured defaulted on premium payments. Nonpayment resulted in the cancellation of each policy at issue. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action lawsuit in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, claiming that GoAuto had not properly cancelled their insurance coverage and had breached its duty of good faith. Upon the filing of an amended complaint, Wright was added as a plaintiff. GoAuto subsequently removed the matter to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties each moved for summary judgment, but limited their arguments to the question of whether GoAuto’s method for cancelling these financed policies met the requirements of Louisiana law. The district court sided with GoAuto, granting its motion for summary judgment and concluding that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures adhered to state legal standards. This led to the district court dismissing with prejudice all claims asserted against GoAuto. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright then filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors in (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering notice of cancellation were met, (2) concluding that GoAuto appropriately received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To resolve these issues, we first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes regarding the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium finance agreements, followed by an analysis of GoAuto’s compliance procedures. Louisiana law stipulates that a premium finance company may request the cancellation of an insurance policy after an uncured default "by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying" the following matters: (i) That the premium finance agreement contains a legally valid power of attorney as described in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) The agreement has gone into default and such default remains unresolved; (iii) Upon default, notice of cancellation was provided to the insured as outlined in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the date the notice was sent by the premium finance company to the insured; and (iv) Copies of the cancellation notice were also forwarded to every person identified by the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the policy, specifying the identities and addresses of relevant governmental bodies, any holders of a security interest in the insured property, or any other third parties to whom the insurance premium finance company has sent a cancellation notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate exact compliance with the statutory provisions when a finance company utilizes its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). Any procedural flaw in this process renders the cancellation of the policy ineffective. See Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Adhering strictly to the statute has two primary purposes: it delineates an unambiguous method for determining the precise moment of cancellation and it establishes a minimum period in which the insured can remedy the default. See Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto implements the following process for policy cancellation: If the insured does not pay on time, APAC issues notice informing the insured that failure to pay within ten days will result in policy cancellation. Should the payment not be made by the conclusion of the tenth day, APAC's computerized system automatically transmits to GoAuto an email including both the cancellation notice and the cancellation request. This request, sent on APAC’s letterhead, certifies the following:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) There is an existing default under the Premium Finance Agreement, which has not been remedied within the required timeframe. (3) Following the occurrence of default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured; copies of these notices are attached, and the affidavit confirming mailing is available if requested. (4) All individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as holding an interest in any potential loss were provided copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

When GoAuto receives this notice, its computer system processes a cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, on the morning following the cancellation, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually bring these documents to a shared filing cabinet used by both GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, after the document is signed, an APAC employee delivers it to a GoAuto employee, who then stamps it as “received” and stores it in the jointly used filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn next to the question of whether these procedures are in accordance with the Louisiana statute we previously examined. The Plaintiffs’ principal contention is that APAC's certification does not bear a signature until shortly after the policy is automatically canceled, but still only afterward. According to the Plaintiffs, the statutory mandate that the premium insurer must “certify” requires the insurer’s signature to precede any cancellation. However, the statute itself does not expressly mention a signature. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If a signature is indeed necessary, such a requirement would have to be derived from the term “certifying.” Except for a single instance we will address later, there have been no Louisiana judicial decisions evaluating the procedural requirements for insurance-premium finance companies under this statute that have squarely confronted the issue of a signature's necessity. One decision restated the statutory condition as obliging “a statement certifying compliance with” the law. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Further authority does not provide substantive guidance on this specific point. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, the absence of a signature was noted as one of the deficiencies of the notice, observing that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court did not rule that a missing signature alone would invalidate a cancellation notice provided other statutory criteria were satisfied. Similar to Benitez, the notices here fail to identify any employee by printed name or signature. In contrast to Benitez, each of these notices does “certify” the information at issue. Absent definitive Louisiana authority to the contrary, we find no basis to conclude that a corporation cannot certify information without naming an individual employee. The relevant statute specifies that “the insurance premium finance company” may effect cancellation by “certifying” particular facts in a notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Demanding “strict adherence” to cancellation protocols does not give the judiciary license to expand those requirements. Accordingly, we hold that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the cancellation notice issued by a premium finance company to an insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures fall short of the statute because GoAuto never actually received a cancellation request. According to the statute, “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs note that before 2017, the location where printed cancellation requests were filed was managed by GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto. They further maintain that, since 2017, GoAuto has not been the recipient of such requests because an APAC employee performs the stamping of notices. Plaintiffs assert that by using the term “receipt”—rather than merely requiring that a request be “sent”—the legislature intended for GoAuto to actually obtain the notice prior to policy cancellation. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not provided an interpretation of the statutory usage of “receipt,” nor is there a statutory definition. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Applying these definitions, we determine that GoAuto is entitled to cancel a policy after taking physical possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request.

As previously stated, after the e-mailed notice arrives at GoAuto’s computer system during the early morning, the system automatically cancels the policy. Testimony from a GoAuto deponent described the interplay between the APAC system and the GoAuto web service as follows: during the cancellation process, APAC’s system initiates a call to a GoAuto web service to either request or execute a cancellation for a policy scheduled to be cancelled on that date. Essentially, a web service functions as a “listener,” constantly waiting for invocation, and once addressed, it solicits instructions and, if those instructions are recognized, carries out the requested action.

No individual becomes involved until later in the day, following the point at which the GoAuto computer system effectuates the cancellation. Subsequent to this, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. To restate the earlier summary of the statutory requirement: before cancellation can occur, the insurer must have had “receipt” of the notice of cancellation provided by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statutory provision is contemporary and recognizes the modern role that computers play within companies in executing tasks once performed by individuals. GoAuto’s process for receiving and responding to the cancellation notice fulfills the requirements of the statute. The district court observed that GoAuto’s cancellation process complied with the statutory mandate, albeit on different grounds: specifically, it found that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” Noting the lack of Louisiana jurisprudence directly interpreting “private carrier,” the district court found guidance in a Fifth Circuit panel’s discussion regarding when a railroad functions as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The lower court’s characterization of “private carrier” was both inventive and non-traditional. Since we determine that e-mail notice complies with the statutory requirement, we do not address the appropriateness of that definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we submit these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court for resolution. They argue that correctly construing these statutory terms is significant for both Louisiana's insurance sector and its overall statutory framework. However, “As a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a state’s highest court has not previously interpreted a given term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Accordingly, we deny the request to certify. Since GoAuto’s procedures satisfy the statutory requirements, the district court correctly denied the Plaintiffs’ summary judgment motion. AFFIRMED.
