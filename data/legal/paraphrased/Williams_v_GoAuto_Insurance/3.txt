Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

This case on appeal obliges us to examine a Louisiana law regulating the cancellation of insurance policies for nonpayment to the premium finance company. The former policyholders argue that the insurer’s process for cancellation failed to adhere to the statutory requirements, thereby constituting misrepresentations and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that no violation of the statute had occurred and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who previously held insurance through GoAuto Insurance Company, are the plaintiffs in this action. Another individual insured by GoAuto, though not a party to this lawsuit, was involved in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, a plaintiff. Williams, Wright, and the driver in the Jenkins accident each arranged to finance their GoAuto insurance premiums by entering into agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these arrangements, APAC would remit the full premium payment to GoAuto upfront, and the insureds would then make monthly payments to APAC. The financing contracts included a provision granting APAC power of attorney, empowering APAC to cancel the respective insurance policies if premium payments were not made. Ultimately, each policy was cancelled due to nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, contending that GoAuto had not validly cancelled their insurance policies and had breached its duty of good faith. Upon the filing of an amended complaint, Wright was added as a plaintiff. GoAuto subsequently removed the matter to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides moved for summary judgment, but limited the dispute to whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures for financed insurance policies met the requirements of Louisiana law. The district court ruled in GoAuto’s favor, granting summary judgment on the basis that GoAuto’s process conformed with state statutory requirements. Consequently, all claims against GoAuto were dismissed with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright then pursued an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors in (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the cancellation notice were met, (2) concluding that GoAuto duly received the requests for cancellation, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. In order to evaluate these claims, we first outline the relevant Louisiana statutory framework governing the cancellation of financed insurance policies, followed by an analysis of GoAuto’s compliance procedures. Pursuant to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company may initiate cancellation of an insurance policy following an uncured default by transmitting to the insurer—whether by mail, private carrier, or electronic mail—a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a statement certifying each of the following: (i) the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as outlined in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) the agreement is in default and the default remains uncured; (iii) upon default, the premium finance company sent a notice of cancellation to the insured as specified in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the date the notice was sent; and (iv) copies of the cancellation notice were sent to all parties identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any possible loss under the policy, specifying the names and addresses of any government agencies, holders of a security interest in the insured property, or third parties who were sent the notice of cancellation by the insurance premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate exact compliance with these statutory provisions when a finance company utilizes its power of attorney to terminate an insurance policy. Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). According to Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990), “any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” The statute's requirement for strict compliance “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). In its procedure for canceling a policy, GoAuto undertakes the following steps. If the insured does not pay within the allotted time, APAC sends the insured a notice that failure to pay within ten days will result in cancellation of the policy. Should the insured fail to remit payment by midnight of the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically transmits to GoAuto an e-mail containing the cancellation notice and a request for cancellation. The cancellation request appears on APAC’s letterhead and includes the following certification: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) There is an existing default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and such default has not been remedied within the required timeframe. (3) Following the occurrence of default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was dispatched to the insured; attached are copies of these notices, and the affidavit of mailing can be provided upon request. (4) All parties indicated in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement were also furnished with copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once the notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system processes the policy cancellation. Subsequently, on the same morning, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually deliver these documents to a filing cabinet jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, after the signature stamp is affixed, the cancellation document is hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who marks the document as “received” with a stamp and then stores it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn next to the question of whether these procedures satisfy the requirements of the Louisiana law previously analyzed. Central to the Plaintiffs’ contention is the fact that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until shortly after, but unmistakably following, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs contend that the statutory mandate for the premium insurer to “certify” obligates the presence of a signature prior to any effective cancellation. However, the statute does not specifically mention any signature requirement. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should a signature be necessary, such a duty would have to be derived solely from the statutory term “certifying.” Except for one case to be addressed, no Louisiana court opinion reviewing insurance-premium finance company procedures under this statute has spoken to whether a signature is required. One court characterized the statutory obligation as needing “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another opinion found cancellation permissible “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional judicial opinions are equally silent on this particular issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

An opinion from a court of appeal referenced the absence of a signature while evaluating the deficiencies of that notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court in Benitez did not rule that a missing signature alone would render a cancellation invalid if all other requirements were satisfied. The notices presented in this case, like in Benitez, also do not provide the name or signature of any specific employee. In contrast to Benitez, these notices do include a “certification” of the pertinent information. In the absence of clearer guidance from Louisiana courts, we find no basis to determine that certification of information must necessarily involve identifying a specific employee. The statute at issue states that “the insurance premium finance company” may effectuate cancellation by “certifying” the necessary details in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Demanding “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not empower courts to impose additional procedural steps. We therefore hold that Louisiana law does not mandate that the premium finance company’s notice to the insurer be signed. Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s process fails to strictly comply with the statute because GoAuto never physically receives the cancellation request. According to the statute, “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs note that before 2017, the shared filing space housing the printed requests belonged to GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself. Since 2017, they claim GoAuto has not received the requests because the employee responsible for affixing the stamp works for APAC. Plaintiffs maintain that the legislature’s choice of the word “receipt,” instead of merely requiring the request be sent, obligates GoAuto to actually possess the notice prior to cancellation. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted the term “receipt” in this statute, nor is the term defined therein. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Applying these definitions, we determine that GoAuto is entitled to cancel a policy when it obtains possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As described earlier, when GoAuto’s computer system receives the e-mailed notice in the early morning, the system immediately executes the cancellation. A GoAuto deponent described the interaction between APAC’s computer system and a GoAuto web service as follows: during the cancellation process, APAC’s process “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until some time later in the morning, after GoAuto’s computer system has completed the cancellation process. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. To reiterate our previous summary of the statute, what is required before a cancellation occurs is that the insurer must have "receipt" of the notice of cancellation that the premium finance company has sent. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary and reflects modern practices, as company computer systems now perform tasks that previously required personal intervention. GoAuto’s process of receiving the notice electronically and responding to it is consistent with the statutory requirement. We note that the district court found GoAuto’s cancellation procedures satisfied the statute for a different reason: “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged there was no Louisiana precedent interpreting the term “private carrier,” but found guidance in this court’s panel opinion regarding when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was novel and unorthodox. However, because we determine that the e-mail process complies with the statute, we need not address whether that definition is correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs have asked that we refer these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court for resolution. They argue that correctly construing the statutory language carries significance for Louisiana’s insurance industry and regulatory framework. However, “as a general rule we are reluctant to certify legal questions unless there is a compelling justification; certification is a critical tool for this court, and we avoid overusing it so as not to jeopardize its continued use.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a state supreme court has not yet construed a particular term “does not, standing alone, justify certification.” Id. We therefore decline to certify the question. Since GoAuto’s processes are in accordance with the statutory requirements, the district court correctly denied the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
