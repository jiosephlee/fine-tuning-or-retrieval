Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

This case requires us to interpret a Louisiana law addressing the cancellation of insurance coverage for nonpayment owed to the premium finance company. The former policyholders argue that the insurance company failed to comply with the statutory cancellation requirements, which they assert led to inaccurate representations and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that no statutory breach occurred and dismissed all of the plaintiffs’ claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this suit, was involved in an accident with plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the driver from Jenkins's accident all obtained their GoAuto policies through financing agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). APAC paid the full insurance premium upfront to GoAuto, while the insureds agreed to repay APAC through monthly installments. These agreements conferred on APAC a power of attorney, allowing it to terminate the respective insurance policies if premium payments were not made. All of the referenced policies were terminated due to nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a putative class action in a Louisiana state court, asserting claims against GoAuto and APAC on the grounds that GoAuto failed to lawfully cancel the policies and did not act in good faith. Wright was later added as a plaintiff through an amended complaint. GoAuto removed the lawsuit to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties subsequently filed cross-motions for summary judgment focused exclusively on whether GoAuto’s cancellation process for financed insurance policies met the requirements of Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, finding its cancellation procedures to be compliant with state statutes. Consequently, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright then appealed the decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) concluding that GoAuto appropriately received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. In evaluating these contentions, we first outline Louisiana’s legal framework concerning the cancellation of financed insurance policies and subsequently analyze the measures GoAuto employed to meet those statutory obligations. Louisiana law provides that a premium finance company is permitted to seek cancellation of an insurance policy, in the event of an uncured default, “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” that: (i) The premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as specified in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) the premium finance agreement is in default, and such default remains uncured; (iii) upon default, a notice of cancellation was issued to the insured in accordance with Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, with the date of mailing specified by the finance company; and (iv) copies of the notice of cancellation were provided to all individuals identified by the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss covered by the policy, including the names and addresses of relevant governmental entities, security interest holders in the insured property, or other third parties to whom the notice was sent by the premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) There is a current default under the Premium Finance Agreement which remains uncured within the required time. (3) Following this default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured. Enclosed are copies of the notices, and the affidavit establishing proof of mailing can be furnished upon request. (4) All parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any loss potentially arising under the agreement were also sent copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

When this notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system processes and executes the policy cancellation. Subsequently, later that same morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually deliver these documents to a filing cabinet that both GoAuto and APAC used. From 2017 onward, the process involves an APAC employee handing the signed cancellation document directly to a GoAuto employee, who then stamps it as “received” and stores it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn to the question of whether these procedures adhere to the Louisiana statute previously addressed. Central to Plaintiffs' position is their assertion that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until after, though closely following, the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs read the statutory mandate that the premium insurer “certify” to require a signature before a policy may be canceled. However, the statute itself does not specifically mention a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If a signature were to be necessary, that requirement must be inferred solely from the term “certifying.” Apart from one exception we will examine shortly, Louisiana courts assessing the procedures of insurance-premium finance companies in this context have not discussed whether a signature is required. For instance, one appellate decision restated the statutory provision by indicating there must be “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another appellate opinion permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional cases provide no more guidance on the signature issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, pp. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, the court referenced the absence of a signature when evaluating deficiencies in a particular notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court stopped short of determining that a missing signature alone would invalidate a cancellation if other requirements were met. Here, as was true in Benitez, the notices at issue do not provide the name or signature of any employee. Unlike Benitez, however, every notice here “certifies” the information it communicates. Given the absence of explicit Louisiana precedent to the contrary, we find no basis to require that a specific employee be identified in order for the company to certify the contents. The statute expressly states that it is “the insurance premium finance company” that enacts a cancellation through “certifying” specific information within the notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The requirement of “strict adherence” to cancellation protocols does not empower courts to impose additional procedural demands beyond those set out in the statute. Therefore, we determine that under Louisiana law, no signature is necessary on the notice a premium finance company sends to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s procedures fail to strictly comply with the statute because GoAuto itself allegedly never receives a cancellation request. The statute states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, the storage room for these paper requests was controlled by GoAuto Management Services, rather than GoAuto. From 2017 onward, Plaintiffs assert that GoAuto does not receive the requests since an APAC employee applies the required stamp. Plaintiffs argue that the statutory use of the word “receipt”—as opposed to a mere requirement that the request be sent—means GoAuto must actually receive and possess the notice before canceling a policy. The meaning of “receipt” under this statute has not been delineated by the Louisiana Supreme Court, nor is it defined within the statute itself. Black’s Law Dictionary provides the following definitions: “receipt” as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession," and “receive” as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Adopting these interpretations, we find that GoAuto is authorized to cancel a policy as soon as it has acquired possession, delivery, or control over a cancellation request. As described previously, when the e-mailed notice is delivered to GoAuto’s computer systems in the early hours, that system processes the policy cancellation. According to deposition testimony from a GoAuto witness, the APAC system interfaces with a GoAuto web service in the following manner: during the cancellation workflow, “the APAC process . . . makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

Human involvement occurs only after the GoAuto computer system executes the cancellation later in the morning. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation paperwork. To reiterate our prior summary of the statutory requirement, before cancellation can occur, the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This provision reflects contemporary realities, where corporate computer systems now perform tasks previously managed by individuals. GoAuto’s procedure for receiving notice and responding to it meets the statutory mandate. Additionally, we note that the district court determined GoAuto’s cancellation procedures fulfilled the statutory requirements in an alternative manner, finding that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged a lack of Louisiana jurisprudence interpreting “private carrier” and found persuasive a Fifth Circuit panel’s discussion of when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was both creative and outside conventional understanding. Since we conclude that the e-mail transmission complies with the statute, we do not reach the question of whether that definition is correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we submit these questions of statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that resolving the meaning of these statutory terms holds significant implications for both Louisiana’s insurance regulatory framework and its broader statutory structure. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). Merely because the state supreme court has yet to define a term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Accordingly, we choose not to certify the issue. Since GoAuto’s procedures are in accordance with the statute, the district court properly denied the motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
