Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we must interpret a Louisiana law relating to the termination of insurance policies due to insureds failing to pay the company that financed their premiums. The former insureds argue that the procedures employed by the insurance company to cancel their policies were not in accordance with the statute, leading to alleged misrepresentations and violations of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that there was no violation of the statute and dismissed all of the plaintiffs' claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who were previously insured by GoAuto Insurance Company, brought this action as plaintiffs. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this suit, was in a collision involving Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Williams, Wright, and the driver from Jenkins’s accident all obtained financing for their GoAuto insurance premiums by entering into agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these agreements, APAC would advance the entire insurance premium to GoAuto and, in exchange, the insureds made monthly installment payments to APAC. The financing contracts authorized APAC through a power of attorney to cancel the insurance policies if the insureds defaulted on payment. Each policy was ultimately cancelled for nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, contending that GoAuto had not validly cancelled the insurance policies and had breached its duty of good faith. Following an amended complaint, Wright joined the lawsuit as an additional plaintiff. GoAuto then removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both parties sought summary judgment limited to the issue of whether GoAuto's methods for cancelling financed policies complied with Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment for GoAuto, concluding that its cancellation procedures met the requirements of state law, and accordingly dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently appealed that decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by: (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation were satisfied; (2) concluding that GoAuto appropriately received the cancellation requests; and (3) denying their request for partial summary judgment. In order to evaluate these claims, we first outline Louisiana law regarding the cancellation of financed insurance policies, followed by a discussion of GoAuto’s compliance procedures with that law. According to Louisiana statutes, an insurance-premium finance company may initiate cancellation of a policy after an uncured default by sending the insurer—by mail, private carrier, or electronic mail—a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a statement certifying: (i) that the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney pursuant to Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) that there exists a default under the agreement, which has not been timely remedied; (iii) that, after default, the premium finance company sent a notice of cancellation to the insured as provided under Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the date it was sent; and (iv) that copies of the notice of cancellation were forwarded to all parties with an interest indicated in the premium finance agreement—listing the names and addresses of relevant governmental agencies, security interest holders, or third parties who received notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes a legitimate power of attorney provision. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement has entered into default, and this default remains uncured within the required time frame. (3) Following default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued and sent to the insured. Copies of these notices are attached hereto, and proof of mailing by affidavit can be furnished upon request. (4) All individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the policy also received copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once such notice is received, GoAuto's computer system processes the cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, on the morning of the cancellation, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the finalized cancellation documents. Before 2017, an APAC staff member would physically bring these documents to a filing cabinet that both GoAuto and APAC shared. Beginning in 2017, after applying the signature stamp, the completed cancellation document is delivered in person to a GoAuto employee, who then stamps it “received” and stores it in the mutual filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn to the issue of whether these processes conform to the provisions of Louisiana law previously analyzed. The Plaintiffs’ main contention is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until shortly after—though unmistakably following—the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs interpret the statutory mandate for the premium insurer to “certify” as imposing a requirement that a signature must exist prior to cancellation. However, La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a) contains no direct mention of any signature requirement. If such a requirement exists, it must arise from the meaning ascribed to the term “certifying.” With one exception to be addressed, no Louisiana court reviewing procedures of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute has held that a signature is necessary. In KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37, a court described the statutory obligation as furnishing “a statement certifying compliance with” the law. Another court in Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89), permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Additional cases provide little further clarification. See, for example, Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate case, the absence of a signature was noted as a deficiency in the notice, with the court observing that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the appellate court did not determine that merely failing to provide a signature would make the cancellation ineffective if other requirements were met. Similar to Benitez, the present record shows that none of the notices contains the printed name or signature of a specific employee. But unlike Benitez, each of the present notices includes a “certification” of the necessary information. Absent clearer precedent from Louisiana courts, we find no justification to require that such certification must include the name of an individual employee. The relevant statutory provision expressly provides that “the insurance premium finance company” is to effectuate cancellation by “certifying” specific information within the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). “Strict adherence” to the cancellation procedures outlined in the law does not empower courts to impose requirements not included in the statute itself. Accordingly, we hold that Louisiana law does not mandate that the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer be signed.

Plaintiffs also argue that GoAuto’s procedures do not fully comply with the statute, asserting that GoAuto never actually “received” a cancellation request. The statutory language provides that “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs contend that, before 2017, printed requests were stored in a shared file room that belonged to GoAuto Management Services, not GoAuto itself, and that since 2017, requests have not been directly received by GoAuto because the employee affixing the cancellation stamp works for APAC. Thus, plaintiffs claim the statutory term “receipt”—rather than simply the act of sending—requires that GoAuto actually take possession of the cancellation notice before it can terminate the policy.

Neither the Louisiana Supreme Court nor the statute itself defines the term “receipt” as it is used here. Black’s Law Dictionary provides that “receipt” means “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” is defined as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Relying on these definitions, we determine that GoAuto becomes entitled to cancel a policy when it takes possession of, is delivered, or otherwise takes custody of the cancellation request.

As previously outlined, once the e-mailed notice is electronically received by GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning, that system automatically processes the policy cancellation. According to deposition testimony, an APAC computer system interfaces with a GoAuto web service: During the cancellation sequence, the APAC system “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until some time later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system processes the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the hard-copy cancellation forms. We reiterate our prior summary of the statute. Before cancellation can occur, it is required that the insurer receives notification of cancellation sent by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This is a contemporary statute reflecting modern practice, with tasks previously performed by people now handled by company computer systems. GoAuto’s procedure for receiving and acting on such notice meets the statutory requirements. We note that the district court determined GoAuto’s cancellation processes were consistent with the statute for a different reason: it concluded “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed there was a lack of Louisiana precedent construing the term “private carrier” and relied on a decision by this circuit analyzing whether a railroad constituted a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was inventive and unusual. Because we conclude the e-mail complies with the statutory provision, we do not address whether this definition is correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs seek certification of these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. They argue that the correct reading of the statutory language is significant for both Louisiana’s insurance industry and the state’s statutory framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a state supreme court has not yet defined a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Thus, we refuse to certify the issue. Since GoAuto’s practices are in accordance with the statute, the district court properly denied the Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
