Case Caption: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket Number: 24-30646  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Leslie H. Southwick

In this case, we must interpret a Louisiana law concerning the termination of insurance coverage due to an insured's failure to pay the premiums to the finance company. The former policyholders allege that the insurer's methods of cancellation were inconsistent with the statutory requirements, thereby constituting both misrepresentation and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no statutory breach occurred, dismissing every claim with prejudice. We AFFIRM the judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another GoAuto policyholder, who is not a party to this case, was involved in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, one of the plaintiffs. Williams, Wright, and the GoAuto policyholder from Jenkins’s accident all entered into premium financing agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these agreements, APAC would advance the full premium to GoAuto, and in return, the insureds would make monthly payments to APAC. Each agreement gave APAC a power of attorney, permitting APAC to cancel the relevant insurance policy in the event of nonpayment. Each of these insurance policies was in fact cancelled for failure to pay premiums. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action suit in a Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto failed to validly cancel their policies and did not act in good faith. Wright joined as a plaintiff upon the filing of an amended complaint. GoAuto removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides submitted motions for summary judgment concerning solely whether GoAuto’s procedures for cancelling financed policies met the requirements of Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, concluding that GoAuto had followed procedures consistent with Louisiana statutory law. Accordingly, the court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright appealed the district court’s decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by: (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) concluding that GoAuto correctly received the requests for cancellation, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To resolve these issues, we first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes concerning the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium finance agreements, followed by a review of GoAuto’s practices for adherence to those statutes. According to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company may initiate cancellation of an insurance policy after a default remains uncured by “sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” each of the following: (i) that the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as stipulated in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) that the premium finance agreement is in default, and the default was not cured within the prescribed period; (iii) that after default, a notice of cancellation was issued to the insured pursuant to Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, specifying the date on which the premium finance company sent this notice to the insured; and (iv) that copies of the cancellation notice were sent to all parties indicated in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in potential losses, including the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, security interest holders in the insured property, or any third parties who received the cancellation notice from the insurance premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney provision. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement has entered default status, which has not been remedied within the required timeframe. (3) Following default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured. Enclosed are copies of such notices; affidavit evidence regarding the mailing of these notices is available upon request. (4) All individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in potential losses thereunder also received copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Upon transmission of that notice, GoAuto’s computerized system executes cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later that morning, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the cancellation documents. Prior to 2017, an APAC staff member would physically store the documents in a filing cabinet jointly used by both GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the completed cancellation document is instead delivered by hand to a GoAuto staff member, who applies a “received” stamp to the document before filing it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn next to the question of whether the procedures at issue are consistent with the Louisiana statute previously analyzed. Central to Plaintiffs’ contention is the absence of a signature on APAC’s certification until a point shortly after—though definitively following—the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs assert that the statutory mandate for “certifying” by the premium insurer necessitates the presence of a signature as a precondition to cancellation taking effect. However, the statute itself does not expressly mention a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If a signature is to be implied as necessary, that implication must come from the interpretation of the word “certifying.” Except for an exception to be discussed further below, Louisiana appellate decisions interpreting insurance-premium finance companies’ practices under this statute have not specifically addressed whether a signature is needed. For instance, one court restated the statutory requirement as necessitating “a statement certifying compliance with” the law. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another court allowed for cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Similarly, other court decisions do not provide meaningful guidance on the specific issue of a signature requirement. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, a court noted the absence of a signature while evaluating deficiencies in a particular notice, stating: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not conclude that a missing signature, standing alone, would invalidate the cancellation as long as other requirements were satisfied. Like Benitez, the notices at issue here also lack identification or signatures of individual employees. Unlike Benitez, these notices do “certify” the pertinent details. Given the absence of specific Louisiana precedent, we find no basis to hold that a company cannot certify information simply because the certification is not linked to a named employee. The relevant statutory language provides that it is “the insurance premium finance company” that carries out the cancellation by “certifying” certain facts in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Mandating “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not authorize courts to require additional steps beyond those set forth in the statute. Accordingly, we determine that Louisiana law does not make a signature on the notice from the premium finance company to the insurer a prerequisite for cancellation.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s methods are not in “strict adherence” with the statutory requirements, alleging that GoAuto never actually receives a cancellation request. According to the statute, “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs note that prior to 2017, the shared filing area where paper requests were kept belonged to GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto itself. Since 2017, Plaintiffs assert that GoAuto fails to receive cancellation requests because the employee responsible for stamping them is employed by APAC, not GoAuto. Plaintiffs maintain that because the legislature used the word “receipt,” rather than simply requiring requests to be sent, the statute demands GoAuto actually take possession of the notice before the policy may be cancelled. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not yet construed the meaning of “receipt” in this statutory context, nor does the statute offer a definition. Black’s Law Dictionary, however, defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Relying on these definitions, we find that GoAuto has authority to cancel a policy as soon as it takes delivery, custody, or possession of a request for cancellation. As previously described, when the e-mailed notice is received by GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning, cancellation occurs automatically. One GoAuto deponent elaborated on the interaction between an APAC computer system and a GoAuto web service, explaining: During cancellation, “the APAC process . . . makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system has processed the cancellation. At that subsequent time, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. To recapitulate the earlier summary of the statute: what is required before cancellation is that the insurer has “receipt” of the notice of cancellation transmitted by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary, reflecting present-day practices in which tasks formerly performed by individuals are now handled by computers. The process employed by GoAuto to receive and act upon the notice meets the statutory requirement. We note that the district court ruled that GoAuto’s cancellation process was in compliance with the statute for a different reason: specifically, that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed that there is no Louisiana jurisprudence interpreting “private carrier” and considered analogous a decision of this court that addressed circumstances when a railroad acts as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was creative and unconventional. Since we conclude the e-mail fulfills the statutory requirement, we do not address the correctness of this definition.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge that we certify these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that the correct construction of the statutory language has significant ramifications for both Louisiana’s insurance industry and its statutory framework. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a term remains undefined by the state’s highest court “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore refuse to certify the question. Given that GoAuto’s methods are in accordance with the statutory requirements, the district court appropriately denied summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
