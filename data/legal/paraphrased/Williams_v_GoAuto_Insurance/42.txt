Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this case, we must interpret a Louisiana law pertaining to the cancellation of insurance policies due to a failure to pay the premium finance company. The appellants, who are former policyholders, assert that the insurer’s method of cancellation contravened the statute, allegedly leading to both misrepresentations and a breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that there was no violation of statute and dismissed the entirety of plaintiffs’ claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this lawsuit, was involved in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, who is also a plaintiff. Williams, Wright, and the driver involved in Jenkins’s accident had all obtained financing for their GoAuto insurance premiums through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). APAC agreed to pay the premiums in full to GoAuto, and the insureds, in turn, made monthly installment payments to APAC. Under each premium finance agreement, APAC was given a power of attorney that enabled it to cancel the policies if premiums were not paid. All three policies were cancelled due to nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins then initiated a class action suit in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, claiming that GoAuto had not validly cancelled their insurance and had breached its duty of good faith. After the complaint was amended, Wright was added as a plaintiff. GoAuto removed the matter to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties then submitted cross-motions for summary judgment solely addressing whether GoAuto’s method for cancelling the financed insurance complied with Louisiana statutory requirements. The district court granted summary judgment in GoAuto’s favor, finding its procedures met state law, and consequently dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently appealed this decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by (1) determining that the statutory procedures governing delivery of cancellation notices had been observed, (2) concluding that GoAuto properly received the cancellation requests, and (3) denying their motion for partial summary judgment. To resolve these claims, we first outline the applicable Louisiana statutes concerning the cancellation of insurance policies obtained through financing agreements and then analyze GoAuto’s methods for meeting those statutory requirements. Louisiana law provides that an insurance premium finance company may seek cancellation of an insurance policy after an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following: (i) That the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as specified in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) That the premium finance agreement is in default, and the default has not been remedied within the allowed time; (iii) That, upon default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured pursuant to Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the date on which the premium finance company sent this notice to the insured; and (iv) That copies of the notice of cancellation were sent to all parties identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss, and specifying the names and addresses of any government entities, security interest holders in the insured property, or other third parties to whom the insurance premium finance company has provided the cancellation notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) There is an existing default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and this default has not been remedied within the required time frame. (3) Following the occurrence of default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured; copies of these notices are attached, and an affidavit of proof of mailing can be provided upon request. (4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were also distributed to all individuals identified by the Premium Finance Agreement as holding an interest in any potential loss under its terms.

When that notice is received, GoAuto's computer system processes the cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, on the same morning following the cancellation, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the cancellation documents that have been printed. In the period prior to 2017, an APAC staff member would physically deposit the documents into a filing cabinet jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. From 2017 onward, once signed, the cancellation document is delivered in person to a GoAuto employee, who then stamps the document as “received” before placing it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn now to the question of whether these procedures satisfy the requirements of the Louisiana statute previously discussed. Central to the Plaintiffs’ position is that APAC’s certification is unsigned until shortly after—and undeniably after—the policy has been automatically cancelled. Plaintiffs read the statute’s mandate for “certifying” by the premium insurer as necessitating that a signature be provided prior to any cancellation occurring. The statute, however, contains no explicit mention of a signature. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If a signature were required, such an obligation would have to stem from the meaning of the word “certifying” within the statute. Except for one instance we will address, Louisiana courts examining procedures of insurance-premium finance companies under this provision have not considered whether a signature is necessary. In one decision, the statutory language was summarized as demanding “a statement certifying compliance with” the relevant statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another opinion found cancellation proper “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). The remaining case law provides no greater clarity on the issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, pp. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

A court of appeal previously observed that the “notice of cancellation” in question was deficient because it “does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not determine that the lack of a signature, standing alone, would invalidate the cancellation where other statutory requirements are satisfied. Consistent with Benitez, the notices presented in this case neither specify an individual employee’s name nor bear a signature. They differ from those in Benitez, though, because each of these notices does “certify” the necessary information. Since Louisiana precedent provides no more definitive guidance, we do not find that identification of a particular employee is a prerequisite for valid certification by the company. The text of the statute expressly provides that “the insurance premium finance company” may effectuate cancellation by “certifying” designated information in a notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Strict adherence to cancellation procedures does not authorize courts to add new procedural requirements. Therefore, we hold Louisiana law does not mandate that a notice from a premium finance company to an insurer must bear a signature.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s process fails to strictly comply with the statute because GoAuto allegedly never actually receives a request for cancellation. The relevant statutory provision reads: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Before 2017, according to Plaintiffs, GoAuto Management Services—not GoAuto—controlled the common filing room where the printed requests were kept. After 2017, Plaintiffs assert, GoAuto does not receive the cancellation requests because an APAC employee applies the necessary stamp. Plaintiffs argue that by employing the term “receipt,” rather than only “send,” the legislature required that GoAuto actually obtain physical possession of the notice before cancellation can proceed. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted “receipt” within the context of this statute, nor does the statute supply its own definition. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and defines “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Relying on these definitions, we hold that GoAuto may effectuate a policy cancellation upon obtaining possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As detailed earlier, policy cancellation occurs when the notice is received electronically in the early morning hours by GoAuto’s computer system. One GoAuto witness described the interaction between APAC’s and GoAuto’s systems, stating: during the cancellation process, APAC’s system “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until after GoAuto’s computer system processes the cancellation later in the morning. At that point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation documents. To restate our previous summary of the statutory requirement: for cancellation to occur, the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice that the premium finance company sent. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute reflects modern practices, in which company computers now perform tasks that individuals previously handled. GoAuto’s process of receiving and acting on the notice through its system meets the statute’s requirements. The district court ruled that GoAuto’s procedures were compliant for a separate reason, reasoning that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged the lack of Louisiana precedent construing “private carrier” and instead found guidance from a Fifth Circuit panel addressing whether a railroad qualified as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was notably creative and unorthodox. However, since we conclude that the e-mail process fulfills the statutory requirement, we decline to address the soundness of that interpretation.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to certify questions regarding the interpretation of these statutes to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that the correct reading of these statutory terms bears significant consequences for Louisiana's insurance regulatory framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a state supreme court has not yet construed a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Accordingly, we decline Plaintiffs’ request for certification. As GoAuto’s practices align with statutory requirements, the district court’s denial of Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment was not erroneous. AFFIRMED.
