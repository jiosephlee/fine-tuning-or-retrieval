Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

This appeal necessitates our examination of a Louisiana law addressing the termination of insurance policies for nonpayment owed to the entity that financed the premiums. The former policyholders assert that the insurer’s cancellation process failed to adhere to the statutory requirements, thereby leading to misrepresentations and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no statutory breach had occurred and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the district court’s judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto—who is not involved in this case—was in an accident with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. All three individuals—Williams, Wright, and the driver involved in Jenkins’s collision—financed their GoAuto insurance premiums via agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these agreements, APAC paid the entire insurance premium to GoAuto and, in exchange, received monthly payments from the insureds. The financing contracts also provided APAC with a power of attorney, empowering APAC to cancel the policies if the insured failed to make premium payments. Each of the insurance policies was cancelled for nonpayment of premiums. Subsequently, Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action in a Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, claiming that GoAuto did not properly cancel their insurance policies and failed to act in good faith. Felita Wright was added as a plaintiff through an amended complaint. GoAuto then removed the lawsuit to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides submitted motions for summary judgment that addressed only whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures for financed policies complied with Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, determining that its cancellation practices adhered to state law requirements. Accordingly, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently filed an appeal.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by: (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the cancellation notice were met; (2) concluding that GoAuto adequately received the cancellation requests; and (3) denying their motion for partial summary judgment. To respond to these claims, we first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes governing the cancellation of insurance policies subject to financing agreements and then analyze GoAuto’s methods of adhering to these statutory requirements. Pursuant to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company is authorized to initiate cancellation of an insurance policy following an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following conditions: (i) the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as required by Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) the premium finance agreement is in default, and that default remains uncured; (iii) upon default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured in accordance with Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, including the date it was sent by the premium finance company to the insured; (iv) copies of the notice of cancellation were delivered to all parties identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in potential losses, specifying the identities and addresses of any relevant governmental entities, security interest holders, or third parties who were sent a notice of cancellation by the insurance premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate strict compliance with statutory provisions when a finance company utilizes its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy. Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). Furthermore, “[a]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Rigid compliance with statutory requirements fulfills two purposes: first, it establishes an unequivocal method for determining the precise moment and date of cancellation; second, it guarantees a minimum period during which a default can be remedied. Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto implements a specific set of procedures when initiating cancellation of a policy. Should an insured neglect to make a timely payment, APAC dispatches a notice to the insured, warning that the policy will be cancelled if payment is not made within ten days. If the insured does not pay by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically transmits an email to GoAuto, attaching a copy of the cancellation notice together with a request for cancellation. This cancellation request, printed on APAC’s letterhead, includes the following certification: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) There exists a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, and such default remains uncured within the required timeframe. (3) Following the default, the insured was provided with a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation. Enclosed are copies of these notices; an affidavit attesting to their mailing can be supplied upon request. (4) All individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in potential losses covered thereby received copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system terminates the policy. Subsequently, on the same morning and following cancellation, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually deliver the documents to a filing cabinet that both GoAuto and APAC used. Beginning in 2017, however, the signed cancellation document is hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who marks it as “received” with a stamp and then stores it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next examine whether these procedures satisfy the requirements of the Louisiana statute previously discussed. The Plaintiffs’ central contention is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until a point in time that is indisputably after the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs argue that the statute’s mandate of “certifying” by the premium insurer necessitates a signature as a prerequisite to effective cancellation. However, the statute does not specifically mention the need for a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should such a requirement exist, it would have to be derived from the term “certifying” itself. With one exception, which will be addressed separately, Louisiana courts reviewing insurance-premium finance companies’ practices under this statute have not ruled on whether a signature is required. For example, one court summarized the statute’s requirement as necessitating “a statement certifying compliance with” the law. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional cases likewise offer little insight on the signature issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

An appellate court decision referenced the absence of a signature as one of the deficiencies in the notice, observing that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court did not decide that lacking a signature alone would invalidate the cancellation notice if the remaining statutory requirements were satisfied. Here, as in Benitez, the notices at issue fail to specify an individual employee either by printed name or signature. Distinct from Benitez, each notice in the present matter does include a “certification” of the relevant details. In the absence of explicit guidance from Louisiana authority, we find no justification to conclude that a corporation cannot provide a certification without identifying a specific employee. The relevant statute expressly indicates it is “the insurance premium finance company” that effects the cancellation by “certifying” certain information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Requiring “strict adherence” to statutory cancellation procedures does not authorize the judiciary to impose additional procedural requirements. We therefore hold that Louisiana law does not mandate the premium finance company’s notice to the insurer bear a signature. Plaintiffs also argue that GoAuto’s process is noncompliant with the statute because GoAuto allegedly never actually receives a cancellation request. The statute states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs note that before 2017, the shared filing area housing the physical requests was owned by GoAuto Management Services, rather than GoAuto. Since 2017, Plaintiffs assert, GoAuto does not receive the requests because the employee applying the stamp works for APAC. They argue that the legislature’s use of “receipt,” as opposed to mere transmittal, requires that GoAuto actually obtain possession of the notice before the policy can be cancelled. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not construed “receipt” as used in this context, nor does the statute define the term. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). We adopt these definitions and find that GoAuto may proceed to cancel a policy upon obtaining possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request. As described previously, the policy is cancelled once GoAuto’s computer system receives the emailed notice during the early morning hours. A GoAuto deponent detailed the interaction between the APAC computer system and a GoAuto web service, explaining: During the cancellation process, the APAC system “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual participates until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system has completed the cancellation process. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. To recapitulate our earlier statutory analysis, prior to cancellation, the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice transmitted by the premium finance company, pursuant to La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute, being contemporary, contemplates that company computers perform functions that previously required human intervention. The way GoAuto receives and processes the notice by computer meets the statutory requirement. Additionally, the district court determined that GoAuto’s cancellation process met the statute for a different reason: “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court noted that Louisiana precedent does not address the term “private carrier,” and found persuasive a panel decision from this court regarding the classification of a railroad as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s characterization of “private carrier” was notably creative and unconventional. Since we hold that the e-mail complies with the statutory requirements, we do not reach the question of whether that definition is valid.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge that we refer these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court for resolution. They argue that correctly construing these statutory terms is significant for Louisiana’s insurance regulatory framework and industry. However, “[a]s a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a term has not yet been construed by the state supreme court “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. Therefore, we deny the request to certify. Since GoAuto’s processes align with the statute, the district court correctly denied the Plaintiffs’ summary judgment motion. AFFIRMED.
