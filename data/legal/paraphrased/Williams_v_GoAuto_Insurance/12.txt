Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana law that regulates the cancellation of insurance policies due to nonpayment of premiums to the financing company. The appellants, who are former insureds, allege that the insurer failed to adhere to the statutory cancellation procedures, thereby committing misrepresentation and violating the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court found no violation of the statute and dismissed the claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM the judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, both formerly insured by GoAuto Insurance Company, serve as plaintiffs in this matter. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not a party to this action, was involved in a collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. Each of Williams, Wright, and the driver in Jenkins’s accident obtained their GoAuto insurance through financing agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these agreements, APAC paid the full premium to GoAuto on behalf of each insured, who then made monthly installment payments to APAC. The agreements conferred upon APAC a power of attorney enabling it to cancel the policies if payments were not made. Subsequently, each insurance policy was cancelled due to nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins instituted a class action in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, alleging, among other things, that GoAuto did not effectively cancel the policies and breached its duty of good faith. Following an amended complaint, Wright was added as a plaintiff. GoAuto then removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties proceeded to file cross-motions for summary judgment, which addressed only whether GoAuto’s procedure for cancelling insurance policies financed through APAC adhered to Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in GoAuto’s favor, concluding that GoAuto had complied with state legal requirements, and accordingly dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently appealed this decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court erred in three respects: (1) by determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) by concluding that GoAuto correctly received the cancellation requests, and (3) by refusing their request for partial summary judgment. To evaluate these claims, we first outline the relevant Louisiana law regarding the cancellation of financed insurance policies, and then analyze the methods GoAuto employed to comply with these legal standards. According to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company is authorized to seek cancellation of a policy when a default remains uncured by “sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” certain facts: (i) The premium finance contract contains a valid power of attorney under Paragraph (1) of this Subsection. (ii) The contract is in default and that default has not been remedied within the prescribed time. (iii) Following default, the insured was sent a notice of cancellation pursuant to Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, and the premium finance company specifies the date the notice was sent to the insured. (iv) Copies of the notice of cancellation have been transmitted to all individuals or entities reflected in the premium finance agreement as having a possible interest in any resulting loss, including listing the identities and addresses of any governmental entities, lienholders, or other parties who were sent a copy of the notice by the finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate strict compliance with statutory procedures when a finance company acts under its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). According to Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990), “any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” The strict construction of the statute serves dual purposes: first, to “provide[] a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation,” and second, to “grant[] a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). In practice, GoAuto has established the following cancellation procedures. Upon an insured’s failure to pay timely, APAC sends a notice indicating that the policy will be canceled if payment is not received within ten days. If payment remains outstanding at the end of the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically transmits an e-mail to GoAuto, which includes both a copy of the cancellation notice and a cancellation request. This request, prepared on APAC letterhead, contains the following certification from APAC:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement has been breached, and the breach has not been remedied within the required timeframe. (3) Following the occurrence of default, the insured was provided with a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and an affidavit verifying mailing is available if requested. (4) The Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was also provided to all parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system terminates the policy. Following the cancellation, later that morning, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the hard copy of the cancellation documents. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually transport these documents to a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the signed cancellation document is hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who stamps it as “received” before storing it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn now to whether these procedures align with the Louisiana law previously analyzed. The Plaintiffs’ principal contention is that APAC’s certification bears no signature until immediately after—but still following—the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs construe the statutory mandate of "certifying" by the premium insurer to necessitate a signature before cancellation may occur. The text of the statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a), contains no explicit directive requiring a signature. Any such mandate would have to be implied from the term “certifying.” With one forthcoming exception, Louisiana courts reviewing insurance-premium finance company practices under this law have not specifically examined whether a signature is essential. For instance, one court described the statutory provision as requiring "a statement certifying compliance with" the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another case permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other relevant decisions provide no further illumination on this specific issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one opinion from a court of appeal, the lack of a signature was discussed among the deficiencies of the notice: the "'notice of cancellation' does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to 'certify' any of the statements contained therein." Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court did not determine that a missing signature, by itself, would invalidate the cancellation if all other requirements were met. As was the case in Benitez, the notices present in the current record also do not include any named employee, whether by printed name or signature. In contrast to Benitez, every notice here does "certify" the required information. In the absence of more definitive guidance from Louisiana courts, we find no basis to conclude that a company is unable to certify information unless a specific employee is identified. The statute expressly provides that “the insurance premium finance company” carries out cancellation by “certifying” specified information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Demanding “strict adherence” to the statutory cancellation procedures does not justify judicially supplementing the existing requirements. Therefore, we determine Louisiana law does not mandate that the notice issued by the premium finance company to the insurer must bear a signature.

The Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s process fails to comply strictly with the statutory requirement because GoAuto allegedly never obtains the cancellation request. Under the statute, “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs assert that prior to 2017, the physical storage of the printed requests was in a filing room owned by GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto. Since 2017, they contend, GoAuto does not obtain these requests because the task of stamping is performed by an APAC employee. According to Plaintiffs, because the statute uses the word "receipt"—instead of simply requiring the request to be sent—GoAuto must physically possess the notice prior to any cancellation. Neither the Louisiana Supreme Court nor the statute itself has specifically defined the term “receipt” within this context. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession," and “receive” as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Adopting these meanings, we find that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it assumes possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As previously described, upon GoAuto’s computer system’s receipt of the e-mailed notice in the early morning, the system cancels the policy. A GoAuto deponent described the interaction between the APAC computer system and the GoAuto web service as follows: for example, during cancellation, the APAC process “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, following the automated cancellation carried out by the GoAuto computer system. Subsequently, at that later point, an APAC employee affixes a signature stamp to the cancellation documents once they have been printed. To reiterate our earlier summary of the statutory language: before cancellation can occur, the insurer must have “receipt” of the notice of cancellation provided by the premium finance company, as required by La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The statute is a modern enactment designed for present-day circumstances, where company computers now perform tasks traditionally handled by humans. GoAuto’s process for receiving and responding to the notice meets the statute’s requirements. Additionally, we note the district court determined that GoAuto’s cancellation process satisfied the statute on an alternate basis: “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged the lack of Louisiana precedent interpreting “private carrier” and considered as persuasive a panel opinion from this court discussing the circumstances under which a railroad could be deemed a private carrier, referencing Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court's interpretation of "private carrier" was innovative and unorthodox. However, because we conclude the e-mail notice fulfills the statutory requirement, we do not reach the question of whether the district court’s definition is correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask that we submit these questions concerning statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that resolving the proper meaning of the relevant statutory terms holds significance for both Louisiana’s insurance sector and the overall statutory framework. However, “[a]s a general rule, we exercise caution in certifying legal questions absent a compelling justification; the opportunity to certify is so valuable to this court that we avoid excessive use to ensure continued access.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a state supreme court has not construed a term “does not, by itself, justify certification.” Id. We therefore decline certification. Since GoAuto’s methods satisfy the statutory requirements, the district court properly denied Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
