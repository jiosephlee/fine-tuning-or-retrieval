Case Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket Number: No. 24-30646  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Leslie H. Southwick

In this appeal, we must interpret a Louisiana law that addresses cancellation of insurance policies due to an insured's failure to pay a premium finance company. The appellants, who were previously insured, allege that the insurance provider's methods for canceling policies failed to meet the statute’s requirements, purportedly causing misrepresentations and constituting a breach of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no violation of the statute occurred and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who previously held insurance policies with GoAuto Insurance Company, serve as plaintiffs in this action. Another GoAuto policyholder—who is not a party to this suit—was in a collision with Nicholas Jenkins, also a plaintiff. Williams, Wright, and the individual involved in Jenkins’s accident each financed their insurance premiums with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”) under separate agreements. Under these agreements, APAC remitted the full premium amount to GoAuto and collected monthly installment payments from the insureds. The financing contracts also conferred upon APAC a power of attorney, permitting it to cancel the insurance policies if the insureds failed to make the required payments. In each instance, the policy in question was terminated due to nonpayment. Subsequently, Williams and Jenkins commenced a class action suit in Louisiana state court against both GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto had not properly cancelled the policies and failed to act in good faith. Wright joined the case as a named plaintiff upon the filing of an amended complaint. GoAuto removed the matter to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties then submitted cross-motions for summary judgment limited to the issue of whether GoAuto’s procedures for cancelling financed insurance policies conformed with Louisiana statutory requirements. The district court granted GoAuto’s summary judgment motion, determining that GoAuto’s cancellation process was in accordance with state law, and consequently dismissed with prejudice all claims against GoAuto. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright subsequently appealed the district court’s decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the cancellation notice were satisfied, (2) concluding that GoAuto correctly received the cancellation requests, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To resolve these assertions, we first outline the relevant Louisiana statutes governing the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium agreements, and then analyze GoAuto’s adherence to those statutory mandates. Louisiana law provides that an insurance-premium finance company may initiate cancellation of a policy after an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” several requirements: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as outlined in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) the agreement has entered default and that default remains uncured; (iii) following default, the company sent a notice of cancellation to the insured in accordance with Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, indicating the date on which the company sent the notice to the insured; and (iv) notices of cancellation were also sent to every person identified by the finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss, specifying the names and addresses of governmental agencies, security interest holders, or other third parties who received the cancellation notice from the finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an effective power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement is currently in default, and that default remains uncured within the required timeframe. (3) Following the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured; enclosed are copies of these notices, and affidavit proof of mailing can be provided upon request. (4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were also distributed to all individuals identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system effectuates the cancellation of the policy. Later in the same morning, after the policy has been canceled, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation documents. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually store these documents in a filing cabinet that was jointly used by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, however, the signed cancellation document is instead delivered directly to a GoAuto employee, who marks the document as “received” and subsequently files it in a shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn next to the issue of whether these procedures are consistent with the Louisiana statute previously examined. Central to the Plaintiffs’ position is the fact that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until shortly after—and definitely after—the policy has been canceled automatically. Plaintiffs construe the statutory mandate that the premium insurer “certify” as necessitating a signature prior to the policy’s cancellation. However, the statute itself does not expressly mention any signature requirement. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If such a requirement exists, it must be inferred from the meaning of “certifying.” Except for an instance to be detailed below, no Louisiana court decision evaluating the procedures of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute has directly addressed whether a signature is necessary. One court paraphrased the statutory obligation as the need for “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another case recognized cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other rulings likewise fail to shed light on this issue. See, for example, Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

A court of appeal referenced the absence of a signature as one of several deficiencies in the notice, observing that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court stopped short of ruling that lacking a signature would, by itself, invalidate a cancellation provided that other criteria were satisfied. Here, as in Benitez, the notices at issue fail to identify any specific employee either by signature or printed name. However, unlike Benitez, the present notices “certify” the pertinent facts. In the absence of more definitive Louisiana precedent, we perceive no basis to preclude a company from certifying facts without a designated employee being named. The relevant statutory provision states that it is “the insurance premium finance company” that executes the cancellation by “certifying” designated facts within the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Requiring “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not authorize courts to read requirements into the process that do not exist. We accordingly find that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the notice provided by a premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s procedures do not comply with statutory requirements because GoAuto itself did not receive a cancellation request. The statute states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs note that before 2017, printed requests were maintained in a communal filing space owned by GoAuto Management Services, rather than GoAuto. Since 2017, Plaintiffs contend, GoAuto does not actually receive the requests because the employee applying the stamp works for APAC. Plaintiffs assert that, based on the legislature’s choice of the term “receipt” instead of a mere sending requirement, the statute obligates GoAuto to physically take possession of the notice before terminating coverage.

The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted “receipt” within this statutory context, and the statute itself supplies no definition. According to a prominent legal dictionary, “receipt” means "[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession," and “receive” is defined as "[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source." Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Accepting these definitions, we hold that GoAuto may lawfully cancel a policy once it gains possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request.

As previously described, once an emailed notice reaches GoAuto’s computer system in the early morning, that system promptly cancels the policy. A GoAuto representative explained the interface between an APAC computer and a GoAuto web service as follows: In the cancellation workflow, the APAC process communicates with a GoAuto web service to request or effectuate cancellation of a policy set to cancel on a given date. The web service functions as a “listener” that constantly awaits instructions; once it is contacted, it determines what is requested and, if it recognizes the task, executes the cancellation.

No individual becomes involved until sometime later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system completes the cancellation process. At that subsequent stage, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the physical copies of the cancellation documents. To restate our previous summary of the statute: before cancellation can occur, the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice provided by the premium finance company, in accordance with La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This is a contemporary statute, and, reflecting modern practices, company computers perform many functions that were once carried out by human beings. The manner in which GoAuto receives and processes the notice, via its computer system, complies with the statutory requirements. Additionally, the district court found GoAuto’s cancellation practices met the statute on another basis, reasoning that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court noted that there is a lack of Louisiana precedent regarding the meaning of “private carrier,” and referred to an opinion from this court discussing when a railroad qualified as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was innovative and unconventional. However, since we determine that the e-mail fulfills the statutory requirement, we need not address whether that definition was correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs have asked that we certify these matters of statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court. They argue that the correct reading of the statute's language is significant to both Louisiana’s insurance industry and its statutory structure. However, “as a general rule, we are hesitant to certify questions of law unless there is a compelling justification; certification remains a valuable tool for this court, and we are cautious not to undermine its future availability by employing it excessively.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a term is not yet defined by the state supreme court “does not, by itself, justify certification.” Id. Thus, we choose not to certify the question. Given that GoAuto’s processes align with the statute, the district court properly denied the motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
