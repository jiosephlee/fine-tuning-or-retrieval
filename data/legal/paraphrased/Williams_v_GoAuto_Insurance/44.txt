Case Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket Number: 24-30646  
Opinion by: Circuit Judge Leslie H. Southwick

In this case, we must interpret a Louisiana law that addresses the cancellation of insurance policies due to an insured's failure to pay the premiums owed to the premium finance company. The former policyholders contend that the insurer's actions in canceling the policies did not adhere to the statutory requirements, alleging that this resulted in misstatements and a breach of the duties of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that the insurance company's conduct did not violate the statute and consequently dismissed the claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that decision.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who were previously insured by GoAuto Insurance Company, are the plaintiffs in this matter. Another individual insured by GoAuto—who is not a party to this action—was in a collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. All three—Williams, Wright, and the individual involved in the accident with Jenkins—had financed their respective GoAuto insurance policies through separate agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under those arrangements, APAC advanced the full premium amount to GoAuto in exchange for the insureds’ promise to make monthly payments to APAC. Each of these financing agreements included a provision granting APAC power of attorney to cancel the insurance policies if the insureds defaulted on payment. Ultimately, all the policies at issue were cancelled due to nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action suit in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, alleging that GoAuto had not properly cancelled their policies and had breached its obligation of good faith. After the complaint was amended, Wright joined the case as an additional plaintiff. GoAuto then removed the lawsuit to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties proceeded to file cross-motions for summary judgment limited to the legal sufficiency of GoAuto’s process for cancelling policies financed through APAC under Louisiana law. The district court ruled in favor of GoAuto’s summary judgment motion, determining that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures met state requirements. Consequently, the district court dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright then sought appellate review of that decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made mistakes in (1) concluding that the statutory process for delivering the cancellation notice was properly followed, (2) finding that GoAuto lawfully received the cancellation requests, and (3) refusing to grant their motion for partial summary judgment. To address these claims, we first review the relevant Louisiana legal standards governing cancellation of insurance policies that have been financed, and then analyze GoAuto’s methods of complying with those standards. In Louisiana, an insurance-premium finance company is permitted to initiate cancellation of an insurance policy following an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following: (i) that the premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney pursuant to Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) that the premium finance agreement is in default and this default has not been timely remedied; (iii) that upon default, a cancellation notice was provided to the insured as required in Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, including the date on which the premium finance company sent it to the insured; (iv) that copies of the cancellation notice were delivered to every person named in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the agreement, and specifying the names and addresses of all government entities, security interest holders, or third parties to whom notice was sent by the insurance premium finance company. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement is currently in default, and the default has not been remedied within the required time. (3) After the occurrence of default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was provided to the insured. Enclosed are copies of these notices, and affidavit evidence of mailing can be furnished upon request. (4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were also transmitted to each individual identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any prospective loss under the agreement.

Upon that notice being received, GoAuto’s computerized system effectuates cancellation of the policy. Subsequently, later that same morning, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the hard copy cancellation documents. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually deliver the documents to a filing cabinet accessible to both GoAuto and APAC. Starting in 2017, after the document is signed, a GoAuto employee marks the document as “received” by stamping it, and then stores it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We turn now to examine whether these practices are consistent with the Louisiana statute previously addressed. Central to Plaintiffs’ position is the contention that APAC’s certification does not bear a signature until shortly after—and indisputably subsequent to—the policy’s automatic cancellation. Plaintiffs construe the statutory mandate that the premium insurer “certify” as necessitating a signature before any cancellation is effected. However, the statute itself does not specifically require a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). Should such a requirement exist, it would have to derive from the meaning of “certifying” as used in the statute. With one exception discussed below, Louisiana courts reviewing the conduct of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute have not addressed whether a signature is necessary. One appellate court restated the statute as requiring “a statement certifying compliance with” its provisions. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional cases do not provide further clarification on this specific issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

A court of appeal opinion once referenced the absence of a signature as one of the deficiencies of a cancellation notice: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court in that matter did not determine that a missing signature would alone invalidate a cancellation if the remaining requirements were met. As in Benitez, the notices found in this record also fail to indicate which employee prepared them, whether by printing a name or by including a signature. However, in contrast to Benitez, each notice in the current case “certifies” the necessary details. In the absence of more explicit Louisiana precedent, we find no justification to hold that certification cannot be accomplished by the company itself, without specifically naming an employee. The statute itself provides that it is “the insurance premium finance company” that may effect cancellation by “certifying” certain facts in a notice. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). The mandate for “strict adherence” to cancellation procedures does not give courts license to supplement or expand those procedures. Accordingly, we find Louisiana law does not impose a requirement that the notice sent by the premium finance company to the insurer must bear a signature.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s process fails the statute’s requirements for strict compliance because GoAuto allegedly never received a cancellation request. According to the statute: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Prior to 2017, the physical space where printed requests were retained was managed by GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto itself. Since 2017, Plaintiffs maintain that GoAuto does not actually obtain the requests because the individual applying the stamp is employed by APAC. Plaintiffs interpret the statute’s use of the term “receipt”—rather than a simple requirement that the notice be sent—as imposing an obligation on GoAuto to actually obtain the cancellation notice before effecting a policy cancellation.

The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted “receipt” as it appears in this statute, and the statute does not offer its own definition. Black’s Law Dictionary, Twelfth Edition (2024), defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and defines “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Based on these definitions, we hold that GoAuto acquires the authority to cancel a policy when it takes delivery, possession, or custody of a request for cancellation.

As previously discussed, once GoAuto’s computer system receives the e-mailed notice in the early morning, the system immediately effects cancellation of the policy. A deponent for GoAuto described the interaction between the APAC system and GoAuto’s web service as follows: During the cancellation process, the APAC procedure “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No human intervention occurs until some time after the GoAuto computer system executes the cancellation. Subsequently, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the generated cancellation documents. To restate our prior summary of the statute: before cancellation may occur, the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice transmitted by the premium finance company, in accordance with La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This law reflects current realities, recognizing that tasks once performed by people are now often managed by corporate computers. GoAuto’s process of receiving the notice and acting in response meets the statutory requirements. We also note the district court’s alternative reasoning for finding GoAuto’s cancellation process compliant: it determined that “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court acknowledged a lack of Louisiana precedent on the meaning of “private carrier,” and found an apt comparison in a panel decision from this court which addressed whether a railroad constituted a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The lower court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was innovative and departed from conventional definitions. As our conclusion rests on the adequacy of the e-mail notice to meet the statute, we decline to address the soundness of the district court’s construction.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs seek certification of these statutory interpretation questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that a correct reading of the statutory language holds significant consequences for Louisiana’s insurance industry and statutory framework. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that the state’s highest court has not yet construed a term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore refuse to certify this issue. Since GoAuto’s practices adhere to the statute, the district court’s denial of the motion for summary judgment was not error. AFFIRMED.
