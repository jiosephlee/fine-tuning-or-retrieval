Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit
Date: October 3, 2025
Docket: No. 24-30646
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

This case calls for an examination of a Louisiana law addressing the cancellation of insurance policies due to nonpayment to the entity that provided premium financing. The former policyholders assert that the insurance company failed to follow the statutory procedures for cancellation, leading to alleged misrepresentations and a violation of the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court determined that no statutory breach occurred and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM that judgment.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, the plaintiffs, are former policyholders with GoAuto Insurance Company. Another individual insured by GoAuto—who is not involved in this action—was in a collision with Plaintiff Nicholas Jenkins. All three—Williams, Wright, and the individual in the accident with Jenkins—obtained financing for their GoAuto insurance policies through agreements with American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”). Under these arrangements, APAC would advance the entire premium payment to GoAuto and receive monthly installments from the insureds. Each of these agreements provided APAC with a power of attorney, enabling APAC to cancel the insurance coverage if the insureds defaulted on payments. Nonpayment led to the cancellation of each policy. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action in a Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, asserting that GoAuto had failed to validly cancel their insurance policies and had breached its duty of good faith. Wright was added as a plaintiff following the filing of an amended complaint. GoAuto subsequently removed the case to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. The parties then submitted motions for summary judgment addressing only the question of whether GoAuto’s cancellation process for the financed insurance policies met the requirements of Louisiana law. The district court granted summary judgment in favor of GoAuto, finding that its cancellation procedures were in compliance with state law. Consequently, all claims against GoAuto were dismissed with prejudice by the district court. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright have appealed that decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made errors by (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) concluding that GoAuto properly received the requests for cancellation, and (3) rejecting their motion for partial summary judgment. To evaluate these claims, we first outline Louisiana’s legal framework regarding the cancellation of insurance policies financed through premium finance agreements, and then review GoAuto’s methods for meeting these statutory requirements. According to Louisiana law, an insurance-premium finance company is permitted to request cancellation of an insurance policy after an uncured default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the following: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as set forth in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection. (ii) The premium finance agreement is in default and the default remains uncured. (iii) Upon default, a cancellation notice was sent to the insured in accordance with Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, specifying the date the notice was sent by the premium finance company to the insured. (iv) Copies of the cancellation notice were provided to all persons identified by the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any loss under the policy, with the certification specifying the names and addresses of any government agencies, security interest holders, or third parties to whom the finance company sent notice of cancellation. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

Louisiana courts mandate rigorous compliance with statutory provisions when a finance company uses its power of attorney to effectuate the cancellation of an insurance policy. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). Any irregularity in the process renders the policy cancellation invalid. Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). This strict compliance with statutory requirements fulfills two purposes: it establishes an unambiguous procedure to determine the precise date and time of cancellation, and it guarantees a minimum period for remedying the default. Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto utilizes the following protocol to cancel a policy: upon an insured's failure to remit timely payment, APAC issues a notice to the insured advising that the policy will be cancelled unless payment is received within ten days. Should payment remain outstanding by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computerized system automatically generates and sends an e-mail to GoAuto containing a copy of the cancellation notice together with a cancellation request. This request is transmitted on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) There exists a default under the Premium Finance Agreement, which remains uncured within the required time period. (3) Following the occurrence of the default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured party. Copies of such notices are attached hereto, and an affidavit verifying the mailing can be provided upon request. (4) Copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation were also delivered to every individual identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in any potential loss covered thereby.

Once the notice is received, GoAuto’s computer system terminates the policy. Subsequently, on the same morning following cancellation, an APAC staff member applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee manually placed these documents in a filing cabinet that both GoAuto and APAC used. Beginning in 2017, the signed cancellation document is instead hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who marks it as “received” with a stamp and then files it in the shared cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We now turn to the question of whether these procedures are consistent with the Louisiana law previously addressed. The Plaintiffs’ central contention is that APAC’s certification lacks a signature until shortly after—though definitively following—the automatic cancellation of the policy. Plaintiffs interpret the statutory requirement that the premium insurer “certify” as necessitating a signature before a cancellation can become effective. The statute itself does not expressly mention a signature. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If such a requirement exists, it must be derived from the term “certifying.” With one exception to be discussed further, no Louisiana court opinion analyzing insurance-premium finance company practices under this statute has considered whether a signature is mandatory. In one instance, a court rephrased the statutory mandate as a requirement for “a statement certifying compliance with” the statute. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another decision permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Additional cases do not provide further guidance on this issue. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

In one appellate decision, the absence of a signature was noted as one deficiency among others: the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, the court did not find that merely lacking a signature would be enough to invalidate a cancellation if the remaining statutory elements were satisfied. In the present matter, as in Benitez, the notices lack identification of a specific employee either by name or signature. But here, each notice does "certify" the pertinent details. In the absence of more explicit guidance from Louisiana courts, we see no justification to conclude that a company must identify a specific employee in order to certify the contents of a notice. The statutory language itself provides that “the insurance premium finance company” effects cancellation by “certifying” specific information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). A mandate of “strict adherence” to the cancellation procedures does not authorize courts to supplement those requirements. Accordingly, we determine that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on the cancellation notice issued by the premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s process fails to strictly comply with the statute because GoAuto itself never receives a cancellation request. The statutory provision states: “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). Plaintiffs note that before 2017, the storage room for printed requests was owned by GoAuto Management Services rather than GoAuto. After 2017, Plaintiffs maintain, GoAuto does not actually receive the requests because an APAC employee applies the stamp. Plaintiffs contend that the legislature’s choice of the term “receipt,” rather than a requirement that the request be merely sent, imposes an obligation on GoAuto to physically possess the notice prior to cancellation. The Louisiana Supreme Court has not interpreted “receipt” as used in this context, and the statute offers no definition. Black’s Law Dictionary defines “receipt” as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” as “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). We adopt these definitions and hold that GoAuto may effect cancellation upon taking possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As detailed, once the cancellation notice is e-mailed and received by GoAuto’s computer system early in the morning, the system processes the cancellation. A GoAuto witness described the way APAC’s system communicates with a GoAuto web service: During the cancellation workflow, APAC’s system “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual becomes involved until later in the morning, after the GoAuto computer system processes the cancellation. At that subsequent point, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation forms. To restate our earlier reference to the statute: before a cancellation may occur, the insurer must have had “receipt” of a notice of cancellation from the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary and acknowledges that tasks once performed by individuals are now handled by company computers. The manner in which GoAuto receives and processes the notice satisfies the requirements of the statute. Additionally, we note that the district court determined GoAuto’s cancellation process complied with the statute on a separate basis: “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed that there is no Louisiana precedent construing the term “private carrier” and considered a panel from this court’s reasoning about when a railroad qualifies as a private carrier. See Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was novel and unorthodox. Since we hold that the email procedure meets the statutory requirements, we do not address whether the district court’s definition is correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs urge us to refer these questions of statutory construction to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that clarification of the statutory language holds significant consequences for Louisiana’s insurance regulatory framework and industry. However, “as a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that a state’s highest court has not yet interpreted a statutory term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore refuse to certify the issue. Since GoAuto’s practices are in accordance with the statute, the district court’s decision to deny summary judgment was correct. AFFIRMED.
