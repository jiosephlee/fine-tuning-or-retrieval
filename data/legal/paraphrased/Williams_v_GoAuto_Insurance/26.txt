Title: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance  
Court: United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit  
Date: October 3, 2025  
Docket: No. 24-30646  
Author: Leslie H. Southwick, Circuit Judge

In this appeal, we are called upon to interpret a Louisiana law pertaining to the termination of insurance policies due to nonpayment to the premium finance company. The former insureds allege that the insurer failed to follow the statutory procedures for cancellation, and as a result, engaged in misrepresentation and violated the duty of good faith and fair dealing. The district court concluded that there was no breach of the statute and dismissed all claims with prejudice. We AFFIRM.

FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

Kimberly Williams and Felita Wright, who were previously insured by GoAuto Insurance Company, are the plaintiffs in this case. Another individual insured by GoAuto, who is not involved in this litigation, was in a vehicle collision with Nicholas Jenkins, a plaintiff. Williams, Wright, and the driver involved in Jenkins’s incident each used financing through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (“APAC”) to pay for their GoAuto insurance policies. APAC would pay the full premium to GoAuto on their behalf, and the insureds would then reimburse APAC in monthly installments. Under each finance agreement, APAC was granted a power of attorney that permitted it to cancel the policies if premium payments were not made. In each situation, cancellation occurred due to nonpayment. Williams and Jenkins initiated a class action suit in Louisiana state court against GoAuto and APAC, claiming that GoAuto did not properly cancel their insurance policies and did not fulfill its duty of good faith. After an amended complaint, Wright was joined as a plaintiff. GoAuto removed the action to the United States District Court for the Middle District of Louisiana. Both sides submitted summary judgment motions focused exclusively on whether GoAuto’s cancellation process for these financed policies was lawful under Louisiana statutes. The district court awarded summary judgment to GoAuto, finding GoAuto’s procedures lawful under state law, and accordingly dismissed all claims against GoAuto with prejudice. Williams, Jenkins, and Wright appealed this decision.

DISCUSSION

The Plaintiffs argue that the district court made mistakes by (1) determining that the statutory requirements for delivering the notice of cancellation were satisfied, (2) concluding that GoAuto appropriately received the cancellation requests, and (3) refusing to grant their motion for partial summary judgment. To address these issues, we first outline Louisiana’s legal framework governing cancellation of financed insurance policies, then review how GoAuto implemented those statutory requirements. Louisiana law permits an insurance-premium finance company to seek cancellation of a policy following an uncured default by transmitting to the insurer—whether through mail, private carrier, or electronic mail—a copy of the notice of cancellation and a statement certifying the following: (i) The premium finance agreement includes a valid power of attorney as referenced in Paragraph (1) of this Subsection; (ii) There has been a default under the premium finance agreement which remains uncured; (iii) After default, notice of cancellation was dispatched to the insured pursuant to Paragraph (2) of this Subsection, with the date of dispatch by the premium finance company specified; (iv) Copies of the cancellation notice were sent to all persons identified in the premium finance agreement as having an interest in any potential loss under the policy, specifying the names and addresses of all relevant governmental agencies, security interest holders in the insured property, or third parties to whom the insurance premium finance company sent the notice of cancellation. See La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv).

“Louisiana courts require strict adherence to these statutory requirements when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel an insurance policy.” Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993). “[A]ny defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). Strict adherence to the statute “serves two functions: (1) it provides a clear method of fixing the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) it grants a minimum time during which the default may be cured.” Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). GoAuto follows the following procedures in order to cancel a policy. When an insured fails to make timely payment, APAC sends notice to the insured that their policy will be cancelled if no payment is submitted within ten days. If no payment is submitted by midnight on the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends GoAuto an e-mail with a copy of the notice of cancellation along with a request for cancellation. The request for cancellation is sent on APAC’s letterhead and states the following: APAC certifies that:

(1) The Premium Finance Agreement includes an enforceable power of attorney. (2) The Premium Finance Agreement is currently in default, and the default has not been remedied within the allotted time frame. (3) Upon the occurrence of default, a Ten Day Notice of Cancellation was issued to the insured. Copies of these notices are attached, and the affidavit of mailing is available upon request. (4) All parties identified in the Premium Finance Agreement as having an interest in potential losses under the agreement received copies of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation.

Once that notice is received, GoAuto's computerized system effectuates cancellation of the policy. Later the same morning, following cancellation, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the printed cancellation paperwork. Before 2017, an APAC employee would manually place these documents into a filing cabinet used jointly by GoAuto and APAC. Beginning in 2017, the signed cancellation paperwork is instead hand-delivered to a GoAuto employee, who stamps the document as “received” before placing it in the shared filing cabinet.

I. Strict Adherence to the Statute

We next examine whether these procedures align with the Louisiana statute previously analyzed. The Plaintiffs principally argue that APAC’s certification is not signed until shortly after—though definitely after—the policy has been canceled automatically. According to the Plaintiffs, the statutory mandate that the premium insurer “certify” requires that a signature be affixed prior to any cancellation taking effect. However, the statute itself does not expressly mention a signature requirement. La. Rev. Stat. § 9.3550(G)(3)(a). If such a requirement exists, it must arise solely from the meaning ascribed to the word “certifying.” With one exception addressed below, Louisiana case law interpreting the procedures of insurance-premium finance companies under this statute has not dealt with the necessity of a signature. For example, one court summarized the statute as necessitating “a statement certifying compliance with” the law. KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood, 12-757, p.5 (La. App. Cir. 4/10/13), 115 So. 3d 34, 37. Another permitted cancellation “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins., 546 So. 2d 898, 901–02 (La. App. 1 Cir. 6/20/89). Other cases provide little further guidance. See, e.g., Lewis v. Julien, 20-152, p. 9–10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/16/20), 309 So. 3d 842, 848–50.

An opinion from a court of appeal referenced the absence of a signature when outlining deficiencies in a notice, noting that the “‘notice of cancellation’ does not identify any particular employee of [the premium finance company] that prepared the document, is not signed by any individual, and does not purport to ‘certify’ any of the statements contained therein.” Benitez v. Elsayed, 19-122, p. 10 (La. App. 5 Cir. 12/4/19), 285 So. 3d 572, 579. However, that court did not determine that merely lacking a signature would, by itself, render the cancellation notice invalid if the other statutory requirements were met. Similar to the circumstances in Benitez, the notices before us also lack an indication—either by signature or printed name—of the specific employee responsible. In contrast to Benitez, though, every notice in the current record includes a certification of the necessary information. Absent specific Louisiana precedent to the contrary, we find no justification for concluding that a company cannot certify the requisite details without disclosing the name of an employee. The statute itself assigns to “the insurance premium finance company” the responsibility to effect cancellation by “certifying” specific information in the notice. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a). Requiring “strict adherence” to statutory cancellation procedures should not be understood as authorizing courts to expand the list of procedural requirements. Therefore, we hold that Louisiana law does not mandate a signature on a notice of cancellation provided by a premium finance company to the insurer.

Plaintiffs further argue that GoAuto’s cancellation procedures do not strictly comply with the statute because GoAuto never actually receives the cancellation request. Under the statute, “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The Plaintiffs point out that, before 2017, the room storing the printed requests belonged to GoAuto Management Services instead of GoAuto. Since 2017, Plaintiffs maintain, GoAuto no longer receives the requests because an APAC employee affixes the cancellation stamp. Plaintiffs thus claim that, by using the term “receipt” instead of merely requiring that the request be sent, the legislature intended to require that GoAuto must physically receive the notice before cancelling the policy. The Louisiana Supreme Court has yet to interpret “receipt” in the context of the statute, and the statute does not define the term. According to a well-known legal dictionary, “receipt” is defined as “[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,” and “receive” means “[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.” Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024); Receive, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024). Adopting these definitions, we determine that GoAuto may effect cancellation once it has possession, delivery, or custody of a cancellation request. As previously explained, when GoAuto’s computer system receives the emailed notice during early morning hours, the system proceeds to cancel the policy. A GoAuto representative detailed the interaction between the APAC computer system and the GoAuto web service: For example, during the cancellation process, the APAC process “makes a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation or perform cancellation for a policy that is canceling on that particular date. . . . Basically, a web service is like a listener. It’s always listening, waiting to be called, and when it hears its name when it’s called, it says, well, tell me what you want to do, and if it understands it, it performs the activity.”

No individual participates until later in the day, following the cancellation processed by the GoAuto computer system. After this occurs, an APAC employee applies a signature stamp to the physical cancellation forms. To restate the relevant statutory language we quoted earlier: the statute requires that, before cancellation, the insurer must have “receipt” of the cancellation notice sent by the premium finance company. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). This statute is contemporary, reflecting that computers now carry out many tasks previously done by people. The process GoAuto employs to receive and process the notice is sufficient under the statute. Additionally, we note that the district court determined GoAuto’s cancellation procedure met statutory requirements on different grounds, stating: “the APAC employee who delivers the notices of cancellation neatly fits the definition of private carrier.” The district court observed that there is no Louisiana precedent interpreting “private carrier” and found it helpful to reference this court’s analysis of what constitutes a private carrier with respect to a railroad, as in Huntley v. Bayer MaterialScience, L.L.C., 452 F. App’x 453, 457 (5th Cir. 2011). The district court’s interpretation of “private carrier” was innovative and atypical. Because we conclude that email notification is statutorily sufficient, we need not address whether the district court’s definition was correct.

II. Certification

The Plaintiffs ask us to refer these questions of statutory interpretation to the Louisiana Supreme Court, arguing that how these statutory terms are construed has significant implications for the state's insurance industry and its legislative framework. However, “As a general proposition we are chary about certifying questions of law absent a compelling reason to do so; the availability of certification is such an important resource to this court that we will not risk its continued availability by going to that well too often.” Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The mere fact that the state supreme court has yet to interpret a particular term “is not sufficient to warrant certification.” Id. We therefore refuse to certify the question. Since GoAuto’s procedures satisfy the statutory requirements, the district court properly denied Plaintiffs’ motion for summary judgment. AFFIRMED.
