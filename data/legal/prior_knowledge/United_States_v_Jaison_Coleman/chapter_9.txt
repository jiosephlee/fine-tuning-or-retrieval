Introduction

A defendant in a criminal case often faces a choice between going to trial and entering a guilty plea. In many instances the defendant wishes to accept the benefits of a plea (reduction of exposure, certainty of outcome, negotiated sentence) while preserving the ability to challenge a discrete legal ruling—most commonly an adverse pretrial motion to suppress evidence. Federal practice provides a mechanism known as a conditional or reserved-issue guilty plea that permits a defendant to plead guilty yet retain the right to appeal specified nonjurisdictional rulings. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework governing such pleas, the requirements imposed by the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure and practice, the scope of issues that may be reserved, the consequences of pleading unconditionally, and the principles that determine whether an appellate court has jurisdiction to hear an appeal following a conditional plea.

# Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 11(a)(2) requirements

Rule 11(a)(2) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure authorizes a defendant to enter a plea of guilty while reserving, with the government’s consent and the court’s approval, the right to appeal a previously denied pretrial motion. The rule is procedural and protective: it allows a defendant to take the practical benefit of a plea agreement while preserving appellate review of certain adverse rulings that may significantly affect the defendant’s decision to plead. To operate as intended, the reservation must satisfy several connected requirements: who may reserve, when and how the reservation must be made, the necessity of governmental consent, and the court’s affirmative approval.

Who may reserve and what must be reserved. Only the defendant may reserve under the rule; it does not authorize the government to preserve issues for appeal through a plea. The reservation must be a reservation of a specific, identifiable issue or issues—typically a discrete legal ruling already made in the case, most commonly a motion to suppress physical or testimonial evidence or other pretrial dispositive or pivotal rulings.

Timing and manner of the reservation. The reservation must be explicit and contemporaneous with the plea. Rule 11(b) colloquy requirements are implicated: to be effective, the record must show the defendant understood that he or she was pleading guilty while preserving a specified appellate issue, and that the court accepted the plea conditionally. Best practice is to place the reservation clearly in the written plea agreement and to state it on the record at the plea colloquy. The plea colloquy must also demonstrate that the defendant’s plea is knowing, voluntary, and intelligent despite the reservation.

Government consent and court approval. The rule requires the government’s consent to the reservation; absent that consent, a plea that attempts to reserve will not qualify under Rule 11(a)(2). Additionally, the district court must expressly approve the reservation. Approval is not ministerial: the court must find that the reservation is consistent with the interests of justice and appropriate in the circumstances. The court’s approval is normally made on the record at the time of the plea. The approval, together with the written agreement and the colloquy, forms the basis for appellate review.

Adequacy of record. A critical practical requirement is that the district court create an adequate record of the reservation and the underlying adverse ruling. Because the defendant will be challenging the ruling on appeal after the plea has been entered, the preserved issue must be clear and sufficiently developed in the district court so that appellate review is meaningful.

# Scope of issues that can be reserved on appeal

Rule 11(a)(2) is commonly used to preserve challenges to adverse pretrial rulings, but the permissible scope is not unlimited. Several interlocking constraints determine what issues can be reserved successfully.

Nature of the issues. The classic and primary subjects of reservation are adverse pretrial motions, particularly suppression motions—that is, rulings excluding evidence based on constitutional (e.g., Fourth Amendment) or statutory grounds, rulings on privilege, and other issues that directly affect the factual basis or the admissibility of evidence. More generally, the reservation is appropriate for legal rulings that are dispositive or sufficiently significant that their reversal could warrant reconsideration of the guilty plea (e.g., rulings that eliminate the prima facie basis for the prosecution, that make the prosecution impossible, or that substantially change the factual record).

Nonjurisdictional claims. Courts routinely limit reservations to nonjurisdictional claims. That is, a defendant may not use a conditional plea to reserve issues that are not suitable for appellate review after a guilty plea, such as issues that are waived by a guilty plea under well-settled law (discussed further below). Similarly, some claims—like certain sentencing challenges, speedy trial complaints, or collateral constitutional claims—may not be appropriate for reservation because they either are not ripe for review at the plea stage or because they are ordinarily forfeited by a guilty plea.

Precision and scope in the reservation. The reservation must identify with clarity the ruling the defendant seeks to preserve. A vague or overly broad reservation (for example, a general reservation of "all pretrial issues" or of unspecified constitutional claims) is likely inadequate. Courts expect either a specific motion or a clearly circumscribed legal question to be identified; appellate courts will not entertain de novo review of issues that were neither raised nor specifically ruled upon in the district court.

Effect on collateral issues. A reservation that concerns evidentiary rulings will allow appeal of the legal question whether the district court erred in excluding or admitting evidence. However, reservation of an issue generally does not open the door to collateral matters that were not the subject of the preserved ruling. For example, a suppression ruling may raise subsidiary questions about the scope of an exclusionary remedy or derivative use issues, but only those matters fairly encompassed by the preserved ruling and raised below will normally be reviewable.

Mutual consent and breadth. Because the government must consent, the parties often negotiate the substance and breadth of the reservation. Plea agreements may specify enumerated issues reserved for appeal and may expressly waive all other appellate rights. Where the government consents to a narrow reservation, the defendant cannot later claim a broader scope on appeal.

Practical limits: factual findings and mixed questions. Pure legal questions—e.g., the legal standard applied by the district court—are the most readily appealable. Mixed questions of law and fact will be reviewed under the applicable standard (de novo for legal components, clear-error for factual determinations). Pure factual disputes that were resolved by the district court at a suppression hearing (for instance, credibility determinations) are difficult to overturn on appeal and are subject to deferential review.

# Consequences of an unconditional plea

A guilty plea typically amounts to a waiver of certain trial rights and precludes appeal of many nonjurisdictional claims. Understanding what is lost by an unconditional plea clarifies why the conditional plea exists.

Waiver of nonjurisdictional defenses and claims. When a defendant enters an unconditional guilty plea, he or she generally waives all nonjurisdictional defects in the prosecution, including most claims relating to the investigation (e.g., Fourth Amendment suppression issues), issues of witness admissibility, jury selection errors, and many other trial-level errors. The rationale is pragmatic: a guilty plea admits the elements of the offense and defeats controversies about the underlying facts; allowing a defendant to plead guilty and then litigate errors that relate to the factual or evidentiary basis for the conviction would undermine finality and the purpose of the plea system.

Limited exceptions. Certain claims survive an unconditional guilty plea. Most courts recognize that a plea does not waive jurisdictional challenges—claims that the court lacked statutory or constitutional power to prosecute the defendant (e.g., subject-matter jurisdiction or double jeopardy in narrow circumstances). Similarly, claims that the plea itself was involuntary—such as due process challenges to the validity of the plea, or claims of ineffective assistance of counsel to the extent they challenge the voluntariness of the plea—are preserved. Sentencing errors are often treated differently: although many sentencing claims are preserved for appeal after a plea, other sentencing challenges may be waived or limited depending on the terms of the plea agreement and statutory restrictions on appeals.

Standard of review for waived claims. Because an unconditional plea implies waiver, appellate courts normally decline to reach waived issues. If a defendant contends that the government breached its obligations relating to the plea or that the plea colloquy was deficient, appellate courts will examine the plea record for voluntariness. But claims that could have been, but were not, preserved by a conditional plea are usually considered forfeited and are subject either to plain-error review (if the defendant did not object in the district court) or are deemed waived.

Practical consequence: loss of appellate review of many rulings. The practical effect is stark: if a defendant pleads unconditionally, he or she forecloses appellate review of most pretrial rulings that might have been decisive in seeking dismissal or suppression. This is why defendants and counsel who perceive a substantial pretrial win to be necessary to the defense often seek to reserve that issue before pleading guilty.

# Appellate jurisdiction after a conditional plea

Appellate jurisdiction following a conditional plea is governed by a mixture of the procedural rule permitting reservations and the standard appellate jurisdiction principles that determine whether a case is final, whether the appeal is timely, and whether the issue preserved is reviewable in the posture presented. Several conceptual and practical considerations arise.

Finality and appealability of convictions entered on conditional pleas. A conviction entered after a conditional plea is a final decision for purposes of appellate jurisdiction in the usual way: the defendant is convicted and sentenced, and the judgment of conviction is appealable. The reservation created under Rule 11(a)(2) supplies the defendant a substantive right to appeal the preserved pretrial ruling even though a guilty plea has been entered. The appeal of the preserved issue is brought as part of the appeal from the final judgment, and the appellate court must determine (1) whether the reservation satisfied the rule’s requirements and therefore preserves review, and (2) whether it has jurisdiction to reach the particular legal question.

Preservation and exhaustion. For appellate jurisdiction to attach, the preserved question must have been properly raised, briefed, and decided in the district court prior to the plea. The appellate court’s jurisdiction is typically limited to issues that were actually litigated below and for which a district-court ruling was obtained. A defendant cannot use a conditional plea to manufacture appellate jurisdiction over an unlitigated question.

Standards of review. The appellate court applies the usual standards of review appropriate to the preserved issue. Pure questions of law (for example, whether a particular statute permits a search) are reviewed de novo. Findings of fact made by the district court at a suppression hearing—such as whether an officer’s testimony was credible or whether exigent circumstances existed—are reviewed for clear error. Mixed questions receive the standard treatment: legal components de novo, factual components for clear error.

Scope of review and remedy. If the appellate court determines that the preserved ruling was erroneous in a manner that prejudiced the defendant, it has several remedial options. Depending on the nature of the error, the appellate court may reverse the district court’s ruling and remand for further proceedings. If reversal of the preserved issue would mean that the conviction cannot stand (for example, because key evidence is suppressed and the government cannot proceed), the appellate court may vacate the conviction and remand with instructions allowing the defendant to withdraw the guilty plea or to proceed in a manner consistent with the appellate disposition. The precise remedy depends on the interplay between the preserved issue and the remainder of the record, and courts are careful to fashion relief tailored to the error and to preserve the parties’ rights.

Jurisdictional limits: what appellate courts will not hear. Not every preserved matter will be heard on appeal. Appellate courts will dismiss appeals of purportedly preserved issues when the Rule 11(a)(2) requirements were not met—when the government did not consent, when the district court did not approve, when the reservation was not adequately specified, or when the record does not demonstrate that the plea was knowingly and intelligently entered with the reservation in place. Appellate jurisdiction also collapses if the appeal is untimely or if the appellate claim fails to present a justiciable controversy after the plea and the judgment—e.g., issues rendered moot by the plea or otherwise deprived of practical significance by subsequent events.

Interplay with statutory criminal-appeal provisions. Appellate jurisdiction after a conditional plea must also be reconciled with statutory limitations on appeals in criminal cases. The defendant’s right to appeal a judgment of conviction is generally preserved under the criminal appeals statutes; the reservation secured by Rule 11(a)(2) places the reserved pretrial issue squarely within the conventional appeal from final judgment. Moreover, the government retains its own limited appeal rights under statutes like 18 U.S.C. § 3731 (appeal of certain interlocutory orders), but those governmental avenues are separate and do not control a defendant’s reserved-issue appeal.

Practical consequences and strategic considerations. From a strategic perspective, conditional pleas reshape the litigation map. The defendant obtains the immediate benefits of pleading guilty—such as an agreed sentence or sentencing exposure reduced by the plea—while preserving appellate review of a critical legal ruling. Defense counsel must take care that the reservation is carefully drafted, that the record demonstrates voluntariness and approval, and that all timeliness and procedural requirements for appellate review are met. Likewise, prosecutors will often condition consent on narrowness of the reservation or on reciprocal waivers of other appellate rights.

Burden of persuasion and appellate posture. On appeal, the defendant bears the usual burdens associated with establishing error and prejudice stemming from the adverse ruling. The appellate posture may be unusual because the court is reviewing a pretrial decision in the shadow of an accepted guilty plea and a final judgment. The appellant must demonstrate both that the district court erred and that the error was not harmless in the broader context of the conviction and sentence.

Conclusion

Conditional guilty pleas under Rule 11(a)(2) offer a structured, pragmatic means by which defendants can accept the benefits of a plea while preserving the right to challenge particular pretrial decisions. The effectiveness of a reservation depends on strict adherence to the rule’s requirements—written and oral specification of the reservation, government consent, and court approval—and on careful framing of the reserved question so that it is both appropriate for review and was actually litigated below. An unconditional plea, by contrast, generally waives nonjurisdictional claims and forecloses appellate review of many pretrial rulings. When a conditional plea is properly crafted and accepted, appellate courts will exercise jurisdiction to review the preserved issues under the ordinary standards of appellate review and will fashion remedies that reflect the nature of any error and its impact on the integrity of the conviction.
