Introduction

Litigation under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), 5 U.S.C. § 552, is predominantly a paper-based enterprise in which the district court decides disputes about disclosure largely through motion practice rather than live testimony or bench trials. The Act authorizes any person to seek agency records and creates a framework in which agencies must release non-exempt records while protecting specified categories of information. When an agency withholds records (in whole or in part) or declines to locate responsive records, the requester commonly files suit seeking disclosure and the agency defends its nondisclosure under one or more statutory exemptions. Because FOIA is structured to resolve disclosure disputes on the administrative record, courts have developed a set of procedural tools and standards — most importantly summary judgment practice, the use and treatment of agency declarations, the Vaughn index, in camera review, and principles governing the scope of the agency record and supplementation. This chapter explains those tools and how courts apply them, and it sets out the appellate standards of review that shape FOIA litigation.

# Summary judgment as the primary vehicle in FOIA cases

Summary judgment occupies a central place in FOIA litigation. The Act’s enforcement provision authorizes district courts to enjoin an agency from withholding records and to order production of records improperly withheld. Because FOIA disputes focus on whether particular documents or portions of documents are covered by statutory exemptions, courts commonly resolve suits by deciding whether the agency’s justification for nondisclosure is legally sufficient and factually supported — a determination amenable to summary judgment.

In practice, an agency typically moves for summary judgment after the administrative record is assembled. Agency summary judgment motions are accompanied by one or more affidavits or declarations from agency officials that describe the agency’s search efforts, identify the records located, and justify redactions or withholdings by invoking specific FOIA exemptions. The requester opposes the motion, often challenging the adequacy of the search, the factual sufficiency of the agency’s affidavits, or the applicability of exemptions. Requesters may also move for summary judgment, arguing that the agency has failed to meet its statutory burden to justify nondisclosure.

The Federal Rules of Civil Procedure govern summary judgment practice in FOIA cases, so the general standards apply: a party is entitled to summary judgment when there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. But FOIA litigation is distinct in two respects. First, the factual record tends to be limited to the agency’s sworn declarations and any material the requester can point to showing inadequacy or bad faith; live testimony and broad discovery are infrequent. Second, the allocation of burdens differs from ordinary civil litigation. When a requester shows that an agency has failed to produce records it should have located and released, the agency bears the burden of proving that each withheld document or redaction falls within a statutory exemption. Courts have described this as the agency having the burden of persuasion as to exemption claims, and it is the central issue at summary judgment: whether the agency has carried that burden through undisputed, adequate, and reasonably detailed affidavits, supported by a Vaughn index when necessary.

Procedural posture shapes how courts resolve disputes. Where discovery has not been permitted or a requester has not challenged the adequacy of the search, courts are more likely to credit the agency’s affidavits and grant summary judgment for the agency. Where the requester presents evidence raising a material factual dispute — for example, showing that the agency failed to search a relevant file system or that declared procedural steps were not taken — summary judgment is inappropriate unless the agency can shore up the deficiencies satisfactorily. Cross-motions for summary judgment are common and require the court to construe the record in light of the parties’ competing legal arguments; the court may grant either motion to the extent that party has shown it is entitled to judgment as a matter of law on the submitted record.

# Agency declarations and the presumption of good faith

Agency declarations — usually sworn affidavits or declarations by agency employees or counsel — are the evidentiary linchpin of FOIA litigation. Because agencies possess the materials and knowledge underlying their disclosure decisions, courts rely heavily on agency declarations to reconstruct searches, explain withholdings, and justify exemptions. To streamline review and respect agency expertise, courts give such declarations a presumption of good faith; that presumption is not absolute, but it establishes an initial trust in an agency’s representations unless the requester shows reason to doubt them.

An adequate agency declaration typically contains several components. First, it describes the scope and method of the search(es) conducted to locate responsive records, specifying the components searched, the search terms used (or the reasoning for why search terms were unnecessary), and the personnel involved. Second, it explains the agency’s process for identifying exempt material and making redaction decisions. Third, for each exemption relied upon, it explains in reasonable detail why withheld information falls within the exemption’s terms. Finally, the declaration should attest to the declarant’s qualifications and their familiarity with the agency’s records and procedures.

The presumption of good faith means that, when an agency’s declaration is plausible, detailed, and uncontradicted, courts will usually accept its representations as true for the purposes of summary judgment. The burden to rebut this presumption rests on the requester. A requester can rebut the presumption by showing inconsistencies in the declarations, providing contrary evidence, demonstrating omissions or implausibilities in the agency’s account, or identifying material factual disputes (for example, by showing the existence of responsive documents the agency failed to identify).

Courts evaluate the sufficiency of declarations with reference to the particular exemption asserted and the nature of the records sought. The required level of detail is pragmatic: declarations must provide enough information to allow the court to determine whether the claimed exemption applies, without requiring disclosure of the very information the agency contends is exempt. Where the exemption involves delicate balancing (e.g., information implicating privacy interests or deliberative processes), courts expect more detailed factual justification. Conversely, where the exemption is categorical or involves clear statutory predicates, declarations can be more concise.

When an agency declaration fails to meet minimal adequacy standards, courts will typically deny summary judgment in favor of the agency and either permit reasonable discovery or order the agency to provide a more detailed declaration or index. In rare cases where the record raises credible allegations of bad faith, courts may order depositions or permit broader discovery into the agency’s search efforts and decisionmaking.

# Vaughn indices and categorical justifications

Because FOIA requires itemized justification for withholdings, courts strongly favor a mechanism that transforms agency assertions into a record the court can review. The Vaughn index is that mechanism: an item-by-item or document-by-document catalogue that identifies each withheld document or portion thereof, states the exemption(s) asserted, and provides a concise explanation of why the exemption applies. The Vaughn index serves three interrelated purposes. It frames the contested issues for the court, allows the court to assess whether the exemption has been properly invoked without exposing the protected information, and enables the requester to test the agency’s assertions.

A typical Vaughn index entry includes: (1) a document identifier (date, originator, recipient, subject description); (2) whether the document was withheld in whole or in part; (3) the specific FOIA exemption(s) claimed; and (4) a nonconclusory explanation of why the exemption covers the withheld material. The explanation should describe the nature of the withheld information and the relationship of that information to the exemption’s statutory criteria. For example, if Exemption X protects deliberative process communications, the index must say why the withheld material is both predecisional and deliberative rather than merely contemporaneous factual material.

Courts accept departures from a strictly item-by-item Vaughn index where categorical justifications better reflect the nature of the documents. Categorical (or representative) justifications group documents by type, by subject matter, or by the exemption invoked, and provide representative descriptions of why all documents within a category are exempt. This approach is especially appropriate where the documents are numerous and share essentially identical exempt characteristics. But categorical justifications are effective only if they are sufficiently specific to permit meaningful judicial review. A court will reject an overly generalized categorical claim (e.g., “all documents are privileged”) that fails to provide the basic descriptive material necessary to evaluate the exemption claim.

Whether a Vaughn index must be produced depends on the facts and the sufficiency of the agency’s other submissions. When agency declarations themselves provide adequate descriptions tied to specific records, courts may accept the declarations without a separate Vaughn index. Still, because a Vaughn index promotes precise review and avoids unnecessary in camera review, many courts routinely require one. If a requester contests the adequacy of the index, the court may either require supplementation, order in camera review of contested documents, or permit further discovery limited to the adequacy of the agency’s justifications.

# In camera review authority under § 552(a)(4)(B)

When the written record is disputed or the court lacks sufficient information to evaluate an exemption claim, the court has the statutory authority to examine records in camera — that is, privately, outside the presence of the parties. FOIA’s enforcement provision, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B), authorizes district courts to order production of agency records improperly withheld and to “review [agency] decisions de novo,” which courts have interpreted to encompass in camera inspection as a fact-finding device.

In camera review serves several functions. It allows the court to verify the accuracy and sufficiency of agency declarations and Vaughn indices, to determine whether the claimed exemptions truly apply to the material at issue, and to protect sensitive information by keeping it from public disclosure during dispute resolution. Courts use in camera inspection sparingly; it is a tool of last resort because it shifts the review from the written record to the judge’s private examination and consumes judicial resources.

Courts generally will not proceed directly to in camera review if the agency can supplement its declarations or Vaughn index to cure deficiencies. Many courts require the agency to provide a detailed administrative record and a robust Vaughn index before considering in camera inspection. If the agency refuses or cannot produce an adequate theoretical description because the very nature of the exemption makes description revealing (for example, claims of certain law enforcement harms), the court may then inspect the documents in camera to balance the competing interests.

When courts conduct in camera review, they must act with procedural fairness and seal any privileged materials. The judge’s examination is fact-sensitive: the judge evaluates whether disclosed portions of documents show that withheld portions are reasonably segregable, whether the exemptions were properly invoked, and whether any portions must nonetheless be released. In camera review results in an order explaining the basis for any disclosure or nondisclosure so that appellate courts can meaningfully review the district court’s decision. The power to review in camera does not relieve the agency of its burden to justify withholding, but it provides the court direct access to the underlying materials where necessary to determine whether the agency met its burden.

# Record scope and supplementation

A central inquiry in FOIA litigation is the scope of the “record” that the court may consider when evaluating the agency’s withholding decision. Unlike administrative record review under the Administrative Procedure Act, FOIA litigation allows courts to consider extra-record materials to the limited extent they bear on issues like the adequacy of an agency’s search or the agency’s claim of exemption. The threshold question is what constitutes the relevant record for the dispute before the court.

The core record typically includes the documents the agency located (and either produced or withheld), the agency’s declarations describing searches and exemption justifications, and any Vaughn index. The requester may seek discovery beyond this core when she makes a claim that the agency’s search was inadequate, that the agency acted in bad faith, or that particular categories of documents exist that were not searched. Courts permit targeted discovery where a requester makes a prima facie showing that the agency’s representations are suspect or where additional factual development is necessary to adjudicate exemption claims fairly.

Supplementation of the record can occur in several ways. An agency may supplement its declarations or Vaughn index in response to a court order or to a requester’s challenges. A requester may present extrinsic evidence — affidavits, sworn testimony, documentary evidence — that contradicts or undermines the agency’s representations. Courts exercise discretion in admitting such material: they allow supplementation that is directly relevant to the adequacy of the search or the agency’s credibility, but they do not permit broad, fishing expeditions into agency files unrelated to the FOIA request.

A key obligation on agencies is the duty to conduct adequate, reasonable searches. Courts assess adequacy by considering whether the searches were reasonably calculated to uncover responsive documents, given the request’s scope and the agency’s record systems. This is a reasonableness test rather than a best-efforts or perfect-search standard. Courts evaluate search reasonableness by examining the search methods described in the agency’s affidavit, the search terms and fields used, the custodians and databases searched, and the agency’s explanations for any limitations or exclusions. If a court concludes the search was inadequate, it may order further searching or limited discovery into the agency’s search practices.

Another important limitation is the requirement of segregability. Even when a document contains exempt material, the agency must disclose any reasonably segregable nonexempt portions unless disclosure would be meaningless or impossible. Agency declarations and Vaughn indices should specify that segregability was considered and applied, and must explain why any nonexempt portions could not be segregated. Courts will not accept bare assertions that segregability is impracticable; they expect some showing that segregation was attempted and that remaining material was wholly exempt.

# Appellate standards: de novo legal review with deference to expertise where appropriate

Appellate review of FOIA decisions is structured to ensure both uniformity of legal interpretation and respect for agency fact-based judgments within the limits of the statute. Courts of appeals review legal questions de novo, and factual findings for clear error, applying this layering to the district court’s grant or denial of summary judgment in FOIA cases.

Specifically, appellate courts review the district court’s legal conclusions — whether a statutory exemption applies as a matter of law — de novo. When the district court’s decision rests on statutory construction or legal analysis of the exemption’s scope, the appellate court independently determines the legal standard and its application. However, appellate courts also review a district court’s findings about factual matters (e.g., the adequacy of a search, the content of a disputed document as described in a court-ordered in camera inspection) under a clearly erroneous standard. This means that an appellate court will defer to the trial court’s factual determinations unless the appellate court is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made.

The unique interplay between de novo review and deference manifests in how appellate courts treat agency declarations and the district court’s fact-intensive credibility assessments. While the appellate court gives no special deference to the district court’s legal conclusions, it gives weight to the trial court’s credibility determinations about agency witnesses and the sufficiency of declarations when those findings are based on the district court’s firsthand consideration of the materials, including any in camera inspection. Similarly, courts recognize that some exemptions require assessment of agency expertise — for example, where disclosure would harm national security interests or ongoing law enforcement operations — and appellate courts may give due weight to agency explanations grounded in specialized knowledge so long as the agency submission contains precise, nonconclusory factual support.

The allocation of burdens on appeal follows the underlying FOIA framework. The agency must justify each withholding; the appellate court evaluates whether the agency carried that burden on the record before the district court. If the district court denied summary judgment because agency declarations were insufficient, the agency may seek to cure shortcomings by providing supplementation in proceedings on remand; appellate courts are usually reluctant to accept post hoc justifications offered for the first time on appeal. Conversely, if the district court granted agency summary judgment based on plausible, unrebutted declarations, the appellate court will affirm unless there is a clear error in the factual predicate or an incorrect legal standard applied.

In short, appellate review in FOIA cases gives careful de novo attention to legal interpretation while recognizing the practical need to defer to sound factual findings and agency expertise when those findings are adequately supported. This balance protects both the public’s statutory right to information and the legitimate interests that FOIA exemptions are meant to safeguard.

Conclusion

Understanding FOIA litigation requires mastery of a specific set of procedural practices and standards: summary judgment as the usual mode of final disposition, the centrality and presumptive reliability of agency declarations, the role of Vaughn indices and categorical justifications in enabling judicial review, the limited but important use of in camera inspection under § 552(a)(4)(B), the principles governing the proper scope of the record and the possibility of supplementation, and the mixed standard of appellate review that combines de novo legal analysis with deference to well-supported factual findings and agency expertise. For the law student preparing to read judicial opinions, appreciating how these tools interact will clarify why courts decide FOIA disputes the way they do and how legal and factual burdens are allocated in this highly document-driven area of public-law litigation.
