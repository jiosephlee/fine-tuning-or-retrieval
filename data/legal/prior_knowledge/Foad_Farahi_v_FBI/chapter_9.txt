Introduction

Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation centers on the tension between the statute’s strong disclosure mandate and a set of statutory exemptions designed to protect interests such as national security, law enforcement, and personal privacy. That tension plays out not only in the claims and defenses the parties present publicly, but also in a recurrent procedural posture in which agencies seek to substantiate their withholdings through material that they will not publicly disclose—either by submitting sealed declarations and documents directly to the court ex parte, or by asking the court to inspect purportedly protected materials in camera. Courts regularly accept such mechanisms as a practical means of evaluating exemption claims, but the practice raises important doctrinal and procedural questions about adequacy of proof, standards of review, adversarial testing, due process, and the preservation of confidentiality on appeal. This chapter explains the statutory and judicial bases for in camera and ex parte submissions under FOIA, the limits on those practices, measures courts use to mitigate the risks to adversarial process and fairness, and the consequences of party conduct such as consent or failure to object.

# Statutory basis under § 552(a)(4)(B)

The primary statutory hook for judicial review in FOIA cases is 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4), which provides district courts with jurisdiction to order production of agency records. Section 552(a)(4)(B) is particularly important for the court’s procedural power: it authorizes district courts to “enjoin the agency from withholding agency records and to order the production of any agency records improperly withheld from the complainant.” The statute also contemplates that courts evaluate agencies’ proofs supporting claimed exemptions.

Although § 552(a)(4)(B) does not explicitly mention in camera review or ex parte submissions, the statute’s grant of jurisdiction and remedial powers has long been understood to permit courts to employ verification techniques necessary to resolve disputes over withheld materials. The Supreme Court and lower courts have treated in camera inspection as an appropriate tool where affidavits or public filings alone do not furnish an adequate factual record for the court to determine whether a statutory exemption applies. That judicial authority is grounded in the court’s equitable powers and its duty to decide cases on an adequately developed record while balancing FOIA’s disclosure purpose against the need to protect legitimately secret information.

Two related obligations fit within the statutory framework and help explain why in camera and ex parte practices arise: (1) the agency bears the burden of justifying nondisclosure, which typically requires explanations reasonably detailed and nonconclusory; and (2) courts must ensure that any withheld information is properly segregable, i.e., only nonexempt portions are withheld and all reasonably segregable material is released. When an affidavit or public index is deficient or when proof would reveal classified or especially sensitive sources and methods if publicly filed, agencies may seek to submit additional material under seal or request in camera inspection.

# The role of affidavits, Vaughn indices, and why agencies sometimes submit sealed materials

In FOIA litigation an agency typically supports a withholding with a declaration from an agency official and a Vaughn index. A Vaughn index is a line-item index describing each withheld document, the exemption claimed, and a concise justification tying the exemption to the specific information withheld. Courts evaluate the sufficiency of these submissions: the affidavit must establish a factual basis for the claim (not merely legal conclusions), and the Vaughn index must permit meaningful judicial review by describing the document’s nature, the exemption implicated, and why the exemption applies.

There are circumstances where public affidavits and Vaughn indices will not be sufficient. For example, disclosure of the factual basis could itself reveal elements of exempt information (e.g., classified operations, confidential law-enforcement sources, or highly sensitive personal information). In such cases, an agency may move to file supplemental material under seal or request that the court examine the documents in camera to determine if the exemption is properly invoked. Courts accept these practices when necessary to prevent the very disclosure that FOIA exemptions were designed to avoid, while still enabling judicial review.

# Perioperative Services & Logistics v. VA: acceptance and limits of ex parte practice

Federal courts have accepted that sealed and ex parte submissions can be used in FOIA litigation as one permissible method to support withholding claims. This judicial acceptance rests on practical considerations: sealed filings allow the court to access the actual records or more descriptive, sensitive affidavits that would otherwise be inadmissible in the public docket, enabling a more searching review without undermining the exemption’s purpose.

However, adoption of ex parte and sealed procedures is not unbounded. Courts have articulated limits to protect the adversarial process and FOIA’s disclosure goals. The limits commonly endorsed by courts include the following principles:

- The agency retains the burden of justifying nondisclosure. Sealed or ex parte material cannot become a carte blanche that relieves the agency from producing a public factual record adequate for review when doing so would not itself cause harmful disclosure.

- Sealed filings are appropriate only when necessary to prevent harmful disclosure. Courts balance the public interest in transparency against the injury that public disclosures would cause; sealed submissions are permitted when their public filing would defeat the exemption’s purpose.

- Courts will scrutinize whether the sealed or ex parte material is truly necessary. Where a public affidavit could be supplemented without revealing sensitive details, courts may require that alternative, less restrictive measures be used instead of ex parte submissions.

- Nonconclusory, detailed justifications are required. Even when submitted under seal, affidavits should be sufficiently specific to permit a meaningful judicial determination; conclusory or merely formulaic claims are insufficient.

- Adversarial testing must be preserved to the greatest extent feasible. Courts often require that, when possible, the agency provide to the requester a redacted version, a protective-order-limited version to counsel for the requester, or at least a sufficiently detailed Vaughn index so the requester can meaningfully challenge exemptions.

The balance courts strike in allowing sealed materials thus reflects both a recognition of practical necessity and a concern to prevent erosion of adversarial testing and FOIA’s public disclosure objectives.

# Due process concerns and mitigating measures

Using sealed and ex parte submissions in a civil litigation context raises due process and fairness concerns. FOIA plaintiffs are ordinarily entitled to know the factual basis for the government’s decision to withhold records so they can mount meaningful challenges. Ex parte filings can deprive the plaintiff of information necessary to test the agency’s claims, potentially resulting in judicial decisionmaking based on evidence to which the plaintiff had no access.

Courts have developed several techniques to mitigate these concerns and to preserve a minimally adequate adversarial process:

- Detailed Vaughn indices: Requiring agencies to produce a thorough Vaughn index that describes withheld records and explains precisely how each claimed exemption applies allows plaintiffs to assess and challenge the agency’s case without revealing sensitive content. The Vaughn index serves as the principal tool for adversarial testing in many FOIA cases.

- Redacted public filings: When possible, agencies may be required to file redacted versions of documents so that the public and the requester receive as much information as can be disclosed without compromising protected interests.

- Protective orders and limited disclosure to counsel: Courts may allow agencies to produce under seal to the requester’s counsel (often under a strict protective order) more detailed information or even the full documents. This preserves adversarial testing while limiting who may see sensitive material. Protective orders may limit further disclosure, bar counsel from using the material except for litigation, or permit counsel only to advise the client without disclosing the documents further.

- In camera inspection with notice and opportunity to be heard: Before relying solely on ex parte material, some courts will require that the agency first submit sufficient public or limited confidential material and that the plaintiff be given notice and an opportunity to be heard about the need for in camera review. When in camera review is necessary, courts sometimes give the plaintiff a summary of the sealed material or an opportunity to submit written objections based on the Vaughn index.

- Use of special masters or court-appointed experts: Where technical expertise is needed to evaluate material or where secrecy must be tightly controlled, courts may appoint a special master or an independent expert who can review the material more fully and report to the court under protective conditions.

- Limiting reliance on ex parte evidence: Courts may treat ex parte or sealed submissions as providing supplementary confirmation rather than primary proof, refusing to decide the merits solely on the basis of material to which the opponent lacked access.

These measures do not eliminate due process concerns, but they help ensure that disputes are decided on an adequate factual record and that the plaintiff is not entirely blindsided by secret evidence. Whether a particular mitigation is adequate depends on the sensitivity of the material, the availability of less restrictive alternatives, and the plaintiff’s ability to respond to whatever level of disclosure the court permits.

# Standards of proof and judicial review when sealed material is used

FOIA places the initial burden on the agency to justify nondisclosure, but courts apply different standards depending on whether the challenge involves legal questions, factual findings, or classified materials.

For legal questions (e.g., whether a particular exemption covers a category of information), courts review de novo. For factual determinations—such as whether the specific withheld information meets the facts of the exemption—courts often apply a mixed approach. When the factual basis rests on nondisputed documentary evidence or official affidavits, courts may review the supporting declarations for plausibility and adequacy: are the factual assertions clear, specific, and logical? Do they allow the court to match exemptions to the withheld material? If a court’s particular factual findings underlie a discretionary determination, appellate review will be for clear error as to those underlying findings, with de novo review of any legal conclusions.

When agencies rely on classified or sensitive evidence submitted only in camera or ex parte, courts must ensure that the sealed filings form an adequate factual record to sustain their judgment. Appellate courts typically insist that the record contain sufficient information—albeit sometimes under seal—to permit meaningful appellate review. A court’s reliance on ex parte submissions without an adequate factual foundation in the record risks being vacated or remanded on appeal because the appellate court cannot meaningfully review whether the exemption applies.

# Appellate review of sealed records and maintaining confidentiality

Sealed records present particular challenges on appeal. Appellate courts must reconcile two competing principles: the need to protect legitimately secret material and the necessity of a sufficient record to permit meaningful appellate review.

Procedural mechanisms for preserving confidentiality while enabling review include:

- Inclusion of sealed material in the record on appeal: A district court may include the sealed material in the appellate record, accessible to the panel and judges under seal. The clerk of the appellate court will usually have procedures to transmit sealed filings only to authorized judges and staff.

- Summaries or redacted versions for public record: Where appropriate, the district court can direct the agency to prepare summaries or redactions adequate to permit the appellant to present arguments and permit the public docket to reflect non-sensitive aspects of the materials.

- Protective orders on appeal: The appellate court can enter protective orders limiting who may access sealed appendices and how they may be used.

- Use of sealed appendices and sealed panels: Appellate courts may accept sealed appendices filed under seal for in-chambers review by judges, or they may appoint counsel with security clearance or bar membership to handle confidential materials.

Substantively, appellate courts will assess whether the district court conducted adequate review when it relied on sealed filings. If the district court relied exclusively on ex parte content without creating an adequate sealed factual record, the appellate court may remand for further proceedings. Conversely, if the sealed record is sufficiently detailed and the appellate panel has access to that material, the appellate court can perform its ordinary standards of review.

In all cases, appellate courts are careful to avoid treating FOIA records as immune from review merely because the government claims secrecy: the appellate process must be able to test legal and factual rulings that justify nondisclosure. The need for confidentiality is accommodated through procedural means, not by surrendering appellate scrutiny.

# Party conduct: consent, failure to object, and consequences

How the parties behave procedurally can materially affect whether, and to what extent, courts rely on sealed or ex parte submissions.

Consent: If the requester expressly consents to the agency’s filing documents under seal or to in camera inspection, the court will be more likely to allow those procedures. Consent can take several forms: agreement to a protective order that allows counsel (but not the client) to view sensitive materials; stipulation that a sealed document be filed; or consent to resolving particular disputes in camera. Consent reduces due process concerns because the opposing party has accepted the limited disclosure regime. Courts, however, may still scrutinize whether consent was voluntary and informed—especially if consent is sought late in the case or under pressure.

Failure to object: If the plaintiff receives notice of an agency’s request to file sealed material and does not object, courts may treat the lack of objection as acquiescence. Procedural default doctrines apply: a party that fails to challenge a procedural move in a timely manner may forfeit the right to later complain that the court relied on ex parte material. Nonetheless, courts sometimes exercise independent duty to ensure fairness; for example, if the court perceives that a party has been misled or if a serious due process issue is apparent, the court may revisit the matter even absent timely objection.

Consequences of objection: A plaintiff who objects to sealed or ex parte submissions may force the agency either to disclose more in the public record or to justify more fully why secrecy is necessary. If the agency cannot provide sufficient alternative proof, the court may order disclosure, deny the agency’s claimed exemption, or require production under protective conditions. Importantly, if the plaintiff objects and the court still accepts ex parte material, the plaintiff preserves grounds for appellate challenge because an objection creates a record of contested procedure.

Waiver and estoppel: Voluntary disclosure by an agency of information it had earlier withheld can waive the assertion of the exemption for that specific material and sometimes for closely related materials. Similarly, a requester’s conduct may lead to waiver of objections (for example, if the requester voluntarily reviews sealed material without raising concerns about confidentiality). Courts will analyze such claims factually, considering whether the disclosure was intentional and whether it undermines the government's claim of secrecy.

Sanctions and costs: Parties that litigate obstructively—e.g., by making voluminous sealed filings without adequate justification or by failing to comply with protective orders—may face sanctions, including costs and fees. Conversely, agencies that submit deficient, conclusory affidavits may be ordered to supplement their submissions, ordered to produce materials, or find their privilege claims rejected.

# Practical considerations for litigators

For agency counsel: When seeking to rely on sealed or ex parte material, counsel should prepare a robust strategy that respects the court’s duty to enable adversarial testing. That strategy includes preparing the most detailed public (or redacted) affidavits and Vaughn index possible without compromising protected interests; considering protective-order disclosure to opposing counsel; explaining precisely why sealing or in camera inspection is necessary; and being prepared to justify the necessity on the public record. Agencies should also be mindful that appellate review will require a sealed record adequate to permit meaningful appellate scrutiny.

For FOIA requesters’ counsel: Counsel should insist on the most complete public description possible and press for protective-order-limited access to sealed materials when needed to challenge exemptions. When a court contemplates ex parte procedures, requesters should timely object if they believe the agency has not shown necessity or if the agency seeks to hide the factual predicate for its claim. When consenting to limited disclosure, counsel should protect the client’s interests through carefully tailored protective orders, and not sign away the right to object on appeal.

For judges: Judges must balance competing values: protecting sensitive interests, maintaining the adversarial process, ensuring a sufficient record for appellate review, and honoring FOIA’s disclosure purpose. Careful case management—scheduling protective-order hearings, ordering detailed Vaughn indices, and limiting use of sealed filings to what is strictly necessary—promotes both fairness and efficient resolution.

# Conclusion

Sealed and ex parte submissions, including in camera inspection, are established, pragmatic tools in FOIA litigation that courts may use to reconcile FOIA’s disclosure objectives with legitimate confidentiality interests. Their permissibility rests on statutory jurisdiction and judicial equity powers, but their use is constrained by the agency’s burden to justify nondisclosure, by courts’ duty to preserve adversarial testing to the greatest extent feasible, and by the necessity of creating a record adequate for appellate review. Due process concerns are real but can be mitigated through detailed public affidavits, Vaughn indices, protective orders, redactions, summaries, and carefully limited in camera inspection. Party consent and procedural conduct shape outcomes: consent and timely objection have predictable consequences, and courts will not allow sealed processes to become a substitute for reasoned judicial supervision. Understanding these doctrines and practices prepares a student to read and critically assess FOIA opinions that rely on sealed or ex parte materials.
