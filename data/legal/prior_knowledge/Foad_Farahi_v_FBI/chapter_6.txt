Introduction

Requests under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) frequently collide with the executive branch’s asserted need to protect national security information. Courts are called upon to reconcile FOIA’s default presumption of disclosure with statutory exemptions that shield classified or sensitive material. Because national security judgments implicate expertise, access to classified information, and institutional responsibility for protecting sources and methods, courts have developed doctrines that often afford deference to the executive. Understanding how and why courts defer, the doctrinal tools they use, and the limits of that deference is essential before reading any opinion in this area. This chapter explains the legal foundations for national-security-related deference, the key D.C. Circuit framework and related authority that has shaped modern practice, the mosaic and risk-aggregation reasoning agencies invoke when asserting exemptions, the tension between de novo judicial review and institutional competence/secrecy, and how these issues interact with law-enforcement exemptions such as FOIA Exemption 7(A).

# Doctrinal basis for deference in national security matters

FOIA establishes a presumption of disclosure but also enumerates nine exemptions that permit an agency to withhold information. Two exemptions are particularly central to national security: Exemption 1 (national defense or foreign policy information properly classified under an Executive order) and Exemption 3 (information exempted by statute), with other exemptions such as 7(A) (records compiled for law enforcement where disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement) sometimes implicated in national security investigations.

Although FOIA requires courts to perform de novo review of an agency’s claim of exemption, the governing doctrine recognizes that courts lack direct access to many of the factual and policy bases that inform national security judgments. The result is a calibrated form of deference: courts require agencies to satisfy evidentiary and justificatory burdens, but often accept agency explanations as to classification decisions, risk assessments, and the potential harms of disclosure when those explanations are plausible, sufficiently detailed, and consistent with the record.

Several legal rationales underpin this approach. First, separation-of-powers considerations: the Executive has constitutional authority and institutional responsibility for national defense and foreign affairs, and courts are reluctant to second-guess sensitive operational judgments. Second, epistemic competence: agencies possess specialized knowledge, operational experience, and access to classified information that courts lack. Third, practical necessity: certain harms the government invokes (e.g., disclosure of sources, operational vulnerabilities) are difficult to demonstrate without exposing further classified information; thus, demanding an elaborate public record would be self-defeating.

At the same time, FOIA’s text and structure constrain deference. Courts have repeatedly emphasized that deference does not mean abdication: an agency must justify withholding with a factual and legal showing sufficient to permit meaningful judicial review, usually through declarations, Vaughn indices, and, when necessary, in camera inspection. The interplay of judicial review and executive expertise produces a body of doctrine that aims to protect both national security and FOIA’s disclosure purposes.

# CNSS and related D.C. Circuit authority

The D.C. Circuit has been a primary incubator of FOIA national-security doctrine, crafting principles that many other circuits and district courts follow. Although factual specifics vary across opinions, several recurring doctrines emerge from the court’s treatment of national-security-related FOIA claims.

One central thread is the treatment of agency declarations. The D.C. Circuit has recognized that a reasonably detailed, plausible, and uncontradicted affidavit or declaration from an agency official who has personal knowledge of the matters at issue and is authorized to speak for the agency will often suffice to establish the exemption asserted. The declaration should explain the connection between the records sought and the bases for withholding (e.g., why disclosure would harm national defense, reveal classified sources, or interfere with ongoing operations). When an agency’s declaration meets these criteria, courts will usually grant summary judgment for the agency.

Related D.C. Circuit authority also emphasizes the role of the Vaughn index as a means for structuring judicial review. The Vaughn index catalogs withheld records and supplies page-by-page descriptions tied to exemption claims; the D.C. Circuit has underscored that a Vaughn index, combined with an explanatory declaration, allows a court—without needless exposure of classified materials—to determine whether the agency has met its burden.

The court has also addressed the limits of the agency’s authority to withhold. It has reiterated that purely conclusory or boilerplate assertions will not suffice; courts may require further explanation if an agency’s declaration lacks sufficient detail, if the record contains apparent contradictions, or if the agency’s claim appears overbroad. When the court requires, it can undertake in camera review of the materials to test the agency’s assertions without public disclosure.

Another feature of D.C. Circuit doctrine is its approach to segmented or partial disclosure. The court routinely requires agencies to release nonexempt segregable portions of documents and to explain why particular passages remain withheld. This demonstrates that deference to the agency’s national-security judgments does not remove the judicial role in ensuring disclosure where FOIA demands it.

Finally, the D.C. Circuit has addressed how courts should treat novel theories of harm—such as aggregated risk arguments or “mosaic” reasoning—often giving those theories purchase when set out in detailed declarations that connect the theory to the specific records withheld. The court’s approach balances respect for agency expertise against the need for courts to ensure that claimed harms are credible and reasonably tied to the requested records.

# The mosaic theory and risk aggregation concepts

A distinctive component of modern national-security FOIA disputes is the mosaic theory and related risk-aggregation reasoning. The mosaic theory contends that individual pieces of information, which might appear innocuous when isolated, can combine with other available information to create a comprehensive and harmful picture—revealing, for instance, the identity of clandestine facilities, vulnerabilities in operations, or sources and methods. Risk aggregation extends the idea by suggesting that the cumulative disclosure of many small items increases the probability of harm even if each item alone would not.

Courts evaluate mosaic and aggregation claims by assessing whether the agency’s affidavits explain the mechanism by which the pieces fit together and whether the agency connects that mechanism to the specific documents or data at issue. Agencies seeking to rely on mosaic reasoning must do more than proffer a generic warning that “aggregation could be harmful.” Effective declarations describe (1) the kinds of external information that could be combined with the requested records; (2) how the combination would meaningfully increase the risk of harm; and (3) why the incremental contribution of the requested records to the aggregated risk is nontrivial.

Judicial responses to mosaic claims vary. When agencies provide reasoned explanations, courts have accepted mosaic theory as a legitimate basis for withholding under Exemption 1 or other provisions. Courts generally expect agencies to identify with sufficient specificity the contextual or external materials that would enable harmful aggregation, or at least to explain plausibly how reasonably foreseeable external sources could be combined with the requested records to produce the asserted harm.

At the same time, some courts are cautious. They require that aggregation arguments be grounded in objective plausibility: hypothetical aggregations that rely on highly speculative or contingent linkages are more likely to be rejected. Courts also scrutinize whether the asserted harms are speculative or concrete, and whether redaction or partial disclosure could mitigate risks without the need for wholesale withholding.

The mosaic theory raises difficult institutional questions for courts. To assess whether aggregation risks are real, a court sometimes must consider classified evidence or technical judgments. Hence courts often afford considerable weight to agency expertise, particularly when declarations explain the technical or operational pathways by which aggregation might lead to harm. Nevertheless, courts retain the authority to test those declarations, through targeted questioning, requests for further detail, or in camera review.

# Balancing de novo review with institutional competence and secrecy needs

FOIA requires courts to conduct de novo review of an agency’s claim that a requested record is exempt. De novo review means the court determines, without deference to agency conclusions, whether the statutory exemption applies. But courts have confronted a practical and doctrinal tension: de novo review is difficult to conduct fully in public when the very information needed to adjudicate the claim is itself classified or sensitive.

To manage this tension, courts have developed a procedural toolkit that reconciles FOIA’s demand for judicial independence with the realities of national security litigation. Key elements of that toolkit include: the use of detailed affidavits or declarations from agency officials; reliance on the Vaughn index; the possibility of in camera review of sensitive documents; summary judgment proceedings structured to minimize public exposure of classified material; and a willingness to accept redaction and partial disclosure where appropriate.

The legal standard that emerges looks like this: the agency bears the burden of proving that a requested document (or portion thereof) falls squarely within the claimed exemption. The court performs a de novo assessment based on the full administrative record, which may include classified submissions reviewed ex parte and in camera. When the agency’s supporting affidavit is detailed and credible and when in camera review confirms the asserted harms, the court will often rule for the agency. But if the agency’s justification is conclusory, inconsistent, or uncorroborated, or if in camera review undermines the agency’s position, the court will order disclosure.

Procedural posture matters. Many FOIA cases are resolved at summary judgment based on agency affidavits and submitted exhibits; the plaintiff rarely obtains discovery into the agency’s national security rationale because courts protect classified reasoning from broad public exposure. In cases where plaintiffs can show factual disputes about the agency’s claims, courts may require additional factual development, but they often limit the scope of discovery to avoid unnecessary exposure. When a case reaches trial—or more commonly, in camera inspection—the court can review the materials directly, preserving secrecy while verifying the agency’s assertions. The prospect of in camera review acts as a check on unfounded secrecy claims without compelling public disclosure.

Another balancing factor is the segregation and redaction requirement: FOIA mandates disclosure of all reasonably segregable nonexempt information. Courts typically insist on a reasonably particularized explanation of why segregability is impracticable or why redaction cannot address the agency’s concerns. This requirement ensures that deference to national security does not swallow FOIA’s disclosure mandate.

Finally, courts sometimes reconcile de novo review and deference through doctrinal formulations that emphasize reasoned explanation rather than categorical deference. The agency’s claim gains weight if grounded in demonstrable expertise, plausible factual descriptions, and a logical link between the records and the asserted harm. Deference in practice thus becomes a function of the quality of the agency’s justification rather than a presumption that the agency is always right.

# Interaction with law-enforcement exemptions, especially 7(A)

FOIA Exemption 7 protects “records or information compiled for law enforcement purposes” and includes several subparts; Exemption 7(A) authorizes withholding where the release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” Exemption 7(A) is often implicated in national-security-related FOIA requests because many sensitive records are generated in the course of intelligence or national-security investigations, which have law-enforcement components.

Applying Exemption 7(A) requires satisfying two threshold inquiries. First, the records must have been “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” Courts apply a broad test: the records are covered if the documents were created or collected with an aim toward enforcing the law—this can include criminal, regulatory, immigration, or certain national-security investigative activities where enforcement is a potential outcome. The mere fact that an agency has some investigative or regulatory function often suffices when it is clear that the records relate to such functions.

Second, the agency must show that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. This is a prospective, harm-based standard focused on the risk of interference with ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement activities. The government must show a reasonable nexus between the requested records and the risk of interference. Courts have accepted a range of interference theories: disclosure might reveal investigative techniques, identify confidential sources, expose witnesses to intimidation, or tip off targets in a way that frustrates ongoing operations. Where national security and law enforcement overlap, agencies often emphasize that disclosure could reveal sources, methods, or the existence and scope of ongoing investigations.

Procedurally, the government typically supports Exemption 7(A) claims with a declaration explaining the nature of the investigation, the connection between the records and enforcement activities, and the plausible pathways through which disclosure could interfere. Courts require that these explanations be sufficiently detailed and credible, and may perform in camera review if the public declaration lacks necessary specificity. The standard “could reasonably be expected” language does not demand certainty of interference; rather, it requires a reasonable expectation grounded in the agency’s particularized explanation.

Exemption 7(A) interacts with other exemptions and doctrines. For records involving classified information, agencies often assert both Exemption 1 and Exemption 7(A). Mosaic or aggregation arguments may be used to justify withholding under Exemption 7(A) by showing how disclosure could, in combination with other sources, expose investigative details. Moreover, the segregability obligation applies: even where Exemption 7(A) applies to certain passages, courts will insist on disclosure of nonexempt material.

Finally, Exemption 7(A) highlights how FOIA’s de novo review and deference interplay in the law-enforcement context. Courts give appropriate weight to an agency’s law-enforcement expertise when confronted with plausible, well-documented assertions of potential interference, but they will not accept bare conclusions. Plaintiffs can challenge the sufficiency of the government’s showing; if successful, the court can order disclosure or further factual development. Thus, as with national-security exemptions more broadly, Exemption 7(A) is governed by a balance between institutional competence and judicial oversight.

Conclusion

National-security deference in FOIA litigation rests on the interplay between statutory text, separation-of-powers concerns, institutional expertise, and FOIA’s disclosure mandate. Courts insist on meaningful explanations and factual support from agencies, using tools such as declarations, Vaughn indices, and in camera review to reconcile the need for secrecy with FOIA’s aims. Doctrines developed in the D.C. Circuit and elsewhere—concerning agency declarations, mosaic theory, aggregation risks, and the administration of law-enforcement exemptions like 7(A)—guide courts in making these difficult judgments. For a law student reading judicial opinions in this area, the key analytic posture is to look for (1) the specificity and credibility of agency justifications, (2) how the court treats aggregation and mosaic arguments, (3) the procedural steps taken to preserve secrecy while enabling review, and (4) the court’s approach to segregability and partial disclosure. Understanding these elements will make the reasoning in national-security FOIA opinions much more intelligible.
