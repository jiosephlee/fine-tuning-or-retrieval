Introduction

In litigation and administrative adjudication under records statutes such as the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) and analogous state statutes, agencies routinely withhold information by claiming exemptions. To justify withholding without disclosing the withheld material itself, an agency typically supplies a descriptive submission that explains why each withheld portion falls within an exemption. Over time courts have developed procedural and substantive rules for evaluating these justifications. Central to that regime are Vaughn submissions (enumerated, itemized justifications), the requirement that categorical descriptions be sufficiently distinct and precise, guidance about the permissible breadth of categories, and judicial tools for testing adequacy such as sampling and in camera review. This chapter explains the purpose and structure of Vaughn-type submissions, the "sufficiently distinct" categories concept as courts apply it, administrative guidance on category breadth and specificity, common pitfalls agencies encounter (including overbroad categories and conclusory assertions), and the techniques courts use to determine whether an agency’s description is adequate.

# Purpose and structure of Vaughn submissions

When an agency withholds records or redacts portions of records under a statutory exemption, courts need enough information to determine whether the exemption actually covers the withheld material. Courts cannot evaluate the claimed exemption if they cannot identify the withheld information or understand the nexus between the claimed exemption and the particular material. A Vaughn submission is the judiciary’s procedural mechanism for bridging that gap: it is a written explanation by the agency that links the withheld segments to the claimed exemption on an item-by-item basis while avoiding disclosure of the exempt material.

Purpose. The primary goals of a Vaughn submission are threefold. First, it supplies the court with a record sufficient to permit meaningful judicial review of exemption claims without requiring public disclosure. Second, it protects legitimately sensitive information by permitting the agency to describe withheld content in non-revealing terms. Third, it affords the requester a generalized understanding of why material was withheld so that adversarial testing of the claim can proceed. The submission therefore serves both transparency and secrecy functions: it provides accountability while preserving exemption values.

Structure. Although practice varies among jurisdictions, a doctrinally adequate Vaughn submission typically contains the following components organized at a granular level:

- Identification of the document, page, and line numbers, or the equivalent specific locator that lets the court associate the description with a precise portion of the records. Specificity is crucial because generalized descriptions tied to broad document groupings make it impossible to test the exemption claim against particular text.

- A concise description of the nature of the withheld information. The description should be precise enough to convey the category of content (for example, "a legal analysis concerning potential labor-law liability" rather than "internal memorandum") while carefully avoiding verbatim disclosure.

- Citation of the specific statutory exemption(s) the agency asserts for that particular withholding, with an explanation of how the exemption applies to the described content.

- A factual or logical explanation linking the content description to the exemption elements. This step involves reciting or paraphrasing nondisclosive facts about the record’s role, authorship, audience, purpose, and context that demonstrate how the exemption’s statutory elements are satisfied.

- Where applicable, an identification of whether the agency is asserting a categorical withholding (i.e., withholding all items in a defined category), and, if so, a precise definition of the category with boundaries or exemplars.

A model Vaughn index is thus an itemized catalog: document identifier → description of withheld material → exemption claimed → rationale. Courts emphasize the itemization and linkage because the adequacy of the index is judged by its ability to permit a line-by-line or at least a segment-by-segment review.

Best practices and judicial expectations. Courts expect agencies to be iterative and granular. A top-level statement that large volumes were withheld under an exemption is insufficient. The describing narrative must do more than recite statutory language; it must provide substantive, nonconclusory facts demonstrating why the exemption applies. Where multiple exemptions could apply, the index should indicate which exemption covers which portion. Agencies often succeed when descriptions include context such as authorship (e.g., "drafted by counsel"), intended recipients (e.g., "for use by senior management"), or function (e.g., "legal advice on potential litigation strategy"), without revealing the protected substantive content.

# The "sufficiently distinct" categories standard

Courts have recognized that categorical withholdings — grouping withheld material into categories rather than describing each line or paragraph individually — can be permissible when the categories are genuine and sufficiently particularized. The “sufficiently distinct” categories standard requires that any categorical description be drawn narrowly enough and described precisely enough to permit judicial review; broad or amorphous categories that simply restate the exemption label are unhelpful and commonly rejected.

Meaning of "sufficiently distinct." A category is sufficiently distinct when it is defined and articulated so that the court can assess whether items that fall within the category logically and factually satisfy the exemption’s criteria. This entails that categories be delineated with respect to subject-matter, document function, authorship, audience, temporal scope, or other meaningful differentiators. For example, categories that separate "legal advice emails" from "factual investigator reports" or "communications with outside counsel" from "internal administrative messages" give the court a concrete way to test claims. The standard rejects catch-all designations like "all exempt information" or "internal deliberations" standing alone because such labels do not create a manageable universe for review.

Why distinctness matters. The distinctness requirement performs two essential functions. First, it preserves the court's capacity for meaningful review by enabling the court to analyze whether the exemption's elements are satisfied for each type of withheld material. Second, it provides fair notice to the requester and the public about the nature and scope of the withholding, thus enabling adversarial testing and potential settlement or narrowing. If categories are indistinct, the court cannot determine whether the agency applied exemptions consistently or correctly.

Applying the standard. In practice, courts examine whether the agency’s categories are particularized along relevant dimensions. Relevant dimensions include the nature of the information (opinion, factual data, legal analysis), the role of the speaker or author (agency counsel, supervisor, outside consultant), the intended audience (decisionmakers vs. broad distribution), the purpose (deliberation, policy formation, planning), and the time frame (communications related to a specific event or ongoing policy). Courts also look to whether the categories overlap or are internally heterogeneous in ways that undermine review. If a category lumps together materially different types of content — say, factual summaries, legal advice, and policy recommendations in a single bucket — the category may be deemed insufficiently distinct.

Limits. Distinctness is not a requirement of perfect granularity. Courts recognize administrative burdens and will tolerate reasonable categorizations so long as they enable the court to apply the law. Thus, a Vaughn submission that bundles similar documents may be acceptable if the grouping is coherent, the agency provides representative examples, and the narrative explains why the exemption fits the group. However, courts will scrutinize categories that appear to be convenience-driven protective shells rather than meaningful classifications.

# Guidance on category breadth and specificity

Administrative and litigation practice guidance emphasizes striking the right balance between avoiding unnecessary disclosure and providing sufficient specificity. Guidance from oversight organizations and accumulated judicial opinions make consistent recommendations about how narrow categories should be and what specificity means in the context of justifying withholdings.

Breadth considerations. Category breadth must be justified on the basis that the grouped items are sufficiently homogeneous in function or content such that a single explanatory rationale will accurately cover all members. Breadth is permissible where the documents share the same authorial role, purpose, audience, and exemption-relevant qualities. For example, a category may encompass "draft interoffice memoranda containing deliberative analyses concerning policy X, authored by senior policy staff and circulated to the policy decision team between dates Y and Z." This breadth is justifiable because the indicated attributes tie directly to the exemption (e.g., deliberative process). Conversely, an overly broad category that simply says "all communications about policy X" without further delimitation is problematic because it likely includes substantive factual material or third-party communications that do not fit the claimed exemption.

Specificity considerations. Specificity requires the agency to provide enough descriptive detail to permit the court to test the exemption claim. Specific features that improve specificity include the document type, the author’s role, the recipient group, the functional purpose (advice, recommendation, factual reporting), temporal boundaries, and representative examples or redacted text-splices. Good practice also calls for indicating whether any segregable nonexempt information exists and, if so, whether it has been produced.

How much is enough. There is no mechanical formula, but courts look for the following attributes to deem a category adequately specific:

1. Clear definitional boundaries so the court can see what is included and excluded.
2. A rationale that ties the category’s defining attributes to each element of the exemption being invoked.
3. Examples or samples when the category contains heterogeneous documents, so the court can understand the nature of the documents without seeing the entire content.
4. Indication of any efforts to segregate and produce nonexempt material.

Guidance documents emphasize that agencies should assume that courts will demand enough detail to make legal determinations; thus agencies should err on the side of providing more contextual description rather than less.

# Pitfalls: overbroad categories and conclusory statements

Agencies commonly encounter two interrelated pitfalls that lead to judicial rejection or remand: overbroad categorical descriptions and conclusory, boilerplate statements that merely recite statutory language or policy rationales without factual fit.

Overbroad categories. Overbreadth arises when a category sweeps in materially disparate types of information that are not, in fact, uniformly covered by the claimed exemption. Typical symptoms of overbroad categories include:

- Use of catch-all phrases such as "all internal communications," "all deliberative materials," or "all inter-agency exchanges" without further qualification.
- Failure to identify authorship, audience, function, or timeframe, thereby making the category something of a black box.
- Mixing documents prepared for different purposes (e.g., raw investigative facts alongside evaluative recommendations) under a single label.

When categories are overbroad, courts cannot perform the necessary line-by-line or segmental review. Overbreadth therefore invites the court to require a narrower index, to demand in camera review, or to reject the justification altogether.

Conclusive and boilerplate statements. Another frequent defect is reliance on conclusory language that simply restates the exemption in generic terms or asserts legal conclusions without supplying supporting facts. Examples include statements like "these documents are exempt because they are predecisional and deliberative" without indicating authorship, timing, content type, or how the withheld content contributed to the deliberative process. Courts have little patience for such circular reasoning: a statutory label alone does not satisfy the agency’s burden.

Consequences of these pitfalls. When an agency’s submission contains overbroad or conclusory descriptions, courts may order a more detailed Vaughn index, require in camera inspection, grant partial production, or remand to the agency for reconsideration. Requesters may also obtain an order for segregated release of nonexempt material. Repeated failures can result in adverse credibility findings that affect litigation dynamics.

How to avoid pitfalls. Agencies can avoid these problems by adhering to detailed, nonconclusory descriptions that connect facts to exemption elements. If categorical descriptions are used, they should be narrowly tailored, described in terms that connect to exemption criteria, and accompanied by representative examples or summaries. When practicable, agencies should provide affidavits or declarations from declarants who had direct knowledge and can explain the reasons for withholding without revealing the exempt content.

# Court tools for testing adequacy: sampling and in camera review

When courts confront disputed withholdings, they employ procedural tools to determine whether the Vaughn submission or categorical description is adequate. The two main tools are representative sampling and in camera review; both serve to assess the veracity and sufficiency of agency claims while balancing confidentiality concerns.

Representative sampling. Sampling is a judicially favored device when the volume of withheld material is large. Instead of compelling production of all withheld documents (which would defeat the purpose of the exemption), the court directs the agency to provide a representative sample of the withheld material for review. Representative sampling allows the court to assess whether the agency’s descriptions in the Vaughn index accurately reflect the content and fit the claimed exemptions.

Design and scope. Courts typically permit the agency to select the sample, subject to the court’s approval. The sampling protocol must be neutral and credible: sampling should cover different categories, different authors, different time periods, and different document types to ensure the sample is not skewed toward particularly advantageous material for the agency. A court may, in some circumstances, allow the requester to propose additional documents for sampling, or the court may itself choose documents to include in the sample.

What sampling accomplishes. A well-crafted sample can demonstrate whether the agency’s general descriptions are accurate and whether exemptions have been applied consistently. If the sample shows that the category descriptions are accurate and the exemptions fit, the court may accept the Vaughn index for the remaining documents. If the sample reveals mischaracterizations or that nonexempt material was withheld, the court will order production or require a more detailed index.

In camera review. In camera review is an inspection of the actual withheld documents by the judge alone, outside the presence of the parties and the public. It is the most intrusive tool because it requires the court to view the content the agency seeks to protect. Courts use in camera review when the Vaughn submission and representative sampling are insufficient to resolve material disputes. In camera review is particularly common when the volume is modest, when categories appear overbroad, or when credibility concerns arise.

Standards governing in camera review. The decision to conduct in camera review lies within the court’s discretion, but courts weigh several factors: the sufficiency of the agency’s index, the importance of the issues, the need to protect sensitive material, the feasibility of sampling, and the potential for narrowing contested issues by other means. Courts perform in camera review with care: they focus only on the information necessary to assess applicability of the exemption and typically do not reveal exempt content in any public opinion.

Limitations and safeguards. Because in camera review requires the court to see the withheld content, courts take steps to limit disclosure. They guard the record, seal opinions when necessary, and confine the extent of review to the minimum necessary. Moreover, courts prefer that agencies first create an adequate Vaughn submission; in camera review is a backstop, not a routine replacement for a proper index.

Other judicial remedies. If sampling or in camera review reveals inadequate descriptions or improper withholdings, courts may order partial production, remand to the agency with instructions for supplemental justification, award fees or costs to the requester in cases of bad faith, or enter judgment for the requester. Conversely, if the court is satisfied that the Vaughn submission and any sampled or reviewed materials support the exemptions, it will grant summary judgment to the agency.

# Concluding principles for practice

The legal regime for categorical withholdings and Vaughn-type submissions reflects a balancing principle: courts must ensure that exemption statutes are not used as shields against judicial review while enabling agencies to protect genuinely sensitive information. Effective Vaughn submissions and categorical descriptions are those that give the court a usable map: specific enough to show why documents fall within an exemption but careful enough to avoid disclosure of the withheld substance.

Practitioners preparing Vaughn indices should prioritize specificity, factual linkage, narrow categorical definitions when categories are necessary, and transparency about segregability. Requesters challenging withholdings should press for itemized indexes, representative samples, and, where appropriate, in camera review. Courts play a gatekeeping role, refusing to accept conclusory assertions and deploying sampling and in camera tools to test the adequacy of justifications without unnecessarily compromising privileged material.

Understanding these principles will equip the reader to analyze judicial opinions that address the adequacy of Vaughn-type submissions and the permissibility of categorical withholdings, and to evaluate whether the procedural posture (motion practice, summary judgment, remand, or in camera inspection) appropriately shapes the court’s review.
