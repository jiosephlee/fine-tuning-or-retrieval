Introduction

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) establishes a general rule of disclosure: agency records are presumptively public and must be released on request unless they fall within one of the statute’s specific exemptions. FOIA’s disclosure mandate is not an all-or-nothing rule; when a document contains both exempt and nonexempt information, the agency must take reasonable steps to disclose the nonexempt portions. The twin ideas that information must be meaningfully presented, and that only legitimately exempt material may be withheld, guide courts’ review. This chapter explains the statutory duties that give rise to the segregability requirement, the standards courts apply to determine whether what remains after redaction is “meaningful” (as contrasted with isolated words or phrases), how agencies may and may not rely on ex parte materials when asserting nonsegregability, the evidentiary showing agencies must make (usually via affidavits and indices), how courts verify agency claims, and the narrow circumstances under which an entire document may be withheld as “inextricably intertwined.”

# Statutory duty: § 552(b) and § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)

FOIA’s fundamental architecture places the burden on agencies to release records unless the information falls within statutory exemptions. Section 552(b) (commonly cited as “Exemption (b)”) enumerates categories of information that Congress has determined may be withheld in whole or in part. Those exemptions are affirmative defenses to disclosure: once an agency contends that a requested record, or portion thereof, falls within an exemption, it must justify withholding under the statutory criteria.

The duty to segregate and disclose nonexempt information is grounded in FOIA’s broader disclosure mandate and specific statutory language requiring release of reasonably segregable portions of a record. Section 552(a) contains the procedural and substantive obligations of agencies in responding to requests. Relevant subsections require that when an agency reasonably segregates nonexempt portions of a record and discloses them, it must not withhold the remaining information unless it falls squarely within an exemption. Section 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) (and adjacent provisions) have been read and applied by courts to reinforce and clarify the obligation that agencies must disclose “reasonably segregable” material. The statutory scheme therefore imposes two linked duties on agencies: (1) to demonstrate that withheld material falls within an exemption in § 552(b); and (2) to take reasonable steps to release any reasonably segregable nonexempt portions under § 552(a) and related provisions.

The legal consequence of these duties is that "segregability" is not a discretionary courtesy: it is an affirmative, enforceable obligation. An agency cannot satisfy FOIA by simply redacting or withholding documents wholesale and offering conclusory averments that everything withheld is exempt. Instead, the agency must analyze documents line-by-line or portion-by-portion as necessary, and must justify why particular text cannot be disclosed. Courts treat the segregability requirement as a limitation on the exemptions: exemptions permit withholding; they do not license blanket non-disclosure when part of a record is clearly nonexempt.

# Mead Data Central v. Air Force: ‘meaningful’ information vs. isolated words; interspersed content

The courts have developed substantive standards for what must be disclosed once segregability is asked. A key doctrinal distinction requires agencies to disclose information in a way that is “meaningful,” rather than merely producing disconnected words or data fragments that lack informational context. This principle addresses both the form and substance of disclosure: it is not enough to reveal bits and pieces if those fragments do not convey usable or intelligible information to the requester.

In assessing whether the disclosed material is meaningful, courts ask whether the released portions, taken together, communicate a comprehensible substantive message, or whether the agency has effectively nullified disclosure by releasing isolated elements whose informative content is nullified by the withheld portions. For example, releasing a sequence of numbers or individual words where redaction has removed the connective, explanatory, or contextual material will usually not satisfy FOIA’s disclosure aims. Courts examine whether the information left after redactions enables the requester meaningfully to understand the subject matter of the record, or whether the agency has “picked apart” a document in a way that strips it of explanatory content.

Agencies contend with the tension between disclosure and legitimately protecting exempt information. When exempt and nonexempt material are interspersed—that is, when protected and unprotected content appear together, sentence-by-sentence or clause-by-clause—courts require a fine-grained, reasonable approach to segregation. The agency must show, with persuasive detail, why each withheld segment is exempt. Blanket assertions that an entire paragraph or half-page is exempt because some exempt content appears within do not suffice. Conversely, courts recognize that some disclosure could reveal exempt information indirectly (by inference), and therefore an agency may permissibly redact connective language that would defeat the exemption. The central question remains whether, after the redactions the agency proposes, the released portions still convey meaningfully useful information rather than mere isolated words.

The Mead Data line of authority thus compels agencies to think about disclosure in communicative terms: FOIA’s purpose is to allow public understanding of government operations and decisions, and meaningful disclosure is measured against that objective. Courts will not accept a strategy that technically produces nonexempt words while defeating FOIA’s informational purpose.

# Perioperative Services & Logistics v. VA: practical application and use of ex parte materials in segregability

Practical application of the segregability doctrine requires courts and agencies to confront complex realities: documents may include material from third parties, classified or sensitive government deliberations, and attachments or notes created ex parte (i.e., in communications not shared with all parties or originated in contexts where disclosure could cause harm). Judicial scrutiny focuses on how agencies use such materials to justify withholding and whether those justifications are supported by the administrative record.

When agencies rely on ex parte materials (for example, internal deliberative memos, attorney work product, or information provided under confidentiality), they must still demonstrate segregability. The fact that a portion of a document originates from an ex parte communication does not automatically preclude disclosure of adjacent or embedded nonexempt content. Agencies must identify precisely which parts of the document embody the ex parte content and show why the remainder cannot be meaningfully segregated and disclosed without compromising the underlying protected interest.

Courts applying these principles perform a fact-sensitive inquiry. They examine whether the ex parte material is intermingled with nonexempt content such that any disclosure would reveal the protected matter, or whether discrete portions can be released without revealing the ex parte substance. The practical lesson is that agencies cannot hide behind the existence of ex parte materials to avoid the segregability requirement. The agency’s justification must explain the relationship between the ex parte content and the allegedly nonsegregable portions, describing with specificity why disclosure of those portions would have a concrete adverse effect that the exemption is designed to prevent.

Perioperative-type analyses have also emphasized that the agency’s processing methods matter: careful redaction and document-by-document consideration weigh in favor of disclosure, whereas ad hoc or generalized approaches to withholding do not. Courts will assess the reasonableness of the agency’s segregability efforts in context, considering the nature of the record, the sensitivity of the withheld material, and the feasibility of producing a version that preserves meaning while protecting exempt content.

# Agency affidavits on non-segregability and court verification

Because courts typically lack access to the entire factual substrate underlying an agency’s withholding decision, FOIA litigation places primary evidentiary weight on agency declarations (affidavits or declarations from agency officials). These statements explain the basis for withholding, describe the record-processing methodology, and detail steps taken to segregate nonexempt material. The judicial task is to evaluate whether the agency has satisfied its statutory burden to withhold only those parts that are properly exempt and to disclose what can reasonably be segregated.

An adequate agency affidavit (sometimes called a Vaughn affidavit when accompanied by an index) must be more than conclusory. It should identify the documents at issue, describe the information in each withheld portion with sufficient specificity to permit judicial review, explain why the exemption applies to each withheld portion, and explain why any nonexempt material cannot reasonably be segregated and disclosed. Agencies should describe the processing steps taken—line-by-line review, consultation with program offices, redaction software used, and consideration of releasing summaries or redacted versions where possible. When an agency declines to segregate on grounds of intermingling or inference, the affidavit should explain the causal chain: how the released text would permit reconstruction or inference of the protected information.

Courts give deference to agency expertise on matters such as national security, privacy, or law enforcement sensitivity, but that deference is not uncritical. Judicial verification takes several forms. First, the court will scrutinize the sufficiency of the affidavit’s factual detail and reasoning. If the affidavit is conclusory, vague, or fails to tie the withheld material to the exemption, the court will order further explanation or deny summary judgment for the agency. Second, courts often require a Vaughn index: a document-by-document catalog that pairs specific withheld portions with legal justifications and factual descriptions. A thorough Vaughn index enables the court and the adversary to test the agency’s claims.

When the documentary record contains especially sensitive information or the agency’s affidavits leave important questions unresolved, courts may conduct in camera review—private judicial inspection of the records—to verify the agency’s representations. In camera review is not routine but is appropriate where the affidavit does not permit meaningful review or where secrecy claims are particularly consequential. Courts balance the need to verify against the risk of further disclosing exempt information during review, and they exercise this power to ensure that the segregability duty has been honored.

Throughout this process, the agency bears the burden of proof. The requester need not prove a negative (that certain portions are nonexempt); rather, the agency must demonstrate that withheld material fits within the claimed exemption and that any nonexempt material has been disclosed or is not reasonably segregable. If the court finds the agency’s showing inadequate, it may order disclosure, require supplementation of the administrative record, or remand to the agency for further processing consistent with the court’s guidance.

# When entire documents may be withheld as inextricably intertwined

Although FOIA favors disclosure of segregable material, there are narrow circumstances where an entire document may be withheld on the ground that exempt and nonexempt information are inextricably intertwined. The “inextricably intertwined” doctrine recognizes that in some documents the exempt content is so integrated with nonexempt portions that releasing any nonexempt fragment would effectively reveal the exempt material or destroy the privacy or protection the exemption is meant to provide.

Determining whether a document is inextricably intertwined requires a careful line-drawing exercise. Courts consider whether redactions could be made that would leave a coherent, comprehensible remainder without enabling derivation or inference of the withheld information. If redaction would leave only isolated fragments or would still permit reconstruction of the exempt material, and if redaction would thus undermine the purpose of the exemption, the document may be withheld in full. The agency must justify this conclusion with specific reasoning—merely asserting that the exempt and nonexempt material are “mixed together” is insufficient.

Three factors inform the inextricability inquiry. First, the extent and nature of the intermixing: are the exempt elements merely adjacent to nonexempt text, or are they embedded throughout so that every sentence depends on knowledge of the exempt content? Second, the risk of meaningful inference: would disclosure of the nonexempt portions allow a reasonably competent individual to piece together the exempt material by context, cross-referencing, or simple logical deduction? Third, the feasibility of alternative disclosures: could the agency provide a summary, segregated excerpt, or redacted version that preserves informational value without revealing the exempt matter?

The judiciary treats claims of inextricability skeptically because withholding entire documents defeats FOIA’s core presumption. Accordingly, courts insist on a showing that segregation efforts were careful and that realistic attempts at redaction or summarization would fail to produce meaningful disclosure. If the agency can demonstrate that any disclosure would either be unintelligible or would reveal the exempt matter by inference, withholding the whole document is permissible; but this is an exception rather than the rule.

Conclusion

FOIA’s segregability requirement operationalizes the statute’s core tension between transparency and legitimate confidentiality. The agency’s duty to disclose “reasonably segregable” nonexempt material is statutory, concrete, and enforceable. Courts require that disclosures be meaningful—not tokenized strings of words devoid of context—and require agencies to support nonsegregability claims with detailed, document-specific affidavits and indices subject to judicial scrutiny, including in camera review where necessary. The inextricably intertwined doctrine provides a narrow escape for agencies confronted with documents whose exempt and nonexempt components cannot be separated without undermining the exemption’s purpose, but agencies must justify that conclusion with clear reasoning and evidence. Understanding these principles prepares the reader to analyze judicial opinions that apply FOIA’s segregability rules to particular records and to evaluate whether an agency’s redactions truly comport with the statute’s commitment to meaningful public access.
