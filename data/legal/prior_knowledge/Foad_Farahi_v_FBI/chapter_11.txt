Introduction

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) establishes a presumption of disclosure for federal agency records, subject to nine statutory exemptions. Congress and the courts have long balanced public access against legitimate government interests in confidentiality. The FOIA Improvements Act of 2016 made targeted but meaningful revisions that recalibrated this balance by clarifying when agencies must withhold information and by imposing affirmative obligations to disclose what can be segregated. Two interrelated changes are especially important for modern FOIA practice: (1) the codification of a “foreseeable harm” standard that limits the scope of exemptions, and (2) an explicit requirement that agencies take “reasonable steps” to segregate and disclose nonexempt factual material. Both modifications affect how agencies justify withholding, how courts review those justifications, and how plaintiffs frame their challenges. This chapter explains those statutory changes, their doctrinal contours, how courts apply them, and practical implications for agency compliance.

# The ‘foreseeable harm’ standard under § 552(a)(8)(A)(i) (overview)

Background and statutory language

Prior to 2016, FOIA law developed through a mix of statutory text and Supreme Court decisions. Some courts had read certain exemptions broadly, permitting withholding whenever disclosure "could reasonably be expected" to cause harm (a predictive standard). The FOIA Improvements Act of 2016 amended FOIA to require that an agency show that disclosure “would” cause a foreseeable harm or harm that is reasonably foreseeable. The relevant text appears in § 552(a)(8)(A)(i), which requires that an agency withhold information only if it reasonably foresees that disclosure would harm an interest protected by one of the exemptions or would cause an identifiable harm where the exemption requires such harm.

Statutory meaning and interpretive effect

The statutory insertion of “foreseeable” operates as a limiting principle on exemptions that predicate withholding on a harm. It signals two related requirements:

1. Causation and likelihood: The agency must show not merely a speculative possibility of harm; it must show that the harm is predictable in light of the subject matter, context, and nature of the record. “Foreseeable” requires a reasonable, non-speculative link between disclosure and the asserted harm.

2. Specificity: The harm must be particularized to the record at issue and to the circumstances of disclosure. Blanket assertions that disclosure generally could harm a category of interests are inadequate absent a showing that the specific records are likely to result in the predicted harm.

These elements align FOIA with the general administrative-law principle that agency action must be supported by reasoned justification, and they reflect congressional intent to prevent overbroad, categorical, or purely hypothetical withholding.

How courts evaluate “foreseeable harm”

Judicial review of an agency’s foreseeable-harm claim is typically conducted under the Administrative Procedure Act paradigm: courts review whether the agency’s explanation is reasonable and supported by the administrative record. The reviewing court asks whether the agency has articulated a logical connection between the withheld information and the asserted harm and whether the claim of predictability is grounded in specific facts or reasonably reliable inferences.

The standard is fact-intensive and contextual. Courts consider:

- The nature of the information (e.g., names, technical details, compiled intelligence, internal deliberations).
- The sensitivity, age, and source of the information.
- The identity of the requester and the likely uses of the disclosed information. Disclosure to a broad public may increase the risk of dissemination; disclosure to a requester with a narrow, demonstrated need may not.
- The foreseeability that third parties will use the information to cause the predicted harm.
- Any countervailing public interest in disclosure.

The degree of deference afforded to agency predictions varies with the nature of the exemption. Where national-security or law-enforcement expertise is implicated, courts often grant greater weight to an agency’s predictive judgments, provided the agency offers reasonably specific and credible explanations. Where the claimed harm is less technical, courts may require more concrete evidence.

Common pitfalls for agencies

Agencies commonly err by relying on categorical, conclusory, or boilerplate declarations that disclosure “could” harm an interest, without explaining how disclosure of the particular record would lead to harm. Vague references to “sensitive law-enforcement techniques” or “national security” without tying those generalities to the withheld material are unlikely to satisfy the foreseeability requirement. Similarly, invoking precedent without record-specific analysis risks reversal.

Burden of proof and procedural posture

The agency bears the burden to justify nondisclosure. In FOIA litigation, the agency typically submits a declaration or affidavit describing the withheld material and why disclosure would foreseeably cause harm. Courts accept classified declarations in camera or ex parte in national-security contexts, but the threshold showing must still be adequate to allow meaningful judicial review. When the case reaches summary judgment, the declaration serves as the primary basis for the agency’s justification; the court determines whether the declaration establishes that the harm is foreseeable as to the particular records.

If an agency’s declaration is deficient, courts may order the agency to release information, to provide a more detailed justification, or to allow limited discovery or in camera review of the records. Procedural posture matters: when the factual record is undeveloped, courts may be more willing to remand for supplementation; at summary judgment, conclusory affidavits will not suffice.

# Codified ‘reasonable steps’ to segregate under § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii)

Statutory change and basic principle

The FOIA Improvements Act of 2016 also added a requirement that agencies take “reasonable steps” to segregate and disclose nonexempt portions of records. This codifies and amplifies traditional case law that required agencies to release segregable, nonexempt material and to provide a reasonably detailed justification for any withheld portions. The new statutory formulation focuses attention on the agency’s affirmative obligation to separate disclosable from nondisclosable content and sets a reasonableness baseline for the methods used.

Elements of the segregability obligation

The segregability analysis contains several discrete elements that courts evaluate:

1. Presumption of disclosure: The default rule under FOIA is disclosure. Any restriction must be justified both with respect to the exemption asserted and by a showing that the nonexempt portions cannot be segregated.

2. Reasonable steps: Agencies must demonstrate that they took reasonable steps to segregate nonexempt information. Reasonableness is measured against what a competent agency would do under the circumstances, considering technological capabilities, resources, and the nature and volume of the records. The statute shifts focus from an idealized absolutist standard to a practical, objective reasonableness test.

3. Segregability of portions: If portions of a record are reasonably segregable — meaning they can be redacted without destroying the integrity or meaning of the remainder — the agency should produce them. Only if nondisclosure of the entire record is necessary should the agency withhold it in full, and the agency must explain why full withholding is required.

4. Burden and documentation: The agency bears the burden to justify its segregability determination. Practically, that means providing a sufficiently detailed declaration that describes the steps taken to segregate, the methods used, and a justification for any whole-record withholdings.

Tests and standards for what counts as “reasonable steps”

Courts have articulated several factors relevant to reasonableness. The FOIA Improvements Act’s language aligns with these factors and emphasizes an objective inquiry. Relevant considerations include:

- Effort proportionality: Whether the effort required to segregate is reasonable in relation to the volume and importance of the information. Agencies are not required to undertake extraordinary measures (e.g., manual review of thousands of pages) when a less burdensome technological solution is available, or when the cost would be disproportionate. Conversely, when responsive documents are limited in number or highly significant, greater effort may be required.

- Technological capability: Whether segmentation can be done automatically or with existing software tools, or whether segregability requires onerous manual processes. Agencies should use reasonable technological tools where available.

- Clarity of redactions: Whether redactions can be made in a way that leaves a coherent, comprehensible record. If redaction renders a document unintelligible — and no intelligible portion remains — withholding the whole document may be justified, but the agency should explain why reasonable redaction would not suffice.

- Interconnectedness of exempt and nonexempt material: Where exempt and nonexempt information are inextricably linked such that segregating content would reveal exempt material by implication, whole-record withholding may be justified. The agency must demonstrate this logical necessity.

- Resource constraints and institutional context: While resource limitations are relevant, courts are skeptical of blanket assertions that segregation is impossible because of lack of staff or money. Agencies should document concrete obstacles and efforts made to surmount them.

How courts review segregability claims

Judicial review focuses on whether the agency’s explanation of its segregation process is adequate and factual assertions are credible. Courts rarely require production of “all” underlying materials for review, but they do require sufficiently detailed affidavits describing the process used. When declarations are conclusory or fail to explain why redactions are not feasible, courts will order disclosure. Conversely, when declarations show careful review, use of reasonable technological measures, and principled application of redaction rules, courts will defer.

Practical consequences of the reasonable-steps requirement

The codified requirement pushes agencies toward systematic processes: records management practices, searchable metadata, and redaction protocols. It creates incentives to invest in technology and training so that redaction can be completed efficiently. For requesters, it narrows the agency’s ability to hide behind resource constraints and encourages challenges to inadequate segregability explanations.

Procedural posture and remediation

When claimants contest segregability at an early stage (e.g., through administrative appeals), agencies should provide detailed segregation logs and sample redactions. In litigation, courts often allow agencies to supplement declarations or to produce a Vaughn index (or similar itemization) to permit meaningful review. If initial declarations are insufficient, courts may remand with instructions to perform and document additional segregability steps.

# Agency best practices for compliance post-2016

Policy and operational recommendations

The statutory changes impose not only legal obligations but also organizational expectations. Agencies should adopt policies and practices that both facilitate lawful withholding and reduce litigation risk. Best practices include:

1. Clear internal FOIA guidelines that reflect the foreseeable-harm and reasonable-steps standards. Agencies should train FOIA officers, program personnel, and attorneys on what constitutes a sufficient foreseeable-harm explanation and what procedures satisfy “reasonable steps” to segregate.

2. Use of standardized, substantive declarations. When withholding, agencies should prepare administrative affidavits that explain, in nonconclusory terms, why disclosure of each withheld portion would foreseeably harm the interest protected by the exemption and what specific harm is likely. Templates should prompt declarants to describe the factual basis, the link to the specific record, and any supporting empirical or institutional evidence.

3. Record-level and system-level metadata practices. Proper indexing, tagging of sensitive fields, and searchable metadata facilitate targeted review and redaction. This reduces the burden of segregation and improves the agency’s ability to show it took reasonable steps.

4. Investment in redaction tools and workflows. Agencies should adopt redaction software capable of identifying personal identifiers, classified markings, or other categories of exempt material. Workflows should include quality control steps and chain-of-custody documentation.

5. Documented segregation protocols. Agencies should keep contemporaneous records of the segregability process: who reviewed documents, what steps were employed, what technological tools were used, and why whole-document withholdings were necessary.

6. Risk assessments for foreseeable harms. For recurring categories of records (e.g., internal investigations, law-enforcement memos, technical manuals), agencies should develop risk assessments that articulate predictable harms tied to those categories. These can serve as building blocks for credible, record-specific foreseeable-harm explanations while still requiring case-by-case tailoring.

7. Proactive disclosure where feasible. To reduce disputes, agencies should proactively release frequently requested categories of records in sanitized forms when possible. Creating FOIA reading rooms and routinely posting sanitized documents reduces the need to assert exemptions.

8. Coordination across components. When multiple components contribute to a record, coordination ensures a coherent approach to segregability and harm predictions, reducing inconsistent or boilerplate justifications.

Recordkeeping and litigation posture

Agency declarations are typically evaluated at summary judgment; therefore, contemporaneous documentation of the analysis is crucial. Agencies should ensure that legal reasoning is reflected in administrative records and that declarants sign statements verifying their personal knowledge of the review. When litigation occurs, courts will look for a documented chain of reasoning rather than after-the-fact rationalizations.

Balancing efficiency and legal sufficiency

While agencies must act efficiently, they cannot sacrifice legal sufficiency. Reasonable-steps is flexible, but to survive judicial review agencies should show that they balanced cost concerns against statutory disclosure obligations and used available tools to maximize disclosure. Routine reliance on generalized resource constraints without detailed documentation is likely to fail.

# Interplay with Exemption 7(A) and national security assertions

Overview of Exemption 7(A)

Exemption 7 protects from disclosure records or information compiled for law enforcement purposes to the extent release could, among other things, reasonably be expected to cause “an unwarranted invasion of personal privacy” (7(C)), or to “reasonably be expected to disclose the identity of a confidential source” (7(D)), and under 7(A) where disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. Exemption 7(A) is harm-based: the statute requires a showing that disclosure would reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings, which makes it a natural candidate for application of the foreseeable-harm standard.

Applying “foreseeable harm” to Exemption 7(A)

The 2016 amendment’s foreseeable-harm language directly affects how courts assess 7(A) claims. Agencies must show that disclosure of the particular records would foreseeably interfere with an ongoing enforcement proceeding or future enforcement activity. The analysis typically examines factors such as:

- The nature and stage of the investigation or proceeding (e.g., open vs. closed, preliminary vs. grand jury or active prosecution).

- The potential mechanisms of interference (e.g., compromising witness cooperation, tipping off targets, alerting subjects to investigative techniques or timing).

- Whether excerpts or redactions could mitigate interference risks.

Agencies should provide specific explanations linking the content of the records to engagement in enforcement activity and showing why disclosure of that content would be likely to frustrate the agency’s ability to carry out enforcement. Boilerplate assertions that publicity is “harmful” are insufficient; predictive judgments must be supported by particularized reasoning.

Interplay with national security and other exemptions

Although Exemption 7(A) arises in the law-enforcement context, similar foreseeability principles apply to national-security exemptions (e.g., Exemption 1, which protects classified information). The key interaction points are:

1. Degree of deference: Courts traditionally give greater deference to national-security and classified harm assertions due to agency expertise and separation-of-powers concerns. Nonetheless, the foreseeable-harm language requires agencies even in national-security contexts to link disclosure of the specific records to a predictable harm, although courts may accept classified declarations in camera and may apply a more deferential standard where the agency provides a credible and context-rich explanation.

2. Segregability obligations across exemptions: Even if national-security or law-enforcement exemptions apply to parts of a record, the reasonable-steps requirement mandates that agencies release nonexempt factual material. In national-security cases, courts may accept that redacting classified portions presents particular technical and legal challenges; nevertheless, agencies should document the technical measures taken and why redaction of classified phrases would not sufficiently protect national-security concerns or would itself reveal exempt material by implication.

3. Classified declarations and judicial review: Because national-security assertions often rest on classified information, agencies may submit classified affidavits and rely on ex parte review. Courts receiving classified declarations balance the need for meaningful review with the practical impossibility of fully public justification. Still, the foreseeable-harm and reasonable-steps constraints require some level of judicial scrutiny; where the declaration is conclusory, courts have tools such as in camera review, remand, or ordering limited disclosure of non-sensitive material.

4. Interaction with other exemptions: Many records invoke multiple exemptions, for example Exemption 7(A) plus privacy-based exemptions. Agencies must justify each claimed exemption in the particular instance, and the foreseeable-harm standard applies to any exemption that predicates withholding on a likely harm. For privacy-based exemptions where harm is intrusion rather than operational interference, agencies must explain how disclosure of the particular information will foreseeably result in the asserted privacy harm.

Strategic and procedural considerations in enforcement and security contexts

In law-enforcement and national-security contexts, agencies often must weigh operational needs against transparency. The foreseeability requirement does not mandate disclosure where legitimate harm is genuinely predictable; instead it prevents speculative secrecy. Agencies should document contemporaneous assessments explaining how disclosure of particular records would interfere with enforcement or harm security, why redactions cannot cure the problem, and what steps were taken to segregate nonexempt material.

Courts reviewing such contexts will consider the sensitivity and context of the records. If an enforcement action is ongoing, agencies will have a stronger showing for 7(A), but they still must articulate how releasing the specific document would interfere. For closed matters, the agency must show a continuing plausible risk of interference rather than rely on historical concerns.

Concluding observations

The FOIA Improvements Act of 2016 anchored modern FOIA practice in two complementary requirements: agencies must show that nondisclosure is tied to a foreseeable harm, and they must take reasonable steps to segregate and disclose nonexempt material. These provisions require agencies to sharpen their factual explanations, improve their records-management and redaction practices, and document their decisionmaking. For courts, the amendments provide a statutory lens for reviewing agency affidavits and rationale: deferential where warranted by expertise and context but demanding specificity and reasoned analysis. For requesters and litigants, the amendments supply clear grounds to attack conclusory or categorical withholdings and to press for detailed segregability showing. Understanding these principles is essential for analyzing modern FOIA litigation and for appreciating how courts will apply exemptions in operational and national-security settings.
