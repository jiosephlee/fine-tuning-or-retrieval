Introduction

Fraud under the federal statutes commonly prosecuted in white‑collar cases—most notably the mail and wire fraud statutes (18 U.S.C. §§1341, 1343) and related provisions such as the securities‑fraud statute (18 U.S.C. §1348)—protects money and property from being taken through deceptive means. Over time, courts have developed multiple theories by which the Government may prove that a defendant “schemed” to obtain money or property by false pretenses. One such theory is the fraudulent‑inducement theory: the allegation that a defendant used material misstatements or omissions to induce a victim to transfer money or property. This chapter explains the doctrinal contours of fraudulent inducement, clarifies what it means for money or property to be “obtained” as the object of a scheme, addresses whether proof of net pecuniary loss to the victim is required, and shows how intent‑to‑defraud and materiality function within this analytical structure. The chapter emphasizes the elements the Government must prove, the defenses available to a defendant, and the way courts apply these concepts at trial and on appeal.

# Fraudulent inducement: using material misstatements to procure transfers of money or property

Fraudulent inducement is a theory of liability premised on the proposition that the defendant’s deception induced a victim to part with money or property. At its core are two related ideas: deception (a false statement or omission of a material fact) and causation (the deception caused the victim to transfer money or property). Under the federal statutory schemes, a fraudulent‑inducement prosecution typically requires proof of the following elements, tailored to the statute charged (e.g., wire fraud, mail fraud, securities fraud):

1. A scheme or artifice to defraud, or a scheme to obtain money or property by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises.
2. An intent to defraud (a specific intent to deceive and to obtain money or property).
3. The use of the mails or wires (or other jurisdictional element).
4. A causal nexus between the deceit and the transfer of money or property (the deception induced the victim to transfer money or property).

Deception. The Government must show that the defendant made a misstatement, misrepresentation, or material omission. Courts evaluate deception in light of ordinary tort and contract doctrines: whether a reasonable person in the victim’s position would have been likely to rely on the misrepresentation, whether the statement was false when made, and whether the defendant knew (or recklessly disregarded) the falsity. The content of the misrepresentation may be oral, written, or embodied in documents, certifications, bids, applications, financial statements, or other communications.

Causation. Fraudulent‑inducement liability turns on causation in a practical sense: did the deception cause the victim to transfer money or property? At trial the prosecution ordinarily must establish that the victim relied on—or at least was induced by—the misrepresentation in making the payment or conveyance. Courts assess reliance both factually (what the particular victim knew and did) and legally (whether reliance was reasonable or foreseeable under the circumstances). Where the victim is an organization—e.g., a government agency or corporate entity—courts consider whether the misrepresentations were made to an agent or decision‐maker whose reasonable reliance can be imputed to the organization for purposes of inducing the transfer.

Character of the transfer. The transfer that constitutes the “obtaining” may be a payment of money, issuance of a check, acceptance of goods or services, award of a contract, granting of a license, or any other relinquishment of a thing of value. The focus is on whether the defendant obtained possession, control, or title to money or property by means of deception, not on labels. Even transfers that leave the victim with something of value in return can be the object of a fraudulent inducement scheme, provided the transfer occurred because of the defendant’s fraudulent misrepresentations.

Proof and procedure. The Government bears the burden of proving each element beyond a reasonable doubt. Defendants may raise defenses that attack causation (arguing the victim would have made the transfer regardless of the misrepresentation), materiality (the misstatement was immaterial), or intent (no intent to deceive or to obtain money/property). Because fraudulent‑inducement prosecutions often arise in complex commercial contexts, courts pay close attention to the precise communications, the process by which the victim made the transfer (bidding, contracting, purchasing), and the timing of the deception relative to the transfer.

# “Obtaining money or property” as an object of the scheme

The phrase “for obtaining money or property” in the federal fraud statutes sets an essential outer boundary: the object of the scheme must be money or property. Courts interpret this phrase narrowly in one respect and pragmatically in another.

Traditional property focus. Federal fraud statutes were historically understood to target wrongdoing that wrongs a person’s property rights. Thus, to sustain a conviction the Government must show that the scheme aimed at obtaining money or property—tangible or, in some situations, intangible interests recognized as property. A defendant cannot be convicted under the property‑based fraud statutes for schemes that merely aim to obtain non‑property interests (for example, advancing political influence or obtaining a nonproperty regulatory benefit) unless courts have recognized those interests as property within the statute’s scope.

Possession, control, or title. “Obtaining” is commonly understood in the ordinary sense: to gain possession, control, or title to money or property. A scheme to cause a victim to transfer cash or deliver an asset, even if the victim receives some quid pro quo in return, may constitute obtaining money or property if the defendant gains possession or a property interest by means of deception. The key is that the defendant’s deception is instrumental in securing the transfer of money or property.

Intangible property. Courts have recognized that the statutory phrase may encompass certain forms of intangible property—interests that are traditionally understood as property under relevant law. Examples include funds, contractual rights, and other commercially recognized intangible assets. However, courts remain cautious about expanding the concept of property to cover amorphous intangibles that are not traditionally treated as property rights. Jurisprudence has limited some expansive theories (such as treating mere information or a generalized “right to control” assets as property) while allowing protection for recognized economic interests.

Organizational transfers. When the victim is an organization, the focus is on whether the scheme sought to obtain the organization’s money or property. If a midlevel agent misrepresents facts and causes the organization to pay money it would not otherwise have paid, the defendant may be said to have obtained organizational property. Courts examine whether the agent’s decisionmaking role, and the agent’s reliance on the defendant’s misrepresentation, is imputable to the organization for purposes of causation.

Causation and the object requirement. The Government must link the deception to the obtaining of money or property as the objective of the scheme. Proof that the defendant engaged in deception and simultaneously realized a financial benefit may suffice where the prosecution demonstrates that the deception was intended to, and did in fact, procure money or property. The focus is forward‑looking: was money or property the object the defendant sought to secure by the deception?

# No net‑pecuniary‑loss requirement; equal‑value exchanges do not negate fraud

A central doctrinal question is whether fraud requires that the victim suffer a net pecuniary loss—i.e., that the victim end up poorer in monetary terms as a result of the deception. Two competing intuitions have animated courts and commentators. One intuition reads common‑law fraud and modern statutes to require a measurable financial loss; the other treats fraud as encompassing the wrongful procurement of property by deception even if the victim receives fair or equivalent value in return.

Net‑loss is not an element of federal property‑fraud statutes. The federal mail, wire, and securities fraud statutes do not themselves include a requirement that the victim sustain a net pecuniary loss. The statutes focus instead on whether the defendant obtained money or property by deceptive means. Consequently, many courts have concluded that a net‑pecuniary‑loss showing is not a statutory element that the Government must prove. Under that approach, the Government can sustain a conviction if it proves that the defendant, by deception, caused the victim to part with money or property—even when, in economic terms, the victim received equivalent or even superior value in exchange.

Deception‑induced deprivation as injury. The doctrinal justification for dispensing with a net‑loss requirement draws on the common‑law treatment of rescission and conveyance cases: deception may be legally injurious because it deprives the victim of the right to make an informed decision about transferring property. Even if the victim receives an item of equal market value, the fact of deception renders the transfer wrongful; the victim has been deprived of the bargained‑for quality or condition—an injury cognizable under fraud doctrines. Courts have described such injury as the wrongful obtaining of property through deception, rather than solely measured economic loss.

Equal‑value exchanges. Where a defendant lies to induce a bargain in which the victim receives something of purportedly equal value, defendants commonly argue that no fraud occurred because there was no net loss. Courts reject this argument where the deception was material and induced the transfer. In other words, whether the victim ultimately received value is not dispositive if the transfer was achieved by material misrepresentation. The Government need not show that the victim could not have obtained equivalent value absent the fraud; it need only show that the defendant obtained money or property through deception and intended to do so.

Practical consequences. The absence of a net‑loss requirement has several practical consequences. It allows prosecutors to pursue schemes where the victim received some performance or benefit but was nonetheless deprived of a bargained‑for condition or a decision made on truthful information. It also creates legal room to prosecute schemes rooted in false certifications, sham subcontracting, sham qualifications, or fraudulent pretenses used to procure government contracts, loans, or other payments even when the victim later received some measurable performance.

Limits and safeguards. Although net‑loss is not a required element, courts remain attentive to limiting principles. The “money or property” requirement still bounds prosecutions: the Government must show that the object of the fraud was money or property. Courts also require proof of intent to defraud and materiality (discussed below). The absence of a net‑loss element does not mean the Government can criminalize every bad business deal or puffery; the deception must be material, the taking must be of money or property, and the defendant must have had a culpable intent.

# Interaction with intent‑to‑defraud and materiality analyses

Intent to defraud and materiality are separate but interrelated components of fraud prosecutions. They function both as elements the prosecution must prove and as limiting doctrines that cabin the scope of criminal liability.

Intent to defraud

Nature of the intent requirement. Intent to defraud is a specific intent element: the Government must show the defendant had a conscious objective to deceive and to obtain money or property. The intent element serves two tasks. First, it distinguishes criminally culpable deception from innocent mistakes, negligent misstatements, or mere poor business judgment. Second, it connects the deception to the defendant’s purpose of obtaining money or property.

Proving intent. Because intent is a state of mind, it is rarely proven by direct evidence. Prosecutors rely on circumstantial evidence: the content and timing of statements, concealment or cover‑up efforts, pattern and course of dealings, misleading documents, implausible explanations, and false statements made to investigators or auditors. Evidence of a scheme’s design—for example, using intermediaries or sham entities to mask a relationship—can support an inference of intent. That inference must be reasonable and grounded in the totality of the evidence.

Intent and equal‑value transfers. Even where a transfer results in the victim receiving some value, intent is central: if the defendant intentionally used deception to cause the transfer, that intent satisfies the mental element. The critical question is whether the defendant knowingly sought to gain money or property through deception. A defendant who honestly believed their statements to be true, or who acted with mere negligence, typically lacks the requisite intent to defraud.

Materiality

Definition and role. Materiality asks whether a misrepresentation or omission has a natural tendency to influence, or was reasonably capable of influencing, the decisionmaking of the victim. Materiality is an objective standard—measured by whether a reasonable decisionmaker would consider the fact important to the transaction—not a subjective inquiry into the particular victim’s idiosyncratic concerns. Materiality functions as a gatekeeper: immaterial falsehoods are not actionable as fraud.

Application in procurements and contracts. In cases involving procurement, contract bids, or regulatory qualifications, materiality commonly focuses on whether the misstatement went to a condition that the victim regarded as important in awarding the contract or making the payment. False statements about compliance with regulatory prerequisites, qualifications of a subcontractor, or the functional role of an entity in a bid process are frequently material because they bear on the identity or suitability of the contracting party.

Interaction of materiality and intent. Materiality and intent interact in important ways. A deliberate falsehood about a trivial, immaterial fact typically will not satisfy fraud because it lacks the capacity to influence a decision. Conversely, a material misrepresentation combined with circumstantial evidence of a conscious effort to secure money or property creates a strong inference of intent to defraud. On appeal, courts evaluate both the materiality of the misstatement and whether the evidence supports an inference of the defendant’s specific intent to procure money or property by deception.

Procedural posture and jury instructions. In criminal trials, courts must instruct juries accurately on the intent and materiality standards. The Government must prove intent beyond a reasonable doubt, and courts often provide guidance that the jury may infer intent from conduct, concealment, and other circumstantial evidence. For materiality, courts typically articulate an objective standard (reasonable capability of influencing the victim) and caution juries not to convict for immaterial inaccuracies or mere puffery.

Burden shifting and defense strategies. Defendants often respond by contesting materiality (arguing the fact was not important to the decisionmaker), denying reliance (arguing the victim did not in fact rely), showing alternative causes for the transfer (arguing the victim would have made the transfer absent the misrepresentation), or attacking the inferences supporting intent (offering benign explanations for concealment or misstatements). Where the defense establishes plausible alternative explanations for the challenged conduct, reasonable doubt on intent will foreclose conviction.

# Practical application: how courts analyze disputed cases

Fact‑intensive analysis. Fraudulent‑inducement cases are intensely factbound. Courts evaluate the communications, the contractual framework, the economic and regulatory context, the parties’ sophistication, and contemporaneous documents. Trial courts make evidentiary rulings about admissibility of documents, prior statements, and expert testimony that bear on materiality and intent. On appeal, courts review evidentiary rulings for abuse of discretion and sufficiency of the evidence under the deferential Jackson v. Virginia standard.

Causation and organizational complexity. When public entities or corporations are victims, tracing causation can be complex. Courts examine who among the victim’s agents received or relied on the defendant’s statements, whether that reliance was routinized in the organization’s decision process, and whether other intervening facts broke the causal chain. If a decision was the product of multiple influences, the Government must show that the defendant’s deception was a substantial and operative cause of the transfer.

Remedial overlap with civil law. Many fraudulent‑inducement prosecutions arise from conduct that also gives rise to civil remedies—contract rescission, tort claims for deceit, or equitable relief. Criminal prosecutions focus on the defendant’s culpable state of mind and the need to punish and deter, whereas civil actions concentrate on compensating loss. Because criminal liability can attach without net pecuniary loss, prosecutions sometimes proceed where a civil plaintiff might only obtain rescission or nominal damages.

Policy constraints. The intersection of commerce and criminal law invites policy-based limits. Courts are wary of turning the criminal law into a routine regulatory enforcement tool for ordinary commercial misrepresentations. The materiality and intent requirements, the property threshold, and the requirement that the Government prove “obtaining money or property” act as substantive and doctrinal safeguards against overcriminalization.

# Conclusion

The fraudulent‑inducement theory rests on the straightforward principle that inducing a victim to transfer money or property through material deception is criminal when the defendant intends to obtain money or property by that deception. The “obtaining money or property” phrase anchors statutory scope to property interests, where courts have recognized both tangible and certain traditional intangible property interests. Importantly, federal fraud statutes do not uniformly require proof that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss; an equal‑value exchange can still be a fraudulent obtaining where a defendant-induced transfer occurred by means of material falsehoods and the defendant acted with intent to deceive. Materiality and intent remain pivotal gatekeeping concepts: immaterial misstatements and mere negligence are insufficient for criminal liability. For students reading cases that apply these doctrines, attention to the factual record—who knew what and when, the role of agents and intermediaries, documentary proof, and the precise communications used to influence the victim—will be essential to understanding how courts determine whether a fraudulent‑inducement theory has been proved beyond a reasonable doubt.
