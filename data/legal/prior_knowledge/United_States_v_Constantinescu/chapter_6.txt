Introduction

Federal fraud statutes such as the mail, wire, and securities-fraud provisions criminalize schemes to deprive victims of money or property by false pretenses, representations, or promises. A critical question that recurs in prosecution and appellate practice is what counts as “money or property” for those statutes. Over decades of doctrine, the Supreme Court has constrained expansive readings that would treat virtually any wrongful interference with a victim’s economic decisionmaking as a federal property injury. The result is a property requirement that focuses litigation on traditional, legally cognizable property interests and rejects efforts to convert loss of or interference with purely informational or decisional interests into property deprivation. Understanding that doctrinal framework — the right-to-control theory that some courts once applied, the limits on intangible interests that federal fraud statutes protect, the line between valuable information and property, and the practical effects of these limits on charging, jury instructions, and appellate review — is essential before reading modern judicial opinions in this area.

# Right‑to‑control theory defined and rejected

The “right‑to‑control” theory is an interpretive approach that some courts developed to expand the scope of federal fraud statutes. Under that theory, a defendant can commit federal fraud by deceiving a victim in ways that deprive the victim of the “right to control” use of its assets — most notably by withholding or corrupting information that the victim needs to make discretionary economic decisions about its assets. For example, a government agency or business that is induced to make a selection based on false information might be said to have lost the right to control how it used its assets because it could not exercise its decisionmaking authority informedly.

Doctrinally, the right‑to‑control theory treated certain decisionmaking or informational interests — the availability of accurate, material information needed to make economic choices — as the kind of “property” that the mail and wire fraud statutes protect. It divorced protection from traditional notions of ownership or tangible property by focusing on an informational or decisional entitlement rather than the victim’s possession of, or legal title to, money or other established property forms.

Judicial reaction to the right‑to‑control theory has been critical in two respects. First, courts have emphasized that the statutory language “money or property” and the historical common‑law understanding of “fraud” anchor the statutes to protect property rights in a traditional sense. Courts have observed that when Congress criminalized schemes to “obtain money or property,” it was enacting a statute keyed to depriving victims of their proprietary interests rather than criminalizing every wrongful interference with economic agency. Second, the theory was criticized for its potential breadth: treating decisionmaking error or the loss of information as property could federalize a vast swath of garden‑variety commercial disputes and regulatory enforcement matters that historically belonged to state tort and contract law.

As a result of these concerns, contemporary Supreme Court precedent and many circuits have rejected or significantly limited the right‑to‑control theory. The modern approach requires that the interest allegedly deprived be a traditional property interest recognized at law (including certain well‑established intangible property interests), not merely the loss of or interference with potentially valuable economic information or discretionary decisionmaking capacity.

# Traditional property interests protected by federal fraud statutes

When courts assess whether a defendant’s conduct falls within the ambit of federal fraud statutes, they ask whether the scheme targeted “money or property” in the traditional sense. That inquiry focuses on two core dimensions: the object of the defendant’s scheme (did the defendant seek money or property?) and the nature of the victim’s loss (did the victim suffer a deprivation of a property interest recognized by law?).

Money and tangible property. The paradigmatic property interests protected are money, negotiable instruments, physical goods, and other tangible assets. Deceiving a victim to secure cash, take possession of physical property, or cause transfer of title or possession fits squarely within the statutes.

Established intangible property. The federal fraud statutes also protect certain intangible property interests where the item at issue is the kind of property that courts and commercial law have long treated as property. Examples include trade secrets that have independent commercial value and are protectable under state or federal law; certain contractual rights (such as enforceable payment streams or ownership interests in securities); and other recognized proprietary rights like patents and copyrights. The governing principle is whether the interest at issue has the characteristics of property — can it be owned, transferred, possessed, and commercially exploited — and whether the legal system has historically treated it as such.

Intangible “property” protected under the statutes typically has three features: it is (1) capable of exclusive control or appropriation; (2) capable of being valued and transferred; and (3) recognized as a protectable interest under existing law. If an intangible interest meets those criteria and is the object of the scheme (i.e., the defendant sought to obtain it), courts will treat it as “property” within the meaning of the federal fraud statutes.

Limits on amorphous intangibles. Not every benefit, advantage, or informational asset rises to the level of property. Courts have been skeptical about reading the statutes to cover amorphous interests such as goodwill in a very broad sense, generalized expectations of favorable regulatory treatment, or information that is valuable only because it will influence a discretionary decision. The protection is narrower: the intangible interest must be treatable as property in the traditional sense.

# Why deprivation of valuable economic information alone is insufficient

The distinction between valuable information and cognizable property is central. Valuable economic information — forecasts, internal analyses, or other data that helps a decisionmaker exercise discretion — is often crucial to economic actors, but its loss does not automatically create the sort of property deprivation the federal fraud statutes were enacted to prevent.

Conceptual reasons. Property, as the common law traditionally understands it, is about exclusive rights and control over things that can be owned and alienated. Information, by contrast, tends to be non‑rivalrous: sharing it does not necessarily exclude others from its use. Even when a piece of information is scarce or costly to obtain, its legal status depends on whether law recognizes an enforceable exclusive right in it (as with trade secrets) or whether its protection is instead a matter of tort, contract, or administrative law.

Historical and textual reasons. The statutes criminalize schemes to “obtain money or property.” The canonical interpretation reads “to defraud” and “money or property” together: the fraud must target property rights. Applying the statute to the mere loss of decision‑critical information stretches the text beyond that historical core. When courts interpret criminal statutes, they are particularly attentive to the textual boundaries that constrain prosecutorial and judicial discretion, and they are wary of vagueness and overbreadth that create prosecutorial temptation to federalize myriad economic misdeeds.

Practical reasons. If obtaining or corrupting information sufficed as property deprivation, nearly any commercial misrepresentation that affects a counterparty’s choice could trigger federal mail or wire fraud liability. That result would sweep in ordinary breaches of fiduciary duty, negligent misstatements, and complex regulatory interactions better handled through civil remedies, regulatory sanctions, or state criminal law. To avoid that expansive scope, courts require a propertylike, legally protectable interest and do not treat mere harm to decisionmaking autonomy or access to information as the invariant predicate of federal fraud.

Exceptions and borderline cases. The key to resolving borderline situations is whether the information is the gateway to a traditional property interest or whether the deception functioned to obtain an identifiable proprietary asset. When deception secures a transferable payment, title, or an enforceable contractual right, the deprivation is property‑centered even if information played a causal role. Similarly, when the deception appropriates a trade secret or misappropriates proprietary data that law protects as property, that information counts as property for fraud purposes. By contrast, false statements that merely influence the exercise of discretion — without thereby causing the transfer or deprivation of money or a recognized property interest — typically do not fall within the statutes.

# Effects on charging theories

The property requirement shapes which charging theories prosecutors may permissibly pursue.

Scheme‑to‑defraud vs. fraudulent‑inducement theories. Prosecutors have tended to frame fraud counts either as prosecuting a “scheme to deprive” (a scheme designed to take money or property away from the victim) or as prosecuting a “fraudulent inducement” (where the defendant obtains an award, contract, or payment by false pretenses). When a prosecution rests on the fraudulent‑inducement theory, the government must show that money or property was the object of the fraud — i.e., that the defendant sought to obtain money or property by deception even if the victim ultimately used or received something of value in return. If a charge is built on the supposed deprivation of the victim’s right to control assets via withholding information, prosecutors must ensure the alleged loss is a cognizable property interest, not mere informational loss.

Charging specificity and counts. Because of the property limitation, indictments must be drafted with care to allege an object of the scheme that is money or property. Pleading an abstract theory that the defendant “deprived” the victim of economic information, without alleging that the defendant obtained or sought to obtain a specific money or property interest, risks dismissal for failure to state an offense or may invite jury instructions that improperly broaden the meaning of property.

Alternative theories and overt acts. Prosecutors sometimes set out alternative theories in an indictment (for example, alleging that the defendant sought to obtain either property A or property B). Each alternative theory should satisfy the property requirement. For scheme counts that allege multiple acts over time, the government must be able to point to particular acts that form the basis for obtaining money or property. If not, the indictment may be vulnerable to a motion for a bill of particulars or a motion to dismiss as insufficiently particularized.

Parallel civil and regulatory remedies. Because information harms are often better addressed under civil law, regulators and prosecutors may coordinate charging decisions. Prosecutors mindful of the property requirement will prioritize cases where a clear property object exists (money, payments, title transfers, misappropriated proprietary data), and leave regulatory remedies, administrative sanctions, or state tort actions to address other types of informational conduct.

# Effects on jury instructions

Jury instructions in fraud cases must accurately state the elements the government must prove and guard against overbroad conceptions of “property.” Several instructional points follow from the property requirement.

Defining “money or property.” Jury instructions should define “money or property” in terms that reflect recognized property interests rather than generic economic benefits. The instruction should make clear that intangible interests are property only if they are of a kind historically recognized as property (e.g., trade secrets, enforceable contracts, proprietary business assets) and that mere loss of or interference with decisionmaking information does not suffice.

Object of the scheme. Instructions should make explicit what the government alleges the defendant sought to obtain: money, title, contractual rights, proprietary information treated as property, etc. Where the government pursues alternative theories, the jury should be instructed that it must agree on which object (if any) the defendant intended to obtain and that liability cannot rest on a theory that stretches “property” beyond its legally recognized meaning.

Materiality, reliance, and causation. While reliance is central to many civil fraud claims, federal statutes criminalizing schemes to obtain money or property do not always require proof of individualized reliance by a particular victim (depending on the theory and statutory context). Still, instructions should address materiality — whether the deception was capable of influencing the victim’s economic decisions — and causation: whether the defendant’s deception was sufficiently linked to the obtaining of the money or property. When reliance is required under the particular theory charged (for example, when the victim’s reliance is a statutory element or the fraudulent inducement theory is used), instructions must explain that the government bears the burden of proving reliance beyond a reasonable doubt.

Intent. Fraud offenses require intent to deceive and to obtain money or property. Jury instructions must clarify that the government must prove that the defendant knowingly devised or participated in a scheme to defraud and acted with the specific intent to obtain money or property. Where the government relies on generalized allegations of deception affecting decisionmaking but fails to tie those to a property object, the intent element may not be met.

Warnings against conflating harm. Instructions should caution jurors that financial disappointment, regulatory infraction, or mere unfairness in a contract dispute are not, on their own, federal fraud. The focus is on intentional deception directed at obtaining money or property or depriving the victim of a legally protected property interest.

# Effects on appellate review

The property requirement affects multiple strands of appellate review: sufficiency-of-the-evidence review, jury‑instruction challenges, vagueness and statutory‑construction claims, and the analysis of harmless error.

Sufficiency of the evidence. On sufficiency review, appellate courts ask whether, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the government, any rational trier of fact could have found the essential elements of the offense beyond a reasonable doubt. When a conviction rests on a theory that implicates the property requirement, appellate courts will assess whether the evidence supports the conclusion that the defendant sought to obtain or deprive the victim of a cognizable property interest. If the government’s proof shows only that the defendant misled a decisionmaker or withheld information but did not obtain money, title, or any legally recognized proprietary asset, an appellate court may reverse for insufficient evidence.

Jury‑instruction review. Appellate courts closely scrutinize instructions that define “property” too broadly or that permit conviction on theories the Supreme Court has rejected. If instructions allowed the jury to convict based on deprivation of mere economic information or the right to control, appellate courts may find reversible error. The standard of review for instructional error typically depends on whether the error was preserved at trial; preserved errors are reviewed for abuse of discretion and may require reversal if the error affected the defendant’s substantial rights, while unpreserved errors are reviewed for plain error.

Vagueness and due process. Expansive readings of “property” risk vagueness problems. Appellate courts will review challenges asserting that the statute, as applied, criminalizes conduct without fair notice or invites arbitrary enforcement. If a conviction is premised on an amorphous notion of property that cannot be reasonably defined, appellate courts may deem the statute unconstitutionally vague as applied.

Harmless error and prejudicial impact. When instructional or evidentiary errors related to the property requirement are raised, appellate courts assess whether the errors were harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. Even if an instruction was imperfect, courts will affirm if the record contains overwhelming evidence of a legally cognizable property deprivation and of the defendant’s intent to obtain it. Conversely, where the evidence of a property object is weak and the only plausible theory of conviction turns on an informational deprivation, error will more likely be deemed prejudicial.

Remand for clarification. When appellate courts are uncertain whether the jury convicted on a legally adequate theory, they may remand for a new trial or to permit the government to elect a proper theory if such an election is consistent with double jeopardy and due process principles. Appellate review thus plays an important gatekeeping role to ensure that federal fraud prosecutions rest on stable and historically grounded conceptions of property.

# Practical litigation implications and best practices

For prosecutors. Carefully identify and plead a concrete property object. If the government believes that information is the key to the scheme, assess whether that information is itself a recognized property interest (for example, a trade secret or proprietary database) and plead that specific theory. Avoid relying solely on the notion that the victim lost the “right to control” assets because of informational deprivation; instead, tie deception to a transfer, payment, title change, or appropriation of a protectable intangible asset.

For defense counsel. Challenge indictments that rest on amorphous informational theories for failing to allege a property object or for inviting jury confusion. Preserve instructional objections where a district court’s definition of “property” departs from the traditional property framework, and press sufficiency arguments on appeal when the proof ties no identifiable property deprivation to the defendant’s conduct.

For trial courts. Provide jurors with clear definitions that distinguish traditional property from informational or discretionary interests. Instruct jurors that they must agree on the object of the scheme and that mere impairment of decisionmaking or access to information, standing alone, is not sufficient. Be attentive to pinpointing the alleged property interest before trial and to tailoring instructions to the theory alleged.

For appellate courts. Maintain fidelity to the statutory text and common‑law roots of “money or property.” Scrutinize expansive jury instructions and curtail doctrinal drift that would federalize routine commercial disputes. When convictions rest on borderline theories, apply harmless-error analysis with care, mindful that conviction on an improper theory can have profound consequences even when the defendant gave something of value in return.

Conclusion

Federal fraud statutes protect money and property in a traditional, legally grounded sense. The right‑to‑control theory — which treated deprivation of valuable economic information or impairment of discretionary decisionmaking as property loss — expanded the statutes beyond their historical moorings and has been repudiated or constrained by higher courts. The modern framework requires proof that the defendant sought to obtain or deprive the victim of a property interest that law recognizes as such, whether tangible or a well‑established intangible like a trade secret or contractual right. This limitation influences how practitioners charge offenses, how trial courts instruct juries, and how appellate courts evaluate convictions. Understanding these doctrinal contours is essential for assessing federal fraud litigation and for distinguishing garden‑variety commercial wrongs from federal crimes.
