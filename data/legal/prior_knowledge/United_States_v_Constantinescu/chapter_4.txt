Introduction

The mail and wire fraud statutes, 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341 and 1343, form the backbone of federal fraud prosecution. Over many decades courts have developed a coherent body of doctrine defining what it means to perpetrate a "scheme to defraud," what kinds of representations or nondisclosures are actionable, how the government's burden differs from the civil fraud context, and how concepts of "property" and "intangible rights" are treated. Those doctrinal lines are now routinely relied upon to interpret and apply other federal fraud provisions, including statutes that target financial institution fraud such as 18 U.S.C. § 1348. A law student preparing to read judicial opinions that construe § 1348 therefore needs a firm grasp of the core principles underlying §§ 1341 and 1343.

This chapter explains the elements of the mail and wire fraud offenses, the concept of a "scheme to defraud" and the requirement that a scheme seek to obtain money or property, the treatment of affirmative misrepresentations and nondisclosures (including half-truths), how courts distinguish property-based theories from intangible-rights theories (including the honest-services limitation), the significance of the absence of a reliance or actual-loss requirement in criminal fraud prosecutions, and the ways courts borrow and adapt mail/wire fraud precedents when construing statutes such as § 1348.

# Elements of 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341 and 1343

The statutes criminalize similar conduct: use of the mails (18 U.S.C. § 1341) or interstate wire communications (18 U.S.C. § 1343) in furtherance of a fraudulent scheme. A straightforward articulation of the elements—sufficient for most doctrinal purposes—is:

1. A scheme or artifice to defraud, or to obtain money or property by means of false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises;
2. Specific intent to defraud; and
3. Use of the mails (for § 1341) or interstate wires (for § 1343) in furtherance of the scheme.

Each element has developed doctrinal subcomponents.

First, the "scheme" element encompasses any plan, strategy, or course of conduct with a false or fraudulent objective. Schemes can be executed through misrepresentations, omissions, deceptive practices, or other artful contrivances. Courts analyze whether the charged course of conduct, taken as a whole, had a fraudulent object.

Second, the mental-state element—intent to defraud—requires proof that the defendant acted with specific intent to deceive or to wrongfully obtain something of value. In federal criminal fraud law "specific intent to defraud" is a demanding standard: proof must show the defendant knowingly devised or participated in a plan to deprive the victim of money or property, or to secure it by deception. Recklessness or negligence is not enough. The mens rea must be proved beyond a reasonable doubt like any other element.

Third, the jurisdictional or "use of mails/wires" element is satisfied when the prosecution shows that the defendant caused or reasonably could have foreseen the use of postal or interstate wire facilities in furtherance of the scheme. The required mailing or wire transfer need not itself contain a fraudulent statement nor be the primary mechanism by which loss occurred; a mailing/wire that is incident to an essential part of the scheme suffices. Temporal proximity between the scheme and the mailing/wire is required in practice—the use must be "in furtherance of" rather than merely tangential.

Finally, the government must demonstrate causation in the sense that the mailing or wire enabled or advanced the fraudulent plan, not that the mailing/wire was the proximate cause of some pecuniary loss. The prophylactic character of these statutes means courts allow fairly attenuated uses as sufficient, so long as they furthered the scheme.

# Scheme to Defraud and Obtaining Money or Property

"Scheme to defraud" is the conceptual nucleus of mail and wire fraud. It covers two complementary categories of federal fraud charges: (1) schemes that deprive a person of money or property, and (2) schemes that obtain money or property by false pretenses, representations, or promises. The difference is subtle but important: the first emphasizes the deprivation of a victim's property interests; the second emphasizes the obtaining of property through misrepresentations. In practice, prosecutions often allege both theories.

To ground a mail or wire fraud charge, the object of the scheme must ordinarily be money or property. "Money" is self-explanatory; "property" is more capacious and has been the focus of much doctrinal development. Courts generally require that the defendant sought to obtain a legally cognizable property interest—something of value that the law recognizes as property. The object can be tangible or intangible, but courts have established limits (discussed below) on what counts as property, particularly when the alleged "property" is an abstract economic interest such as a right to fair dealing.

Prosecutors typically frame a fraud claim by identifying a specific transfer or withholding of money or property. For example, the scheme may be aimed at diverting funds, inducing payments, securing loans, obtaining property titles, or causing a bank or investor to surrender property. The government does not need to show that the scheme succeeded; an attempt suffices if the other elements are proved. Thus, an unsuccessful effort to induce payment through misrepresentation can still be punishable.

Courts evaluating the "obtain money or property" requirement focus on whether the deception related to a tangible transfer of wealth or a definable proprietary interest. If the defendant's misconduct only manipulated expectations or induced bad bargains without creating a separate, identifiable property right, courts may reject a property theory and instead consider whether an honest-services theory applies (if at all). The boundary between cognizable property and impermissibly broad economic expectations is often litigated.

# Material Misrepresentations, Omissions, and Half-Truths

Fraud can be effected by affirmative false statements, by omissions, or by half-truths—statements that are literally true but misleading because they omit material facts or give an incomplete impression. The doctrinal treatment of these different modes of deception balances two principles: a desire to punish fraudulent deceptions that cause wrongful transfers of property, and a concern to avoid criminalizing ordinary business puffery or purely subjective statements of opinion.

Material misrepresentations. A misrepresentation is material if it has a natural tendency to influence, or is capable of influencing, the decision maker. Courts employ an objective materiality standard: would a reasonable person consider the fact misrepresented important in making the relevant business decision? This is not a subjective test of the particular victim's reliance; rather, it asks whether the misrepresentation was of a kind that could affect the transactional calculus. Materiality is an element that the government must prove beyond a reasonable doubt.

Omissions and half-truths. Not all lies are verbalized; sometimes deceit is accomplished by silence or by telling only part of the truth. Liability for omissions requires that the defendant had a duty to disclose the omitted fact and that the omission caused the victim to act in a way that yielded the defendant an improper advantage. Duties to disclose can arise from statutory obligations, fiduciary relationships, preexisting agreements, or where the defendant's statements are literally true but misleading without further disclosure. In practice, courts focus on whether the omission rendered other statements deceptive—if a defendant makes a representation that only tells part of the truth, the law treats the omission that makes the representation misleading as an integral part of the fraudulent scheme.

Half-truths repeat a recurrent theme in fraud doctrine. A half-truth is an utterance that contains accurate factual statements but omits material facts necessary to prevent the overall communication from being misleading. Courts evaluate a half-truth by looking at the totality of the communication and asking whether, considered as a whole, it would mislead a reasonable recipient. If so, the half-truth is treated as a false representation for purposes of the mail and wire fraud statutes.

Causation and reliance. In criminal mail and wire fraud prosecutions, the government does not always need to show that a particular victim actually relied on a misrepresentation; it is sufficient that the misrepresentation was material and that the scheme was designed to recruit victims to transfer money or property. That said, proof that the misrepresentation induced the victim's actions is strong evidence of intent to defraud, and in many cases actual inducement is part of the government's proof.

Mens rea with respect to statements. The government must prove that the defendant knowingly made the false statement or knowingly concealed the material fact, and that the defendant did so with the intent to cause the victim to part with money or property. Negligent misstatements or innocent omissions lacking fraudulent intent do not satisfy the mens rea element.

# Property Versus Intangible-Rights Theories; Honest-Services Limits

One of the most litigated issues in mail and wire fraud law is the distinction between property-based theories and intangible-rights theories—especially the "honest services" theory. Understanding this distinction is crucial because courts treat these theories differently when defining what constitutes "property" for purposes of the statutes.

Property-based theories. Under a property-based theory, the government must show that the defendant intended to obtain money or property, or to deprive another of money or property. The "property" at issue may be tangible (money, goods, title to property) or an identifiable intangible property interest (such as contractual rights or proprietary data). Courts have allowed intangible property to qualify where the interest is clearly definable and traditionally recognized as property in the economic sense.

For example, rights that are created or transferred by contract—such as the right to receive payments under a loan agreement—are readily treated as property. Similarly, confidential business information or trade secrets can, in many contexts, qualify as property because they have clear commercial value and legal protection.

Intangible-rights theories and honest services. An intangible-rights theory of mail or wire fraud seeks to punish schemes that deprive victims of nonproprietary intangible benefits—most notably, an employee's or official's "right to honest services" from agents, employees, or public officials. Under this theory, the government argues that a public official's or private agent's undisclosed bribe-taking or self-dealing defrauds the public or principal of its intangible right to honest, faithful service.

Courts have treated honest-services prosecutions with caution because such an expansive reading risks criminalizing a broad range of ordinary business or political conduct. To avoid overbreadth, courts often require a limiting principle: the intangible-rights theory will only sustain criminal liability when the scheme involves classical forms of corruption such as bribes or kickbacks, or where statutory language expressly contemplates such deprivation. In other words, honest-services liability typically requires proof of a bribe or kickback, or something functionally equivalent.

The line between property and intangible rights matters because the mail and wire fraud statutes penalize schemes to obtain "money or property." If the government seeks to rely on the intangible-rights theory absent a clear statutory hook, courts may reject the prosecution as exceeding the statute's scope. Conversely, if the intangible interest at stake resembles an identifiable property right—such as contractual rights to payment—courts are more likely to treat it as property for mail/wire fraud purposes.

Limitations and concerns. The primary concern courts express in this area is avoiding federal criminalization of garden-variety unethical conduct that lacks the hallmarks of corruption. As a result, when the government frames a scheme as a deprivation of an intangible right, courts insist on clear evidence that the defendant accepted something of value in exchange for breaching a duty, or otherwise engaged in conduct traditionally understood to be a paradigmatic form of fraud.

# No Reliance or Actual Loss Requirement in Criminal Fraud

An important distinction between criminal fraud law and civil fraud doctrines is the absence, in many criminal prosecutions, of a requirement that the victim actually relied on the defendant's misrepresentations or actually suffered a pecuniary loss. This contrasts with common-law civil fraud where reliance and resulting damages are typically elements of the plaintiff's case.

No requirement of actual reliance. In criminal mail and wire fraud cases, the government need not prove that each victim actually relied upon the false statement or omission. Instead, it is sufficient to show that the scheme was designed to deceive and that the misrepresentations were material and capable of influencing the class of potential victims. This doctrine recognizes that the danger created by a fraudulent scheme justifies punishment even if particular victims were skeptical or the scheme ultimately failed.

The absence of a reliance requirement lowers the government's evidentiary burden in prosecuting complex, multi-target frauds, where it would be impractical to prove individualized reliance for each intended victim. However, evidence of actual reliance remains relevant insofar as it helps establish intent to defraud and makes proof of the scheme's objective more concrete.

No requirement of actual pecuniary loss. Criminal liability does not necessarily depend on proof that a victim suffered a concrete monetary loss. An attempted scheme to obtain money or property can yield criminal liability even if no money changed hands. The statutory focus is on the wrongful object and the defendant's intent, not on the actual financial consequences.

Practical consequences. Although neither reliance nor actual loss is a required element, both are often central to the government's proof and to sentencing or restitution determinations if a conviction occurs. For sentencing and restitution, courts will assess actual loss to determine the seriousness of the offense and appropriate punishment. Consequently, defendants are frequently motivated to litigate factual issues related to inducement and loss even when those concepts are not elements of the offense.

# Doctrinal Cross-Pollination: Using §1341/§1343 Precedents to Interpret §1348

18 U.S.C. § 1348 criminalizes fraud against financial institutions and was enacted to address schemes that exploit or damage banks and similar entities. Because § 1348 is a more specialized statute but shares core language and criminal purposes with §§ 1341 and 1343, courts often import doctrinal principles from the mail and wire fraud corpus when construing § 1348. Understanding how that cross-pollination operates helps predict the application of § 1348 and similar statutes.

Shared elements and interpretive practices. § 1348 typically has elements that parallel mail and wire fraud: a scheme to defraud, intent to defraud, and a protected victim class (financial institutions). Courts look to the rich jurisprudence of §§ 1341 and 1343 to resolve ambiguities about materiality, the nature of the property interest at stake, and the sufficiency of the government's proof of intent and use of a facility in furtherance of the scheme. For instance, courts construe "scheme to defraud" with the same core understanding: deception, design to obtain property, and specific intent.

Property and intangible-rights analogies. When § 1348 requires that the defendant sought to obtain money or property from a financial institution, courts use mail/wire fraud doctrine to determine whether the government has identified a legally cognizable property interest of the institution. The mail/wire cases provide tests for deciding whether an alleged interest is a concrete property right or an abstract expectation. Likewise, where the government pressures that a financial institution's intangible rights (like honest services) were violated, courts apply the mail/wire fraud limitations to avoid expansive readings that would criminalize ordinary financial-sector conduct.

Materiality and omissions. Courts applying § 1348 borrow the objective materiality framework developed in mail/wire fraud cases when evaluating allegedly deceptive statements or omissions that induced a bank to act. Thus, a misrepresentation to a bank is actionable under § 1348 only if it is material in the sense described above. Omissions and half-truths are treated similarly: if an agent's partial disclosures would reasonably influence a bank's decision, those partial disclosures are material and may support criminal liability when combined with the requisite intent.

Reliance, loss, and proof burdens. § 1348 prosecutions also reflect the mail/wire principle that neither actual reliance by the financial institution nor actual loss is necessary to prove the offense. An attempted scheme to obtain money or property from a bank can suffice, and the government's burden of proof focuses on the defendant's fraudulent intent and the materiality of the deception rather than post-hoc measures of loss. At sentencing and restitution phases, however, courts will assess actual loss as required by the sentencing guidelines and restitution statutes.

Avoiding overbreadth through limiting principles. Because financial institutions are broad, diverse, and central to commerce, courts are attentive to concerns about overcriminalization when applying § 1348. They therefore import from mail/wire fraud doctrine the limiting principles that prevent conversion of routine mistakes, poor business judgment, or garden-variety breaches of contract into federal crimes. Requiring a specific intent to defraud, objective materiality, and a definable property interest helps ensure that § 1348 targets classic, culpable frauds rather than innocuous errors.

Procedural posture effects on analysis. How courts apply mail/wire doctrines to § 1348 depends on procedural posture. At the indictment stage, prosecutors can plead schemes with reasonable detail, but courts will scrutinize whether the indictment alleges the required mens rea and a cognizable property interest. At the summary-judgment or motion-to-dismiss stages, courts assess whether the alleged facts, even if taken as true, could show a scheme to obtain money or property and whether the statements alleged were materially deceptive. At trial, the government must prove intent and materiality beyond a reasonable doubt. On appeal, courts often review legal questions of statutory interpretation de novo while reviewing sufficiency-of-the-evidence claims under the familiar standards that require careful consideration of whether the jury's verdict is supported by substantial evidence.

Conclusion

A principled understanding of mail and wire fraud doctrine is essential to comprehending how courts approach statutes such as § 1348. Core concepts—what constitutes a scheme to defraud, what counts as money or property, how courts treat misrepresentations, omissions, and half-truths, and why criminal fraud does not require proof of actual reliance or loss—recur across federal fraud jurisprudence. When judges confront § 1348 issues, they draw heavily on the settled rules developed under §§ 1341 and 1343, while adapting them to the specific context of financial-institution protection and the safeguards against overbroad prosecution. Mastery of these foundational doctrines will equip the student to analyze how courts evaluate allegations, assess evidentiary sufficiency, and delineate the boundaries of criminal fraud in the financial sector.
