Introduction

Federal criminal securities fraud is principally codified at 18 U.S.C. § 1348. Congress enacted § 1348 to create a specialized federal criminal offense targeted at frauds involving securities and other financial instruments. While it borrows concepts familiar from the civil securities laws, particularly Rule 10b-5 under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, § 1348 functions in the criminal law context, with the attendant requirements of mens rea, prosecutorial charging decisions, and constitutional protections afforded to criminal defendants. A working understanding of § 1348’s statutory structure, its constituent elements, the doctrinal standards that govern materiality and the required nexus to securities transactions, and the contours of available defenses — especially good faith — is essential before reading judicial opinions that apply the statute to particular fact patterns.

# Statutory text and structure of § 1348(1) and § 1348(2)

Section 1348 creates two primary subsections addressing different types of securities-related fraudulent conduct. The statute is deliberately broad and covers both schemes that defraud in connection with securities transactions and the making of false statements, representations, or promises intended to influence securities-related transactions.

Although the precise statutory language may be updated from time to time, the statute commonly is organized as two clauses: one that criminalizes devising or executing a scheme to defraud in connection with the purchase or sale of a security, and a second that criminalizes making materially false or misleading statements, omissions, or promises in connection with such purchases or sales. Carefully parsing these components permits courts and practitioners to map distinct categories of conduct onto the statute.

Subsection (1) typically targets schemes: the planning, initiating, or carrying out of a scheme or artifice to defraud investors or purchasers in connection with the purchase or sale of securities. The gravamen is the fraudulent scheme itself. Prosecutors charging under this subsection often proceed on evidence of an overall plan, deceptive practices, or a pattern of conduct designed to cheat investors or manipulate market behavior.

Subsection (2) typically targets affirmative misstatements or omissions: knowingly making materially false statements or engaging in omissions or promises intended to deceive or influence investors in connection with the purchase or sale of securities. Under this clause, the focus is on the defendant’s communicative act — the statement, omission, or promise — and whether it was false, material, and made with the requisite culpable state of mind.

Although the two subsections overlap and often are charged together, they emphasize different facets of fraudulent conduct: the overall fraudulent scheme versus specific deceptive communications. Each subsection requires attention to the same core elements — a scheme or statement, materiality, the in-connection-with nexus to securities transactions, and intent — but the manner in which those elements are proven and applied varies with the statutory language and the factual predicate.

# Elements: scheme to defraud; material misrepresentation or omission; in-connection-with requirement; intent to defraud

Prosecuting a § 1348 offense requires the government to prove multiple elements beyond a reasonable doubt. The principal elements commonly are: (1) the existence of a scheme or an act (such as a misrepresentation or omission) to defraud; (2) materiality of the misrepresentation or omission (or the scheme’s deceptive nature); (3) a sufficient “in connection with” nexus to the purchase or sale of a security; and (4) the requisite mens rea, typically intent to defraud (scienter). Each element entails doctrinal tests and evidentiary approaches courts use to analyze factual claims.

Scheme to defraud. When the statute is charged under its scheme provision, the government must show that the defendant engaged in a deliberate plan, design, or pattern intended to deceive or cheat victims. A “scheme” is not a formal document or single statement; it can be an orchestrated set of actions (misrepresentations, omissions, misappropriations, trades timed to exploit deception) that together operate to mislead. Courts examine whether the actions were deceitful in character and had the purpose of causing financial loss to victims or wrongfully obtaining money or property. A scheme charge does not require the defendant to have executed every overt act necessary to succeed; rather, it requires proof that the defendant knowingly participated in or executed a plan to deceive.

Material misrepresentation or omission. When the statute is charged as a false statement offense, the government must prove that the defendant made a misrepresentation or omission that was materially false or misleading. A misrepresentation is an affirmative false statement; an omission is the failure to disclose information where there is a duty to disclose or where the omission would make affirmative statements misleading. Materiality and falsity are analytically distinct: falsity concerns whether the assertion was factually wrong or misleading; materiality concerns whether a reasonable investor would consider the information important in making an investment decision. The government must typically show both that a statement or omission was false or misleading and that it was material.

In-connection-with requirement. A distinctive feature of § 1348 is that the fraudulent conduct must be “in connection with” the purchase or sale of a security. This is a nexus requirement tying the defendant’s conduct to a securities transaction. Courts interpret this requirement to encompass a broad range of relationships between the fraud and the securities transaction: the fraud need not be integrated into the transaction documents nor must it be contemporaneous with the trade; rather, the deception need only have been sufficiently related to the trading activity or have been intended to influence the decision to buy or sell a security. The “in connection with” requirement ensures the statute targets securities-related harms, rather than frauds with an incidental or tangential connection to securities markets.

Intent to defraud (scienter). Criminal liability under § 1348 requires proof of a culpable state of mind. The government must establish that the defendant acted with intent to deceive, manipulate, or defraud. This mens rea is stricter than mere negligence. Depending on the charging theory, courts evaluate whether the defendant knowingly made false statements, acted with reckless disregard for the truth, or engaged in conduct the defendant knew would likely mislead investors. In criminal cases, prosecutors must usually prove specific intent to defraud; generalized negligence-based theories are often insufficient. The precise standard can vary with the alleged conduct (scheme versus statement) and the jurisdiction’s interpretive approach to scienter in securities crimes.

# Materiality: reasonable investor standard

Materiality is central in both civil and criminal securities law, but its doctrinal contours are similar across the two regimes. Materiality asks whether a reasonable investor would have considered the information important in deciding whether to buy or sell a security. Courts commonly articulate a test that examines whether a statement or omission would have assumed actual significance in the deliberations of the reasonable investor, i.e., whether it could have been viewed by a reasonable investor as having significantly altered the total mix of information made available.

The reasonable investor standard is objective: it does not depend on the subjective views of a particular individual investor but on how a hypothetical reasonable investor would view the information. Evidence of actual market reaction (for example, price movement following disclosure) can be powerful circumstantial proof of materiality, but it is not required. Materiality is contextual: whether a given fact is material depends on the circumstances in which the particular investment decision is made, including the kind of security, the market conditions, the investor’s access to information, and what constitutes the “total mix” of information available at the time.

Factual assertions and omissions interact with materiality in predictable ways. Affirmative falsehoods about key financial metrics, business prospects, or regulatory compliance are likely material. Omissions may be material where disclosure is required by law or where the omitted fact would have rendered prior disclosures materially misleading. Courts also recognize gradations of materiality: trivial inaccuracies are not material, while systematic misstatements that consistently embellish performance or hide liabilities are more plausibly material to the reasonable investor.

In criminal prosecutions, materiality is often contested. Defendants may argue that the alleged falsehoods were immaterial puffery or that the omitted information was not of the kind that a reasonable investor would have considered significant. The government bears the burden of proving materiality beyond a reasonable doubt, and judges often give jury instructions that articulate the objective reasonable-investor standard and its application to the facts of the case.

# The “in connection with” nexus to securities transactions

The “in connection with” element ties a defendant’s fraudulent conduct to the purchase or sale of a security. Unlike statutes that require specific transactions to be completed, the in-connection-with phrase is broadly interpreted to capture conduct that has a sufficient relation to securities trading. Courts employ several analytical approaches to determine whether the requisite nexus is present.

Causal or influence-based approach. One approach asks whether the defendant’s deception was intended to influence, and did in fact have the potential to influence, the decision to buy or sell securities. Under this view, communications to investors, analysts, rating agencies, or other market participants are in connection with a purchase or sale if they are reasonably calculated to affect market participants’ investment decisions. The effect need not be actualized—what matters is the reasonable connection between the deceptive conduct and the investment decision.

Transaction-focused approach. Another approach examines whether the fraudulent conduct was linked to an identifiable securities transaction or series of transactions. This is often satisfied when the defendant makes misrepresentations to induce purchases or sales by specific victims or when the deception is integrated into the circumstances of an offering, private placement, or takeover. Here the nexus is satisfied by showing the deception was part of the transaction’s context.

Temporal and geographic breadth. Importantly, “in connection with” does not require simultaneity. A statement made before, during, or after a trade can be deemed in connection with that trade if there is a sufficient relationship. Similarly, the relationship can cross jurisdictions; the national or international character of trading does not defeat the nexus so long as the deceptive conduct is tied to securities transactions that are within federal reach.

Limitations. Not all frauds that touch the securities world meet the in-connection-with test. If the fraud targets unrelated commercial dealings, employment disputes, or consumer transactions with only incidental effects on securities markets, the nexus may be lacking. The government must show more than a mere tangential relationship; courts will scrutinize whether the deceptive conduct was integrally related to investment decisions or securities trading. Because this element preserves the statute’s focus on securities fraud, it is frequently central in pretrial challenges and jury instructions.

# Comparisons and contrasts with SEC Rule 10b-5 (civil vs. criminal; overlap and differences)

Rule 10b-5, promulgated under Section 10(b) of the Securities Exchange Act, is the principal civil anti-fraud provision in federal securities law. It proscribes fraud in connection with the purchase or sale of securities by making it unlawful to (a) employ any device to defraud, (b) make any untrue statement of material fact or omit a material fact necessary to make statements not misleading, or (c) engage in any act or practice that operates as a fraud or deceit. Comparing § 1348 and Rule 10b-5 helps illuminate both overlap and divergence between civil and criminal securities enforcement.

Substantive overlap. The core prohibitions are similar: both target deceptive schemes and materially false or misleading communications in connection with securities transactions. Many of the doctrinal concepts—materiality, the reasonable investor standard, and the in-connection-with nexus—derive from the same body of securities jurisprudence and are applied in similar ways. Thus, a set of underlying facts that support a civil 10b-5 claim may also support criminal charging under § 1348.

Mens rea differences. A key difference is the required state of mind. Civil 10b-5 claims brought by private plaintiffs typically require proof of scienter—commonly meaning intent to deceive, manipulate, or defraud—or at least severe recklessness, depending on the claim and remedies pursued. However, courts sometimes permit liability based on recklessness in private actions. Criminal liability under § 1348 typically requires proof of specific criminal intent (knowingly or intentionally committing fraud), and the standard for prosecutorial proof is higher because of the constitutional stakes implicated in criminal prosecutions. Consequently, some conduct that might be actionable civilly under a negligence or recklessness theory may not meet the higher mens rea threshold required for a criminal conviction.

Remedies and procedural posture. Rule 10b-5 actions are typically civil, brought by the SEC or private plaintiffs seeking remedies such as injunctions, disgorgement, restitution, and damages under Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5. The burdens of proof are lower (preponderance of the evidence in civil cases), and procedural rules for discovery and remedies differ. Section 1348 is criminal; prosecution can result in imprisonment and criminal fines, and the government must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt. Criminal cases also implicate heightened constitutional protections for defendants, such as the Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination, Sixth Amendment rights to counsel and jury trial, and the requirement of proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

Elements and defenses. The frameworks for establishing elements overlap, but certain doctrines are emphasized differently. For example, private civil plaintiffs typically must prove reliance (that the plaintiff relied on the defendant’s misrepresentation), although there are doctrines like fraud-on-the-market that can establish a rebuttable presumption of reliance in efficient markets. In criminal prosecutions, direct proof of individual reliance by a particular victim is generally not required; the government need only prove the in-connection-with nexus and the defendant’s intent. Defenses also diverge: good-faith and lack of scienter are available in both regimes, but defendants in criminal cases will often press these defenses more vigorously because the stakes are higher.

Enforcement actors. The SEC enforces civil law and can refer matters to the Department of Justice for criminal enforcement. The DOJ brings criminal cases under § 1348. The coordination between civil and criminal enforcement can lead to parallel proceedings, but the separate legal standards and remedies mean that identical facts can yield different outcomes in civil and criminal fora.

# Good-faith defense and limits (e.g., puffery vs. false statements)

Good faith is a central defense in securities fraud prosecutions. Because criminal liability generally requires specific intent to defraud, a defendant who genuinely and reasonably believed their statements to be true, or believed they lacked an intent to defraud, may escape criminal culpability. Good faith can function as either an affirmative defense or as negative evidence that the government has not proved scienter beyond a reasonable doubt.

Nature of the defense. Good faith is both subjective and objective. Subjectively, the defendant must have believed the truth of their statements or the absence of an intent to deceive. Objectively, however, the belief must often be reasonable when evaluated in light of the facts known to the defendant. Courts will examine the defendant’s state of mind, contemporaneous records, communications, and steps taken (or not taken) to verify information. A bona fide mistake, a reasonable business judgment, or reliance on professional advisors can support a good-faith defense.

Puffery versus materially false statements. Not all statements of optimistic opinion or promotional hyperbole constitute actionable misrepresentations. “Puffery” — vague, subjective expressions of optimism or belief about future prospects — is generally considered non-actionable because it is not the kind of factual, objectively verifiable representation on which a reasonable investor would rely. Examples include generalized statements that a company has a “bright future” or that it is “well positioned.” By contrast, specific factual assertions (e.g., about past earnings, sales numbers, regulatory compliance, or the existence of material contracts) that are false or misleading may be actionable. Courts distinguish puffery from false factual claims by asking whether the representation is capable of objective verification and whether it alters the total mix of information available to investors.

Limits of good faith. Good faith does not protect defendants who act recklessly or with deliberate ignorance. Recklessness — an extreme departure from the standards of ordinary care that presents a danger of misleading buyers or sellers — can supply the necessary scienter in some civil contexts and can defeat a claim of good faith in criminal cases when the evidence shows the defendant disregarded facts that would have revealed the untruth. Willful blindness — intentionally avoiding verifying inconvenient facts — similarly undermines a good-faith claim in criminal prosecutions.

Affirmative burden and thrust in trial. Typically, the government bears the burden of proving criminal intent beyond a reasonable doubt; the defendant need not shoulder the burden of proof for good faith. However, defendants often present evidence of good faith to cast doubt on the prosecution’s proof of scienter. In some jurisdictions, once the defendant raises the issue, the court may give jury instructions explaining how a reasonable belief in the truth of one’s statements negates the required criminal intent. Where statutory language or jury instructions create an affirmative defense that must be proved by the defendant by a preponderance of the evidence, prosecutors and defense counsel must be attentive to the statutory allocation of burdens and associated constitutional constraints.

Practical limits and example distinctions. It is one thing to defend a company executive who made an honest forecasting mistake; it is another to defend an executive who fabricated invoices, doctored books, or systematically concealed adverse information while outwardly representing that the business was performing well. The former scenario is more amenable to a successful good-faith defense; the latter is not. Similarly, reliance on outside accountants or counsel may support a good-faith claim if the reliance was reasonable and the defendant furnished complete and accurate information to the advisers. Conversely, concealing known adverse facts from advisers or failing to exercise minimal due diligence will undercut such a defense.

# Procedural posture and evidentiary considerations

Understanding how procedural posture affects the analysis is critical. At the charging stage, prosecutors decide whether to bring felony or misdemeanor counts and whether to pursue charges under § 1348 or other statutes. Pretrial motions often target the sufficiency of the indictment (whether the charging instrument adequately alleges the statutory elements), the “in connection with” nexus, and whether pretrial discovery can obtain privileged materials such as counsel communications or internal deliberations.

At trial, the government must prove each element beyond a reasonable doubt. Jury instructions about elements like materiality, scienter, and the in-connection-with requirement are commonly contested, and appellate courts scrutinize whether instructions properly conveyed the necessary standards (for example, whether the jury was told to apply the reasonable investor test for materiality). Defendants may seek dismissal under Rule 29 post-verdict motions arguing insufficient evidence of scienter or nexus; appellate review typically applies a substantial-evidence standard, asking whether any rational jury could have found the elements beyond a reasonable doubt.

Sentencing consequences also shape litigation strategy. Criminal convictions under § 1348 carry the prospect of incarceration, fines, and restitution; defendants therefore may weigh plea bargains, cooperation, and mitigation. The interplay between civil SEC enforcement and criminal DOJ actions can influence prosecutorial charging decisions, discovery battles, and defense tactics. For example, parallel SEC investigations may influence whether the government pursues criminal charges and what remedies or restitutive calculations are sought.

# Conclusion

Section 1348 criminalizes fraudulent schemes and materially false statements in connection with securities transactions. While it shares many doctrinal touchstones with civil securities law under Rule 10b-5 — materiality, the reasonable-investor standard, and the in-connection-with nexus — its criminal character imposes heightened mens rea and proof requirements, as well as constitutional protections for defendants. Understanding the statute’s structure, the elements that the government must prove, how courts evaluate materiality and nexus, and the contours of the good-faith defense equips a reader to analyze judicial opinions that apply § 1348 to concrete fact patterns. As with all fraud statutes, the particular facts — the nature of the statements at issue, the defendant’s state of mind, and the temporal and causal relationship to securities transactions — will determine liability under the statute.
