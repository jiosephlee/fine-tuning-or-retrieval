Introduction

This chapter explains the key Fifth Circuit guideposts that govern prosecutions under 18 U.S.C. §1348 (securities fraud) and clarify how courts define and apply the substantive elements shared across federal fraud statutes. Fifth Circuit doctrine draws heavily on lines of mail- and wire-fraud jurisprudence, and the circuit has articulated recognizable frameworks for the “scheme to defraud” element, the intent required to commit fraud, and the nature of the property interest that the statutes protect. Understanding these doctrinal contours is essential for reading and briefing §1348 opinions because they determine which factual patterns qualify as securities fraud, how courts evaluate sufficiency of evidence, how indictments must be pleaded, and what jury instructions are legally adequate.

This chapter proceeds in four parts. First it describes the Fifth Circuit’s approach to importing mail-and-wire fraud concepts into §1348 and the elemental framework judges apply. Second it analyzes how courts characterize a “scheme to defraud” and the requisite intent to defraud—particularly the dual aspects of intent to deceive and intent to cause some legally cognizable harm. Third it treats the Fifth Circuit’s teaching that fraud liability does not require a literal, mirror‑image net pecuniary loss to the victim, explaining why obtaining money or property by deception can be sufficient even when the ostensible victim receives some value in return. Finally, the chapter sets out concrete implications of these principles for pleading, proof, and jury instructions in fraud prosecutions.

# Greenlaw: importing mail/wire fraud concepts into §1348; elemental framework

The Fifth Circuit treats §1348 as a specialized application of the broader mail- and wire-fraud jurisprudence rather than as a free‑standing, novel offense with wholly different elements. Section 1348—criminalizing schemes to defraud investors or to obtain money or property by false or fraudulent pretenses in connection with the purchase or sale of securities—is read through the established structure courts use for mail and wire fraud: an underlying “scheme to defraud” and use of an instrumentality of interstate commerce (the wires, mail, or, here, securities markets). That analogy imposes two practical consequences.

First, courts borrow the familiar tripartite elements that the mail/wire-fraud caselaw has long required: (1) a scheme or artifice to defraud, or to obtain money or property by false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises; (2) the defendant’s intent to defraud; and (3) a nexus between the scheme and the particular commerce or instrumentality (for §1348, the use of means or conduct in connection with securities transactions). Treating §1348 through this lens preserves doctrinal continuity: the same substantive inquiries—whether the conduct constitutes a “scheme to defraud” and whether the defendant acted with the requisite intent—inform analyses under §1348 as under §§1341–1343.

Second, the borrowing approach imports limiting principles developed in mail/wire cases. Those principles include the requirement that federal fraud statutes target wrongs against property or property-like interests (as reflected in Supreme Court precedents that emphasize protection of traditional property), while also recognizing theories—such as fraudulent inducement—under which the object of the fraud is obtaining money or property even if the victim receives something of value in exchange. In short, the Fifth Circuit’s importation balances protection of property interests against doctrinal caution about judicial expansion of the statutes beyond their textual scope.

In applying this elemental framework, district judges and juries typically parse the “scheme” question into objective and contextual inquiries: what misrepresentations or concealments are alleged, whether they were material, and whether they were part of an overall plan to obtain money or property. The intent inquiry—separately analyzed—asks whether the defendant acted knowingly and with a culpable state of mind sufficient to effectuate the scheme.

# Evans: defining scheme to defraud and intent to defraud (intent to deceive and cause harm)

Scheme to defraud

Within the Fifth Circuit, a “scheme to defraud” subsumes conduct by which the defendant devised or executed a plan to deceive another person or entity to obtain money, property, or an economic advantage. The inquiry is pragmatic and fact‑sensitive. Courts look to whether the challenged conduct involved affirmative false statements, half‑truths, or deliberate concealments and whether those communications were part of a plan designed to mislead the victim about facts that the law recognizes as material to the victim’s economic decisionmaking.

Materiality is a threshold component of the scheme analysis. A misrepresentation is material if it has a natural tendency to influence, or is capable of influencing, the decision of the decisionmaker to whom it was addressed. Materiality is ordinarily a question for the jury unless reasonable minds could not differ. In securities cases, materiality also invokes an objective standard tied to the reasonable investor or the reasonable market actor: would a reasonable investor have considered the misstated or omitted information important in deciding whether to buy, sell, or hold securities, or in pricing those securities?

The scheme analysis also accounts for the relationship between the fraudulent act and the defendant’s objective. Courts examine whether alleged deceptive statements were incidental misstatements or whether they were functionally indispensable to executing the broader plan to obtain money or property. The presence of a systematic pattern of misrepresentations, cover-ups, or manipulative shipping/accounting practices is often persuasive evidence of a scheme rather than isolated error.

Intent to defraud

The Fifth Circuit conceptualizes intent to defraud as a mens rea with two complementary components: (1) intent to deceive and (2) intent to cause harm by depriving another of money or property (or by wronging a property interest recognized under the statute). The first component—intent to deceive—requires that the defendant knowingly made, or caused to be made, false statements or omissions with awareness of their falsity or with reckless disregard for the truth. Knowledge, recklessness, and deliberate falsity are frequently probative of this element.

The second component—intent to cause harm—focuses on the actor’s goal to effectuate a legally cognizable deprivation. That is, the defendant must intend that the deception result in the victim’s loss of money, property, or a property-like right, or that the defendant obtains money or property by means of the deception. The harm may be immediate or prospective and may take several legally recognized forms: conversion or transfer of title; wrongful diversion of funds; unauthorized procurement of contracts or assets; or induced transfer of value premised on false pretenses.

Both components are assessed in light of the surrounding circumstances. Courts accept multiple forms of proof of intent: direct evidence (statements of intent), circumstantial evidence (the defendant’s course of conduct, concealment, or pattern of misrepresentations), and inferences from motive and opportunity. Importantly, the intent element is not satisfied merely by negligent misstatements or innocent mistakes; the government must carry its burden to show that the defendant acted with the requisite culpable state of mind.

Operationalizing “intent to cause harm” often requires attention to the target of the deception and the object sought. For example, where the scheme aims to induce a counterparty to make a payment, the government must show that the defendant intended to obtain that payment through deception—even if other non-fraudulent benefits might also accrue to the counterparty. Conversely, where the defendant claims a business justification, courts weigh that evidence against proof of deliberate concealment, misreporting, or manipulation that would undercut a benign explanation.

# Baker: no mirror-image loss/gain requirement

Traditional property protection and the absence of a mirror‑image loss requirement

A common misunderstanding is that federal fraud statutes require a literal, one-for-one net pecuniary loss to the victim that exactly mirrors the gain to the defendant. The Fifth Circuit follows controlling Supreme Court decisions that reject the notion that a defendant can be free from liability simply because the victim allegedly received an economic benefit equal to or exceeding any loss. Rather, what matters is whether the defendant obtained money or property by means of deception, or wrongfully deprived the victim of money or property in a way the statute protects.

The courts distinguish between two analytically separate considerations. On the one hand is the statutory requirement that the object of the scheme be money or property (or an intangible right Congress has expressly protected). On the other hand is whether the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss as a matter of economics. The Fifth Circuit has recognized that a defendant can violate federal fraud statutes when the scheme’s object is obtaining money or property through fraudulent representations, even if the victim later received some value for or from the transaction.

Why a mirror-image loss is not required

Several rationales undergird the exclusion of a mirror-image loss requirement. First, the criminal text targets wrongful acquisition—“obtaining money or property by means of false or fraudulent pretenses.” The verb “obtain” looks to possession or control gained through deception; it does not expressly condition culpability on a showing that a victim’s net wealth decreased. Second, the law has long treated deceit that induces transfer of property of a different character or condition (even if roughly equivalent in monetary value) as actionable; common-law antecedents recognized the injury in being deprived of what one was led to expect. Third, requiring proof of net economic loss would create perverse incentives and a narrow tunnel through which many deceptive schemes could pass unpunished whenever the victim in some sense benefitted from the transaction.

Practical contours

In practice, courts focus on whether the defendant’s deception was the operative cause by which the defendant procured money or property. If so, the fact that the victim later received some offsetting benefit does not necessarily negate the fraud. Nevertheless, evidentiary circumstances that show a genuine, bargained-for exchange (where the victim knowingly accepted the counterperformance or risk) may undermine the inference of fraudulent intent. Thus, the absence of a requirement of net loss does not mean all deceptive misrepresentations are criminal: the elements—deception, intent to obtain money or property thereby, and causation—must still be proved beyond a reasonable doubt.

# Implications for pleading, proof, and jury instructions in fraud cases

Pleading

Indictments must allege the statutory elements with sufficient particularity to inform the defendant of the charges and to enable preparation of a defense. For fraud offenses, this typically requires describing the scheme’s nature, the time frame, the means of deception, and the particular acts by which the defendant sought to effectuate the scheme. When the prosecution advances theories that are legally contested—such as fraudulent inducement vs. right-to-control or novel intangibles—the indictment should clearly specify the object of the alleged fraud (e.g., “to obtain money or property” or “to obtain securities by materially false representations”) and, where possible, identify the property or economic interest claimed to have been targeted.

Particularity is especially important in securities cases because the protected interest must be tied to securities transactions. Pleadings should articulate how the alleged misstatements or omissions related to the purchase or sale of securities, and which communications or instruments were used. When the government relies on a course-of-conduct theory (e.g., misleading financial statements, channel-stuffing, sham transactions), the indictment should give notice of the key acts and documents that form the basis for the charges.

Proof

At trial the government bears the burden beyond a reasonable doubt of proving every element, including the existence of a scheme to defraud, materiality, and fraudulent intent. Given the dual nature of intent in Fifth Circuit law, trial proof generally emphasizes both deception and the defendant’s objective to obtain money or property. Because direct evidence of intent is uncommon, prosecutors rely on circumstantial evidence: systematic misrepresentations, concealment or falsification of records, inconsistent explanations, steps to evade detection, insider communications demonstrating motive, and the timing and pattern of transactions.

Causation and the object-of-the-fraud

Proof must connect the deception with the obtaining of money or property. That causal nexus can be established by showing that the defendant’s misrepresentations induced the victim to transfer money or to award a contract or to make an investment. Where the government proceeds under a fraudulent‑inducement theory, it need not show the victim suffered an overall economic loss; it must show the defendant obtained money or property by deception. But where the alleged property interest is novel or intangible, prosecution risk is greater: courts will scrutinize whether the interest resembles a traditional property right or falls outside the statute’s protective scope.

Defense proof

Defendants often respond with evidence of legitimate business purpose, contemporaneous disclosures, the victim’s knowledge or assumption of risk, or absence of wrongful intent. Evidence that a transaction was negotiated at arms-length, that disclosures were made to the relevant decisionmakers, or that the alleged victim received meaningful consideration for the transaction can undermine the inference of intent to defraud, even though it does not automatically defeat a fraudulent-inducement theory.

Jury instructions

Jury instructions in fraud cases must accurately state the legal elements and the required mental state. Three instruction-related issues frequently arise in appellate review.

1. Defining “scheme to defraud” and “obtain”

The instruction should explain that a “scheme to defraud” means any plan or course of conduct intended to deceive another to obtain money or property, and that “obtain” refers to gaining possession, control, or title to money or property through deception. When the government relies on obtaining money or property as the object, the charge should clarify that obtaining may occur even if the victim receives some benefit in return—so long as the defendant acquired money or property by means of false pretenses.

2. Mental‑state instruction

Given the Fifth Circuit’s twofold approach to intent, the jury must be instructed that the government must prove that the defendant acted with intent to deceive and with intent to cause a deprivation of money or property (or to obtain money or property by deceit). The instruction should distinguish the culpable mental state from negligence or mere mistake. If recklessness is sufficient under the proof, the charge must specify what constitutes reckless disregard for the truth.

3. Materiality and causation

The instruction should define materiality and explain that it is ordinarily for the jury to decide whether a misstatement or omission was material. The jury must also be told that the government must prove a causal connection between the deception and the obtaining of money or property: that the deception was a substantial factor in causing the victim to part with money or property.

Common instructional pitfalls

Overbroad or ambiguous instructions that fail to connect intent to obtain money or property, or that conflate mere misleading statements with the specific intent required, are reversible in close cases. Conversely, overly restrictive instructions that import a requirement of net economic loss or that present the right‑to‑control theory without grounding it in a traditional property interest may be erroneous. The defense may also press for an affirmative instruction explaining that legitimate business motives and truthful disclosures negate the element of intent to defraud.

Procedural posture effects

Different procedural postures shape how these doctrines are applied. During pretrial motions to dismiss, courts test the indictment’s sufficiency but do not weigh credibility; district judges will ensure the indictment alleges each element, including that the scheme targeted money or property when required. On motions for judgment of acquittal or sufficiency appeals, appellate courts evaluate whether any rational trier of fact could have found the elements beyond a reasonable doubt, giving due deference to the jury’s credibility determinations but closely reviewing whether the government presented evidence of both deception and intent to obtain money or property. In the context of sentencing or convictions challenged for variance or constructive amendments, courts scrutinize whether the proof at trial remained within the scope of the charges and instructions provided to the jury.

Conclusion

Fifth Circuit authorities frame §1348 within the established mail-and-wire-fraud architecture while preserving certain modern clarifications: the scheme-to-defraud element requires a showing of material deception; intent to defraud encompasses both intent to deceive and an intent to cause a legally cognizable loss or obtain money/property; and liability does not hinge on a literal mirror-image net loss to the victim. These guideposts affect how prosecutors plead and prove securities fraud, how defendants marshal exculpatory business evidence, and how courts craft jury instructions that fairly present complex mixtures of fraud, contractual performance, and economic exchange. Mastery of these concepts prepares a student to analyze how appellate courts review fraud convictions, to evaluate the sufficiency of indictments and evidence, and to assess the correctness of jury charges in §1348 and related fraud prosecutions.
