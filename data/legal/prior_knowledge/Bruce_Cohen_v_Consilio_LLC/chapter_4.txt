Introduction

Article III’s case-or-controversy requirement is more than an abstract constitutional limit on federal adjudication; it governs who may invoke federal courts and what relief courts may award. One doctrinal corollary that has attained particular importance in recent years is that standing is not a single, global concept that attaches to a lawsuit as a whole. Rather, a litigant must demonstrate Article III standing with respect to each claim pressed and, importantly, with respect to each form of relief requested. This chapter explains the doctrinal structure and practical consequences of claim-specific and remedy-specific standing. It covers the analytic elements courts use to determine whether a plaintiff (or intervenor or class representative) may pursue particular forms of relief—damages, civil penalties, injunctive relief, and the like—and how those requirements interact with representative litigation and the evolving posture of a case as it moves from pleading to final judgment (or mootness). The chapter emphasizes how courts distinguish jurisdictional prerequisites from merits questions and how burdens of proof shift across procedural stages.

# Claim-specific and remedy-specific standing

Standing is grounded in three familiar elements: (1) injury in fact (a concrete and particularized invasion of a legally protected interest), (2) causation (a fairly traceable connection between the injury and the defendant’s challenged conduct), and (3) redressability (a substantial likelihood that a favorable decision will remedy the injury). These elements must be satisfied for Article III jurisdiction to exist.

Remedy-specific standing requires that a plaintiff show these elements not only in relation to the legal theory or claim it asserts, but also in relation to the specific relief it seeks. The principle animating this requirement is that Article III authorizes courts only to resolve concrete disputes; if a form of relief will not meaningfully ameliorate the plaintiff’s particular injury, ordering that relief would not remedy a live controversy. Courts therefore require plaintiffs to demonstrate that the relief requested would provide redress for the injury alleged.

Two interrelated features deserve emphasis.

First, standing is claim-specific. A plaintiff must demonstrate Article III standing for each distinct legal claim it presses. When different claims rely on different asserted injuries, or on different causal connections, the standing inquiry may yield different outcomes for each claim. For example, a plaintiff who suffered an economic loss traceable to one policy may lack standing to challenge a separate policy that caused no injury to that plaintiff, even though the two policies are pleaded in the same complaint.

Second, standing is remedy-specific. Courts ask whether the particular remedy sought—monetary damages, a declaratory judgment, an injunction, civil penalties payable to government treasury, restitution, or some hybrids—would likely redress the plaintiff’s injury. The redressability analysis turns on causal effectiveness: will the remedy, materially and directly, provide relief to the plaintiff? Declaratory judgments or injunctions often satisfy redressability when they alter the defendant’s future behavior or clarify rights, but they may fail when the defendant’s conduct has already ceased and there is no realistic prospect of repetition. Monetary relief typically satisfies redressability when it compensates for past injury; but when monetary relief would be payable to the government (civil penalties), a plaintiff must show that securing such penalties would still redress the plaintiff’s injury (for example, by deterrence that prevents recurrence).

At the core, courts treat standing as a fact-sensitive inquiry. When jurisdictional facts are contested, the plaintiff bears the burden of establishing standing at the outset. If standing requires proof of facts beyond the pleadings, courts may require additional evidence at the summary-judgment stage or at trial.

# Implications for suits seeking damages, penalties, and injunctive relief

The remedy requested shapes the redressability analysis and can transform the standing inquiry in important ways.

Damages. Monetary damages directed to a plaintiff’s economic loss are the clearest example of redressable relief: compensation for a past, cognizable injury commonly satisfies the redressability prong. However, even a simple damages request requires that the plaintiff demonstrate causation and that the damages sought are compensable under applicable law. For class actions or representative suits, individualized damage claims may raise manageability and standing issues when different class members experienced different injuries. Courts scrutinize whether at least one litigant has standing to pursue the damages sought and, where the relief would be individualized, whether representative standing mechanisms or procedural devices can lawfully and effectively supply necessary redress.

Civil penalties and fines. Civil penalties present a nuanced problem. Penalties often are payable to the government treasury rather than to private plaintiffs, so a private plaintiff cannot ordinarily expect direct monetary payment even if the court orders a penalty. That raises a redressability concern: if the plaintiff’s interest is purely monetary restitution, awarding a penalty to the government will not compensate the plaintiff’s past loss and thus might fail redressability.

Nonetheless, courts have recognized circumstances where civil penalties can redress a private plaintiff’s injury indirectly. Two common theories permit private plaintiffs to pursue penalties consistent with redressability:

- Deterrence: A penalty imposed on the defendant may remove or reduce the risk of future injury to the private plaintiff (or class) by discouraging recurrence of unlawful conduct. Where the plaintiff alleges a continuing or prospective injury, and a penalty is likely to deter future violations, the penalty can be sufficiently redressive to satisfy Article III.

- Ancillary benefits: Penalty awards sometimes trigger collateral relief that benefits private plaintiffs—such as prompting injunctive compliance, creating a record that supports future relief, or altering the statutory enforcement landscape. Courts evaluate whether such secondary effects are sufficiently likely and concrete to constitute redress.

Because these theories turn on forward-looking assessments, courts assess standing for penalty requests rigorously. If the defendant has already ceased the challenged conduct and has taken steps that make recurrence unlikely, the plaintiff must overcome judicial skepticism that a penalty will provide real redress (this is where the voluntary-cessation and mootness doctrines intersect, discussed below).

Injunctive relief. Injunctions are prospective remedies aimed at preventing future harm. To have standing for injunctive relief, a plaintiff must ordinarily show a credible threat of future harm that is traceable to the defendant and that would be remedied by the requested injunction. Where the complained-of conduct is specific and ongoing, an injunction is an appropriate redressive remedy. But if the defendant voluntarily ceases the challenged behavior, the court must decide whether the cessation moots requests for prospective relief. A mere cessation does not automatically eliminate jurisdiction; courts ask whether it is “absolutely clear” the defendant cannot reasonably be expected to resume the practice. If the threat of recurrence remains, injunctions can still serve as redress.

Unique features across remedies. Several crosscutting rules influence remedial standing:

- A plaintiff cannot bootstrap standing for one remedy from standing for a different remedy unless the first remedy would, in fact, redress the legally cognizable injury. For example, having standing to seek injunction does not necessarily mean the plaintiff may recover statutory penalties unless the penalties themselves redress the plaintiff’s injury.

- Remedies that alter non-monetary legal relations (declaratory judgments, injunctions) often satisfy redressability when they remove an immediate threat or clarify rights; damages require particularity of loss.

- Where remedies confer benefits on third parties or the public at large (civil penalties payable to treasury, public injunctive relief), courts scrutinize whether the plaintiff’s stake is concrete enough to support redress.

# Interplay with class or representative allegations

Representative litigation introduces layered standing problems because a suit often seeks relief on behalf of multiple persons, sometimes including relief forms that would affect class members differently or confer relief only on some.

Constitutional requirement for class representatives. For a class action under Rule 23, at least one named plaintiff must have Article III standing for each claim and for each form of relief sought on behalf of the class. This is more than an idiosyncratic pleading rule; it follows the proposition that federal courts cannot adjudicate the rights of absent persons without a plaintiff who has a personal stake in the requested relief. If no class representative can demonstrate standing to seek a particular form of relief, the court lacks jurisdiction to award that relief on behalf of the class.

Typicality and commonality do not substitute for standing. Typicality and commonality under Rule 23 address whether claims and defenses align across the group; they do not relieve the named plaintiffs of the constitutional burden to establish standing. Thus, even if a class’s injuries are homogeneous, if the named plaintiffs lack the concrete stake required for an injunctive remedy or particular damages, the court cannot grant that relief for the class.

Representative and derivative suits. Suits brought by private representatives (e.g., related to statutory enforcement schemes where private litigants can sue on behalf of the public or where statutes allow the recovery of civil penalties) require careful analysis to ensure the representative plaintiff has individualized standing to pursue each remedy sought. The required showing differs with the remedy:

- For injunctive relief on behalf of a class, the representative must show the likelihood of future injury for itself and that the injunction would redress that injury.

- For statutory penalties payable to the state, representative plaintiffs must show that the penalties would provide an adequate form of redress—commonly by deterrence of future violations or by if statutes explicitly authorize private enforcement that benefits private parties via statutory fee-shifting or other mechanisms.

Intervenors and additional plaintiffs. The introduction of intervenors or additional plaintiffs raises an important doctrinal point: a litigant who seeks to pursue relief not already requested by an existing plaintiff must show Article III standing in its own right for that relief. If the intervenor seeks identical relief already sought by an existing plaintiff who has standing, courts may allow intervention without re-proving standing, but if the intervenor pursues different or additional relief (particularly individualized monetary judgments), it must independently satisfy the standing requirements. The existence of a party with standing for each relief sought is the minimal constitutional requirement to proceed.

Practical consequences. Practically, counsel for class plaintiffs must ensure their named representatives possess injuries (and threatened future harm, where relevant) that align with all requested forms of relief. Defense counsel can and do challenge class representatives’ standing as to particular relief at early stages, and courts will sometimes refuse to certify classes or will narrow the scope of permissible relief when standing is absent.

# Jurisdictional inquiries at different stages of litigation

Standing issues arise repeatedly during litigation, and the nature of the inquiry shifts with procedural posture. Courts distinguish between the initial jurisdictional question at the pleading stage and subsequent evaluations of mootness, ripeness, or standing on summary judgment or trial, each of which imposes different evidentiary expectations.

Pleading stage. At the motion-to-dismiss or pleading stage, courts accept the complaint’s factual allegations as true and ask whether the plaintiff has plausibly pleaded the elements of standing. The allegations must be sufficiently particular to allow a reasonable inference that the plaintiff suffered a concrete injury traceable to the defendant that would be redressed by a favorable decision. Courts evaluate factual plausibility, not whether the plaintiff will ultimately prevail on the merits.

Summary judgment and trial. If the defendant contests standing and the record contains disputed facts, the plaintiff must produce evidence supporting the standing elements. The burden remains with the party asserting federal jurisdiction; thus, on summary judgment, the plaintiff must show genuine issues of material fact on injury, causation, and redressability. At trial, the plaintiff must prove those facts by the applicable standard (typically preponderance of evidence for jurisdictional facts).

Mootness and voluntary cessation. Even when a plaintiff establishes standing at the outset, the case can become moot if subsequent events eliminate a live controversy. Mootness is the doctrine that Article III requires a live case or controversy at all stages of litigation. Key principles include:

- Voluntary cessation: A defendant’s voluntary cessation of allegedly wrongful conduct does not automatically moot a case. If a defendant merely stops a practice because litigation is pending, courts are wary that the defendant could restart the conduct after dismissal. To show mootness based on voluntary cessation, the defendant bears a heavy burden: it must demonstrate that the challenged behavior cannot reasonably be expected to recur. Courts use a stringent test—mere assertions of changed practice are insufficient; objective changes and legal constraints that make recurrence unlikely are necessary to render a controversy moot.

- Absolutely clear standard for mootness: Where a defendant asserts mootness through changed practices, courts look for evidence making it “absolutely clear” that the allegedly wrongful conduct could not reasonably be expected to happen again. This is a demanding standard built to prevent litigants from evading judicial review simply by temporarily altering conduct.

Ripeness and future injury. For claims requiring prospective relief, courts evaluate whether the plaintiff’s asserted threatened injury is sufficiently imminent. Ripeness doctrine overlaps with standing in assessing the concreteness and immediacy of the harm. If the threat is speculative or contingent on future events that may not occur, the plaintiff may lack standing for prospective relief.

Intervenors and parties later joining the case. When new parties seek to join or intervene, courts analyze their standing to pursue the relief they seek at the time of the motion to intervene. If an intervenor wishes to assert relief not sought by existing plaintiffs, the intervenor must demonstrate standing at that procedural moment. In contrast, a permissive intervenor joining identical claims already pursued by an existing plaintiff generally shares the standing status of the original plaintiff.

Timing and burden allocation. The party asserting federal jurisdiction (typically the plaintiff) always bears the burden of proof on standing. That burden is context-dependent:

- At the pleading stage, allegations need plausibility.

- At summary judgment and trial stages, evidence is required and the burden is to prove standing by a preponderance of evidence (for jurisdictional facts).

- For voluntary-cessation claims by defendants seeking mootness, defendants carry the unusual burden of persuading the court that recurrence is not reasonably possible.

Jurisdictional threshold versus merits. Courts often must separate jurisdictional standing questions from merits arguments, which may require careful parsing. Courts follow the principle that if resolution of the merits is needed to decide whether Article III jurisdiction exists, the jurisdictional question still precedes the merits. Courts generally refrain from resolving merits issues in the guise of threshold jurisdictional determinations when the merits are substantive and separable. That said, factual overlap is unavoidable in some cases, and courts must sometimes decide contested facts that bear both on jurisdiction and merits, always mindful that jurisdictional findings are not merits determinations.

# Practical guidance and doctrinal pitfalls

Several practical points flow from the doctrine described above:

- Draft complaints to align plaintiffs’ injuries with the remedies sought. Ensure each named plaintiff plausibly alleges injury, causation, and redressability for every claim and every form of relief requested on behalf of the plaintiff individually and, if relevant, on behalf of a class.

- When pursuing penalties payable to a government, explain carefully how a penalty would redress the private plaintiff’s injury: show a credible threat of repeat violation and explain how deterrence or statutory enforcement mechanisms will materially benefit the plaintiff.

- Anticipate challenges to remedial standing early. Defendants commonly attack standing as to particular remedies in motions to dismiss or in motions for judgment as a matter of law; thoughtful factual development that targets the redressability element helps meet evidentiary burdens later.

- In intervention contexts, recognize the distinct rule: an intervenor seeking relief beyond that requested by an original plaintiff must independently show standing for that relief. Counsel for intervenors must therefore document individualized injury when seeking different remedies.

- In class certification, confirm that at least one named plaintiff has standing for each claim and each remedy requested. If necessary, tailor the scope of the class or the remedies sought to align with representative plaintiffs’ standing.

- Be prepared to supplement the record on standing at summary judgment and trial. Courts require more than conclusory assertions once facts are in dispute.

- Watch the difference between a plaintiff’s standing at the outset and mootness later. Even where a plaintiff had standing initially, post-complaint developments (e.g., a defendant’s compliance, changes in behavior, or intervening statutory enforcement) can moot or narrow the relief available. Conversely, a defendant’s voluntary cessation will not necessarily end the litigation unless the defendant proves recurrence is not reasonably possible.

Conclusion

Claim- and remedy-specific standing reinforce Article III’s mandate that federal courts adjudicate concrete disputes and grant relief that genuinely redresses a plaintiff’s injury. Plaintiffs, intervenors, and representatives must therefore tailor claims and remedy requests to the actual legal and factual stake they possess. Remedies that indirectly benefit private plaintiffs—such as civil penalties payable to the treasury—require a particularized showing that those remedies will actually reduce the plaintiff’s injury, most commonly by deterring future violations. Procedurally, standing is litigated at many points, and the burdens and evidentiary requirements grow as the case progresses. Understanding how courts analyze the interplay of claims, remedies, representative litigation, and procedural posture is essential before approaching judicial opinions that apply these principles to concrete disputes.
