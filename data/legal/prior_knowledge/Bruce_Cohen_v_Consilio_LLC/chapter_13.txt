Introduction

Appeals are primarily about legal error. But an appellate court cannot correct an error that was never brought to the trial court’s attention in a way that gave the court an opportunity to correct it. Consequently, a critical part of civil appellate practice — and of effective litigation generally — is preserving issues for appeal, knowing when a party may introduce new theories, and complying with the form and content rules that govern appellate briefing. This chapter explains the governing principles: how to preserve objections and legal theories at the summary judgment stage; how and when new claims may be introduced through amended pleadings rather than raised solely in briefing; the key content requirements appellate rules impose on briefs (including the requirement in Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 28(a)(9)(A)); the doctrines of waiver and forfeiture that limit appellate review; and the standards appellate courts apply to inadequately briefed issues.

The focus is doctrinal: what must be done in the trial court record, why it matters on appeal, what burdens attach to parties, and how courts exercise discretion when confronted with new or underdeveloped arguments.

# Preserving arguments at summary judgment

Summary judgment presents frequent preservation issues because it is a dispositive motion where parties must crystallize their factual and legal disputes. Preservation at summary judgment has two distinct dimensions: evidentiary preservation — ensuring the record contains the factual materials necessary to support an argument — and legal preservation — making the trial court aware of the legal theory or objection so it can rule.

Evidentiary preservation

Rule 56 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure sets the framework. A party moving for summary judgment must identify the parts of the record showing there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact; a party opposing summary judgment must present materials showing there is a genuine dispute. To preserve issues on appeal, a party must ordinarily:

- Produce admissible evidence (or show why evidence the opposing party objects to is admissible) and cite the record so the trial court and, later, the appellate court can find the evidence.
- Make timely and specific evidentiary objections when necessary (for instance, to hearsay, lack of authentication, or improper opinion testimony) so the trial court has an opportunity to rule on admissibility. If an objection is not made, appellate courts frequently treat the evidence as properly in the record.
- If the opposing party’s evidence is incomplete because discovery is incomplete or a fact is unavailable, move under Rule 56(d) (formerly 56(f)) for discovery or for denial or continuance of the motion until discovery is had. A failure to seek relief under Rule 56(d) when discovery would produce needed facts can result in forfeiture of the right to complain on appeal that summary judgment was premature.

A common mistake is to rely on factual assertions in briefs without citing to evidence in the record, or to cite deposition testimony or documents without attaching them or otherwise making them part of the summary-judgment record. On appeal, courts review only what is in the trial-court record; assertions that are not substantiated by record citations are likely to be rejected as forfeited.

Legal preservation

Legal preservation means giving the trial court an opportunity to consider and rule on the precise legal argument you plan to make on appeal. For preserved review:

- The party opposing summary judgment should clearly articulate the legal ground on which denial is sought. Saying in general terms that “genuine issues of material fact exist” without tying that assertion to specific record evidence or legal theories is often insufficient.
- If the court rules on a motion for summary judgment, the appellate review will focus on the bases the trial court understood and addressed. If a party waits until its appellate brief to assert a new legal theory — for example, a new statutory interpretation that was not raised below — appellate courts will often refuse to consider it because the trial court never had the chance to apply the law to facts.
- When cross-motions for summary judgment are filed, preserve differing legal theories by explicitly arguing them in the respective motions and responses. A party that does not make a particular argument in its summary-judgment papers will have a hard time asserting that theory on appeal.

Standard of review and consequences

When an appellate court addresses preserved summary-judgment issues, the standard of review is de novo for questions of law and for the court’s conclusion whether a genuine issue of material fact exists; factual findings are viewed most favorably to the nonmoving party. But the ability to obtain de novo review depends on proper preservation: a legal theory not presented to the trial court is ordinarily forfeited and therefore not subject to appellate de novo review. Moreover, evidentiary matters — such as whether a particular affidavit was properly considered — may be reviewed for abuse of discretion if the trial judge made a discretionary determination concerning admissibility.

# Raising new claims through amended pleadings versus briefing

A fundamental precept of civil litigation is that the pleadings frame the issues. The pleadings give notice to the opponent and the court of the claims and defenses at stake. Amending pleadings and raising new claims via briefing are governed by separate doctrines and have different procedural consequences.

Amending pleadings: Rule 15 and strategy

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15 governs amendments. The rule allows a party to amend its pleading once as a matter of course within a short window after service; thereafter, amendments require either the opposing party’s written consent or the court’s leave, which the court should freely give “when justice so requires.” Key points for preservation and introduction of new claims:

- Timeliness: Seek leave to amend at the earliest reasonable opportunity. Delay can be a factor in denial if it is inexcusable and will prejudice the opposing party.
- Relation back: Rule 15(c) allows an amendment to “relate back” to the date of the original pleading when the claim arises out of the conduct, transaction, or occurrence set out in the original pleading — a doctrine that matters for statute-of-limitations defenses. To secure relation back, the facts alleged in the amendment must be adequately tied to the original pleading’s allegations.
- Notice and prejudice: Courts weigh prejudice to the defendant, including whether additional discovery will be required, the stage of litigation, and whether the defendant had notice of the factual basis for the new claim.
- Preservation for appeal: Where a claim is added by amendment and adjudicated, the claim is preserved and can be appealed like any other adjudicated claim. If a party attempts to pursue an entirely new claim for the first time on appeal (i.e., raising a claim that was never pleaded), appellate courts will usually decline to consider it because the trial court never had the opportunity to rule.

Raising claims in briefing: why this is risky

Raising a new theory or legal claim for the first time in a motion or brief rather than by amending the complaint is problematic because it may fail to satisfy notice, joinder, and procedural requirements. Distinguish three contexts:

- Trial-court briefing: If a plaintiff attempts to assert a new theory in opposition to a motion or in a reply brief without amending the complaint, the trial court may decline to address it as unpled. Defendants are entitled to respond and develop their defenses, and the court may require an amended pleading if the new theory is to be considered.
- Summary-judgment briefing: Raising a new legal theory in summary-judgment briefing may preserve the argument only if the theory is a responsive legal argument tied to facts before the court and the opposing party has an opportunity to respond. Yet introducing a wholly different cause of action or an unpled theory is less likely to be considered; courts will often treat it as waived or require amendment.
- Appeals: Attempting to present a new claim for the first time on appeal — even if it was suggested in a lower-court brief — is generally disfavored. Appellate courts expect the record to reflect the live issues below and may refuse to consider newly minted claims or theories that were not the subject of a ruling.

Practical guidance

If a party wishes to pursue an alternative theory of liability or a new legal theory, best practice is to seek leave to amend the pleading before trial or before the court rules on dispositive motions. If an amendment is impracticable, the party should at least put the opposing party and the court on notice that the new theory is being asserted, request leave to amend, or otherwise obtain a ruling that addresses the theory on the merits. Without such steps, the claim is at substantial risk of being forfeited or deemed waived.

# Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 28(a)(9)(A) requirements

Appellate briefs must meet specific form and content requirements intended to aid the court’s work. Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 28 prescribes these requirements; a failure to comply can lead to the reading judge or court refusing to consider arguments, striking briefs, or treating portions of a brief as inadequately presented.

Core content requirements

Although the Federal Rules have been amended over time, the central, enduring obligations for appellants are these: the brief must present the issues clearly and concisely, state the basis of jurisdiction, state the standard of review for each issue, and, crucially, support the party’s contentions with citations to the parts of the record and to authority. Rule 28(a)(9)(A), in particular, requires that an appellant’s brief include a concise statement of the issues presented for review and must support each contention with citations to the record and legal authority. The rule targets two essential functions:

- Focus the court: The issues presented statement tells the appellate court what specific questions are before it and narrows the appeal to the matters the appellant wishes to press.
- Facilitate judicial review: The requirement that arguments be supported by citations to the record and legal authorities enables the court to verify assertions and apply the appropriate standard of review and legal precedents.

Common deficiencies that run afoul of Rule 28(a)(9)(A)-style requirements

- Vague or overly broad issue statements that do not identify a specific ruling below or the precise question of law or fact on appeal.
- Failure to state the applicable standard of review for each issue. The standard of review determines the court’s role (de novo, abuse of discretion, clear error, etc.) and is essential context for evaluating the argument.
- Argument unmoored from the record: assertions about facts without pinpoint citations to trial or summary-judgment materials, transcripts, or exhibits.
- Argument without legal support: failing to cite statutes, rules, or controlling authority for legal propositions.

Consequences of noncompliance

Appellate courts have discretion to impose sanctions or to decline to consider inadequately presented issues. Typical remedies include ordering rebriefing, striking portions of the brief, deeming the argument waived, or in extreme cases dismissing the appeal. Courts also may treat a deficient argument as forfeited and confine their review to the arguments properly presented.

Practical drafting tips

When preparing the brief, the advocate should state each issue precisely, state the relevant standard of review immediately, and then present the argument with sequential citations to (a) the parts of the record (transcript pages, exhibits, docket entries) that support the factual assertions, and (b) the legal authorities that support the interpretive or doctrinal claims. This structure both complies with Rule 28 obligations and maximizes the chance that the appellate court will treat the issues as properly presented.

# Waiver and forfeiture doctrines on appeal

Appellate courts operate under two related doctrines that limit the matters they may address: waiver and forfeiture. Although the terms are sometimes used interchangeably in casual discussion, they have distinct meanings and consequences.

Waiver

Waiver is intentional relinquishment or abandonment of a known right. In civil procedure, waiver arises when a party deliberately chooses not to assert a right or objection, or affirmatively acts in a way inconsistent with later raising the issue. Courts treat waiver as a binding consequence of a party’s strategic choices; appellate courts will not consider waived issues regardless of their merit.

Examples of waiver include knowingly consenting to a procedure and later objecting to it, or explicitly withdrawing an objection during trial. Because waiver involves deliberate choice, evidence of intent to abandon the claim or right may be determinative. Where the record reflects informed and voluntary conduct that shows a party intended to relinquish a right, appellate relief is unavailable.

Forfeiture

Forfeiture, in contrast, involves a failure to preserve an argument or objection that must be made in a timely manner in the trial court so that the trial court can correct any error. Forfeiture is not necessarily intentional; it often results from neglect, oversight, or insufficient advocacy below. In most civil appeals, issues that were forfeited — that is, not raised in a timely and proper manner in the trial court — will not be considered by the appellate court.

Appellate consequences differ: whereas waiver is treated as binding, forfeiture sometimes permits limited appellate relief under exceptional circumstances. The degree of leniency varies by context and by the type of error asserted.

Invitation to error and estoppel

Two related doctrines that can bar appellate relief are invited error and judicial estoppel. If a party’s own conduct induced the trial court to take a particular course, that party may be barred from later complaining about the consequences. Similarly, where a party successfully advocates a certain view in the trial court, that party may be estopped from advancing a contrary position on appeal.

Plain-error review and exceptional relief

Because forfeiture is ordinarily excused only in narrow circumstances, appellate courts sometimes apply an extraordinary standard (akin to “plain error” in the criminal context) to excuse forfeiture in civil cases. Where a forfeited objection implicates substantial justice, a clear and obvious error, and a result that affects the fairness or integrity of the proceedings, an appellate court may recognize the error even without preservation. However, this is uncommon in civil cases; appellants should not rely on appellate leniency.

Interplay with standards of review

When an issue is preserved, the standard of review is determined by the type of issue — legal questions typically receive de novo review, findings of fact receive clear-error review, and discretionary rulings (e.g., evidentiary rulings, discovery rulings) receive abuse-of-discretion review. When an issue is forfeited, appellate courts normally decline to review it; if appellate review is permitted under an exception, the intervening standard is typically stricter — e.g., an appellate court may require a showing of “manifest error” or “plain error” and may consider whether the error affected substantial rights.

# Standards for considering inadequately briefed issues

Appellate courts expect advocates to brief issues clearly and adequately. When briefs do not comply with the court’s expectations, courts have several options, informed by the need to allocate judicial resources efficiently and to be fair to the opposing party.

What counts as inadequately briefed?

An issue is “inadequately briefed” when the appellant has failed to provide adequate argumentation, lacks citations to the record, cites no authority for a proposition, or fails to identify the standard of review. Examples include a single-sentence argument, an issue mentioned only in a headings list but not developed, or an assertion of fact with no record citation.

Doctrinal consequences

- Deeming waived: The most common outcome is that the appellate court will deem the inadequately briefed issue waived and decline to consider it. Courts often state that they will not craft an argument for an appellant or serve as an advocate.
- Limited consideration: In some circumstances, a court may elect to consider an inadequately briefed issue if the necessary facts and law are easily ascertainable from the record and the parties’ other submissions. This tends to happen more in pro se contexts or in cases where the issue is simple and closely tied to issues fully briefed.
- Striking or ordering rebriefing: The court may strike the deficient portion of the brief and allow rebriefing with a warning, especially where the deficiency is curable and the other side will not be prejudiced.
- Sanctions: In extreme cases, the court may impose sanctions for repetitive or deliberate noncompliance with briefing rules.

Factors courts consider

When deciding whether to consider an inadequately briefed issue, courts typically weigh:

- Prejudice to the opposing party: If the opposing party had no opportunity to address the inadequately briefed issue, the court is less likely to consider it.
- The complexity of the legal question: For clear-cut legal errors or questions of law that require little factual development, courts may be more willing to consider an underdeveloped argument.
- The completeness of the record: If the factual record is undeveloped on the point, courts will ordinarily decline to consider the argument.
- The public importance or judicial economy: Sometimes, if an issue is likely to recur or is central to the case and resolution on appeal would avoid further litigation, a court may exercise discretion to decide the point despite inadequate briefing.
- Whether the opposing party raised the issue in its brief: If the appellee addresses the issue fully, the court may have sufficient briefing to resolve it on the merits.

Standards of review applied when an inadequately briefed issue is addressed

If an appellate court elects to address an inadequately briefed issue, it will still apply the appropriate standard of review for that type of issue. However, courts often state that they will afford less favorable treatment to arguments raised only on appeal and inadequately developed, and they may undertake review with heightened caution or reluctance. When appellate intervention is permitted despite inadequate briefing, the appellant should expect a stricter scrutiny of its showing that any error affected substantial rights.

Practical counsel

From a practical standpoint, parties should assume the appellate court will not consider perfunctory arguments. Present each issue with a concise statement of the applicable standard of review, specific pinpoint citations to the record, and a developed legal argument with supporting authority. If an issue must be raised but the appellant cannot prepare a full brief by the filing date, counsel should seek an extension or move for permission to file supplemental briefs. Relying on the court’s discretion to salvage an inadequately briefed contention is risky.

Conclusion

Issue preservation, proper amendment of pleadings, and careful appellate briefing are not mere technicalities; they are structural rules that shape the problems a court can resolve. Parties who do not present their claims and defenses clearly at the trial level or who fail to brief their appeals in compliance with the rules will find appellate relief difficult to obtain. A disciplined approach in the trial court — making timely objections, seeking necessary discovery under Rule 56(d), moving to amend pleadings when new theories arise, and ensuring the trial record contains admissible evidence — paired with meticulous compliance with appellate briefing rules (including the requirement to state issues and support contentions with record citations and legal authority), will preserve the maximum opportunity for review and avoid the application of waiver, forfeiture, and rules that treat issues as inadequately briefed.
