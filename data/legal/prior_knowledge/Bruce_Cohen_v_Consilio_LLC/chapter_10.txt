Introduction

Wage-and-hour litigation typically seeks to make whole workers who have been underpaid for hours worked, denied overtime, or otherwise deprived of contractual or statutory compensation. Remedies in this area serve several distinct functions: they compensate injured employees for lost earnings, deter future violations, deprive employers of unjust enrichment, and cover the costs of vindicating rights (including attorneys’ fees and interest). The remedial rules are statutory and equitable. Statutes such as federal and state wage laws set out remedies and sometimes limit or modify equitable relief; courts develop complementary equitable doctrines that shape when and how relief is granted. This chapter surveys the principal monetary and equitable remedies encountered in wage-and-hour cases, explains the legal standards that govern them, and highlights procedural and factual features — such as class/collective posture, statute-of-limitations issues, and proof burdens — that affect relief.

# Back wages, overtime premiums, and restitution

Back wages and overtime premiums are the most common direct monetary remedies. They compensate employees for wages that should have been paid during the relevant statutory or equitable time frame.

Back wages. Back wages ordinarily mean the net compensation an employee would have received but for a statutory or contractual violation. Under a statutory wage scheme, back wages are calculated over the period for which the employee’s claim is timely or for which equitable tolling applies (see limitations discussion infra). Calculation typically requires determining the employee’s proper rate(s) of pay and the hours properly compensable.

Regular rate and its role. The regular rate is the baseline for calculating overtime under many statutes. Its calculation is governed by substantive rules: it generally equals total remuneration for employment (excluding certain statutorily excluded payments) divided by hours worked in the pay period. Non-discretionary bonuses, shift differentials, and some nondiscretionary incentive pay are usually included; gifts, expense reimbursements, and truly discretionary bonuses are commonly excluded. In practice, employers and plaintiffs litigate what compensation must be aggregated into the regular rate.

Overtime premiums. For periods when an employee worked overtime as defined by statute (for example, hours over 40 in a workweek under the federal model), the employer must pay a premium. Many statutory schemes require pay at a higher rate such as “one and one-half times the regular rate” for overtime hours. The overtime remedy may be structured either as full payment at the higher rate for overtime hours or as an overtime premium added to straight time already paid. Two common computational approaches are:

- Full overtime pay: For overtime hours $H_{OT}$, pay $H_{OT} \times 1.5 \times R$, where $R$ is the regular rate.
- Premium approach: If straight time for all hours already paid at $R$, then add premium $H_{OT} \times (1.5R - R) = H_{OT} \times 0.5R$.

Some legal regimes and doctrines (e.g., the fluctuating workweek method) modify the premium calculation when a fixed salary covers varying hours; in that method the premium can be computed as half the regular rate because salary already compensates some straight time.

Mitigation and setoffs. Courts commonly reduce back-pay awards by amounts the employee actually earned after the violation (mitigation) but usually not by amounts the employee could have earned but did not search for without a proper showing (no duty to accept inferior or substantially equivalent work). Employers sometimes obtain setoffs for benefits the employee received (e.g., unemployment compensation), but statutory or equitable restrictions may limit those offsets.

Restitution and disgorgement. Restitution operates to return to employees monies unjustly withheld or to disgorge ill-gotten gains from employers. The doctrinal character of restitution varies. When framed as compensatory back pay it is remedial and focused on the victims’ losses. When framed as disgorgement of profits, the remedy is remedial-plus-deterrent and may be equitable disgorgement where the employer is stripped of unjust enrichment. The distinction matters because equitable restitution may be available only where the claimant lacks an adequate legal remedy, and remedies available differ (e.g., restitution may be limited by statutes of limitations and may implicate limitations on awards of interest and counsel fees). In wage contexts, courts scrutinize whether the remedy is compensatory (calculated to make employees whole) or punitive/disgorgement (aimed at depriving the employer of profits) because the latter can implicate more exacting equitable limits.

# Liquidated damages: purposes and calculation

Liquidated damages in wage-and-hour law are statutory additional monetary awards that serve both compensatory and punitive/deterrent functions. They frequently take the form of a fixed multiple of back wages (often a 100% or “double damages” provision), designed to make employees whole and to deter violations by increasing expected liability.

Purpose. Liquidated damages recognize the difficulty in proving the exact amount of harm from wage violations and the need to deter future breaches. They reduce the incentive for employers to underpay and to delay complying with wage laws by making the expected cost of noncompliance higher than the savings from underpayment.

Calculation. The basic mathematical structure under many statutes is straightforward: liquidated damages equal a set percentage or multiple of the unpaid wages award. Where the statute provides for double damages, liquidated damages often equal the amount of unpaid wages, so the total recovery doubles the principal back-pay award. In formula form, if $W$ is unpaid wages, total statutory liquidated damages may be:

- Double damages: Liquidated damages $L = W$, total recovery $= W + L = 2W$.
- Percentage-based: If the statute specifies a penalty rate $p$, then $L = p \times W$.

Some statutes cap liquidated damages or provide schedules; others tie them to willfulness or bad faith.

Good-faith defense and equitable discretion. Many statutory schemes temper liquidated damages with defenses or grants of discretion. For example, an employer who can show that the wage violation arose from a bona fide dispute about the correct pay or from a reasonable reliance on authoritative administrative rulings or legal advice may avoid liquidated damages. Courts commonly use a two-part analysis: the employer must show that (1) it acted in subjective good faith, and (2) it had objectively reasonable grounds for believing its conduct complied with the law. If both are met, courts may not award liquidated damages. The burden of proof often shifts: plaintiffs typically must show the failure to pay, and once that is shown, the employer bears the burden of proving that the good-faith defense applies.

Willfulness and extended limitations. Willful violations sometimes expand liability. Where statutes impose liquidated damages only for willful violations, the plaintiff must prove willfulness (often requiring evidence that the employer knew or showed reckless disregard for statutory requirements). Willfulness also commonly extends the statute of limitations from a two-year to a three-year period, thereby increasing the potential exposure for liquidated damages.

Interaction with equitable doctrines and mitigation. Liquidated damages may be treated differently depending on whether they are characterized as punitive, compensatory, or remedial. This characterization affects whether equitable defenses (unclean hands, laches) or limitations apply. Liquidated damages are often assessed without reduction for mitigation unless the statute or case law mandates reduction. In class or collective actions, courts may need to allocate liquidated damages among claimants and deal with opt-ins/opt-outs and distribution mechanisms.

# Civil penalties: purpose, payee, and deterrence

Civil penalties are statutory amounts imposed in addition to compensatory awards to punish noncompliance and deter future violations. They may be payable to the government, to affected employees, or split between both, depending on statutory design.

Purpose. Penalties serve primarily a public-regulatory function. They signal societal condemnation of conduct and increase the cost of violating wage laws beyond simply making employees whole. By making violations financially unattractive, penalties aim to change employer behavior across the regulated population.

Payee and allocation. Statutory schemes vary: some direct penalties to the government’s treasury, others require employers to pay penalties to affected employees, and several employ hybrid approaches where a portion goes to the government and a portion to the aggrieved workers. The identity of the payee affects the remedial posture: penalties paid to the government often implicate enforcement discretion and public interests; penalties paid directly to employees function more like multiple damages and may be considered part of the private recovery.

Deterrence and proportionality. Courts frequently analyze whether a penalty is proportional to the violation, especially if the statute allows discretionary assessment. Though deference to legislative judgment is typical, constitutional concerns (excessive fines clause) can arise where penalties are grossly disproportionate. Courts may consider factors such as the employer’s size, the degree of culpability, history of violations, and the economic impact on the employer.

Procedural and substantive prerequisites. Penalties often require procedural steps before they attach (for example, notice or an opportunity to cure). Some statutes limit penalties where the employer makes a good-faith attempt to comply, or they create graduated penalty schemes based on the number of violations or duration.

Interaction with compensatory relief and liquidation. When both penalties and compensatory damages are available, courts must avoid double recovery and clarify whether penalties replace, supplement, or are cumulative with other remedies such as liquidated damages. Statutory text controls; where ambiguous, courts construe remedial provisions to best effectuate legislative purpose, consistent with avoidance of duplication.

# Prejudgment interest and attorneys’ fees under fee-shifting provisions

Prejudgment interest

Purpose and operation. Prejudgment interest compensates plaintiffs for the time value of the delayed payment of funds they were entitled to earlier, and it discourages defendants from delay. It is a distinct remedy from punitive or liquidated damages and is geared to make the plaintiff whole by approximating the interest the lost wages could have earned.

Availability. Availability depends on statutory authorization or equitable principles. Some wage laws expressly authorize prejudgment interest; where they do not, courts may award prejudgment interest as equitable relief to prevent unjust enrichment or to compensate for the delay. Statute-specific rules often govern when interest begins to accrue, what rate applies, and whether compounding is permitted.

Start date and accrual. Courts adopt varying approaches for the start date of interest: some use the date each unpaid wage fell due (which requires period-by-period computations), while others use the date of suit or a single representative date. The former is more precise and equitable in wage cases because unpaid wages typically accrue at known dates (paydays).

Rate and compounding. The rate can be statutory (e.g., tied to a federal rate, state statutory rate, or prime rate plus a margin) or equitable (reasonable rate reflecting market or statutory norms). Courts must specify whether interest is simple or compounded; compounding increases total recovery and is less common unless explicitly authorized.

Calculation methods. Practically, computing prejudgment interest may require summing interest across unpaid amounts originating at multiple dates. If unpaid wages for period $i$ amount to $W_i$ and interest rate $r$ is per annum measured continuously or discretely, interest to judgment date $T$ is

- Simple interest: $\sum_i W_i \times r \times (T - t_i)$,
- Compound interest (discrete compounding annually): $\sum_i W_i \times (1+r)^{T - t_i} - W_i$,

where $t_i$ is the date each $W_i$ fell due and time is measured in years.

Attorneys’ fees under fee-shifting provisions

Statutory fee-shifting. Many wage statutes include fee-shifting provisions that award reasonable attorneys’ fees and costs to prevailing plaintiffs (and sometimes to prevailing defendants). The policy is to encourage private enforcement by making litigation economically feasible for employees who generally have smaller individual claims.

Standards for awarding fees. The lodestar method is the most common: multiply reasonable hours expended by a reasonable hourly rate to produce a lodestar figure. Courts then consider whether an upward or downward adjustment is necessary based on factors such as the results obtained, the complexity of the case, and the risk of contingent representation. Elements courts evaluate include the skill required, customary rates in the community, the attorney’s reputation and experience, duplication of effort, and whether the case was brought on a contingency.

Prevailing party requirement. Fee awards commonly require a party to be “prevailing,” which courts interpret as obtaining a material alteration of the parties’ legal relationship via judgment, settlement, or enforceable consent decree. Partial success may be compensated proportionally; where a plaintiff achieves significant relief for the class or collective, courts may award fees even if some claims fail.

Timing and enforcement. Fee awards can be made at final judgment or by separate post-judgment motions; some statutes permit interim fee awards. Fee awards are enforceable as judgments and may be subject to post-judgment interest or appeal.

Special issues: common fund, lodestar multipliers, and class actions. In class or collective actions, fee awards may be calculated as a percentage of the common fund created for class members or via lodestar. Some courts prefer percentage-of-recovery for large common funds; others prefer lodestar to ensure careful accounting of hours and rates. Multipliers above the lodestar are sometimes applied to account for contingency risk or exceptional results, but courts scrutinize such multipliers for reasonableness.

Interaction between fee-shifting and other remedies. Fee awards are typically separate from compensatory damages, liquidated damages, and penalties. Whether fee awards are paid from the recovery (reducing what class members receive) or from the employer’s pocket depends on the structure of settlement or judgment and applicable rules about cy pres and attorney liens. Procedural posture — settlement structure, class certification, and distribution mechanisms — affects how and when fee awards are taxed against the recovery.

# Standards and prerequisites for injunctive relief

Nature and types of injunctions. Injunctive relief in wage-and-hour law may be preliminary (temporary restraining orders or preliminary injunctions) or permanent. Injunctions may compel payment (specific performance), order employers to change pay practices, prohibit continuing unlawful practices, or require recordkeeping. Because wage remedies often include adequate money damages, courts carefully assess whether equitable relief is appropriate.

Preliminary injunction standards. Courts typically require plaintiffs seeking preliminary injunctive relief to show: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits (or at least serious questions going to the merits and a balance of hardships tipping in their favor in some jurisdictions), (2) irreparable harm absent an injunction, (3) that the balance of hardships favors the movant, and (4) that an injunction is in the public interest. The exact formulation varies by jurisdiction, but irreparable harm and inadequacy of legal remedies are central.

Irreparable harm and adequacy of legal remedies. To obtain injunctive relief, plaintiffs usually must show that legal remedies (i.e., money damages) are inadequate. This may be satisfied where continuing violations would produce harm that is difficult to quantify, where employers are likely to dissipate assets, or where ongoing statutory violations injure public interest. Mere economic loss does not automatically constitute irreparable harm, but when violations threaten ongoing losses or systemic abuse, courts are more willing to enjoin.

Likelihood of success and scope. The likelihood of success assessment considers the substantive elements of the wage claim: whether plaintiff can prove employee status, hours worked, applicable statutory or contractual entitlements, and willfulness. The scope of injunctive relief must be tailored to the violation; courts implement the equitable principle of narrow tailoring to ensure the injunction addresses the specific unlawful conduct without overbroad regulation of lawful practices.

Permanent injunctions and standards. A permanent injunction requires the plaintiff to demonstrate (1) actual success on the merits, (2) that remedies at law are inadequate to compensate for the injury, (3) that considering the balance of hardships and public interest, equitable relief is warranted, and (4) that the remedy is appropriately tailored. Permanent injunctive relief against ongoing wage violations is often grounded in a finding that the employer’s conduct will likely continue absent court intervention.

Special procedural issues: class or collective posture. In representative actions, courts evaluate whether injunctive relief can bind non-parties, and whether class certification is required for broad injunctive orders. Injunctions that affect non-parties may implicate due process and must be carefully justified.

Equitable defenses and limitations. Equitable remedies are subject to defenses such as laches, unclean hands, and equitable estoppel. Employers asserting such defenses may show that plaintiffs’ delay prejudiced them, that plaintiffs acted improperly, or that granting equitable relief would be unjust under the circumstances. Moreover, courts may decline to issue injunctions if the employer made a good faith effort to comply or corrected the practice promptly upon notification.

Remedies involving recordkeeping and supervision. Injunctions often include affirmative duties — for example, requiring recordkeeping improvements, posting notices, or training managers. Courts can supervise compliance through monitoring, requiring reporting, or appointing special masters. However, courts avoid micro-managing business operations and confine equitable orders to remedial measures proportionate to the violation.

Conclusion

Remedies in wage-and-hour litigation combine statutory formulas and equitable discretion. Back pay and overtime premiums form the core compensation for employees; liquidated damages and civil penalties add layers of deterrence and punishment; prejudgment interest and fee-shifting provisions address the costs and delay of litigation; injunctive relief addresses ongoing or systemic wrongs. The availability and scope of each remedy depend on statutory language, doctrinal defenses (such as good-faith or laches), and procedural posture (individual, collective, or class action). Understanding how courts calculate awards and balance equitable considerations is essential to assess potential outcomes and to design effective litigation and settlement strategies.
