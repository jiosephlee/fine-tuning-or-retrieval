Introduction

Article III of the Constitution confines federal judicial power to “cases” and “controversies.” Two central doctrines that flow from that limitation are standing and mootness. Standing determines whether a plaintiff may bring a case at the outset; mootness determines whether a case remains justiciable as circumstances change during litigation. Both doctrines focus on whether there is a concrete, live dispute that a court can resolve. Payments, stipulations, offers of relief, or other unilateral acts by a defendant can affect both standing and mootness in important ways. This chapter explains the legal framework courts use to assess whether a dispute remains justiciable after some or all relief is tendered, how partial payments or concessions affect remaining claims, the special considerations that arise for claims seeking injunctive relief or statutory penalties, and the equitable powers appellate courts exercise when a case becomes moot on appeal (including vacatur and remand). The goal is to give you the doctrinal tools and procedural sensibilities you need before reading judicial opinions that turn on these issues.

# Stipulated payments and remaining live controversies

At core, Article III requires a continuing “case or controversy” between adverse parties. When a defendant offers or makes a payment to a plaintiff, courts ask whether that action eradicates the concrete dispute the plaintiff brought to court.

Two related but distinct inquiries animate the analysis: standing (initially, did the plaintiff have a cognizable injury that can be redressed by the court?) and mootness (subsequently, has a change in circumstances removed the live dispute so that the court can no longer provide meaningful relief?). The paradigmatic tests are derived from Lujan for standing and from the mootness doctrine for subsequent justiciability.

Standing. A plaintiff must show injury in fact, causation, and redressability. A stipulated payment that cures the injury in fact and is adequate to provide the relief the plaintiff seeks will often render the plaintiff unable to satisfy redressability, because there is no prospective relief the court can grant to remedy an already-cured injury. But this depends on the relief sought. For example, if the plaintiff seeks only monetary compensation for a past loss and the defendant pays that sum, standing to pursue monetary relief is generally extinguished because the plaintiff no longer suffers a cognizable injury that a court can redress. By contrast, if the plaintiff also seeks declaratory relief, equitable relief, or prospective relief that would alter future conduct, the payment may not suffice to eliminate standing.

Mootness. Even if a plaintiff had standing when suit was filed, intervening events (including defendant payments) can render the case moot. The controlling principle is whether the court can grant any effectual relief that will redress the plaintiff’s injury. If a stipulated payment resolves all aspects of the plaintiff’s claim such that a favorable judgment would not change the plaintiff’s legal situation, the case is generally moot. However, if the plaintiff retains a legally cognizable interest—ongoing or prospective harms, collateral consequences, or other non-monetary stakes—mootness is not established by a mere payment.

The sufficiency of a payment or stipulation depends on completeness and certainty. A conditional, partial, or contested tender may not remove the dispute. Courts scrutinize whether the offer was unconditional, sufficiently certain (e.g., whether funds were actually transferred or merely offered), and whether it fully addresses each claim and the forms of relief sought.

Procedural posture matters. If the payment occurs prior to filing suit and the plaintiff nevertheless proceeds, the defendant may move to dismiss for lack of standing. When payment occurs during litigation, parties frequently present stipulations to the court that the defendant has paid specified amounts. Courts accept such stipulations as a basis for dismissal only when the stipulation unambiguously resolves the contested legal issue; otherwise, the court will evaluate whether any remaining claims preserve a live controversy.

# Partial resolution and the continuing case-or-controversy requirement

Payments or stipulations may resolve some parts of a case while leaving others intact. Two principal problems arise: (1) distinguishing resolved from unresolved claims and (2) determining whether the remaining claims suffice to maintain federal jurisdiction.

Severance of claims. When some claims are resolved by payment and others remain, courts may (a) dismiss the resolved claims and proceed on the remaining claims, (b) sever claims into separate actions, or (c) dismiss the entire action if the remaining claims are moot or lack jurisdictional footing. The decision often hinges on whether the claims are factually and legally separable and whether the remaining issues present a live, non-frivolous dispute. When claims are closely intertwined, courts may be reluctant to carve away resolved portions if doing so would leave the court without a coherent remaining controversy.

Ancillary and collateral issues. Even where a payment cures the primary monetary injury, ancillary issues—such as allocation of attorney’s fees, costs, or statutory interest—can preserve a live controversy. For example, if a defendant pays the principal amount but disputes entitlement to fees or contends the payment was insufficient as a matter of law, those controversies may remain justiciable. Similarly, disputes about liability, restitution, or other equitable remedies may survive a payment of damages.

Procedural effect of partial mootness. Partial mootness can influence jurisdiction in several ways. If a court’s subject-matter jurisdiction depends on the existence of a live claim (e.g., federal-question jurisdiction predicated on a particular claim), elimination of that claim can strip jurisdiction unless other claims maintain it. Appellate jurisdiction is likewise affected by which claims remain live at the time of appeal; appellate courts generally decide only live controversies and lack jurisdiction over claims that have become moot during the appeal unless an exception applies.

The role of parties’ stipulations. Parties may stipulate to facts, payment, or relief and ask the court to enter judgment accordingly. While courts typically accept stipulations that clarify or resolve matters, they are not bound mechanically to enter an order that would be an advisory opinion or outside the court’s Article III power. Courts will generally enter judgment where the stipulation resolves the controversy and no remaining justiciable claims exist. However, courts retain discretion to scrutinize whether stipulations are genuine, complete, and not attempts to manipulate jurisdiction or avoid appellate review.

# Impact on claims for penalties and injunctive relief

Different forms of relief interact differently with stipulations and payments because they address different kinds of injuries. Distinguish statutory penalties (often intended to punish or deter, or intended to vindicate public policies), compensatory damages for past injury, and equitable or prospective relief (injunctions, declaratory judgments, prospective restitution).

Monetary payments and penalty claims. Statutory penalties present special challenges. Some statutory schemes allow private suits to recover penalties, sometimes for the benefit of the public, sometimes partly for the private plaintiff. A defendant’s payment to a plaintiff may not moot a claim for statutory penalties when the penalty is payable to the state, or where the statutory purpose is one of public deterrence that the plaintiff’s individual recoupment cannot fully satisfy. Courts ask whether the plaintiff’s payment resolves the plaintiff’s personal stake and whether there are non-monetary or public interests that keep the controversy alive. If the statutory penalty serves a public enforcement function, merely paying the plaintiff may not eliminate the public interest in resolving whether the defendant’s conduct violated the statute.

Some statutes also permit recovery of attorney’s fees, statutory multipliers, or penalties that are not fully satisfied by the principal amount. A defendant’s payment of principal may leave open claims for these additional statutory elements, preserving a live controversy. Moreover, if the defendant’s payment does not fully conform to the statutory formula for penalties (for example, a statute sets a per-violation penalty and the payment does not match that computation), courts may find that controversy remains.

Injunctions and prospective relief. An offer to pay money rarely moots a plaintiff’s request for prospective injunctive relief when the plaintiff alleges an ongoing or recurrent violation. Injunctive relief is forward-looking: it aims to prevent future harm. A payment addressing past injury does not necessarily redress the plaintiff’s concern about future harm. Therefore, claims for injunctive relief often survive a monetary stipulation.

When a defendant offers a covenant not to repeat the challenged conduct (a “covenant not to sue,” “no-repeat” assurance, or injunction-equivalent promise), courts evaluate whether that assurance is credible and whether it fully removes the risk of future harm. The “voluntary cessation” doctrine governs many of these cases: if a defendant voluntarily stops the allegedly wrongful behavior, the defendant bears the burden of showing that the cessation is permanent and that there is no reasonable expectation that the wrong will recur. Where the defendant’s promise is backed by demonstrable, enforceable commitments (for example, changes in policy, contractual obligations, or binding injunctive relief already in place), courts are more likely to find the claim moot. But a bare, unilateral promise of future non-repetition, especially without independent enforcement mechanisms or when the defendant retains incentives to resume the conduct, is frequently insufficient.

Declaratory relief. The relationship between payments and requests for declaratory relief parallels that of injunctive relief. If a plaintiff seeks a judicial declaration about the legality of a defendant’s conduct beyond any monetary damage, a payment that fully compensates past loss may not obviate the need for a declaratory determination, particularly if that determination has continuing practical consequences for the parties.

Collateral consequences and continuing harms. Collateral consequences can keep an action alive despite payment. Examples include reputational consequences, ongoing regulatory consequences, contractual disabilities, or legal consequences that persist beyond the payment of money. Courts may treat such collateral effects as continuing injuries that support injunctive or declaratory relief.

Third-party and public interests. When relief sought implicates the public interest—such as enforcement of consumer-protection laws or civil rights statutes—courts may be reluctant to let a defendant’s unilateral payment extinguish the claim. This is particularly true where the statute vests enforcement authority in a government entity or where the statutory scheme contemplates public remedies that cannot be conveyed by a private party’s payment.

# Court authority to vacate and remand for jurisdictional determinations

When a case becomes moot during appeal, appellate courts have several equitable tools to shape the disposition, chief among them vacatur of lower-court judgments and remand for further proceedings. These doctrines aim to preserve fairness between parties and to prevent a litigant from manipulating the judicial process to erase an adverse decision by mooting the case after judgment.

Vacatur. Vacatur is the remedy by which an appellate court sets aside a lower-court judgment. One traditional justification is to prevent a party from benefiting from a mootness-causing act by allowing the lower-court judgment to stand as precedent or as a win when the dispute can no longer be litigated on the merits. The controlling considerations include the equities between the parties and the public interest. Courts consider whether the party seeking vacatur caused the mootness voluntarily, whether that party was a successful litigant in the lower court, whether the party had fully litigated the issues, and whether vacatur would be equitable.

When a defendant tends to moot a case on appeal by tendering the relief sought, courts evaluate whether such mootness was self-inflicted and deliberate and whether vacatur is necessary to prevent that party from reaping a windfall. Conversely, if mootness results from external factors beyond the control of the parties, vacatur may be inappropriate because vacatur would unfairly penalize the party that prevailed below through no fault of the other.

Remand for jurisdictional determinations. Appellate courts sometimes remand a case to the district court for further findings, particularly when questions of jurisdiction were not adequately addressed below or when facts material to standing or mootness remain undeveloped. Remand may serve multiple purposes: to allow the lower court to determine whether the stipulation genuinely resolves the controversy, to consider whether any exceptions to mootness apply, or to craft an appropriate disposition that preserves judicial economy and the parties’ rights.

The district court’s role on remand can include deciding whether the payment or stipulation was unconditional and final, whether the remaining claims present a live controversy, and whether any exceptions to mootness (such as voluntary cessation, capable-of-repetition doctrine, or collateral consequences) apply. The appellate court may remand with instructions to vacate or modify prior rulings if the jurisdictional inquiry reveals that the court lacked authority when issuing those rulings.

Standards and burdens on vacatur/remand. The party seeking vacatur typically requests it on equitable grounds, and courts exercise discretion. The moving party bears the burden of persuading the court that vacatur is warranted under the circumstances. Key factors include whether the moving party caused the mootness, whether the party’s conduct was designed to manipulate the judicial process, whether the parties had a fair opportunity to litigate the issues, and whether vacatur will serve the public interest in developing settled precedent.

The appellate court must also be mindful of finality and the efficient administration of justice. Vacatur and remand are not automatic remedies; they are calibrated responses intended to prevent misuse of the doctrine of mootness while maintaining respect for judicial resources and the legitimate outcomes of fully litigated disputes.

Conclusion

Payments, stipulations, and offers of relief can terminate or materially alter federal cases by eliminating standing or rendering claims moot, but their effect depends on the nature and completeness of the relief, the kinds of remedies sought, and the presence of collateral consequences or public interests. Monetary payments commonly resolve past-damage claims but are less likely to moot claims for injunctive or declaratory relief or statutory penalties that implicate public enforcement objectives. When cases become moot during appeal, appellate courts have equitable tools—most notably vacatur and remand—to allocate the equities and preserve the integrity of the judicial process. Understanding these doctrines requires attention to the form of relief sought, the timing and sincerity of concessions, and the procedural posture in which the concession occurs; these factors determine whether a statement of stipulation is a final settlement or merely a tactical maneuver that cannot deprive courts of the power to adjudicate.
