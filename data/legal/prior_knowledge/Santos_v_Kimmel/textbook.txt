Introduction

A motion to dismiss under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) tests the legal sufficiency of a complaint. At this early stage, courts evaluate whether the plaintiff's pleaded facts, taken in the light most favorable to the plaintiff (with certain limits), state a claim that is plausible on its face. The law governing this inquiry defines what allegations must be credited, what may be disregarded, and what tools a court may use when deciding whether dismissal is appropriate. This chapter explains the central pleading rules and doctrines that govern Rule 12(b)(6) decisions: the Rule 8(a) “short and plain statement” requirement as shaped by the plausibility standard; what a court must accept as true and what it may disregard; when affirmative defenses may be resolved on the pleadings; the role of exhibits and documents incorporated by reference; the use of judicial notice of public records (including issues that frequently arise with copyright registrations); and the standards for leave to amend and the concept of futility.

# Rule 8(a) and the plausibility standard (Twombly/Iqbal)

Rule 8(a) requires that a pleading that states a claim for relief contain: (1) a short and plain statement of the grounds for the court’s jurisdiction; (2) a short and plain statement of the claim showing that the pleader is entitled to relief; and (3) a demand for the relief sought. The most contested prong in practice is the second: the requirement to show entitlement to relief. The modern federal pleading regime is shaped by two Supreme Court decisions that articulated the “plausibility” inquiry commonly referred to as Twombly and Iqbal.

The plausibility standard is a two-step analytic framework. First, a court identifies the pleadings’ factual allegations and distinguishes them from legal conclusions or conclusory statements. Conclusions couched as factual allegations—labels, formulaic recitations of elements, or bare legal conclusions—are not accepted as true for purposes of the motion. Second, the court assumes well-pleaded factual allegations to be true and determines whether those facts, if accepted, plausibly suggest an entitlement to relief. Plausibility requires more than a sheer possibility that a defendant acted unlawfully; it requires allegations that allow the court to draw the reasonable inference that the defendant is liable. The inquiry is context-specific and considers the factual content pleaded, the elements of the claim, and common sense.

Plausibility is not a probability requirement; it does not demand detailed evidentiary facts or a prima facie case. Rather, the standard asks whether the facts alleged raise a reasonable expectation that discovery will reveal evidence to support the claim. Courts therefore look for factual content that permits the court to infer wrongdoing, rather than bare assertions of misconduct. Where a complaint alleges facts that are equally consistent with lawful conduct, the claim will often fail the plausibility test because the pleaded facts do not support a reasonable inference of illegality.

The plausibility standard interacts with other pleading rules and doctrines. When federal statutes provide a particularized pleading regime or special elements, the court applies plausibility to the elements actually implicated. For certain claims—e.g., fraud—Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 9(b) imposes a heightened particularity requirement, and courts apply the plausibility framework while ensuring that aspirational rules like particularity are honored.

# Rule 12(b)(6) framework and what must be accepted as true

A Rule 12(b)(6) motion challenges the legal sufficiency of the complaint; it does not resolve factual disputes except in narrow circumstances. The framework the court applies has several core components.

First, procedural posture matters. On a Rule 12(b)(6) motion, courts typically accept all well-pleaded factual allegations as true and draw all reasonable inferences in favor of the plaintiff. The court does not, at this stage, weigh evidence, assess credibility, or decide competing inferences beyond what is reasonable from the pleadings. The basic standard is whether, accepting the plaintiff’s allegations as true, the complaint states a claim that is plausible.

Second, courts do not accept as true legal conclusions, bare recitals of claim elements, or conclusory statements. These are identified and disregarded in the initial step of the plausibility test. For example, a complaint that merely alleges “defendant acted negligently” without factual allegations describing the conduct or contextual facts will not survive. Likewise, formulaic legal conclusions about intent, knowledge, or motive that lack supporting facts are treated as conclusory.

Third, courts draw reasonable inferences from the facts alleged. A well-pleaded factual allegation may permit multiple inferences; the court must determine whether the inference of liability is plausible in light of the pleaded facts and common sense. The standard is permissive toward the pleader but not unbounded: inferences must be reasonable, not extravagant.

Fourth, the court’s review is usually limited to the complaint and any documents properly considered with it (attachments, documents incorporated by reference) and items of which the court may take judicial notice. If a court considers matters outside the pleadings — such as affidavits, depositions, or other extra-pleading evidence — the motion to dismiss must be treated as one for summary judgment under Rule 56, with the attendant procedural protections (notice to the parties and an opportunity for discovery and response), unless the extraneous matters fall into exceptions discussed below.

Finally, procedural doctrines tied to pleading timing and specificity also govern. For claims that require special pleading (e.g., fraud or mistake under Rule 9(b)), a plaintiff must plead circumstances constituting the fraud with particularity, even though the general plausibility standard still applies to the core factual allegations.

# Affirmative defenses resolved on the pleadings when apparent on the face of the complaint

Affirmative defenses generally place the burden on the defendant to prove facts outside the plaintiff’s prima facie case. Because affirmative defenses often raise factual matters external to the complaint, they typically cannot be resolved on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion. Yet courts recognize exceptions: where the complaint itself establishes the existence of an affirmative defense — i.e., the defense is apparent on the face of the complaint — dismissal on the pleadings may be appropriate.

Commonly litigated affirmative defenses that may be resolved on the face of the complaint include statute of limitations, release, waiver, prior adjudication (claim preclusion or issue preclusion in narrow circumstances), lack of ownership where the complaint alleges facts incompatible with ownership, and extinguishment of a right by express contractual language pleaded by the plaintiff. The key principle is that if the complaint’s allegations include dates, documents, or other facts that conclusively establish an affirmative defense, the defendant may invoke that fact pattern on a motion to dismiss.

For the statute of limitations, courts will dismiss where the complaint discloses all material facts necessary to establish the statute of limitations defense, including the timing of the alleged wrongful acts and the date of accrual. If the complaint alleges facts showing the claim accrued outside the governing limitations period and the plaintiff cannot plead facts that would invoke a tolling doctrine or relate-back, dismissal is proper. However, where resolution requires inquiry into disputed facts (e.g., date of discovery, equitable tolling), dismissal is generally inappropriate at the pleading stage.

Courts approach such dismissals cautiously because resolving affirmative defenses on the pleadings can improperly deprive a plaintiff of the opportunity to plead contrary facts. The defendant bears the burden to demonstrate that the complaint itself establishes the defense. If the defense rests on documents beyond the complaint or facts not undisputed, the court will typically deny dismissal or convert the motion into one for summary judgment.

# Consideration of exhibits and documents incorporated by reference

Although a Rule 12(b)(6) court is generally limited to the complaint, several doctrines allow consideration of documents without converting the motion to summary judgment. The two principal doctrines are (1) the incorporation-by-reference doctrine and (2) the “integral to the complaint” or “documents central to plaintiff’s claim” doctrine.

Under the incorporation-by-reference doctrine, if a plaintiff’s complaint explicitly refers to a document and the document is central to the plaintiff’s claim, the court may treat the document as part of the complaint. This is most straightforward when the plaintiff attaches the document to the complaint as an exhibit or when the complaint quotes or relies heavily on the terms of a document. When a document is incorporated by reference, the court may consider its contents for purposes of a motion to dismiss.

The “integral” or “central” doctrine is similar: where the plaintiff’s claim is based on the contents of a document, or where the document is integral to the claim (for instance, a written contract that the plaintiff alleges was breached and that is central to the claim), the court may examine the document because it is necessarily embraced by the pleadings. Examining such documents prevents plaintiffs from pleading around unfavorable documents by omitting them and enables courts to apply the plausibility standard with the full context of the operative documentary evidence already before the pleader.

Courts must be careful to ensure that a document is properly considered. The court may consider documents attached to the complaint, documents relied upon in the complaint and central to the plaintiff’s claims, and matters of public record subject to judicial notice. If the defendant attaches a document to a motion to dismiss that is not incorporated by reference and not subject to judicial notice, the court generally must either exclude it or treat the motion as one for summary judgment.

When a document considered by the court contradicts the allegations in the complaint, the court may give the document controlling effect, and the complaint’s contrary allegations may be ignored. For example, if a plaintiff sues on an assumed oral agreement but attaches a signed writing that shows different terms, the writing may displace the pleading. Thus, the incorporation doctrine can be dispositive.

# Judicial notice of public records (including copyright registrations)

Federal Rule of Evidence 201 authorizes courts to take judicial notice of adjudicative facts that are not subject to reasonable dispute because they are generally known within the trial court’s territorial jurisdiction or can be accurately and readily determined from reliable sources. Judicial notice may be taken at any stage of the proceeding and may be taken sua sponte. The scope of judicial notice at the motion-to-dismiss stage is limited: courts may note the existence and contents of certain public records and matters of public record and treat them as established for purposes of the pleadings.

Public records commonly judicially noticed include court filings and orders, legislative materials, governmental reports, official statistical data, and other documents from reliable public sources. Judicial notice is appropriate for the fact of existence or nonexistence and for indisputable facts within those records, but courts should not judicially notice disputed facts contained within public documents when those facts are subject to reasonable dispute.

Copyright registrations often arise in litigation and present particularities. The Copyright Act provides that a certificate of registration constitutes prima facie evidence of the validity of the copyright and of the facts stated in the certificate as of the certificate’s date (17 U.S.C. § 410(c)). In practice, courts may judicially notice the existence of a registration (or consult the Copyright Office records) to determine whether a registration was issued and when. The registration’s existence and issuance date are typically appropriate for judicial notice because they are ascertainable from a public and reliable source. However, the legal effect of the registration — for example, whether it conclusively establishes ownership or the merits of the claim — depends on statutory and doctrinal rules: preexisting nonregistration or errors in the registration can affect how much evidentiary weight the registration receives. While a registration creates a presumption of validity, that presumption can be rebutted by evidence, and judicial notice does not remove contested substantive issues from adjudication.

Courts should not take judicial notice of disputed facts merely because they appear in public records. For example, factual statements in an investor presentation, press release, or a government database may be contested and may not be appropriate for notice if they are subject to reasonable dispute. The safe path is to take notice of the fact that a public document exists and of indisputable, noncontroversial facts within public records, but to avoid accepting as conclusive contested factual assertions contained in those records.

# Leave to amend and futility

When a complaint is dismissed under Rule 12(b)(6), the court must consider whether dismissal should be with or without leave to amend. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 15(a) provides that leave to amend shall be freely given when justice so requires. Courts interpret this to mean that plaintiffs should ordinarily be granted an opportunity to amend their pleadings to cure deficiencies, unless there is a reason to deny leave consistent with the principles of Rule 15: undue delay, bad faith or dilatory motive, repeated failure to cure deficiencies by amendments previously allowed, undue prejudice to the opposing party, or futility of amendment.

Futility is an important ground for denial. Amendment is futile if the proposed amended complaint would still fail to state a claim under Rule 12(b)(6). When a defendant argues futility, courts test the proposed amendment under the same pleading standards applied to the current complaint: the court assumes the allegations of the proposed amended complaint as true, disregards conclusory legal statements, and assesses whether the pleaded facts would plausibly entitle the plaintiff to relief. If the proposed amendment cannot cure the complaint’s deficiencies — for example, if the proposed facts remain legally insufficient to establish an essential element or are time-barred without a viable tolling or relation-back theory — leave to amend may be denied as futile.

Relation-back doctrine under Rule 15(c) also intersects with futility. If an amendment would relate back to the filing date of the original pleading for purposes of the statute of limitations, the amendment may not be futile on statute-of-limitations grounds. Conversely, if the relation-back requirements cannot be met and the amendment would be time-barred, the court may properly find futility.

Courts also consider prejudice to the defendant. Leaving a claim with bare or conclusory allegations may impose discovery burdens or strategic harms; yet the mere prospect of litigation cost is usually not sufficient to deny leave. Denial for undue delay typically requires a showing that the plaintiff knew of the claim or the facts earlier and intentionally delayed. Multiple prior amendments and repeated failure to cure identify a pattern that may justify denial.

In striking the balance, courts favor resolution on the merits and generally permit a curative amendment unless one of the Rule 15 factors weighs strongly against it. A plaintiff facing dismissal should attempt to demonstrate how additional facts would cure the pleading deficiency and, where appropriate, submit a proposed amended complaint to streamline judicial consideration of futility.

Conclusion

Rule 12(b)(6) motions are central to the federal litigation lifecycle; they define the boundary between pleading and discovery and demand careful application of doctrinal limits. The plausibility standard requires factual content that supports a reasonable inference of liability but does not demand a full evidentiary showing. Courts must accept well-pleaded factual allegations and reasonable inferences, but they may disregard legal conclusions and consider certain documents attached to or incorporated into the complaint as well as adjudicative facts from public records subject to judicial notice. Affirmative defenses apparent on the face of the complaint may justify dismissal at the pleading stage, but courts proceed cautiously because factual disputes often preclude early resolution. Finally, leave to amend is generally granted unless amendment would be futile or other Rule 15 factors counsel against it; futility is assessed by applying the same pleading standards to the proposed amendment. Mastery of these rules and their application prepares a student to understand and analyze judicial opinions resolving Rule 12(b)(6) motions.

Introduction

Dismissals under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 12(b)(6) for failure to state a claim are among the most common early dispositive rulings in federal court. Because such orders often terminate a claim before discovery and adjudication on the merits, the doctrines governing appellate review of Rule 12(b)(6) rulings are central to civil litigation strategy and to the allocation of adjudicative resources. This chapter explains when a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal can be appealed, the standard and scope of appellate review, the limits on the appellate record, and doctrines that allow an appellate court to affirm on alternative grounds or to disregard harmless errors. The guidance below focuses on doctrinal rules and practical consequences for how courts apply legal standards to the record and how procedural posture affects review.

# Final judgment rule and appealability

The starting point for appellate review is the final judgment rule embodied in 28 U.S.C. § 1291: federal courts of appeals have jurisdiction to review “final decisions” of the district courts. A final decision is one that “ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the district court to do but execute the judgment.” A dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) can be a final decision if it disposes of the claim or claims such that the district court has no further matters to address. Whether a dismissal is final, and therefore appealable as of right, turns on the nature of the dismissal: dismissal with prejudice typically is final because it precludes refiling in the district court, whereas dismissal without prejudice may not be final if the plaintiff retains an opportunity to amend or otherwise pursue the claim in the district court.

The finality inquiry is fact-specific and procedural posture-sensitive. If the district court dismisses a complaint without leave to amend and grants judgment against the plaintiff, that order ordinarily qualifies as a final judgment and may be appealed immediately. By contrast, if the court dismisses for failure to state a claim but expressly grants leave to amend, the order is generally interlocutory because the plaintiff retains the opportunity to cure defects. Even where an order is labeled “with prejudice” or “without leave,” appellate courts will look beyond labels to determine whether anything remains for the district court to do.

Not all interlocutory decisions are unreviewable. There are established exceptions to the final judgment rule. The collateral order doctrine permits immediate appeal of a small class of decisions that are conclusive, resolve important questions separate from the merits, and would be effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment. The collateral order doctrine is narrowly applied and seldom reaches typical Rule 12(b)(6) dismissals because those dismissals ordinarily resolve the merits of a claim rather than an ancillary, collateral issue. Section 1292(b) allows the district judge and the court of appeals, by certification, to authorize immediate appeal of an interlocutory order if the order involves a controlling question of law and there are substantial grounds for difference of opinion; appellate courts review such certified interlocutory appeals under the terms of the certification. Mandamus is an extraordinary remedy available in exceptional circumstances to compel or prevent district-court action, but it is rarely invoked successfully to review a Rule 12(b)(6) decision because mandamus requires showing absence of adequate alternative relief and a clear and indisputable right.

In short, the appealability of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal depends on whether the order is final in the statutory sense or falls within a narrow interlocutory exception. Practically, practitioners should be attentive to whether the dismissal is with prejudice or without leave to amend, whether the district court certified the order under § 1292(b), and whether any extraordinary remedy is appropriate.

# De novo standard of review for Rule 12(b)(6) dismissals

When a court of appeals has jurisdiction to review a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, the governing standard of review is de novo for questions of law and for the legal sufficiency of the complaint. De novo review means the appellate court examines the district court’s legal conclusions anew, without deference, and asks whether, applying the governing pleading standards, the complaint states a claim upon which relief can be granted.

De novo review does not mean that appellate courts reweigh factual evidence or entertain new factfinding; rather, it is a fresh legal assessment of whether the pleaded facts, taken as true (to the extent appropriate), suffice to state a claim. The de novo inquiry is shaped by the controlling pleading law, which currently reflects decisions that require that a complaint contain enough factual matter to state a claim that is plausible on its face. Under this framework, courts distinguish factual allegations, which must be accepted as true for purposes of the motion, from legal conclusions, which are not entitled to the presumption of truth. The appellate court evaluates the complaint by isolating conclusory statements and determining whether the remaining factual allegations permit a reasonable inference that the defendant is liable.

Under de novo review the appellate court also assesses questions of statutory interpretation or constitutional law that may govern the availability of a cause of action. When the district court’s dismissal rests on pure questions of law—such as whether a statutory provision creates a private cause of action—the appellate court will independently interpret the statute and precedent. Where the dismissal rests on mixed questions—application of a legal standard to particular factual allegations—the appellate court examines whether the legal standard was correctly applied to the pleaded facts.

The de novo standard also applies to determinations about whether materials outside the pleadings may properly be considered without converting the motion to dismiss into a motion for summary judgment. If the district court considered extraneous materials and treated the motion as one for summary judgment, the appellate court reviews whether that conversion was proper and whether summary judgment standards (including Rule 56) were satisfied. If the conversion was improper, the appellate court often confines review to the complaint and any properly considered materials.

# Scope of the appellate record

Appellate review of a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal is ordinarily confined to the record that was before the district court at the time of its ruling. The appellate court evaluates whether the complaint, along with documents properly considered by the district court, supplied an adequate basis for dismissal. This focus on the existing record preserves the trial-court role in fact development and respects procedural rules about preservation of issues.

What counts as properly considered materials is a critical part of the scope analysis. The general rule is that a district court deciding a motion to dismiss must consider only the complaint, documents attached to the complaint, documents incorporated by reference in the complaint, matters of which the court may take judicial notice, and exhibits central to plaintiff’s claim that are referred to in the complaint. Courts may also consider documents attached to a motion to dismiss if the complaint necessarily relies on them and they are authentic, but courts must be cautious to avoid resolving factual disputes inappropriate for the Rule 12(b)(6) stage.

On appeal, an appellate court will consider those same materials. It may take judicial notice of appropriate facts and documents, such as public records, statute, or prior judicial opinions. The appellate record may also include items that the district court formally filed into the record—docket entries, pleadings, and orders. New evidence that was not part of the district court record typically cannot be introduced on appeal to bolster or defeat a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal, because appellate courts do not receive live testimony or hear new evidence in the ordinary course. There are limited exceptions—if the case is remanded, parties may present new evidence to the district court, which can then be the subject of subsequent review; alternatively, if the appellate court determines that the dismissal must be treated as summary judgment due to reliance on extrinsic materials, the court may remand for fuller factfinding under Rule 56.

Preservation is important. Arguments or factual matters not raised below are generally forfeited on appeal. Appellate courts typically will not consider novel theories raised for the first time on appeal unless they involve pure questions of law and doing so would not prejudice the other party. Similarly, a plaintiff who wishes the district court or the appellate court to consider facts outside the complaint must have raised those facts at the district court stage in a way that is procedurally proper.

# Affirmance on any ground supported by the record

An appellate court’s power to affirm a lower court’s judgment extends beyond the specific reasoning articulated by the district court. If the judgment is correct on any legal ground that is supported by the record, the appellate court may affirm even if the district court gave different reasons or provided insufficient analysis. This principle prevents reversal for error when the outcome is nonetheless correct under alternate legal theories. The appellate court, however, must ensure that the alternative ground invoked is purely legal or can be resolved on the existing record without additional factfinding.

There are limits. The appellate court typically may not affirm on an alternative ground that was not advanced and that would require further factual development or evidence that was not presented to the district court. For example, if affirmance would rest on a factual determination or the resolution of contested facts that were not decided below, the appellate court should not resolve those issues for the first time on appeal; instead, it will remand for the district court to consider the unresolved factual matters. Similarly, if the alternative ground was not briefed and its resolution would prejudice the opposing party’s rights, the appellate court may deem the issue forfeited.

This doctrine has practical consequences for litigants. When defending a dismissal on appeal, appellees should identify and preserve alternative, purely legal grounds that support dismissal based on the existing record. When attacking a dismissal, appellants should anticipate and address plausible alternative legal grounds that an appellate court could use to affirm.

# Harmless error and alternative holdings

Even where an appellate court identifies error in the district court’s reasoning or in procedural handling of a motion to dismiss, the harmless error doctrine may uphold the judgment if the error did not affect substantial rights or the ultimate outcome. Harmless error analysis asks whether the district court’s error was likely to have affected the judgment; if not, the error is harmless and reversal is not warranted. The burden often rests with the party challenging the error to show that it was prejudicial.

In the Rule 12(b)(6) context, harmless error can arise in several ways. A district court may mistakenly characterize a legal standard, include erroneous statements of law, or consider extraneous materials improperly. If those mistakes did not alter the essential legal conclusion—because, for instance, the complaint plainly fails to allege elements required by law, or because other determinative defects exist—the appellate court may conclude that the errors were harmless. Conversely, if the error has a plausible effect on the disposition—if, for example, the district court applied the wrong pleading standard in a close case—remand or reversal may be required.

Relatedly, appellate courts frequently issue alternative or supplemental holdings. Even if the court affirms on one ground, it may also articulate alternative reasons for affirmance or explain why certain arguments fail. Alternative holdings are permissible so long as they rest on the record and on legal reasoning properly before the court. Such holdings serve both to clarify the law and to provide a fuller basis for the judgment should further review occur. Appellate courts will typically avoid issuing advisory opinions that decide issues the parties did not preserve or that require factfinding beyond the record.

The interaction between harmless error, alternative holdings, and preservation rules means that litigants should carefully preserve arguments at the trial level and present fully developed legal reasoning on appeal. If the district court’s order contains legal deficiencies, an appellant should show that those deficiencies were prejudicial; conversely, an appellee should identify alternate legal grounds that support dismissal under the existing factual record.

Conclusion

Appellate review of Rule 12(b)(6) dismissals is shaped by a balance between finality and the need for correct application of law. Whether an order may be appealed depends on finality rules and narrow interlocutory exceptions; once the court of appeals has jurisdiction, it reviews legal sufficiency de novo, focusing on the complaint and materials properly before the district court. Appellate courts may affirm for any ground supported by the record, but they will not create new factual findings on appeal. Harmless error and alternative holdings further refine appellate power to sustain judgments that are correct on the record despite errors in reasoning. Understanding these doctrines helps litigators craft pleadings, preserve arguments, and anticipate how an appellate tribunal will approach a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal.

Introduction

Copyright law creates a bundle of exclusive rights in original works of authorship fixed in a tangible medium. Before analyzing a judicial opinion about infringement or defenses, a student must understand the statutory core: what kinds of works copyright protects, who is the author and who owns the rights, what exclusive rights the law gives to owners, the role of registration and its procedural prerequisites, key definitional terms such as “publication,” and the principal remedies and defenses that structure litigation. This chapter sets out those foundations, emphasizing the doctrinal tests courts apply, how parties meet burdens of proof, and how procedural posture (for example, pleading, summary judgment, and the request for equitable relief) affects legal analysis.

# Subject matter of copyright and fixation (17 U.S.C. § 102)

Section 102 of the Copyright Act defines the subject matter of copyright as “original works of authorship fixed in any tangible medium of expression…from which the work can be perceived, reproduced, or otherwise communicated, either directly or with the aid of a machine or device.” To be copyrightable a work must satisfy at least two requirements: originality and fixation.

Originality. Originality has two dimensions. First, the work must be independently created by the author — not copied from another source. Second, it must possess at least a minimal degree of creativity. The creativity standard is low: a work need not be novel in a patent sense or possess aesthetic merit; a trivial but original selection, arrangement, or expression suffices so long as it is not merely the unavoidable product of mechanical processes. The doctrine of originality excludes purely discovered facts, but protects expressive selections or presentations of facts that reflect minimal creative choices.

Fixation. Fixation requires that the work be embodied in a sufficiently stable medium such that it can be perceived, reproduced, or otherwise communicated for more than a transitory duration. Fixation is usually satisfied by recording in writing, on film, by sound recording, or on a digital storage medium. Ephemeral performances that are not fixed do not receive copyright protection, although many modern communications are fixed as a matter of course. Fixation also marks the point at which a work becomes copyrightable independent of publication.

Categories of works. Section 102 lists categories of works of authorship: literary works, musical works (with accompanying words), dramatic works (including accompanying music), pantomimes and choreographic works, pictorial, graphic, and sculptural works, motion pictures and other audiovisual works, sound recordings, and architectural works. The statutory categories are illustrative and courts analyze whether a particular subject matter fits within these categories or is excluded. Certain categories, such as ideas, procedures, processes, systems, methods of operation, concepts, principles, or discoveries, are not protected, even if expressed, because the Act protects expression but not underlying ideas.

Separability and useful articles. For visual arts and design, the distinction between utilitarian function and expressive features is critical. A “useful article” that has an intrinsic utilitarian function is not copyrightable for its functional aspects; however, pictorial, graphic, or sculptural features that are conceptually or physically separable from the utilitarian elements may be protected. Courts apply tests of conceptual or physical separability to determine whether expressive elements can receive protection without granting monopoly over functional design.

Idea-expression dichotomy, scènes à faire, and merger. Doctrines that limit protection derive from the idea-expression dichotomy: only expression is protected, not the underlying idea. The merger doctrine applies where there are so few ways to express an idea that the idea and expression merge; in such cases, copyright protection is denied to avoid preventing access to the idea. Scènes à faire refers to stock elements that are standard or indispensable to a particular topic or genre; such elements are not protectable because they are dictated by external factors. These doctrines inform the substantial similarity analysis in infringement litigation.

# Ownership and authorship; works made for hire

Authorship and initial ownership. The initial owner of a copyright is generally the author or authors of the work, defined as the person or persons who create the work. For works created by a single individual, that individual is both author and initial owner unless there is an agreement to the contrary. For joint works, described below, co-authors are co-owners of a single copyright in the work.

Joint authorship. A joint work is a work prepared by two or more authors with the intention that their contributions be merged into inseparable or interdependent parts of a unitary whole. Courts examine both intent to combine contributions and the presence of separately copyrightable contributions that are incorporated into the whole. Co-authorship requires that each contributor contribute copyrightable expression and intend that the contributions be merged. The mere collaboration of multiple persons or provision of ideas, instructions, or mechanical assistance without copyrightable authorship typically does not create joint authorship. Each joint author generally owns an undivided interest in the whole work and may exploit the work nonexclusively, subject to accounting to co-owners for profits and restrictions on licensing of exclusive rights without co-owner consent. Disputes over authorship often hinge on factual questions about creative control, direction, and the nature of each party’s contribution.

Works made for hire. The works-made-for-hire doctrine provides that in certain circumstances the employer or commissioning party, rather than the individual who physically created the work, is considered the author and initial copyright owner. There are two ways a work can be a work made for hire. First, a work prepared by an employee within the scope of employment is a work made for hire. Determining employee status generally requires application of common-law agency principles, analyzing factors such as the hiring party’s right to control the manner and means of creation, the skill required, source of tools and location, duration of the relationship, right to assign additional projects, extent of the hired party’s discretion, method of payment, provision of employee benefits, and tax treatment. No single factor is dispositive; courts weigh them to determine whether a creator is an employee or an independent contractor.

Second, a work specially ordered or commissioned may be a work made for hire if two conditions are satisfied: the parties expressly agree in a written instrument signed by them that the work is a “work made for hire,” and the work falls within one of nine statutory categories (contribution to a collective work, part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work, a translation, a supplementary work, a compilation, an instructional text, a test, answer material for a test, or an atlas). Independent contractors’ works are not works made for hire absent this signed written agreement and category fit.

Consequences of works made for hire. If a work is a work made for hire, the employer or commissioning party is the statutory author and initial copyright owner. This has implications for duration, moral rights in jurisdictions where they apply, and the ability to assert rights without assignment. For creators classified as independent contractors, failure to secure a written work-made-for-hire agreement may preserve the creator’s own ownership rights.

Transfer and exclusive licenses. Copyright is transferable by assignment, will, or operation of law. Transfers of exclusive rights must be in writing and signed by the owner of the rights conveyed. Nonexclusive licenses can be granted orally or by conduct, unless an exclusive license is involved; exclusive licenses historically required a written instrument but many courts treat exclusive licenses as assignments for some purposes. Parties litigating ownership disputes often contend with chain-of-title issues, authenticated written transfers, and whether purported licensees received sufficient rights to sue.

# Exclusive rights (17 U.S.C. § 106)

Section 106 enumerates the exclusive rights conferred to the copyright owner. These rights define the contours of infringement and include: the right to reproduce the work in copies or phonorecords; to prepare derivative works based upon the copyrighted work; to distribute copies or phonorecords of the work to the public by sale or other transfer of ownership, or by rental, lease, or lending; to perform the work publicly; to display the copyrighted work publicly; and, with respect to sound recordings, to perform the work publicly by means of a digital audio transmission. Each of these rights creates an exclusive domain; unauthorized exercise of any one may constitute infringement.

Reproduction right. The reproduction right protects making copies or phonorecords of the original fixed expression. Courts focus on whether the defendant actually copied protectable expression and whether the defendant’s materials qualify as “copies” (i.e., material objects in which the work is fixed). Mere use of underlying ideas or facts is not reproduction of protectable expression.

Derivative works. The derivative-right inquiry turns on whether the defendant created a work based on the copyrighted work that recasts, transforms, or adapts the original. Some transformations may be so substantial or creative that they themselves qualify for copyright, but a derivative work that appropriates protectable expression without authorization infringes the owner’s right to prepare derivatives. The scope of what constitutes a derivative work versus an independent, original work often requires careful analysis of the degree of appropriation.

Distribution right and first sale doctrine. The distribution right covers public dissemination of copies or phonorecords by sale or other transfer of ownership, and by rental, lease, or lending. The first sale doctrine constrains the distribution right: once a lawful copy is lawfully sold, the copyright owner’s distribution right in that particular copy is exhausted, permitting resale or lending by the owner of the copy. The first sale doctrine does not, however, permit the owner of a copy to create new copies or make derivative uses.

Public performance and display. The public performance right protects performing a work publicly, which requires performance at a place open to the public or at a place where a substantial number of persons outside of a normal circle of family and social acquaintances are gathered, or transmitting to the public by a device or process such that members of the public can receive the performance at different places and times. Public display protects displaying a work publicly, either directly or by means of a device or process. These rights have particular importance for musical works, audiovisual works, and visual art, and they intersect with statutory licensing schemes for public performance in certain industries (for example, music licensing collectives).

Digital transmission and sound recordings. Copyright law distinguishes traditional public performance rights from the public performance right of sound recordings by digital audio transmission; statutory provisions govern digital music services differently from interactive or noninteractive transmissions. Digital reproduction and distribution of copyrighted works raise additional statutory and technical complexities (for example, technological protection measures and anti-circumvention rules under other statutes).

Scope and limitations. The exclusive rights are subject to statutory limitations and exceptions, notably fair use (17 U.S.C. § 107) and other specific statutory exceptions. Whether a challenged use falls within the exclusive rights turns on both the particular statutory right implicated and the context of use.

# Registration and § 411(a) prerequisite to suit; effect of registration

Registration is an administrative process provided by the Copyright Office. Although copyright exists from fixation, registration provides important procedural and evidentiary benefits in litigation.

§ 411(a) Prerequisite to suit. Section 411(a) generally requires registration of the copyright (or a refusal of registration by the Copyright Office) as a precondition to bringing an infringement action in federal court for works of U.S. origin. For foreign works, compliance with Berne Convention formalities is relevant. The registration requirement is jurisdictional in many courts’ analyses; in others it is treated as a precondition that must be satisfied before the suit proceeds. If a plaintiff brings suit without having registered and without a timely decision by the Copyright Office, the court will typically dismiss or stay the action until registration is complete, depending on circumstances and courts’ interpretations of § 411(a).

Timing and strategies. Plaintiffs often file an application to register soon after fixation in order to preserve the ability to sue quickly. Registration can be applied for before or after publication. Because registration is a prerequisite to suit, parties must consider the timing of infringement claims and the administrative processing time for applications.

Evidentiary effect of registration. A timely registration creates a rebuttable presumption of the validity of the copyright and of the facts stated in the certificate, such as authorship and ownership. This presumption arises when registration is made within five years of publication, which gives registration enhanced evidentiary weight; registration made later still serves as evidence but not the same level of prima facie proof. The practical consequence is that a plaintiff with a timely registration has an initial procedural advantage: the burden shifts to the defendant to rebut the presumption of ownership or originality.

Statutory damages and attorneys’ fees. Registration has additional substantive consequences for remedies. Statutory damages and attorneys’ fees are generally available only if registration occurs before the infringement begins or within three months after the first publication of the work. A timely registration thus affects the remedies a successful plaintiff may obtain. If registration is untimely, the plaintiff may be limited to actual damages and disgorgement of profits, which can be harder to prove and quantify.

Registration of derivative works and collective works. Registration practice varies with derivative works and collections. A registration for a derivative work covers only the new material contributed by the claimant unless the registrant has previously obtained a registration for the underlying work or has the rights to register the preexisting material. Registration of a compilation or collective work requires particular attention to what elements are claimed in the certificate and whether the registration includes newly created selection, coordination, or arrangement.

Correction and supplementation. If factual errors appear in a registration application, the registrant can file a correction. Registrations may be supplemented with revised applications or additional filings to reflect changes in ownership or to add omitted authors. Courts scrutinize registration certificates and amendments in ownership disputes.

# Definitions, including “publication” (17 U.S.C. § 101)

The Copyright Act’s definitional section (17 U.S.C. § 101) clarifies terms that recur throughout statutory provisions and litigation.

Publication. The statute defines “publication” as the distribution of copies or phonorecords of a work to the public by sale or other transfer of ownership, or by rental, lease, or lending. The offering to distribute copies to a group of recipients for purposes of further distribution, public performance, or public display also constitutes publication. Notably, a performance or display of the work does not by itself constitute publication unless it is accompanied by distribution of copies. In modern digital contexts, courts analyze whether online posting or streaming constitutes publication; the key question remains whether tangible or sufficiently fixed copies have been offered or distributed to the public.

Other definitions. Section 101 also defines “copy” and “fixed” (a work is ‘‘fixed’’ when its embodiment in a copy is sufficiently permanent or stable to permit it to be perceived, reproduced, or otherwise communicated for a period of more than transitory duration), “phonorecord,” “pseudonymous,” and terms related to public performance and display. “Publicly” performing or displaying a work involves either performing or displaying it at a place open to the public or transmitting it to the public by means of a device or process so that members of the public may receive it. These definitions interact with rights under § 106 and with statutory exceptions and licensing schemes.

Publication consequences. Publication has multiple legal consequences. It influences the duration of copyright in older statutory regimes, the availability of certain statutory remedies and registrations, and defenses such as the first sale doctrine. For unpublished works, the evidentiary presumption of confidentiality may produce different approaches to damages and to the timing of registration.

# Available remedies and defenses overview

Remedies

Equitable relief. Copyright owners can seek injunctive relief to prevent or restrain infringement. A permanent injunction requires proof of irreparable harm and traditional equitable considerations; in modern practice, courts often consider the likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable injury, balance of hardships, and public interest when addressing preliminary injunctions, and similar equitable factors for permanent injunctions following judgment. Courts exercise discretion in tailoring injunctive relief — for example, by specifying the scope of prohibited conduct, imposing takedown or blocking orders, or directing destruction or impoundment of infringing copies.

Impounding and destruction. Courts may order impoundment of infringing copies, materials used to produce them, and devices that facilitate infringement, and may order destruction of infringing materials. These remedies require procedural safeguards and typically follow a finding of infringement.

Monetary relief: actual damages and profits. A successful plaintiff may recover the defendant’s profits attributable to the infringement and the plaintiff’s actual damages. Actual damages measure the loss suffered by the copyright owner; profits are measured as the defendant’s gross revenue attributable to the infringement less deductible expenses, subject to proof and apportionment rules. Plaintiffs bear the burden of proving actual damages; the defendant bears the burden of proving deductible costs and nonattributable profits.

Statutory damages. If the work is registered within the statutory windows, the plaintiff may elect statutory damages instead of actual damages and profits. Statutory damages are recoverable for each work infringed and offer a range per work, set by statute, that courts may adjust based on willfulness, innocent infringer status, and other factors. Statutory damages provide a remedy where actual damages may be difficult to prove and serve as a deterrent.

Attorneys’ fees and costs. Courts have discretion to award reasonable attorneys’ fees and full costs to the prevailing party. Fee-shifting is available under the statute as a means of encouraging meritorious claims and deterring opportunistic litigation. The award of fees depends on the equities of the case; courts may consider the frivolousness of the claim, objective unreasonableness, and the need to advance considerations of compensation and deterrence.

Criminal penalties. Willful infringement for commercial advantage or private financial gain may be prosecuted criminally. Criminal sanctions require willfulness and other elements established by statute.

Statute of limitations. A civil claim for copyright infringement must be brought within three years from the date the claim accrued. Accrual generally occurs when the plaintiff discovers, or with reasonable diligence should have discovered, the infringement and resulting injury. The limitations period is measured per claim; each act of infringement may give rise to a new claim if it is a discrete act occurring within the limitations window. Tolling and equitable doctrines may affect limitations analysis.

Defenses

Fair use (17 U.S.C. § 107). Fair use is the principal open-ended defense to infringement. It permits uses such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research under a flexible multi-factor test that requires courts to consider: (1) the purpose and character of the use (including whether the use is commercial or for nonprofit educational purposes and whether it is transformative); (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole; and (4) the effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. No single factor is determinative; courts weigh all factors in light of the overall equities and whether the use serves the public interest in access to information, innovation, and expression. The transformative nature of the use (i.e., whether it adds new expression, meaning, or message) has become a central consideration in modern fair-use analysis, particularly in the context of parody, commentary, search engines, and user-generated content.

De minimis copying. If the copying is too trivial to constitute a substantial taking of protected expression, courts may find that no actionable copying occurred. The de minimis doctrine overlaps with substantial similarity analysis: where copying is minimal, fair use may be unnecessary to consider, because no legally cognizable infringement exists.

Idea-expression dichotomy, merger, and scènes à faire. These doctrines operate as defenses by narrowing the scope of protectable expression. If the defendant’s allegedly infringing expression falls into these categories, it is not within the copyright owner’s exclusive domain and therefore cannot form the basis of infringement.

First sale doctrine. Once a lawful owner has acquired a particular copy, the owner's right to dispose of that copy is not restricted by the copyright owner’s distribution right. The first sale doctrine thus allows resale, rental (with certain exceptions), and lending of lawfully acquired copies. The doctrine does not permit public performances or the creation of new unauthorized copies.

Licenses and consent. A valid license, written or implied, defeats an infringement claim to the extent it authorizes the defendant’s use. Courts will look to the scope of the license, any limitations on use, and issues of breach to determine whether the license remains operative. Consent or contractual authorization that is validly conferred and not exceeded is a complete defense.

Innocent infringer. Statute and case law sometimes reduce statutory damages for innocent infringers who were not aware and had no reason to believe that their acts constituted infringement. The availability and degree of reduction vary by statute and judicial discretion.

Estoppel, laches, and acquiescence. Equitable defenses may bar or limit relief. Estoppel arises when the plaintiff’s conduct led the defendant reasonably to believe the infringements were permitted and the defendant relied to its detriment. Laches — unreasonable delay in asserting rights causing prejudice — has limited application in modern copyright law, particularly regarding statutory damages and where Congress has set fixed statutory time limits. However, courts still consider laches and acquiescence in equitable relief matters, such as injunctive relief and the scope of remedies.

Procedural considerations and proof

Pleading infringement. A plaintiff must plead ownership of a valid copyright and copying by the defendant of protectable elements. Copying may be proved directly (rare) or indirectly by showing access to the plaintiff’s work plus substantial similarity between the works. Where direct evidence of copying is absent, courts identify legally protectable elements and exclude unprotectable material in assessing substantial similarity.

Substantial similarity. To determine whether copying of protectable expression occurred, courts apply tests of substantial similarity. Tests vary by jurisdiction and by the type of work (e.g., total concept and feel, ordinary observer, or fragmented literal similarity), but they generally aim to ascertain whether the ordinary observer would recognize appropriation of the plaintiff’s protected expression. Courts often perform two-step analyses: first, filter out unprotectable elements (ideas, scènes à faire, public domain material), and second, compare the remaining protectable elements for similarity.

Burden of proof and presumptions. The plaintiff bears the initial burden of proving ownership and copying. A valid timely registration shifts the evidentiary posture by creating a presumption of ownership and validity. Once a plaintiff establishes a prima facie case, the defendant must rebut with evidence casting doubt on ownership, proving independent creation, or showing that the expressive elements at issue are unprotectable or that an affirmative defense applies.

Summary judgment and motions to dismiss. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, courts assess whether the complaint pleads facts sufficient to infer ownership of a valid copyright and alleged copying of protectable elements. Motions for summary judgment require courts to view evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party; factual disputes over access, authorship, or substantial similarity may preclude summary judgment. For copyright cases, courts often engage in a two-part comparison at summary judgment: separating protectable from unprotectable material and then assessing whether genuine issues of material fact remain regarding substantial similarity.

Preliminary and permanent injunctive relief. Requests for injunctive relief invoke equitable standards. For a preliminary injunction, courts generally require a showing of a likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm in the absence of relief, a balance of hardships tipping in the movant’s favor, and that the injunction serves the public interest. For permanent injunctions following a finding of infringement, courts consider whether monetary damages are an adequate remedy and weigh equitable factors before ordering prospective relief. The availability of statutory damages does not automatically establish irreparable injury for injunction purposes; courts may consider likelihood of ongoing infringement and harms to the market for the work.

Conclusion

A solid grasp of these statutory fundamentals — what is protected, who owns it, what rights owners possess, how registration interacts with litigation, the definitions that shape rights and exceptions, and the available remedies and defenses — is essential before approaching judicial opinions in copyright law. Litigation hinges on evidentiary proof of ownership and copying, nuanced doctrinal limits on protectability, and strategic procedural decisions about registration, remedies sought, and defenses asserted. Mastery of the statutory framework and the tests courts employ will enable a student to follow and evaluate judicial reasoning in specific infringement disputes.

Introduction

Fair use is a flexible, equitable doctrine that operates as a safety valve in copyright law, permitting certain unauthorized uses of copyrighted works without permission. Enacted in Section 107 of the Copyright Act, fair use reflects a balancing judgment: it protects socially valuable secondary uses—such as criticism, commentary, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research—while preserving the economic incentives and exclusive rights that copyright grants to authors. Because fair use is context-dependent and fact-intensive, courts apply it as a multi-factor test rather than through bright-line rules. Understanding fair use requires close attention to statutory text, the four statutory factors, how courts weigh and balance those factors, the allocation of burdens, and the doctrinal role of commerciality and licensing markets. This chapter explains the governing concepts and doctrinal mechanics students must know before reading judicial opinions applying Section 107.

# Statutory text and non-exhaustive list of purposes (criticism, comment, news reporting, etc.)

Section 107 begins by identifying certain purposes for which the reproduction of a copyrighted work "is not an infringement of copyright." The statute provides a non-exhaustive list of illustrative purposes: criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching (including multiple copies for classroom use), scholarship, and research. These categories are descriptive rather than prescriptive: they describe types of socially beneficial secondary uses that traditionally have been permitted or tolerated, but they do not limit the doctrine to those categories alone.

The statutory list performs several doctrinal functions. First, it signals that fair use concerns the communicative and social value of a secondary use. Uses that promote public discourse—such as criticism and comment—naturally weigh in favor of fair use, because copyright is not intended to stifle speech and debate. Second, the examples highlight contexts in which the law has historically tolerated copying for particular public purposes, such as classroom teaching and scholarly research. Third, because the list is non-exhaustive, courts are required to consider novel uses that fall outside traditional labels; they must inquire whether the new use serves comparable public interests and whether the balance of statutory factors favors copying.

When courts analyze whether a particular purpose aligns with the examples in the statute, they typically examine both the objective nature of the use and the actor’s stated purpose. For instance, courts look at whether the use adds to public knowledge (a scholarly or critical aim), whether it informs the public about matters of current interest (news reporting), or whether it facilitates education. But courts do not mechanically treat a use as fair simply because the user asserts a protected purpose; courts evaluate the entire context and the other statutory factors to determine whether the asserted purpose is bona fide and whether the secondary use advances the public interest in light of the copyright owner’s interests.

# The four statutory factors and holistic balancing

Section 107 identifies four factors courts must consider in determining whether a particular use is a fair use:

1. The purpose and character of the use, including whether such use is of a commercial nature or is for nonprofit educational purposes.
2. The nature of the copyrighted work.
3. The amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole.
4. The effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.

These factors are to be weighed together in a holistic balancing. No single factor is determinative; rather, courts consider the cumulative effect of the factors on the equitable judgment whether the use is fair. Because the statutory factors are plural and non-exclusive, courts can also consider other relevant equities and doctrinal considerations that fit within the structure of the factors.

Purpose and character of the use. This first factor asks why the defendant used the work and what the use contributes. Courts consider whether the use is transformative—whether it adds new expression, meaning, or message to the original work—or merely a superseding substitute that serves the same purpose as the original. A transformative use tends to favor fair use because it advances the goals of copyright by contributing new insights, criticism, or information without displacing the market for the original. The inquiry into transformativeness examines not only whether the use is comment on the original but also whether it repurposes the work for a different audience or function.

The statutory language also prompts attention to commerciality: uses that are commercial are less likely to be considered fair under the first factor. But commerciality is only one element of the first factor and must be weighed alongside transformativeness and other considerations. A commercial use that is highly transformative may still be fair. Conversely, a noncommercial use that is not transformative and harms the market may be unfair. Courts therefore assess the overall character of the use, asking whether it is productive in a way that augments public discourse or merely exploits the original’s value.

Nature of the copyrighted work. The second factor considers the type of work used. Works that are fictional, creative, or unpublished typically enjoy stronger protection against copying than works that are factual or publicly distributed, because copyright is strongest at the point where it most closely approximates the author's original expressive choices. Uses of factual works—such as news items, reports, and scientific data—are more likely to be fair than uses of highly creative works that embody aesthetic choices. Similarly, uses of published works weigh more favorably toward fair use than uses of unpublished works, because the right of first publication is an important aspect of an author's statutory entitlement. This factor requires attention to the work’s expressive nature and the degree to which the use appropriates core creative elements.

Amount and substantiality of the portion used. The third factor requires courts to compare the amount and qualitative importance of the portion taken to the copyrighted work as a whole. Courts consider both quantitative measures (how much was taken) and qualitative measures (whether the taken portion is the "heart" of the work). Using a small percentage of a work may nonetheless be substantial if it captures the essence of the original. Conversely, taking a larger portion may still be fair if that portion is necessary for the secondary purpose and the use is transformative. This factor is sensitive to context: the amount reasonable for a critic or commentator may differ from the amount reasonable for a parody or for archival purposes.

Effect upon the market. The fourth factor examines whether and to what extent the secondary use substitutes for the original or harms the market for the original or its derivatives. Courts evaluate both actual market harm and potential market harm, including harm to traditional licensing opportunities that authors might reasonably exploit. This factor often carries substantial weight; evidence that the use usurps a significant market for the original or for likely derivatives tends to undermine a claim of fair use. Importantly, however, not every potential market harms the fourth factor. If the secondary use targets different markets, serves different audiences, or is sufficiently transformative so that it does not function as a market substitute, the fourth factor may favor fair use even when the defendant profited.

Holistic balancing. In practice, courts do not apply the factors mechanically. Rather, they engage in a holistic assessment, synthesizing the interplay among the factors. For example, a highly transformative work that uses the heart of the original may nonetheless be fair if the transformation is substantial and market substitution unlikely. Conversely, a nontransformative copy that leaves the original market intact may still be unfair if it reproduces a substantial portion of a creative, unpublished work. The court's judgment must articulate why the balance of the factors, given the specific factual matrix, favors or disfavors fair use.

# Burden of proof and the nature of the defense

Fair use is an affirmative defense to copyright infringement. As an affirmative defense, the defendant bears the burden of raising and proving it. However, the allocation of burdens has nuanced practical effects in litigation and on appellate review.

The defendant must plead fair use and then produce evidence supporting the statutory factors. The plaintiff, in turn, bears the burden of proving liability—infringement—by establishing ownership of a valid copyright and copying (or access plus probative similarity). Once the plaintiff establishes a prima facie case of copying, the defendant may introduce evidence and argument that the use was justified under Section 107. The standard of proof for the affirmative defense is typically preponderance of the evidence. Courts evaluate whether the defendant has shown, by a preponderance, that the statutory factors favor fair use.

Procedural posture matters. At the summary judgment stage, courts assess whether the evidence permits only one reasonable conclusion about fair use or whether genuine factual disputes exist. Because fair use is a mixed question of law and fact, some aspects (e.g., the existence of market harm) may be fact-intensive and inappropriate for resolution without a trial. Conversely, where the undisputed facts clearly establish the statutory factors (for example, a blatantly transformative use or measureless market harm), courts may enter judgment on fair use as a matter of law. On appeal, the standard of review varies depending on whether the determination is one of law or fact; legal conclusions (such as pure issues of transformativeness) are reviewed de novo, whereas factual findings (such as market effect) are reviewed for clear error.

Because fair use is equitable and flexible, courts sometimes treat it as intrinsically for the judge to decide in appropriate circumstances. The law does not demand that every fair use case proceed to a jury; where the facts are undisputed and the fair use analysis resolves purely as a matter of law, judges may decide the defense. In contrast, when material facts about intent, market effect, or the nature of the use remain contested, a jury may be required to resolve those factual disputes.

The nature of the defense is both protective and limited. It protects socially valuable secondary expressions but does not excuse wanton or opportunistic copying that usurps an author’s market. As an equitable doctrine, the defense invites courts to tailor the remedy and to calibrate relief—sometimes limiting injunctive relief or awarding nominal damages—consistent with fairness.

# Commerciality and its non-dispositive role

Commerciality—whether a use is "of a commercial nature"—is explicitly referenced in the first statutory factor and plays a recurring role in fair use doctrine. Yet the doctrinal posture of commerciality is nuanced: it is an important consideration but not determinative.

Commercial uses typically weigh against fair use because they suggest the defendant derived economic benefit from the use, would have an incentive to displace the market for the original, and thus undermine the copyright owner's statutory entitlements. Courts consider a variety of indicia of commerciality: whether the defendant sold copies, integrated the use into a commercial product, used the work to promote a business, or otherwise profited financially.

However, the mere presence of a profit motive does not automatically foreclose fair use. A use can be both commercial and transformative; in such circumstances, courts evaluate the degree of transformativeness alongside the commercial nature. For example, a highly transformative work that repurposes the original for new expression or insight may still be fair even where the defendant realized commercial gain. Likewise, some uses that appear commercial may nonetheless be trivially commercial (a nominal fee for distribution) and not foreclose a fair use finding.

The doctrine also recognizes that many socially valuable secondary uses will have incidental commercial aspects. Academic publishers, commentators, and journalism organizations frequently operate within commercial markets, yet their uses often further public discourse. Therefore, courts examine commerciality in context: they assess whether the commercial aspect makes the use a market substitute for the original or whether it is a marginal adjunct that does not significantly weigh against fair use.

In short, commerciality is an influential but non-dispositive factor. It is a negative indicator that must be weighed with the other factors, particularly transformativeness and market effect.

# Licensing markets versus socially valuable secondary uses

A central tension in fair use doctrine is the relationship between fair use and existing or potential licensing markets. Copyright law contemplates that authors can exploit rights through licensing, and courts consider harm to licensing markets as part of the fourth factor. Yet fair use exists to protect socially valuable secondary uses that often would not be licensed or that society deems should remain unlicensed to preserve free expression and access.

Courts distinguish between two kinds of markets when evaluating the fourth factor. First, they look at traditional and reasonably foreseeable markets that the copyright owner might exploit: the original work's primary markets and derivative markets that authors traditionally develop or expect to develop. Harm to these markets weighs strongly against fair use. Second, courts consider new markets that would arise solely because of the secondary use; they are more skeptical of treating such prospective markets as decisive. If every secondary use could be characterized as a potential licensing market, fair use would be swallowed by copyright’s economic regime. The proper inquiry asks whether the market is realistic and within the author's legitimate expectations.

Licensing markets can inform the inquiry in two ways. When a licensing market already exists for the type of secondary use at issue—say, for sampling in music or for stock photography—courts are more likely to find that unlicensed use harms that market and thus undermines fair use. Conversely, when no licensing market exists because society has traditionally allowed free use (for example, for certain kinds of factual reporting or commentary), the absence of a license market counsels in favor of fair use.

Courts also recognize public-interest exceptions: even when a licensing market might be feasible, social value can outweigh economic considerations. Uses integral to public discourse—such as quotation for news reporting or critical commentary—may be fair despite the lack of a license or the existence of a latent licensing market. The law resists converting every socially beneficial secondary use into a commodity, because doing so would constrain speech and learning.

This balancing requires caution. Courts avoid treating hypothetical markets as decisive where there is no realistic expectation that the rightsholder would have monetized the secondary use. At the same time, courts do not ignore substantial market displacement simply because no license mechanism presently exists; where a secondary use goes to the heart of a work’s commercial exploitation, substituting for that exploitation, fair use is unlikely.

Practical guidance for applying licensing-market considerations includes assessing whether the secondary use usurps a traditional format for licensing, whether the rightsholder has actively sought to license similar uses, and whether the secondary use would plausibly depress demand for licensing. Courts evaluate admissible economic evidence—market studies, expert testimony, licensing records—to decide how much weight to give potential market harm.

Conclusion

Fair use under Section 107 is a doctrinally rich, flexible defense that requires courts to engage in detailed, context-sensitive balancing among statutory factors. The non-exhaustive list of illustrative purposes helps orient judicial inquiry toward socially valuable secondary uses, while the four statutory factors provide a framework for balancing the interests of users and authors. Because fair use is an affirmative defense, defendants must plead and prove it, but courts resolve the defense either as a legal conclusion or fact-based determination depending on the posture and evidentiary record. Commerciality is a significant but non-dispositive consideration, and courts carefully weigh licensing market concerns against the societal value of free secondary uses. Mastery of these structural principles prepares readers to analyze judicial opinion applying fair use: attention to factual nuance, the interplay among the statutory factors, and the evidentiary posture are essential to understanding how courts reach equitable judgments in this domain.

Introduction

In the United States copyright system, fair use is the central, flexible limitation that allows uses of copyrighted material without the owner's permission. Courts assess fair use through a multi-factor inquiry. The first statutory factor—“the purpose and character of the use”—has received particular attention because it encompasses both normative judgments about the social value of reuse and doctrinally important doctrines, most notably “transformative use.” This chapter explains how courts evaluate purpose and character in modern fair use analysis. It focuses on the criteria that make a use “transformative,” the role of an objective reasonable-observer perspective in determining purpose, the doctrinal distinctions among parody, satire, commentary, and criticism, how courts treat a user’s good or bad faith, and the weight given to commercial motivation. The goal is to equip you with the doctrinal tools and reasoning patterns that courts employ so you can read and analyze judicial opinions where factor one is pivotal.

# Transformative use and “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character”

At the heart of modern factor-one analysis is the notion that a use that “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character” is more likely to be fair. This language captures the idea of transformativeness: a secondary use that alters the original with new expression, meaning, or message is differently situated from mere reproduction. Courts have developed several related contours of the concept.

What “transformative” means. A use is transformative when it does not simply supersede the original work’s expressive purpose but instead repurposes the original for a new communicative objective. This can occur by adding new creative content, commenting on or criticizing the original, placing the original in a new context that produces a new meaning, or using the original for an entirely different functional purpose (for example, as raw material for a new expressive work, or for information, education, or parody).

Identifying the new purpose or character. Determining whether a use “adds something new” requires an inquiry into what the secondary user did with the original material and what communicative goal they sought to achieve. Courts look for evidence that the secondary work’s purpose differs qualitatively from the original. Examples include using an image of a painting as the subject of social commentary about the artist or society, altering a photograph so as to change its expressive content, or incorporating text excerpts into scholarly analysis to make a point about the original or about broader themes.

De minimis and trivial alterations. Not every change or addition qualifies. Minor alterations, cosmetic edits, or simple republishing generally do not convert a use into a transformative one. The inquiry focuses on whether the change contributes to a new expression, rather than merely disguising the copy or substituting for the original in the market.

Degrees of transformativeness. Transformative character is not binary; courts may treat it as a spectrum. A use can be highly transformative (e.g., when it recontextualizes and comments on the original to convey a new message) or only modestly so. The degree of transformation matters because it influences how much weight factor one receives in the overall fair-use balance: stronger transformative character can outweigh other considerations like commerciality or substantiality of copying.

Functional or utilitarian transformations. Transformation may also occur when the secondary use converts a work from one kind of use to another, such as from expressive to informational or functional use. For example, excerpting a novel to provide factual information or using an artistic image as historical evidence in a documentary can be transformative because the purpose shifts from entertainment or art to information or scholarship.

Interplay with other factors. Although a highly transformative use is favorable under factor one, it does not resolve the fair use calculus alone. Courts still weigh the other statutory factors—nature of the copyrighted work, amount and substantiality taken, and effect on the potential market—and consider transformativeness in that broader context.

# Objective reasonable-observer perspective on purpose

Because subjective intent can be manipulated or ambiguous, courts have adopted an objective perspective to assess purpose and character. Rather than focusing on an actor’s private mental state, courts ask how a reasonable, informed observer would perceive the secondary use.

What the reasonable-observer standard means. The question is whether, viewed objectively and with knowledge of the works and the context of reuse, a typical observer would regard the secondary work as having a new purpose or character that differentiates it from the original. This standardized viewpoint helps ensure predictability and prevents litigants from insulating non-transformative conduct behind self-serving testimony about intent.

Evidence considered under the objective test. Courts may consider a variety of contextual evidence to determine how an ordinary observer would understand the use: the content and form of the secondary work, how the copyrighted material is presented, accompanying materials (such as captions, titles, or paratextual commentary), the medium and audience, and the communicative relationship between the original and the secondary work. The source and transmission of the work—e.g., whether it appears in an artistic gallery, a news article, a parody film, or a commercial advertisement—can shape an observer’s perception of purpose.

Limits of the objective perspective. The standard is not purely formalistic. The “reasonable observer” is assumed to have some knowledge of relevant artistic or factual background; courts often presume a baseline competence in the relevant audience rather than a blank slate. Nevertheless, because the inquiry excludes an actor’s subjective good faith or malevolent intent as dispositive, the analysis centers on outward, perceivable characteristics and contextual cues.

Role when the secondary work is ambiguous. If the objective cues are mixed or equivocal, courts must weigh competing indicia of purpose. Ambiguity tends to favor the copyright owner because it is harder for a defendant to establish that their use would be reasonably seen as transformative. However, clear paratextual signals or explicit commentary can tip the balance toward recognizing a new purpose.

# Parody, satire, commentary, and criticism distinctions

Different categories of secondary uses occupy different positions on the transformation spectrum. Parody and satire are a classic example: courts have generally been more receptive to parody as transformative than to satire, and commentary and criticism are frequently treated as paradigmatically transformative. It is important to understand the doctrinal reasons for these distinctions.

Parody. Parody uses elements of an original work in order to evoke it and at the same time to comment on, criticize, or mock the original. The essential feature of parody is that the new work targets the original as its object of ridicule or commentary; it must conjure up the original so that the parody’s commentary has a referent. Because parody necessarily invokes the original to make a critical point about it, courts view parody as particularly likely to be transformative: the secondary work’s purpose differs sharply from the original’s expressive aim.

Doctrinal consequences. Because parody’s point often requires copying recognizable elements of the original, courts apply a contextual balancing: they acknowledge that substantial copying may be necessary for the parody to “recall” the original but still treat the parodic purpose as a strong indicator of fair use under factor one. However, parody is not an automatic shield; courts still analyze other factors, especially market effect and substantiality of copying.

Satire. Satire uses elements of an original work to criticize or comment on something else—society, political figures, or cultural trends—rather than the original work itself. Because satire does not necessarily need to bring the original to mind for its critical point, courts are less willing to treat satire as inherently transformative. A satirical work that relies heavily on a copyrighted work without commenting on it may be less protected by factor one than a parody.

Practical importance. The parody-satire distinction matters because it affects how courts balance the need to copy against the transformative purpose. Parodies are likely to survive factor one scrutiny more readily; satires face a tougher showing that the copying is necessary for transformation.

Commentary and criticism. Commentary and criticism—scholarly or journalistic uses that analyze, interpret, or evaluate an original—are widely recognized as transformative purposes. When the secondary work uses excerpts or reproduces the original to illustrate points of analysis, to critique aesthetic choices, or to situate the work within a broader discourse, courts typically credit a transformative character. The key is that the secondary work’s purpose is to augment understanding or provide new insight, rather than to substitute for the original.

Derivative works vs. commentary. Not all uses that build on an original qualify as commentary or criticism. A derivative work that merely extends the plot or style without adding critical or interpretive content is less likely to be deemed transformative. Courts look for genuine critical engagement—a new message or meaning—not mere extension or replication.

Examples to illustrate distinctions. Consider two uses of the same song excerpt: one appears in a humorous sketch that lampoons the song’s lyrics and style (parody); the other places the song in a larger critique of social phenomena without directly targeting the song (satire). The former is more likely to be seen as transformative under factor one. Alternatively, a scholarly article that reproduces portions of a poem to analyze meter and theme will likely be treated as transformative commentary.

# Relevance (and limits) of good or bad faith in fair use

A user’s motives and state of mind can appear relevant to whether a use is fair, but courts treat these considerations with caution. The statutory factor centers on purpose and character, yet courts avoid elevating subjective good faith or bad faith to dispositive status.

Permissible consideration of intent. Evidence of good faith—such as a genuine belief that the work is freely usable, or an intent to comment, educate, or criticize—can provide corroborating support for a finding that the use is transformative. Conversely, evidence that a user intentionally copied to exploit the market for the original or to mislead consumers may undermine claims of transformative purpose. Thus, motive can be probative of how an objective observer would perceive the use.

Limits on the weight of subjective intent. Courts generally hold that subjective bad faith does not automatically preclude fair use. A user who acted in bad faith may still be found to have made a transformative use if the objective features of the secondary work are strongly consistent with a new purpose. The policy reason is that fair use doctrine should not be hostage to unprovable or surmised mental states; instead, courts prefer to rely on observable indicia and functional effects.

When bad faith matters more. A pattern of willful copying aimed at usurping the market or deceiving consumers can influence factor one negatively and may also be relevant to factor four (market effect) or to equitable remedies. Particularly egregious behavior (e.g., deliberate removal of attribution, copying after receipt of a takedown) can persuade a court to give less weight to any claimed transformative character.

Practical evidentiary role. In practice, plaintiffs often present evidence of the defendant’s knowledge of the copyrighted work, communications indicating intent, or commercial planning documents to argue bad faith. Defendants respond with explanations and objective contextual cues. The court uses this evidence to flesh out the reasonable-observer analysis but does not typically make subjective intent determinative.

# Commercial motivation weighed against transformative character

Commerciality is an enduring, but nuanced, indicator under factor one. The statute explicitly asks the court to consider whether the use is “of a commercial nature” or “for nonprofit educational purposes.” The modern approach is that commercial motivation weighs against fair use, but it is not dispositive, and a strong transformative purpose can outweigh commerciality.

General rule. Purely noncommercial uses often have an easier path under factor one because they are perceived as less likely to be market competitors and more closely aligned with socially favored uses like education or commentary. Commercial uses—especially those producing direct monetary gain or substituting for the original in the marketplace—are more suspect. That said, the test is not categorical: courts examine how commerciality interacts with transformativeness and market harm.

Mixed motives and profit-seeking. Many secondary uses have mixed motives: they may be expressive but also generate revenue through sales, advertising, or attracting business. Courts recognize that almost all expressive activity in a market economy has some commercial aspect; therefore, mere profit-seeking does not preclude fair use. The key question is whether the commercial nature of the use is merely incidental to a genuinely transformative purpose, or whether the use is primarily a market substitute that appropriates the value of the original.

Weighing competing considerations. When a use is both commercial and transformative, courts balance the two factors. If the new work adds significant new meaning or message, that transformative character can “tip the scales” in favor of fair use despite commercial exploitation. Conversely, a commercial reuse that merely republishes or slightly alters the original is unlikely to be saved by factor one.

Market substitution and commerciality. The more a defendant’s commercial use acts as a market substitute for the original or interferes with the copyright owner’s licensing opportunities, the more the commercial nature weighs against fair use. Courts therefore consider not only whether money is involved but the nature of that involvement. Selling prints that directly compete with an artist’s market presents different concerns than selling a critical book that incidentally reproduces short passages.

Context matters. The industry context and customary licensing practices also shape the analysis. If licensing markets exist and the secondary use supplants those markets, commerciality will carry more negative weight. In contrast, if no licensing market exists or if the use serves purposes that licensing does not typically cover (such as scholarly analysis or political commentary), then commerciality is less determinative.

# How factor one fits into the overall fair use calculus and procedural posture

Factor one is one piece of a statutory balancing test. Courts do not determine fair use by mechanistically applying factor one alone; they synthesize the findings on purpose and character with those from the other three factors. A highly transformative use can reduce the weight of other adverse factors (for example, if the new use is transformative, even substantial copying may be tolerable), but transformation does not automatically override clear evidence of market harm or wholesale appropriation.

Affirmative defense and burdens. Fair use is an affirmative defense; the defendant bears the burden of proof to persuade the factfinder that the statutory factors favor a finding of fair use. Nevertheless, courts decide fair use as a matter of law when the relevant facts are undisputed and no reasonable factfinder could find otherwise. On summary judgment, courts assess whether, taking the undisputed evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, fair use is established or defeated as a matter of law.

Factfinding and credibility. Because factor one includes contextual and interpretive judgments—how a reasonable observer would perceive the purpose—some cases raise genuine factual disputes that preclude resolution on summary judgment. In such cases, a jury may need to determine perceptions, audience understanding, and contested facts about how the secondary work was created and marketed.

Interplay with remedies and equitable considerations. Finally, findings about purpose and character can influence equitable relief and the scope of remedies even when fair use is not dispositive. Evidence of transformative purpose may counsel against injunctive relief or punitive damages in close cases, while evidence of deliberate, bad-faith copying can justify stronger remedies.

Conclusion

Factor one—purpose and character of the use—operates as a normative and pragmatic check on exclusive copyright rights. The modern courts’ emphasis on transformativeness and an objective reasonable-observer perspective steers the inquiry away from unilateral assertions of intent toward an outward-looking assessment of how the secondary work functions and communicates. Parody, commentary, and criticism generally sit closer to the transformative pole, while satire and straightforward republishing often do not. Good or bad faith is relevant but limited in weight, and commercial motivation is an important but not dispositive factor: strong transformation can mitigate the negative inference of commerciality. Ultimately, factor one must be read and applied in conjunction with the other statutory factors and in light of the procedural posture of the case, including whether the facts are sufficient for summary judgment or require factfinder resolution. Understanding these doctrinal contours equips you to analyze judicial opinions where courts wrestle with the competing values of creativity, commentary, and market protection.

Introduction

When courts evaluate a claim of fair use under the Copyright Act, they balance four statutory factors. Two of those factors—the nature of the copyrighted work (factor two) and the amount and substantiality of the portion used (factor three)—operate together to define what the defendant borrowed and how the character of the original affects the propriety of that borrowing. Understanding these factors requires both doctrinal rules and practical guidance about how courts translate abstract standards into case-specific outcomes. This chapter provides the doctrinal background a student needs to assess how courts analyze the nature of the work and the amount taken: how they treat factual versus creative works; the special status of unpublished material; the quantitative and qualitative dimensions of substantiality (including the “heart of the work” concept); how necessity for a secondary purpose is evaluated; and when use of an entire work is permissible in contexts such as commentary and parody. Throughout, attention is paid to legal reasoning, common tests, burdens of proof, and how different procedural postures shape the analysis.

# Factual versus creative works; unpublished versus published status

Factor two asks courts to consider “the nature of the copyrighted work.” This is a qualitative inquiry that recognizes that not all works are equally deserving of strong protection. Two principal dimensions of the work’s nature typically influence a court’s analysis.

First, courts distinguish factual works from highly creative ones. Factual or informational works—such as news reports, biographies, scientific articles, and compilations of facts—are generally closer to the free-exchange values underlying fair use. Because the dissemination of facts advances public knowledge and is often accorded weaker proprietary protection, copying portions of factual works is more likely to be found fair than copying similar amounts of highly expressive or imaginative works. By contrast, works of fiction, poetry, music, films, and other expressive creations sit at the core of copyright’s concern; such works typically command stronger protection against unlicensed appropriation. This distinction is not absolute: highly original selection and arrangement of facts can imbue even factual works with protectable expression, and an apparently “creative” work may contain bits of uncopyrightable fact. In application, courts ask whether the copied portion derives from the expressive or the informational dimension of the work.

Second, courts assess whether the work is published or unpublished. The unpublished/published axis has traditionally been significant. Courts treat an author’s right to control the first public appearance of her expression as an important copyright interest. Accordingly, use of unpublished works weighs more heavily against a finding of fair use than use of published works. The policy rationale is that the author’s right to decide when and how to reveal creative choices is an element of the exclusive rights that copyright grants. Thus, even if the subsequent use would be transformative or for a socially beneficial purpose, use of unpublished material will often be given less weight in favor of fair use.

These two dimensions are evaluated together. For example, copying a small factual excerpt from an unpublished scientific manuscript may be less disfavored than copying the “heart” of an unpublished poem. Courts do not treat either axis mechanistically; instead, they synthesize them with other evidence (including evidence of transformation and market effect) to reach a fair-use conclusion.

From a procedural standpoint, factor two often involves questions of law and questions of fact. Whether a work is factual or creative is largely a legal characterization, but assessing whether a particular excerpt is expressive or informational may involve factual determinations about the work’s content. Similarly, whether a work is unpublished is a factual finding. On motions to dismiss, courts sometimes accept a plaintiff’s characterization of the work as creative; on summary judgment the defendant seeking fair use must produce record evidence to support an argument that the copied material is factual or published, or that other features make fair use likely.

# How publication status affects factor two

Publication status plays an outsized role in factor two because the right of first publication is one of the core exclusive rights conferred by copyright. The general rule is that the unpublished status of a work weighs against a finding of fair use. Several practical consequences flow from this principle.

First, unpublished status can be dispositive when the use depends on the audience’s lack of access to the original. If the defendant’s use effectively amounts to a first public disclosure of the copyrighted expression (for example, reproducing private letters or an unreleased manuscript), courts will treat this as a strong reason to deny fair use. The concern is not merely the volume of copying but the nature of the harm to the author’s right to decide how the work first reaches the public.

Second, the effect of publication status is not absolute. Courts will temper its weight when the defendant’s use is highly transformative or when the use serves values like reporting of historical facts, criticism, or scholarship that are especially important to the public interest. Thus, if transformative commentary adds substantial new meaning or purpose and does not substitute for the market for the unpublished work, the unpublished status may carry less weight than it otherwise would.

Third, the timing and context of publication matter. If a work is formally unpublished but the author has voluntarily made the material publicly available in some context (for example, posting to an open blog, releasing parts to the press), courts will weigh the degree and form of availability. A work that has been effectively disseminated to a broad audience may be treated more like a published work for fair-use purposes than a work kept entirely private.

In litigation, parties must be prepared to put publication status into factual context. A defendant arguing fair use must show not only publication or public availability but also how that status reduces the author’s interest in exclusive control. Conversely, a plaintiff asserting unpublished status should produce evidence about the author’s efforts to withhold or control dissemination and why the defendant’s use undermines the right of first publication.

# Quantitative and qualitative substantiality (“heart of the work”)

Factor three asks courts to evaluate “the amount and substantiality of the portion used” in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole. This factor has two distinct but related components: quantitative substantiality (how much was taken) and qualitative substantiality (how important or central the taken portion is).

Quantitative analysis is straightforward in concept: courts look at the portion of the original that was reproduced and assess whether that proportion is small or large relative to the whole. Percentages, page counts, word counts, runtime minutes, or number of images reproduced can all serve as measures. However, strict numerical cutoffs are rare because context matters: taking 300 words from a 500-word poem is very different from taking 300 words from a 100,000-word novel. Quantitative measures provide helpful evidence but do not determine the outcome.

Qualitative substantiality addresses the symbolic and functional importance of the copied material. Even a small excerpt can be “substantial” if it captures the essence, the climax, or the most memorable expressive element of the work—the so-called “heart” of the work. Courts often ask whether the copied portion constitutes the most expressive or distinctive part of the original and whether use of that portion usurps the audience’s desire to experience that expressive core. Examples of qualitatively substantial taking would include quoting the punchline of a joke, reproducing a chorus or refrain from a song, or copying the climactic chapter of a novel.

The two components work together: a small but qualitatively substantial excerpt may weigh against fair use, while a larger amount that is not expressive (for instance, repetitive technical data) may be treated more neutrally. Courts also consider whether the portion taken contains protectable expression as opposed to unprotectable ideas, facts, or scènes à faire (elements that naturally flow from the genre). Reproducing factual data or unprotected passages, even in large quantity, is less likely to be deemed substantial in the sense relevant to copyright.

In application, courts examine the effect of the taking on the original’s expressive value. If the defendant’s use leaves little or none of what made the original attractive—because the defendant appropriated the essence—factor three will weigh against fair use. On the other hand, if the taken material is incidental, purely functional, or necessary to a secondary, transformative purpose, factor three may favor the defendant even when the quantity is nontrivial.

From a procedural point of view, questions of substantiality are often for the trier of fact because they require nuanced judgments about centrality and audience perception. But courts may resolve them as matters of law when the facts admit no reasonable dispute—for example, when a defendant reproduces an entire work and no plausible argument of transformative purpose can justify that taking.

# Necessity of the amount taken to achieve the secondary purpose

Even when copying is substantial, the third factor is not dispositive if the defendant can show that the amount taken was reasonable and necessary to accomplish the secondary, often transformative, purpose. The doctrine recognizes that some secondary uses—criticism, scholarship, news reporting, parody, and certain types of commentary—cannot practically be achieved without reproducing significant portions of the original.

The necessity inquiry therefore asks whether the defendant used more of the original than reasonably required for the asserted secondary purpose. Courts assess: (1) the defendant’s stated purpose for the use; (2) whether the taking was carefully tailored to that purpose; and (3) whether less could have accomplished the same end without substantially impairing the communicative or critical objective. If alternative means—summaries, descriptions, or quotations of smaller scope—would have achieved the same result, courts may find the amount taken excessive and weigh factor three against fair use.

However, the standard of necessity is not strict or absolute. Courts recognize that adequate quotation sometimes requires direct reproduction to let readers see the specific language, tone, or imagery under critique or discussion. For example, scholarship that closely examines an author’s diction may need verbatim excerpts. Parody often requires invoking elements of the original to “conjure up” the prior work for comedic contrast. In such contexts, courts balance the defendant’s need against the owner’s right to control the work.

Key elements of the necessity analysis include:

- Purpose alignment: The amount taken must be tied to the defendant’s stated transformative or secondary purpose. Gratuitous copying for unrelated ends undermines the necessity argument.

- Proportionality: The taking must be proportional to the needs of the secondary use. Excessive reproduction (for instance, copying entire chapters where a sentence or two would suffice) suggests lack of necessity.

- Good faith and tailoring: Evidence that the defendant attempted less intrusive alternatives supports a finding that the amount taken was necessary. Conversely, evidence that the defendant could have used summaries or redactions but did not, undermines the defense.

Procedurally, courts scrutinize evidentiary support for necessity at summary judgment and at trial. Defendants bear the burden of articulating the secondary purpose and justifying the amount taken as necessary. Plaintiffs may rebut by showing available alternatives, the availability of licensing, or that the defendant’s use was poorly tailored.

The necessity inquiry also interacts with the transformation analysis under factor one. If the use is highly transformative, courts will be more willing to accept larger quantitative appropriations as necessary. But transformation is not a free pass; necessity must still be assessed on its own terms.

# Use of complete works in commentary and parody contexts

Certain contexts, especially commentary (including criticism and scholarship) and parody, press the bounds of factor three because they sometimes require use of the copyrighted work in its entirety to be meaningful. Courts approach whole-work use in these settings with an eye toward the purpose and audience expectations.

Commentary and criticism often rely on quoting passages verbatim to demonstrate the point under critique. When the central subject of the commentary is the work itself, reproduction of substantial portions—occasionally the whole—may be justified if it is necessary to permit the audience to follow the commentator’s argument. Courts will consider whether the verbatim reproduction is sufficiently tied to a critical aim and whether the amount used is no more than required. For instance, in a line-by-line literary analysis, reproducing longer passages may be appropriate to show stylistic features. But a commentator who reproduces entire books without meaningful analysis may be using more than necessary.

Parody presents a special category. A parody must use elements of the original to “recall” it so that the audience recognizes the target of the joke. Because recognition is often essential to the parody’s success, courts have acknowledged that a parody may need to take more material, and sometimes the entire work, to achieve its purpose. Nonetheless, the necessity and proportionality principles still operate: a parody that simply republishes the whole work with minimal transformative commentary or derisive juxtaposition may not be fair. Courts consider whether the parody uses the original as raw material for new expression, comment, or critique, and whether the taking is measured toward that expressive goal.

When whole-work use is claimed, courts weigh the following considerations:

- Centrality of the original to the secondary purpose: Is the original itself the object of critique or parody, or is it being used for a different purpose? Whole-work copying is more defensible when the original is the very subject of the secondary use.

- Degree of transformation: The more the new work adds new expression, meaning, or message, the more tolerable the entire-use appropriation becomes. Conversely, whole-work copying that merely republishes the original in a different packaging will be disfavored.

- Market substitution: Even if the copying is for commentary or parody, courts examine whether the new work acts as a market substitute for the original. If it does, the taking is less likely to be fair.

- Proportionality and necessity: Courts require that the defendant use only what is necessary to “conjure up” the original and to make the critical or parodic point.

In practice, whether use of complete works in these contexts is fair requires a fact-sensitive balancing of the statutory factors. Defendants asserting fair use in such cases should be prepared to articulate precisely why full reproduction was needed and how their work transforms the original’s meaning or purpose. Plaintiffs should counter with evidence that less copying would have sufficed and that the secondary work usurps the original’s market.

# Interaction of factors two and three with transformation and market considerations; procedural implications

Although this chapter focuses on factors two and three, it is important to situate them within the larger fair-use framework. The nature of the work (factor two) and the amount taken (factor three) do not operate in isolation. Courts routinely weigh these factors alongside the purpose and character of the use (factor one), especially the degree of transformation, and the effect on the market for the original (factor four). A highly transformative purpose can mitigate the weight of an unfavorable nature or large quantity taken; conversely, use of a creative, unpublished work can amplify the significance of even modest copying.

From a procedural perspective, the interaction among factors affects how and when courts decide fair-use issues. Fair use is an affirmative defense; the defendant bears the burden of proving it. Courts may resolve fair-use disputes on motions to dismiss when the complaint and judicially-noticeable materials show that the challenged use is plainly fair. More commonly, summary judgment is the vehicle for resolving fair use when the parties present adequate factual records about the nature of the work, what was taken, and the defendant’s purpose. Where material facts about centrality, transformation, or market effects remain disputed, the issue goes to the trier of fact.

When arguing factor two or three at summary judgment or trial, lawyers should assemble evidence that makes the qualitative questions concrete: expert testimony about genre conventions, demonstrative comparisons showing the excerpt relative to the whole, contemporaneous communications revealing the defendant’s intent and tailoring, and market analysis showing whether the taking competes with the original. Judges often frame their legal analysis as a balancing exercise; the more complete and precise the evidentiary record on the nature and the extent of the taking, the more persuasive a party’s invocation of the statutory factors will be.

Conclusion

Factors two and three of the fair-use inquiry require courts to examine what was taken and the character of what was taken. The factual/creative distinction and publication status shape the protective weight of the original; quantitative and qualitative assessments determine whether the portion taken usurps the original’s expressive core. Necessity for a secondary purpose and the special context of commentary and parody can justify broader copying, but only if the taking is tailored and transformative and does not substitute for the original market. These considerations are inherently fact-sensitive, and their resolution depends on the evidentiary record and the procedural posture at which the question is posed. Mastery of these principles equips a student to evaluate how courts will apply the law to concrete fact patterns and to anticipate the kinds of evidence that will matter at each stage of litigation.

Introduction

When courts analyze whether a secondary use of copyrighted material weighs against fair use, they often focus on the effect of that use on the market for the original work and for legally protected derivatives. This inquiry—commonly called the market-effect analysis or "factor four"—asks whether the secondary use usurps demand for the original or for markets that the copyright owner could reasonably exploit. The analysis is both fact-intensive and doctrinally nuanced: courts distinguish between actual harms (lost sales or licensing opportunities) and speculative harms (possible future markets), they examine whether the secondary work functions as a market substitute, and they treat expressive critiques such as parody and criticism differently from copying that competes in commercial markets. Understanding how courts evaluate market harm, what counts as usurpation of licensing markets, and what evidence shifts burdens or creates presumptions is essential before reading judicial opinions that apply factor four.

# Actual and potential markets considered under factor four

Factor four asks courts to evaluate the effect of the secondary use on two classes of markets: (1) actual markets—the present, existing markets in which the copyrighted work is bought, sold, licensed, or otherwise exploited; and (2) potential markets—reasonably expected markets that the copyright owner could enter in the future. The inquiry considers both the nature of the secondary use and the realistic contours of markets that a copyright owner traditionally exploits or could be expected to develop.

Actual markets are straightforward in concept though often complex in proof. They include retail sales of the original work, rental or streaming markets, licensing markets for excerpt use, and derivative markets such as adaptations, sequels, or formats closely tied to the original. Courts look for evidence that purchasers are likely to choose the secondary work instead of the original, or that the secondary use diminishes demand for the original in the channels where the owner competes.

Potential markets raise two important questions: which future markets are "reasonable" to consider, and what level of speculative evidence suffices to show likely harm. Courts do not permit a copyright owner to claim protection for fanciful or speculative markets. Instead, the owner must show that the market allegedly usurped is one that is traditional, reasonable, or likely to develop—based on the nature of the work, the owner's established practices, or commercial realities of the relevant industries. A potential market may be cognizable where the copyright owner has a history of licensing certain derivative uses, where the use is a customary commercial exploitation for works of that type, or where there is a reasonable plan or industry expectation to develop that market.

The balancing role of factor four is context-specific: a secondary work that substitutes for a market the owner actually uses or reasonably could use tends to weigh heavily against fair use; but if the secondary work occupies a different market or creates a new transformative use, factor four will weigh against a finding of market harm.

# Usurpation of traditional, reasonable, or likely-to-develop licensing markets

Usurpation occurs when a secondary use performs the function of the original or occupies a market in which the copyright owner has traditionally or reasonably would seek to exploit the work. Courts pay close attention to licensing markets because copyright owners commonly monetize works by granting permissions for adaptations, excerpts, or commercial uses. If the defendant’s use supplants the need for such licenses, factor four disfavors fair use.

Identifying a licensing market requires a normative assessment of what uses are "traditional" for a given category of work. For example, publishers and rights holders often license excerpts for inclusion in anthologies or textbooks, grant mechanical licenses for musical compositions, and license rights for film or television adaptations. When a defendant’s work performs the same market function—i.e., it displaces the need to obtain a license or otherwise captures the revenue stream a license would provide—the use can be characterized as usurping a licensing market.

Three interrelated considerations guide the court’s assessment of usurpation:

1. The nature of the alleged licensing market. Courts ask whether the market being claimed is one that copyright owners in the relevant field commonly exploit. An asserted market for a specialized or novel use, with no history of licensing, is less likely to be protected.

2. The reasonableness of predicting that the copyright owner would have entered the market. Evidence that the owner had a business plan, prior licensing practices, or industry norms supporting such exploitation strengthens the claim that the market was reasonably likely to develop.

3. The functional similarity between the secondary use and the licensed use. The more the secondary work performs the same function (e.g., providing the same information, entertainment, or commercial benefit) as the licensed derivative, the more likely a court will find usurpation.

Usurpation is not established simply by a negative impact on the value of the original: it must involve displacement of economic demand in a market the owner could reasonably have exploited. When that displacement is present, courts treat factor four as a substantial counterweight to a fair-use defense, especially when other factors (such as the defendant’s commercial purpose and lack of transformation) also point against fair use.

# Why harm from criticism, parody, or ridicule is not cognizable market harm

One of the more delicate distinctions in market-effect analysis is between harms to reputation or esteem—arising from criticism, parody, or ridicule—and harms that reflect market substitution. Criticism and parody are paradigmatic expressive reactions to a copyrighted work, and while they may diminish the original work’s appeal or reputation, that non-economic effect is generally not cognizable as market harm under factor four.

The doctrinal rationale is twofold. First, fair use doctrine protects transformative uses and social commentary; allowing owners to insulate their works from criticism by claiming market harm would unduly chill protected expression. Second, factor four focuses on economic substitution: the inquiry is whether consumers would choose the secondary work instead of the original, or whether the secondary use supplants a market the owner can exploit. An expressive attack that damages reputation but does not displace market demand does not meet that threshold.

Consequently, courts distinguish mere reputational loss from substitution. If parody or criticism operates as a substitute—if consumers would forgo purchasing the original because they can obtain the same expressive content or satisfy the same demand through the secondary work—then market harm may be found. But if the secondary work’s purpose is to comment upon, criticize, or ridicule the original, and the secondary work occupies a different expressive market (for example, political commentary or satire), then the loss of goodwill or marketability resulting from the critique is not the kind of harm factor four protects.

In practical terms, courts examine the secondary work's purpose and effect: transformative critical works that add new expression or meaning and that target the original for commentary are less likely to be deemed market substitutes, even if they affect the original's reputation or marketability. By contrast, a work that merely republishes large portions of the original for commercial gain or as a competing entertainment product will more readily be found to cause cognizable market harm.

# Evidence and presumptions concerning market substitution

Proving market harm is ultimately a question of evidence. The plaintiff bears the burden of showing the secondary use harms the market for the original or a reasonable licensing market. Courts assess a variety of evidence types and, in certain circumstances, apply presumptions that shift how much evidence is needed.

Direct evidence is the most persuasive: lost sales demonstrable by revenue declines, abandoned licensing opportunities, contemporaneous communications indicating purchasers chose the defendant’s work instead, or market surveys showing consumer substitution. Such evidence may include sales figures, licensing agreements, email exchanges, or deposition testimony that purchasers preferred the secondary work over the original.

Indirect evidence may involve circumstantial indicators, such as correlation between the availability of the defendant’s work and reduced sales, overlaps in target markets or consumer demographics, or expert economic testimony modeling likely market effects. Courts evaluate whether such correlations plausibly indicate substitution rather than coincidence or other market forces.

Licensing history is especially powerful. Evidence that the copyright owner has historically licensed the work for similar uses, or that industry practice supports a market for the kind of use in question, supports a finding that the defendant’s work substitutes for that licensing market. Conversely, a lack of licensing history for the specific kind of use weakens the plaintiff’s claim that a reasonable market was usurped.

Courts sometimes apply rebuttable presumptions. Where the defendant's use is a near-exact copy and serves the same market function—particularly when the defendant is engaged in a commercial enterprise—courts may presume that substitution is likely. The strength of this presumption varies with context: full reproductions of a work for the same commercial purpose generate a stronger presumption than partial or fragmented uses. Even when a presumption arises, the defendant may rebut it by showing the secondary use is transformative, targets a different market, or otherwise does not function as a substitute.

The procedural posture affects evidentiary expectations. At an early stage (for example, a motion to dismiss), plaintiffs need only plead plausible facts suggesting market harm; courts accept reasonable inferences about substitution. At summary judgment and trial, plaintiffs must produce concrete evidence—documents, testimony, expert analysis—showing that the secondary use has or is likely to have economic effects on the relevant market. Defendants, in turn, may present their own evidence that there is no substitution: differences in audience, divergent purposes, or lack of economic overlap can overcome a plaintiff’s claim.

Finally, courts are attentive to temporal considerations: immediate market impact is easier to prove than speculative harms decades in the future. However, reasonable plans to develop a market in the near term, especially where the owner has taken concrete steps toward commercialization, may be sufficient to show potential market harm.

# Derivative markets versus transformative markets

A crucial conceptual distinction in factor four is between derivative markets—those for which the copyright owner traditionally licenses derivative works or adaptations—and transformative markets, which involve new, different expressive purposes and audiences. This distinction informs whether the defendant’s work is a substitute for the original’s market or an independent, non-substitutive use.

Derivative markets are the copyright owner's conventional commercial arenas: adaptations (films from novels), sequels, translations, abridgements, and other exploitations that preserve the core expressive content of the original while repackaging it for commercial consumption. When a secondary work serves the same function as a derivative—i.e., it occupies the demand for a film adaptation or a translation—courts treat factor four as implicating usurpation. The central question is whether the secondary use usurps or frustrates the owner’s ability to obtain revenue from those derivative markets.

Transformative markets, by contrast, arise when the secondary work adds new expression, meaning, or message, and thus addresses different audiences or fulfills different purposes. Transformative works often remix, critique, or repurpose the original in ways that do not supplant demand for the original or for the owner’s derivatives. For example, scholarly criticism, parody, or uses that incorporate the original as raw material for a new artistic expression typically fall into transformative markets. Because transformative uses offer new functions for which the copyright owner could not reasonably expect licensing revenue, courts treat them as less likely to cause cognizable market harm.

Courts evaluate the transformative/derivative distinction by looking at the secondary work’s purpose, the degree of alteration, and the likely consumer perception. If the secondary work is likely to satisfy the same market demand—entertainment, information, or functional purposes—as the original or its derivatives, it is more likely derivative in market effect. If the secondary work introduces new insights, critiques, or creative reinterpretations that attract a different audience or serve different needs, it is more likely transformative and less likely to have cognizable market harm.

However, transformation is not a categorical shield. Even a work with transformative elements can cause market harm if it substantially supersedes derivative markets. Likewise, a nontransformative, commercial secondary use is not automatically a usurpation unless it replaces the demand for the original or a reasonable licensing market. The assessment is therefore pragmatic: courts weigh qualitative aspects of expression and quantitative evidence of market substitution together.

Conclusion

Factor four centers on whether the secondary use causes real economic displacement of the original or of otherwise protectible licensing markets. Courts consider both actual and potential markets, recognize protections for reasonable expectations of future exploitation, and carefully exclude reputational harms from the scope of market effect unless those harms amount to substitution. The analysis is driven by evidence—direct proof of lost sales, licensing histories, and market overlap—alongside legal presumptions that may arise in cases of near-exact copying or clear commercial competition. Understanding the difference between derivative markets (which copyright owners routinely exploit) and transformative markets (whose expressive aims create independent demand) is essential for grasping why some uses weigh heavily against fair use while others do not. Procedural posture shapes the intensity of the evidentiary burden, but the doctrinal endpoints remain focused on whether consumers are likely to choose the secondary work in lieu of the original or the licensing opportunities the owner could reasonably have pursued.

Introduction

Fair use is an affirmative defense to copyright infringement that often turns on mixed questions of law and fact. At the pleading stage—typically on a motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) or its state analogs—courts must decide how much of the fair use dispute they may resolve based on the complaint and materials properly before the court. Determining fair use at this early juncture requires careful attention to what documents the court may consider, whether their authenticity is undisputed, which factual disputes are material, and how appellate review will treat the lower court’s decision. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework courts use when resolving fair use on a motion to dismiss: how courts may consider the works themselves, how audiovisual works and transcripts may be incorporated into the record, the role of judicial notice and undisputed authenticity, the line between factual disputes and legal inferences, and standards of appellate review for pleading-stage fair use rulings.

# Considering the works themselves on a motion to dismiss

Fair use analysis rests on the four statutory factors in 17 U.S.C. § 107: (1) the purpose and character of the use (including whether it is transformative and whether commerciality is relevant); (2) the nature of the copyrighted work; (3) the amount and substantiality of the portion used; and (4) the effect of the use on the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work. Because these factors are applied to the particular works and the use at issue, courts frequently need access to the works themselves to resolve a fair use question.

At the pleading stage, the central question is whether the plaintiff’s complaint and any documents properly before the court present a record on which no plausible set of facts could defeat the fair use defense. Under the governing pleading standard, the complaint’s well-pleaded factual allegations must be accepted as true and viewed in the plaintiff’s favor. But courts are not required to accept legal conclusions or allegations contradicted by documents that are incorporated into the complaint or otherwise subject to judicial notice. When the works at issue (the plaintiff’s copyrighted work and the defendant’s allegedly infringing work) are before the court, the court can compare them and decide whether, as a matter of law, the fair use factors—especially transformativeness, amount and substantiality, and market effect—compel a finding of fair use.

Two practical principles govern whether a court may look at the works themselves at the motion-to-dismiss stage. First, if the complaint quotes from or describes the defendant’s work and the plaintiff’s allegations implicitly require reference to the work, the work may be considered part of the pleadings. Second, documents attached to, quoted in, or central to the complaint can be considered without converting the motion into one for summary judgment. When the works are properly before the court, the judge may make comparative textual or visual judgments—e.g., about transformation or the scope of copied material—so long as those judgments do not depend on disputed factual issues outside the pleadings.

Courts must take care to distinguish straightforward questions of law from disputed matters of fact. For example, whether a secondary use is transformative often presents a mixed question: the judge can determine whether, on the face of the works, the secondary use adds new expression, meaning, or message. But if the plaintiff alleges facts about how consumers perceive the use, the defendant’s intent, or other extrinsic matters that bear on transformation or market harm, those factual allegations are credited at the pleading stage unless contradicted by materials properly before the court.

# Incorporation by reference of audiovisual works and transcripts

Many disputes involve audiovisual works (video, film, or other multimedia) and related text—such as transcripts, screen captures, or time-stamped excerpts. Incorporation by reference doctrine defines when those materials may be treated as part of the pleadings.

Incorporation by reference is a narrow doctrine: a document not physically attached to the complaint may be considered on a motion to dismiss if the complaint “relies heavily upon” the document or if the document is “integral” or “central” to the plaintiff’s claim and its authenticity is undisputed. For audiovisual works, courts often consider embedded links, references in the complaint to particular timestamps or scenes, or quotations or descriptions that make the audiovisual work central to the allegations. When a complaint alleges that a defendant copied or repurposed a specific portion of a film or video, attaching a transcript or clips, or directing the court to a publicly available video, can enable the court to assess the scope and nature of the use without converting the motion into a summary-judgment motion.

However, incorporation requires more than mere relevance. The text or audiovisual file must be the kind of document that the claimant relies on as the basis for the claim; it must be attached, quoted in full or part, or clearly relied upon; and its authenticity must not be in dispute. Transcripts often accompany audiovisual works to facilitate textual analysis. A court may incorporate a transcript when it is accurate, corresponds to the portions of the audiovisual work alleged in the complaint, and is not contested. Where the transcript was prepared by the plaintiff or defendant and accurately reflects the publicly available audiovisual work, courts commonly accept it as incorporated. But if the transcript is disputed, incomplete, or materially inaccurate, relying on it at the motion-to-dismiss stage risks resolving contested factual issues prematurely.

Finally, courts must be cautious when the audiovisual material on which incorporation is sought is not readily accessible (e.g., behind a paywall or subject to removal). In those circumstances, courts may decline to consider the work, or may take judicial notice that the work exists while reserving judgment on the fair use defense until later stages where authenticity and completeness can be established.

# Judicial notice and the role of undisputed authenticity

Judicial notice and undisputed authenticity are separate but related doctrines that permit courts to consider extra-pleading materials on a motion to dismiss.

Judicial notice (Fed. R. Evid. 201 or state analogs) allows the court to accept as true certain adjudicative facts that are “not subject to reasonable dispute” because they are generally known within the court’s territorial jurisdiction or can be accurately and readily determined from sources whose accuracy cannot reasonably be questioned. Courts may take judicial notice of facts about the existence and content of materials that are publicly available and not reasonably disputed—such as a video posted on a widely accessed site, the text of laws, or factual information in government records. Judicial notice is not appropriate for factual propositions that are subject to reasonable dispute or that require credibility determinations. When a party asks the court to judicially notice the content or meaning of an audiovisual work, the court should take care to limit the notice to the existence and basic content of the work, not inferences about state of mind, market effects, or consumer perception which remain factual questions.

Authenticity is a separate concept: the proponent of a document or recording must establish that it is what it purports to be (Fed. R. Evid. 901). When materials are offered in support of a motion to dismiss, courts often treat undisputedly authentic documents as proper to consider: if both parties agree that a video clip and its transcript are genuine, the court may compare them to the complaint’s allegations. Authenticity can be established by admission, certification, or other evidence, but at the pleading stage the court should not require formal authentication if the record reflects undisputed authenticity (for instance, a public posting by the defendant or a published work plainly identified and unchallenged).

Using judicial notice and undisputed authenticity together, courts can consider public and indisputable materials at the pleading stage. But they must avoid taking judicial notice of contested facts. The fact that a video exists and contains certain words or images may be judicially noticeable; whether those words or images have a particular transformative effect or cause market harm generally remains an evidentiary question for later proceedings unless the materials manifestly show the contrary.

# Limits on resolving factual disputes versus legal inferences

Fair use is often a mixed question that blends pure legal determinations with underlying factual predicates. At the pleading stage, courts must respect the plaintiff’s well-pleaded factual allegations while recognizing that some aspects of fair use can be determined as a matter of law when the record is plain and undisputed.

The line between what is legal and what is factual is critical. Pure legal issues—such as whether a particular use, on its face, is transformative—may be decided at the motion-to-dismiss stage if they can be resolved solely by examining the works and the complaint. Conversely, factual disputes—such as the defendant’s intent, the extent of public dissemination, consumer perception, the market for licensing, and any actual market substitution—cannot be resolved on a Rule 12 motion where the plaintiff’s allegations, accepted as true, could lead a reasonable factfinder to rule against the defendant.

Courts should avoid premature findings that rest on disputed or unpleaded facts. For example, when the determination of transformative use depends on how an ordinary observer will perceive the work, resolution may require evidence about consumer perception and context, which is a factual inquiry. Similarly, the fourth factor—market effect—often depends on evidence about licensing practices, specific markets, and potential substitution; competing inferences from the same set of facts can make disposition at the pleading stage inappropriate.

Evaluating the amount and substantiality factor illustrates the tension. When the complaint and incorporated materials clearly show that only nonproprietary or insubstantial portions were used, a court may find this factor favors fair use as a matter of law. But when the amount taken is large or comprises the “heart” of the work—matters that can reasonably be disputed based on credibility or context—the court should decline to resolve the issue on a motion to dismiss.

Additionally, courts must be mindful of who bears the burden at different stages. Fair use is an affirmative defense, so the defendant bears the burden of proving it. On a motion to dismiss, however, courts ask whether the complaint states a claim for which relief can be granted; if the defendant’s publicly available and undisputed materials demonstrate that the use is fair as a matter of law, dismissal is appropriate despite the defendant’s status as the proponent of the defense. If, instead, disputed factual or contextual issues are material to the fair use analysis, the proper course is to deny the motion and allow discovery so factual matters can be developed.

# Appellate review of pleading-stage fair use determinations

When a trial court resolves fair use on a motion to dismiss, the question of appellate review arises. The standards of review differ depending on whether the appellate court is reviewing pure legal rulings, factual findings, or mixed questions.

A motion to dismiss under Rule 12(b)(6) presents primarily a legal determination: whether, accepting the complaint’s well-pleaded facts as true and considering materials properly before the court, the complaint states a viable claim. Appellate courts typically review de novo the lower court’s dismissal of a complaint. Thus, if a trial court concluded, as a matter of law, that the defendant’s use was fair based solely on the complaint and incorporated materials, an appellate court will reexamine that determination without deference.

However, when a dismissal rests on subordinate factual findings—for example, judicially noticeable facts or undisputed documentary evidence that the trial court accepted—those factual determinations may receive different treatment on appeal. Appellate courts generally review factual findings for clear error if they arise from evidence developed at trial. At the dismissal stage, where the court has taken judicial notice or relied on undisputed documents, appellate review remains de novo with respect to the legal implications of those facts, but appellate courts will be cautious about overturning discretionary judgments about whether materials were properly considered without converting the motion into one for summary judgment.

Appellate courts also recognize the mixed nature of fair use: many components (like transformation when clearly visible on the face of the works) are legal, while others (consumer perception, actual market harm, intent) are factual. On appeal, then, legal conclusions are reviewed de novo, while any underlying factual determinations made in support of those legal conclusions may be reviewed for clear error if they are findings of fact.

Finally, appellate courts may consider whether the district court erred in resolving disputed issues of fact at the pleading stage. If the lower court dismissed the complaint despite plausible factual allegations that would defeat the defense, an appellate court may reverse and remand for further proceedings. Conversely, if the materials before the court—properly incorporated and undisputed—compel only one reasonable conclusion about fair use, an appellate court will affirm a dismissal as a matter of law.

Conclusion

Resolving fair use at the pleading stage requires balancing the court’s duty to accept well-pleaded factual allegations against the practical reality that some fair use questions can be resolved by comparing the works themselves. Incorporation by reference, judicial notice, and undisputed authenticity permit courts to consider audiovisual works and transcripts without converting a 12(b)(6) motion into a motion for summary judgment, but they impose limits: courts must not resolve genuinely disputed factual issues or rely on inferences contradicted by the complaint. Appellate review will treat legal conclusions de novo while giving more deferential review to factual findings. Students should be alert to the procedural postures and evidentiary rules that control what materials are before the court and how those materials shape the fair use analysis at the earliest stages of litigation.

Introduction

Section 301 of the Copyright Act is the principal federal-preemption provision that limits the ability of plaintiffs to use state-law causes of action to obtain protection for subject matter that federal copyright law covers. For students preparing to read judicial opinions in this area, it is critical to understand the two-step preemption test courts apply, how courts identify when a state-law claim is truly independent of copyright, the contours of the “extra element” or “qualitative difference” doctrine, and the practical consequences for litigants and drafters. This chapter explains the governing statutory framework and doctrine, elaborates how courts apply the rules to common claims (contract, implied contract, fraud), describes drafting strategies that can produce state-law claims that survive preemption, and surveys remedial implications when a state-law claim is either preempted or deemed independent.

# The two-prong § 301 test: subject matter and equivalency of rights

Text and basic structure. Section 301(a) provides that all legal or equitable rights that are equivalent to any of the exclusive rights within the general scope of copyright are governed exclusively by federal law. The provision is aimed at creating a uniform federal regime for the protection of works of authorship that fall within the scope of federal copyright law. Courts therefore apply a two-prong inquiry before concluding that a state-law claim is preempted.

Prong one: the work must be of a type protected by federal copyright (the subject-matter requirement). The first prong asks whether the subject matter of the state-law claim falls within the subject matter of copyright as defined in §§ 102 and 103, i.e., whether it is an “original work of authorship fixed in a tangible medium of expression” (copyrightable expression, and for compilations or derivative works, whether the aspects at issue are the kinds of works copyright covers). If the state-law claim seeks to protect something outside the statutory ambit — for example, an unprotectable idea, a purely factual compilation that lacks the requisite originality, an uncopyrightable process or method, or a trademark-related interest in source-identifying goodwill — then § 301(a) ordinarily does not apply and the state claim survives the first-prong inquiry.

Prong two: the rights asserted must be equivalent to the exclusive rights provided by federal copyright (the equivalency requirement). If the subject matter is of a type covered by federal copyright, the second question is whether the state-law claim asserts rights that are “equivalent” to one or more of the exclusive rights enumerated in § 106 (the rights to reproduce, prepare derivative works, distribute copies, perform publicly, and display publicly). A state-law right is equivalent and thus preempted if it consists of the same bundle of exclusive entitlements that federal law provides, even if the state claim is couched in different legal terms or labeled otherwise.

Operationalizing the equivalency inquiry. Courts typically perform the equivalency analysis by comparing the “elements” or the relief sought under the state-law claim to those of a copyright infringement claim. If establishing liability under the state cause of action requires proof of rights or activities that correspond to reproduction, distribution, public performance, public display, or preparation of derivative works — and there is no additional element that qualitatively alters the nature of the claim — then the state law claim is preempted. Conversely, if the state claim contains an “extra element” that changes the nature of the action so it is qualitatively different from a copyright claim, the state claim is not preempted (this leads into the extra element doctrine discussed below).

Procedural posture and burdens. Preemption is a question of law and frequently arises on motions to dismiss for failure to state a claim or on motions for summary judgment. When raised early, courts may resolve preemption by examining the complaint and the works attached or incorporated by reference. Plaintiffs bear the burden of pleading a plausible state-law claim that is not preempted; defendants often use § 301 preemption as a threshold defense to remove or dismiss state-law claims that merely replicate copyright causes of action.

# The “extra element” or “qualitative difference” doctrine

Nature and purpose of the doctrine. The “extra element” (also called “qualitative difference”) doctrine is the principal mechanism courts use to determine whether a state-law claim that touches upon sexually similar subject matter nonetheless survives preemption because it contains additional elements that change its nature. The doctrine recognizes that Congress did not intend to displace all state-law protections merely because they involve works of authorship; rather, preemption applies only when the state causes of action protect the same exclusive rights that federal copyright covers. The presence of an extra element can make a state claim qualitatively different from a copyright claim, and thus outside § 301’s preemptive sweep.

What counts as an “extra element.” An extra element is an element of the state-law cause of action that is not required under federal copyright law and that changes the nature of the action in a meaningful way. Typical examples include: breach of fiduciary duty, breach of confidence, fraud or misrepresentation, conversion of physical property distinct from the copyrighted expression, violation of a privacy or publicity right, or a contract obligation independent from any reproduction or distribution right. The extra element must be more than a merely “different label” applied to the same underlying fact pattern; it must impose a legally significant requirement beyond what copyright law demands.

Qualitative difference versus mere factual allegations. Courts distinguish between state-law claims that add factual details to a copyright claim and those that add a legal element that alters the claim’s legal character. For example, pleading an intentional misrepresentation with elements of reliance and scienter is an extra quantum of proof beyond mere copying or unauthorized reproduction. In contrast, a state claim that simply alleges “unauthorized copying” without additional legal content is unlikely to satisfy the extra-element test.

Tests courts apply. Courts often apply two slightly different formulations. One asks whether the state cause of action contains “extra elements” that change the nature of the claim so that it is qualitatively different from a copyright infringement claim. Another asks whether vindication of the state-law right would afford the plaintiff “legal or equitable rights that are equivalent” to those protected by copyright. Both approaches focus on whether the state claim protects a legally distinct interest or merely overlaps with exclusive copyright rights.

Practical consequences and pitfalls. The extra-element analysis can be fact-intensive. Courts look at the elements of the state cause of action, the relief sought, and the underlying conduct alleged. Pleading surplus facts will not save a claim from preemption if the additional allegations merely describe how the copying occurred; plaintiffs should aim to articulate legal obligations or distinct harms that do not track the exclusive rights enumerated in § 106. Moreover, the presence of an extra element does not guarantee survival if that element is insubstantial or if the remedy sought would still amount to enforcement of exclusive copyright rights.

# Preemption and breach of contract, implied contract, and fraud claims

Breach of contract. Express contract claims often arise in disputes about rights to use or exploit copyrighted works. Whether a breach-of-contract claim is preempted depends on whether the contract creates obligations independent of the exclusive rights afforded by copyright. If the contract simply restates the license to copy or distribute and the plaintiff seeks to enforce the same rights as a copyright owner, courts will typically find preemption. By contrast, contracts that impose duties that differ qualitatively from copyright rights — for example, confidentiality covenants, noncompete restrictions, covenant to return or destroy specific tangible embodiments, or contractual obligations to perform or deliver services — can provide the “extra element” needed to avoid preemption.

Key analytical points for contract claims. First, examine the nature of the contractual obligation: is it essentially a grant of a license to reproduce or create derivative works, or is it an independent promise (e.g., an undertaking to keep disclosed material secret, or to provide specified services)? Second, analyze the relief sought: whether the remedy seeks to vindicate the copyright owner's exclusive rights or enforces an independent contractual duty. Third, consider whether the contract terms materially add to obligations under copyright law—if so, the claim is more likely to survive. Courts also consider whether the contract merely clarifies ownership of copyright (a question often reserved for federal law) versus imposing separate duties concerning noncopying, delivery of material, or confidential handling.

Implied contracts. Implied contract claims are trickier because they often seek to vindicate expectations rooted in the exchange of creative materials. An implied contract claim requires proof of mutual intent or assent, consideration, and promise terms inferred from the parties’ conduct. The courts scrutinize whether the implied promise sought to be enforced is distinct from the bundle of exclusive rights under § 106. If the implied promise is simply that one party will not reproduce or distribute the other party’s copyrighted work, that promise is essentially the same as a copyright right and will be preempted. On the other hand, if the implied contract includes obligations that are not within the scope of copyright — for example, an implied agreement to maintain the confidences of a manuscript, or to pay consideration for noncopying duties — the claim may avoid preemption.

Special concerns. Proving the terms of an implied contract is fact-intensive, and courts may be reluctant to find an enforceable implied promise that is not expressly stated, particularly where federal copyright law supplies an integrated licensing framework. Plaintiffs asserting implied contract claims should clearly identify the independent promise and the distinct legal duty it creates.

Fraud and misrepresentation. Fraud claims assert that a defendant obtained something (often access to creative material) through deception, false representations, or concealment. The typical elements of a fraud claim are a false representation or omission of a material fact, scienter (knowledge of falsity), justifiable reliance by the plaintiff, and damages caused by the reliance. Fraud claims are not categorically preempted under § 301 merely because they concern copyrighted works; many courts hold that fraud claims survive preemption when they require proof of elements that are not part of a copyright infringement claim — notably scienter and reliance.

Analytical distinctions. The critical question is whether the fraud claim’s gravamen is the copying or other exclusive acts that federal law regulates, or whether it seeks to remedy the deceit itself as an independent wrong. For example, a misrepresentation that induces a plaintiff to part with marketable rights or property can be actionable independently of whether the defendant later reproduced the work. Conversely, alleging fraud stripped to its essential allegations of unauthorized copying (i.e., claiming a defendant lied about authorization and then copied) may be treated as an attempt to repurpose a copyright claim in tort and be preempted if the element of deceit does not change the claim’s nature.

Practical pleading considerations. To avoid preemption, fraud pleadings should emphasize the specific false statements, the plaintiff’s reliance, the intent to deceive, and the harms flowing from the deception that are not merely the loss of control over copyrighted expression. Courts sometimes permit parallel claims in the alternative — for example, a state-law fraud claim alongside a copyright claim — but preemption can still dispose of the fraud claim at motion practice if its substance merely mirrors infringement.

# Drafting around preemption with independent contractual promises

Principles for drafting. Those who draft agreements involving copyrighted works can take active steps to create state-law claims that survive § 301 preemption by incorporating obligations and remedies that are qualitatively distinct from the federal copyright grant. The two core drafting principles are: (1) create obligations that go beyond the exclusive rights enumerated in § 106, and (2) define remedies or breach consequences that are not merely duplicative of remedies available under federal copyright law.

Specific drafting approaches

1. Confidentiality and non-disclosure covenants. Contracts that impose duties to keep materials confidential or to restrict use to a particular purpose introduce an extra element — a duty of confidentiality — which state courts often treat as independent from copyright. A confidentiality breach claim does not require proof of copying and can seek damages and equitable relief based on the breach of the promise.

2. Return or destruction obligations for tangible media. Requiring the recipient to return or destroy tangible copies or to provide certifications of destruction creates an obligation tied to the physical property, rather than the copyrighted expression per se. If a party fails to return or destroy tangible items, a conversion or replevin claim can involve distinct elements and remedies.

3. Noncompete and exclusivity provisions. Contractual restrictions on the parties’ conduct that are broader than federal copyright rights (for instance, covenants not to exploit similar subject matter or to refrain from soliciting certain customers) can be enforced as breach-of-contract claims without invoking copyright preemption, provided they comply with whatever state law constraints apply to noncompetes.

4. Confidentiality plus limited use licenses. Draft licenses that explicitly limit authorized uses, require reporting, audit rights, or obligations to segregate material. Such operational duties (an obligation to account, to segregate client lists, or to implement security measures) help show the contract imposes extra elements beyond the bare right to reproduce.

5. Warranties, indemnities, and specific damages provisions. Including express warranties (e.g., that the licensed material is original and does not infringe third-party rights) and specific contractual remedies (liquidated damages, contractual penalties, or role-specific remedies) create rights that are distinct from federal statutory rights and may change the remedial landscape even if some overlap exists.

6. Procedural and enforcement mechanisms. Adding dispute-resolution mechanisms (escrow requirements, mandatory return on termination, or express limitations on remedies) can influence the nature of the claim and the available relief, and may be treated as creating contract-based duties.

Drafting caveats. Draft drafters must ensure that the added contractual obligations are real and enforceable under general contract law. A purported “extra element” that is illusory, unenforceable as a matter of public policy, or indistinguishable from the exclusive rights of copyright is unlikely to prevent preemption. Also be mindful of other federal laws that might preempt state-law terms, and of state-specific limitations on noncompete or liquidated-damage provisions.

# Remedial implications of preemption determinations

Effect on remedies. A key practical consequence of preemption is that it can deprive a plaintiff of state-law remedies that might otherwise be available while leaving intact federal remedies. If a state claim is preempted, it is dismissed; the plaintiff must rely on federal copyright law and its remedial scheme. Conversely, if the state claim survives because it contains an extra element, the plaintiff can pursue state-law remedies in addition to — or instead of — federal remedies, subject to any overlap limitations.

Comparative remedies: what is at stake. Federal copyright law provides a specific remedial palette, including injunctive relief, statutory damages for certain timely registered works, actual damages and the infringer’s profits, and reasonable attorneys’ fees in “exceptional cases”. State-law claims may allow remedies not available under federal law, such as consequential damages, punitive damages (where state law permits), contractual damages specified in agreements (including liquidated damages), and alternative equitable remedies like replevin for tangible property. Thus, whether a claim is preempted can have significant consequences for recoverable damages and the form of relief.

Statute of limitations and tolling. Preemption can interact with statutes of limitations. In some cases, federal copyright law provides different limitation periods than state law. If a state-law claim is preempted and dismissed, plaintiffs are limited to federal claims and their operative limitations. Conversely, if a state claim survives, the plaintiff may be able to take advantage of a longer state statute of limitations. Procedurally, defendants sometimes move early to dismiss state claims on preemption grounds to avoid exposure to different remedial regimes, and plaintiffs should be aware of potential waiver or estoppel issues if they plead inconsistent theories.

Attorney’s fees and costs. The availability of attorneys’ fees differs substantially between state and federal causes of action. Where an independent state claim survives preemption, attorneys’ fee rules are governed by state law unless federal law independently provides a basis. Plaintiffs and defendants should therefore carefully assess the costs and benefits of avoiding preemption.

Injunctions and equitable relief. Both state courts and federal courts can award injunctive relief for state causes of action that survive preemption. However, if a state-law claim is preempted and the plaintiff proceeds under the Copyright Act, equitable relief is still available under federal law. The scope and standards for injunctive relief, including preliminary injunctions, may differ under state law (equitable balancing, irreparable harm presumptions) and federal law (factor tests that include likelihood of success and irreparable harm). Parties should consider how the choice of forum and the characterization of claims affect access to injunctive remedies.

Remedies overlap and double recovery concerns. Courts are attentive to the risk of double recovery when plaintiffs pursue both federal and surviving state claims. Even when both causes of action are theoretically available, courts often limit recoverable damages to prevent duplication — for example, by offsetting awards or allowing only non-overlapping elements of damage to be recovered under state law. Drafting remedies in contracts with clear statements of remedies and exclusivity of recovery can reduce uncertainty in litigation.

Procedural posture and strategic considerations. Because preemption is often resolved early, defendants frequently raise it in motions to dismiss; plaintiffs may attempt to plead alternative claims to preserve remedies. Settlement leverage and bargaining positions are affected by whether state-law claims survive; defendants facing potential punitive or consequential damages under state law may be incentivized to settle. Conversely, plaintiffs may tailor pleadings and agreements (as discussed above) to preserve contract-based remedies that offer advantages over federal relief.

Conclusion

Section 301 preemption is a critical doctrinal gatekeeper in litigation involving creative works. The two-prong test — subject matter and equivalency of rights — establishes the initial framework, while the extra-element doctrine provides the principal means for state claims to survive preemption by demonstrating a qualitative difference from federal copyright claims. Contractual drafting can create independent obligations that will likely avoid preemption, but care must be taken to craft real, enforceable covenants and remedies distinct from § 106 rights. Finally, preemption determinations have important remedial effects, influencing available damages, equitable relief, statutes of limitations, and settlement leverage. Understanding how courts analyze the elements of state causes of action and the relief sought is essential for predicting outcomes and for advising clients on both litigation strategy and contract drafting.

Introduction

Illinois contract law recognizes that a contractual promise made by one party for the benefit of another may, in limited circumstances, give that nonparty the right to enforce the promise. But Illinois law construes such third-party beneficiary claims narrowly. The determination whether a nonparty may enforce a contract turns on a mix of contract interpretation (what the contracting parties intended) and doctrine about privity and standing (who has a legal interest sufficient to sue). For students preparing to read judicial opinions on this topic, it is important to understand the doctrinal categories—intended versus incidental beneficiaries—the evidentiary demands for establishing an intent to benefit, how courts treat online platform agreements (clickwrap, browsewrap, and similar terms), and how choice-of-law and forum-selection provisions interact with third-party enforcement. The procedural posture of a case—whether the claim is being tested on a motion to dismiss, a motion to compel arbitration, summary judgment, or at trial—also shapes the analysis and the relief available. This chapter explains the governing concepts, typical judicial tests, and how courts apply them to fact patterns common in the Internet and platform context.

# Intended versus incidental beneficiaries

A foundational distinction in third-party beneficiary law is between intended beneficiaries and incidental beneficiaries. Only intended beneficiaries can enforce a contract; incidental beneficiaries cannot. The line between the two depends on the contracting parties’ objective intent at the time of contracting.

An intended beneficiary is a nonparty whom the parties to a contract meant to benefit directly or to confer a right of enforcement. The critical question is not whether a benefit actually flows to a nonparty, but whether the contracting parties manifested an intention to bestow enforceable rights on that nonparty. Under Illinois law, courts look to the language of the contract and the surrounding circumstances to decide whether the beneficiary was intended or merely incidental.

An incidental beneficiary, by contrast, is someone who happens to receive a benefit from the contract but who was not the object of the contracting parties’ intention to confer enforceable rights. Incidental beneficiaries cannot sue to enforce the contract because the parties did not intend to create a legal duty owed to them.

How courts decide this classification

Illinois courts apply objective principles of contract interpretation. The primary source is the contract text itself. If the language expressly names or otherwise clearly identifies a nonparty and indicates that the promise was made for that person’s (or class’s) direct benefit, courts will typically find an intended beneficiary. If the contract confers a benefit only collaterally or incidentally (for example, performance that merely has a side effect helping a third party), courts will label the beneficiary incidental.

When the contract is ambiguous, courts may examine extrinsic evidence to determine what the parties intended. That evidence can include precontract negotiations, the circumstances leading to the agreement, the relationship of the parties, industry context, and other indicia that the parties expected the nonparty to rely on or enforce the promise.

Typical factual patterns

- Express designation: A contract that names a particular third party (e.g., “Company A shall pay $X to Beneficiary B”) or a clearly defined class (e.g., “employees of Company A”) typically creates intended-beneficiary rights for the named or defined nonparty.

- Contract for benefit of third party: When one party expressly contracts to pay or perform for the benefit of a third party (for example, an insurer promising to pay a named beneficiary), the third party is ordinarily an intended beneficiary.

- Incidental benefit from performance: Arrangements that merely secure a benefit indirectly (for example, a construction contract that improves neighborhood property values) will not create enforceable third-party rights.

Vesting and scope of rights

Even when a third party is an intended beneficiary, the beneficiary’s rights may be subject to conditions and may vest only upon satisfying those conditions (e.g., acceptance, reliance, bringing suit within some timeframe). Illinois law follows general contract doctrine: the contract may specify when and how a third party’s right vests, and absent clear language courts will consider whether the beneficiary has accepted the benefit, materially changed position in reliance, or brought suit to enforce the right. Vesting affects whether the original parties can modify or rescind the contract without the beneficiary’s consent.

# Strong presumption against third-party enforcement

Illinois courts begin with a presumption against allowing nonparties to enforce contracts. This presumption reflects two policy considerations: the preservation of privity as a default rule limiting contractual enforcement, and the principle that creating legal obligations for a nonparty must be the product of the contracting parties’ deliberate choice, not an implication. Consequently, mere receipt of a benefit or foreseeability that a third party would benefit is not sufficient to overcome the presumption.

Practical implications of the presumption

When faced with a third-party enforcement claim, courts apply a skeptical lens to the nonparty’s assertion of rights. The presumption means plaintiffs bear the burden of affirmatively showing that the contracting parties intended to confer enforceable rights on them. Courts will not infer such intent lightly from general or ambiguous language. The following consequences flow from that posture:

- Language must be clear. To overcome the presumption, contractual language indicating intent to benefit a third party should be express, precise, or at least sufficiently definite to identify the beneficiary and the interest conferred.

- No reliance on incidental benefits. If a third party simply benefits from a contract’s performance in a foreseeable but incidental way, courts will not grant enforcement rights.

- Close scrutiny of classes. When a contract refers to a broad or amorphous class (“users,” “customers,” the “general public”), courts examine whether the class is defined with sufficient certainty to support third-party enforcement.

- Policy and public-interest exceptions are narrow. Claims that public policy or equitable considerations require third-party enforcement face an uphill battle because the presumption reflects the priority of contractual autonomy.

Burden of proof

In Illinois litigation, the party asserting third-party enforcement bears the burden to prove that the parties intended to create a third-party right. Thus, in a motion to dismiss, a complaint must plead facts plausibly showing such intent; in summary judgment, admissible evidence must support it; and at trial, the claimant must convince the factfinder by a preponderance of the evidence.

# Requirement of clear or express declaration of intent to benefit

Closely related to the presumption against third-party enforcement is the rule that courts require a clear or express declaration of intent to create enforceable rights for a third party. The approach centers on interpreting the contract: courts look for language that indicates that contracting parties meant to make the third party a beneficiary with rights to enforce.

What constitutes a “clear or express” declaration

There is no single magic formula, but the declaration must do more than incidentally recognize a benefit. Sufficient indications include:

- Naming the third party explicitly (e.g., an identified person, class, or consortium), especially where the contract ties a promise to the beneficiary.

- Using words that show intent to confer a right (e.g., “shall be for the benefit of,” “for the express benefit of,” “enforceable by [third party]”).

- Granting specific remedies or rights to the third party (e.g., the right to receive payments, the right to enforce obligations, or to demand performance).

- Defining a narrow, ascertainable class of beneficiaries with terms that show the parties contemplated conferring rights on that class.

By contrast, language that is generalized or permissive—such as a statement that performance “may benefit” or “is intended to help” a broad group—will typically not meet the standard.

Contract formation and the intent requirement

The requirement of a clear declaration is an interpretive rule rather than an additional doctrinal element layered on top of contract formation. The court first determines whether a contract was formed between the parties; if so, the court then interprets the contract to ascertain whether the parties manifested an intent to benefit a third party. Thus, the analysis often proceeds in two steps: (1) was there a valid contract between the signatories?; and (2) does the contract’s language and context show an intent to give enforceable rights to a third party?

Modification, rescission, and defenses

Even when a third party is an intended beneficiary, contracting parties ordinarily retain the power to modify or rescind the contract before the beneficiary’s right vests. If the contract explicitly reserves the right to change terms or condition performance, courts will enforce those reservations unless the beneficiary’s rights have vested by acceptance, detrimental reliance, or by bringing suit. Similarly, defenses that are valid against the promisor (e.g., fraud in the inducement, illegality, unconscionability) can be raised against a third party seeking enforcement unless the contract or law provides otherwise.

# Application to clickwrap/browsewrap/platform agreements

Online agreements pose particular challenges in third-party beneficiary analysis because of their often-standardized wording, class references (e.g., “users”), and the realities of assent and notice. Illinois courts apply the same substantive principles described above, but formation and interpretation issues arising from the online context figure prominently.

Formation and assent issues

Before reaching the third-party-beneficiary question, a court must determine whether the online terms are a binding contract between the relevant signatories. Issues that commonly arise include whether the user manifested assent (clickwrap vs. browsewrap), whether the terms were reasonably conspicuous, and whether the person entering into the contract had authority to bind the account holder.

- Clickwrap agreements: These require an affirmative action—typically clicking “I agree” or checking a box—after being presented with terms. Courts generally view clear clickwrap assent as forming a contract, provided the terms were available to be read and the user had a reasonable opportunity to review them.

- Browsewrap agreements: These purport to bind users by posting terms on a website without requiring explicit assent. Courts are more skeptical about formation in browsewrap cases and look for adequate notice (e.g., conspicuous links, repeated presentation of terms, or language that puts the user on inquiry notice). Browsewrap contracts often fail formation challenges for lack of assent.

- Hybrid and modified presentations: Many platforms use layered notices, banners, or other techniques. Courts examine the totality of presentation to determine whether a reasonable person would have understood the terms to be part of the bargain.

Interpretation of “user” and class descriptions

Platform agreements frequently use sweeping labels—“user,” “customer,” “viewer,” or “subscriber”—and often include terms that purport to benefit or regulate such classes. Courts consider whether such terms identify a sufficiently definite class so as to permit a member to sue as an intended beneficiary. Key considerations include:

- Specificity and certainty: A contract that refers to a vague, broad class without further definition is less likely to create enforceable rights in any individual member.

- Direct tie to the promise: Courts look for an explicit link between the promise and the class. A clause that, for instance, prohibits certain conduct by the platform “for the benefit of users” but offers no mechanism to enforce rights may not suffice.

- Commercial context: In certain settings (e.g., terms that specify delivery or payment obligations for subscribers), specificity and direct reliance may support a finding that the platform intended to confer rights on subscribers.

Third-party enforcement of arbitration and forum-selection clauses

A common issue is whether a nonparty may enforce an arbitration clause or compel arbitration under platform terms. Courts in Illinois apply contract interpretation principles: a nonparty can only enforce such clauses if the parties’ intent to allow third-party enforcement is clear. In the absence of an express delegation or an explicit grant of enforcement rights to third parties, courts will typically deny a nonparty’s motion to compel arbitration. Similarly, for forum-selection and limitation-of-liability clauses, a nonparty must show that the agreement was intended to create enforceable rights in that nonparty.

Estoppel and agency doctrines

Even when third-party-beneficiary status is not established, courts sometimes allow enforcement by nonparties under related doctrines such as equitable estoppel, agency, or assignment (where appropriate). Illinois courts are attentive, however, to maintain the presumption against third-party enforcement: equitable doctrines are applied only when the nonparty’s ability to enforce is justified by the contract’s structure or the parties’ conduct. For example, if a nonparty has acted in reliance on the contract or the contracting party has represented that the nonparty could enforce the agreement, courts may apply estoppel principles. Students should note that these doctrines have distinct elements and requirements separate from third-party-beneficiary rules.

Procedural issues unique to online contexts

The procedural posture—e.g., motion to dismiss versus motion to compel arbitration—matters greatly. Many disputes over online terms are resolved early via motions that test formation and interpretation. Courts may resolve whether a plaintiff is a member of the intended-beneficiary class at the pleading stage if the contract is unambiguous, but will allow factual development if aspects of assent or identity of the contracting parties are contested. Summary judgment is common once contract text and platform evidence (screenshots, timestamps, account records) are presented.

# Interplay with choice-of-law clauses and forum selection

Choice-of-law and forum-selection clauses are fixtures in many contracts, including online terms. When a nonparty seeks to enforce or resist enforcement of a contract under Illinois law, the interaction between those clauses and third-party-beneficiary doctrine raises several issues: which law governs the interpretation of third-party rights, whether the forum-selection clause applies to the nonparty, and whether public policy considerations permit application of a foreign clause.

Choice-of-law: Which law governs third-party rights?

The first question is whether contract interpretation (including whether a third party has enforceable rights) is governed by the law selected in the contract. Under general principles, parties may validly specify the governing law for their contract, and courts will enforce a reasonable choice-of-law clause if the selected jurisdiction has a substantial relation to the contract or if the chosen law is not arbitrary and does not contravene a fundamental policy of a jurisdiction with a materially greater interest.

When the dispute involves a nonparty, courts examine three issues:

- Is the choice-of-law clause enforceable between the contracting parties? If yes, the contract interpretation—including whether the parties intended to benefit a third party—is typically governed by the chosen law as among them.

- Does the chosen law address third-party-beneficiary doctrine in a materially different way? If so, applying it could produce a different outcome about third-party rights.

- Does public policy in the forum prevent applying the foreign law, especially when applying it would deny parties or claimants protections the forum would otherwise provide?

Illinois courts will respect a valid choice-of-law clause, but they also consider whether applying the foreign law violates Illinois’ fundamental policies. When the chosen law expressly deals with third-party beneficiaries (for example, by allowing broad third-party enforcement where Illinois would not), a court may scrutinize whether permitting the clause to dictate the outcome is appropriate.

Forum selection: Who must litigate where?

Forum-selection clauses (including mandatory forum clauses and arbitration clauses specifying a venue) state where disputes are to be adjudicated. Their enforcement against nonparties depends on the contract language and the extent to which the clause expresses an intent to bind or benefit third parties.

- Express coverage: A forum-selection clause that expressly applies to “users and their representatives” or includes language extending rights and obligations to third parties may be enforceable by or against those third parties.

- Ambiguous scope: If the clause’s reach is ambiguous, courts are likely to interpret it narrowly, favoring allowing a nonparty to litigate in the forum of its choosing unless clear language indicates otherwise.

- Public-policy limits: Even where a forum-selection clause would otherwise apply, courts may decline to enforce it if doing so would offend fundamental fairness or statutory protections, particularly when the nonparty is a consumer or has no meaningful connection to the choice-of-law forum.

Procedural consequences

The interplay of third-party-beneficiary status with forum and choice-of-law clauses has immediate procedural consequences. If a nonparty is recognized as an intended beneficiary and the contract contains a binding forum-selection or arbitration clause, the nonparty may be compelled to arbitrate or to litigate in the selected forum. Conversely, if the nonparty cannot meet the intended-beneficiary standard, the clause will not bind the nonparty, and the dispute may proceed in the nonparty’s chosen forum or be governed by the forum rules that would otherwise apply.

Conflict-of-law and constitutional considerations

Where the selected forum is foreign or distant, courts may also evaluate whether enforcing the clause would violate due process if the clause is being invoked against a party with insufficient contacts with the chosen forum. These constitutional and jurisdictional limits live separately from the substantive third-party-beneficiary analysis but can be decisive in shaping whether a nonparty can be bound to a forum-selection or choice-of-law clause.

# Putting doctrine into practice: procedural postures and evidentiary considerations

Understanding how courts apply the rules in different procedural contexts is essential.

- Motion to dismiss: Courts decide whether the complaint plausibly pleads contractual intent to benefit the plaintiff. If the contract text is attached to the complaint and shows no express or clear intent, the court may dismiss the third-party-beneficiary claim. Where formation or assent is contested, the complaint may survive to allow evidentiary development.

- Motion to compel arbitration: Courts consider whether an arbitration clause is part of the binding contract and whether the nonparty is permitted to enforce it. This often requires a closer reading of the contract and may involve evidence regarding assent (screenshots, account logs). The mover bears the initial burden of showing a valid arbitration agreement; if that is met, the burden shifts to the opposing party to show invalidity or inapplicability.

- Summary judgment: At this stage, courts weigh competing evidence—contract drafts, deposition testimony, account records, industry practice—about whether the parties intended third-party enforcement. Summary judgment is appropriate when no genuine dispute of material fact exists about the contracting parties’ objective intent.

- Trial: When intent is contested and factual, the issue goes to the factfinder. The court instructs on the legal test (intended vs. incidental; clear declaration of intent) and the jury (or judge in a bench trial) resolves credibility and weigh the evidence.

Evidence types and documentary material

In online contexts, the documentary record is critical. Screenshots, archived copies of terms, timestamps, email confirmations, and transactional logs can show when and how a user was presented with terms and whether specific language identified the user or class as a beneficiary. Contract drafts and correspondence between the contracting parties can show their intent to create enforceable rights for third parties—particularly where the contracting party expressly discussed third-party enforcement in negotiations.

Strategic considerations for counsel

For contractors drafting agreements, express language can make third-party intentions clear (if that is desired), specifying the beneficiaries, the rights conferred, and the conditions for vesting. Conversely, parties that do not want nonparties to have enforceable rights should include unambiguous reservation-of-rights and anti-third-party-beneficiary clauses.

For litigants, pleadings should allege (and early discovery should seek) evidence showing deliberate intent to benefit the claimant. Plaintiffs should preserve evidence of assent and any conduct indicating reliance; defendants should seek to show that any benefit was incidental or that assent was never obtained.

Conclusion

Illinois law permits third-party enforcement of contracts only where the contracting parties clearly intended to bestow enforceable rights on the nonparty. Courts start with a strong presumption against third-party enforcement and require an express or clear declaration of intent to benefit to overcome that presumption. These principles apply in the online context but interact with additional concerns about assent and notice in clickwrap and browsewrap environments. Choice-of-law and forum-selection clauses will govern interpretation and procedure when validly adopted by the contracting parties, but their application to nonparties depends on whether the contract manifests intent to bind or benefit those nonparties and on public-policy and jurisdictional limits. Procedural posture matters: motions testing formation and intent are common in early stages of litigation over platform terms, and the factual record frequently determines whether a claimant is an intended beneficiary or merely an incidental recipient of a contractual benefit.

Introduction

Implied-in-fact contracts are legally enforceable agreements whose existence and terms are inferred from the parties’ conduct rather than from explicit written or spoken words. Under New York law, courts treat implied-in-fact contracts as true contracts: they require assent, consideration, sufficiently definite terms, and a meeting of the minds as those concepts are understood in contract doctrine. At the same time, implied-in-fact contract claims must be carefully distinguished from quasi-contractual remedies—commonly called unjust enrichment or restitution—which impose liability despite the absence of a contractual relationship. For a student preparing to read New York decisions addressing implied agreements, it is crucial to understand how New York courts infer assent from conduct, what constitutes adequate consideration, how definite the essential terms must be to create an enforceable bargain, how the objective “meeting of the minds” is determined, how unjust enrichment differs from implied-in-fact contract, and the particular pleading requirements that apply—especially in contexts such as entertainment and media where fact patterns are often ambiguous.

This chapter explains these doctrinal elements, the governing tests and burdens, how courts apply rules to facts, and how procedural posture (pleading, motion to dismiss, summary judgment, trial) affects the analysis.

# Assent inferred from conduct; consideration

Assent and consideration are the dual pillars of contractual formation. For express contracts, assent typically appears in explicit offer and acceptance. For implied-in-fact contracts, courts infer an offer and acceptance from the parties’ conduct and the surrounding circumstances. New York courts ask whether the parties acted in a manner that reasonably manifests mutual assent to be bound to the indicated terms.

Assent inferred from conduct. The central inquiry is objective: would a reasonable person, placed in the parties’ positions, conclude that an agreement had been reached? This objective test focuses on outward manifestations rather than the parties’ unexpressed mental intent. Relevant evidence includes communications, the sequence of events, conduct consistent with performance under an agreement (e.g., provision of services, payment, reciprocal acts), customs and usage of the trade, and the existence of dealings that historically produced enforceable bargains between the parties.

A key feature of implied-in-fact assertions is that the conduct must be sufficiently unequivocal to permit inference of assent. Courts generally require more than mere opportunity to perform, more than unilateral performance without any evidence of a request or expectation of compensation, and more than speculative or conclusory pleadings that “an agreement” existed. A plaintiff seeking relief on an implied-in-fact theory must identify facts showing that the defendant requested, accepted, or acquiesced in the plaintiff’s performance, or otherwise knowingly accepted benefits under circumstances that would support the inference that the defendant agreed to pay.

Consideration. Consideration for an implied-in-fact contract is the reciprocal inducement that makes a bargain enforceable. In the implied-in-fact context consideration is frequently measured by the plaintiff’s performance of services or the plaintiff’s conferral of a benefit on the defendant in exchange for the defendant’s promise, in circumstances where the defendant promised (expressly or by conduct) to pay. New York adheres to the classic rule that past consideration—that is, services rendered before any promise is made—is not consideration for a later promise unless the promise was given in recognition of services previously rendered under circumstances creating a moral obligation and the promise is necessary to avoid injustice. More commonly, consideration in implied-in-fact cases involves contemporaneous exchanges: services rendered upon request, supplies delivered after negotiation, or other performance accepted with knowledge and under circumstances that make payment reasonably expected.

When courts assess consideration in inferred agreements, they look for evidence of a bargained-for exchange: plaintiff’s performance in exchange for defendant’s promise (express or implied). If the defendant’s conduct amounts merely to acceptance of a gratuitous benefit, without any manifestation of obligation to pay, courts will often deny contract relief and may instead consider unjust enrichment. In sum, for implied-in-fact contracts New York requires objective manifestations that a party expected compensation and that the other party knowingly accepted that performance as part of a reciprocal understanding.

# Essential terms must be definite and pleaded with specificity

A contract—express or implied—must have sufficiently definite essential terms so that a court can understand what the parties agreed to and can give remedy for breach. New York courts will not enforce a purported agreement that is too indefinite or vague to permit judicial enforcement.

What are essential terms? The answer depends on the subject matter, but commonly includes identification of the parties, the subject matter or scope of the work or goods, price or a method for fixing price, quantity, and duration or time for performance when such elements are central to the bargain. The level of definiteness required is pragmatic: if a court can ascertain the parties’ obligations with reasonable certainty from conduct, trade usage, course of dealing, or objective standards, it may enforce the agreement even if some details were left to be negotiated, provided the parties manifested an intention to be bound.

Pleading specificity in New York. New York follows a fact-pleading approach at the trial-court level: complaints must contain factual allegations that, if true, establish the elements of the cause of action. For contract claims that assert an implied agreement, courts require plaintiffs to plead the essential terms with particularity sufficient to permit the court and the defendant to understand the claimed bargain and to frame responsive pleadings. Pleading that “an agreement was reached” without specifying the terms (e.g., price, duration, scope of work, or other essential obligations) will be dismissed when those terms are material to the claim and their absence makes the asserted contract too indefinite for enforcement.

Specificity functions at multiple procedural stages. At the motion-to-dismiss stage, courts typically evaluate whether the complaint alleges facts giving rise to a plausible inference of an agreement with definite terms. At summary judgment, the plaintiff must produce admissible evidence creating a triable issue of fact about the existence and content of the agreement. At trial, the plaintiff bears the burden to prove the agreement’s terms by a preponderance of the evidence.

Courts will sometimes supply missing contract terms when the parties’ actions demonstrate a clear scheme and when there are objective standards to fill gaps (e.g., reasonable value, market price, industry practice). However, courts are cautious about “judicially manufacturing” material terms or selecting among competing versions of an alleged bargain. The more essential a term is to the parties’ exchange (for example, price in a one-off sale), the less likely a court will enforce an agreement that leaves that term unspecified.

# Meeting of the minds and objective manifestations

The traditional phrase “meeting of the minds” captures the mutual assent necessary for contract formation; yet in practice New York courts operationalize this concept through an objective test of manifestations. That formulation avoids inquiry into subjective mental states and instead looks to whether the parties’ words and conduct would lead a reasonable person to conclude that they reached agreement.

Objective manifestations. Courts examine the outward expression of assent: written or oral statements, emails or other correspondence, actions that indicate performance in fulfillment of an expected bargain, payments made or accepted, and any contemporaneous negotiation history. Evidence of an offer and acceptance can be constructed from a sequence of acts: a proposal made and acted upon, an explicit request for services followed by performance, or repeated interactions in which one party performed to the other’s benefit and the other party accepted or retained the benefit with assurances or conduct indicating assent.

Course of dealing and trade usage. In assessing whether an implied-in-fact agreement exists, courts pay attention to the parties’ prior dealings and the customs of the relevant industry. Long-standing patterns of conduct between parties—such as repeated services supplied and paid for without formal contracts—may support inference of a binding agreement in a later transaction. Likewise, recognized trade usages may supply missing terms or demonstrate that certain conduct carries the ordinary implication of an acceptance or a promise.

Objective standard at different stages. The evidentiary weight of objective manifestations varies by procedural posture. On a motion to dismiss, courts accept well-pleaded factual allegations as true but will nonetheless dismiss conclusory allegations that do not identify the facts from which a reasonable inference of meeting of the minds can arise. On summary judgment, courts evaluate admissible evidence and will grant relief if there is no genuine dispute that no objective manifestations show assent. At trial, the jury (or the factfinder) determines whether a reasonable person would have inferred agreement from the manifestations, with the plaintiff bearing the burden to prove that inference.

Limits of the objective approach. The objective test does not permit the court to infer assent from every instance of benefit conferred or polite negotiation. Courts look for conduct that signals a present commitment to be bound or at least an implied present promise to pay for requested services. Mere pre-contractual conversations, exploratory proposals, or gratuitous performances without indication of expectation of payment do not, standing alone, establish a meeting of the minds.

# Distinguishing implied-in-fact from quasi-contract/unjust enrichment

A critical doctrinal distinction in New York is between implied-in-fact contracts—where the court infers a contract from conduct—and quasi-contractual remedies, commonly labeled unjust enrichment (or quantum meruit when measuring recovery for services). The distinction matters because equitable restitution is available only when contract law does not provide a remedy and because the measures of recovery differ.

Elements and focus. An implied-in-fact contract claim rests on the plaintiff’s showing that the defendant agreed (by words or conduct) to pay for certain performance. The focus is on the parties’ mutual assent and the terms of the bargain. By contrast, an unjust enrichment claim requires the plaintiff to show that (1) the defendant received a benefit, (2) the defendant has knowledge or appreciation of the benefit, and (3) the defendant retained the benefit under circumstances that make retention inequitable without payment. The focus here is not on assent but on the fairness of allowing the defendant to keep a benefit without compensating the provider.

Why the distinction matters. If the parties had an enforceable contract—even an implied one—plaintiff’s proper remedy lies in contract law: breach of contract damages measured by the agreed terms or, if terms are omitted but the agreement is enforceable, by expectation damages subject to proof. Unjust enrichment, by contrast, is an equitable remedy designed to prevent unjust retention of a benefit when no enforceable contract governs the matter. Courts resist allowing plaintiffs to plead both causes as alternative theories in ways that would permit duplicative recovery, and they scrutinize plaintiffs who assert unscrupulous attempts to convert an alleged contract claim into unjust enrichment when their pleadings and facts show a contract.

Sequential analysis. New York courts typically proceed in a two-step way when both theories are pleaded: they first determine whether an enforceable contract governs the dispute. If a valid contract (express or implied-in-fact) governed the parties’ relationship, claims of unjust enrichment generally fail because the contract provides the measure of the parties’ obligations. If no contract existed, the court will then consider whether equity requires restitution to prevent unjust enrichment. This approach prevents overlap and preserves the distinct remedial aims of contract and restitution doctrines.

Proof and measure of recovery. For implied-in-fact contracts, the plaintiff must prove the contract’s existence and the defendant’s breach; damages are ordinarily expectation damages—what the plaintiff would have received under the contract—subject to mitigation and offsets. For unjust enrichment, the recovery is typically restitutionary: the reasonable value of the benefit conferred or the defendant’s unjust enrichment, measured by the value of the benefit retained, rather than the plaintiff’s lost expectation. Quantum meruit—recovery for the reasonable value of services—is the usual measure where plaintiff seeks restitution for services performed absent an enforceable contract.

Common fact patterns that require careful distinction. Situations where a plaintiff renders services at the defendant’s request but no explicit agreement on compensation exists are classic borderline cases. If the defendant requested services and the circumstances show that the defendant implicitly agreed to pay, an implied-in-fact contract may exist. If the services were unsolicited but defendant knowingly accepted and retained their benefit, unjust enrichment may provide relief. Courts will also evaluate whether an express agreement exists or whether the purported agreement falls within the statute of frauds, which can affect whether contract law can be enforced.

# Pleading standards for implied agreements in entertainment/media contexts

Entertainment and media contexts raise particular challenges for pleading implied-in-fact contracts because industry practices often involve informal negotiations, oral promises, drafts, and performance over time. New York courts therefore require plaintiffs in these settings to plead the facts underlying the alleged agreement with particularity so that assertions of implied bargains are not speculative.

Specificity of allegations. Plaintiffs should plead concrete facts establishing the elements of an implied-in-fact contract: the circumstances giving rise to a request or offer, the content of any communications and conduct that constitute acceptance, the specific services performed and when, the defendant’s responses or confirmations, the compensation agreed or the method for calculating it, and any course of dealing or industry custom that supplies context for interpreting conduct. General allegations that the parties “agreed” or that the defendant “accepted” without factual detail are often insufficient.

Dates and sequences. Courts expect plaintiffs in the entertainment context to identify key dates and sequences: when an idea was presented, when discussions took place, when services were performed, when any payments or reimbursements were made or refused, and when the alleged breach occurred. Establishing a timeline helps the court assess whether the conduct reasonably supports an inference of agreement and whether any alleged agreement was contemporaneous with the performance.

Nature of the contribution and expectation of compensation. Plaintiff must explain the nature of the creative contribution—e.g., treatment, script, pitch, production services—and whether the work was submitted at the defendant’s request or unsolicited. Where a pitch or material was unsolicited, plaintiffs face a heavier burden to show that the defendant’s subsequent conduct manifested assent to pay; by contrast, a submission in response to a request or an explicit invitation to provide material at a stated stage of development more readily supports an inference of an implied contract.

Industry custom and trade usage. Entertainment and media plaintiffs often rely on industry customs (e.g., the practice of commissioning treatments, paying option fees, or compensating writers according to specified guild standards) to fill gaps in the alleged agreement or to show that conduct should reasonably have put the defendant on notice of an obligation to pay. Pleadings should therefore allege the applicable standards and explain how the parties’ conduct aligned with them.

Dealing with the statute of frauds. Some entertainment agreements implicate the statute of frauds—for example, agreements not to be performed within one year or agreements involving the sale of an interest in real property or certain other subject matters. Plaintiffs must plead facts demonstrating either that the contract falls outside the statute of frauds, that it was performed (thereby taking it out of the statute’s bar), or that equitable doctrines such as part performance or promissory estoppel make enforcement appropriate.

Promissory estoppel and reliance. When an implied-in-fact contract is difficult to establish because terms are vague or assent is not clear, plaintiffs sometimes advance promissory estoppel as an alternative theory. To survive dismissal on promissory estoppel, plaintiffs should plead that the defendant made a clear and definite promise, that the plaintiff reasonably relied on it to their detriment, and that injustice can be avoided only by enforcing the promise. In media cases, substantial reliance—incurring expenses, foregoing other opportunities, or expending time and creative effort at the defendant’s request—can be persuasive evidence, but courts scrutinize whether the reliance was reasonable given the circumstances and whether a contract claim properly addresses the dispute.

Avoiding pleadings that invite dismissal. Plaintiffs in entertainment disputes should avoid conclusory recitations that fail to identify the content of any alleged agreement, the identity of the individual or entity authorized to contract, the concrete acts constituting acceptance and performance, and the measure of damages sought. Because entertainment work often involves collaboration and multiple contributors, pleadings should specify which interactions relate to the plaintiff’s claim and should identify any central communications—emails, scripts, invoices, or drafts—that substantiate the asserted bargain.

# Procedural considerations: how posture affects analysis

The procedural posture of a dispute crucially influences how courts treat implied-in-fact contract claims. At the pleading stage, courts focus on whether the complaint alleges factual matter that, taken as true, plausibly demonstrates a contract with definite terms, mutual assent inferred from conduct, and consideration. Vague or conclusory allegations risk dismissal.

Motion to dismiss. When faced with a motion to dismiss, courts apply standards that test legal sufficiency. Allegations of an implied contract must meet the standard for pleadings in New York: factual specificity supporting the elements. Courts will dismiss claims that lack essential terms or that rest on speculative inferences about uncompacted promises. Where the complaint alleges enough specific facts—dates, communications, acts of performance, accepted benefits—courts generally deny dismissal and allow discovery to proceed.

Discovery and summary judgment. Discovery frequently supplies the objective manifestations necessary to prove an implied agreement—emails, invoices, draft agreements, witnesses who describe negotiations, and records of payment. On summary judgment, the moving party must show there is no genuine dispute of material fact about the existence of an enforceable agreement. If objective evidence (e.g., documents or contemporaneous admissions) shows no intent to be bound, summary judgment may be appropriate. Conversely, if reasonable minds could differ about whether conduct manifested assent or whether terms are sufficiently definite, summary judgment should be denied.

Trial and burdens of proof. At trial, the plaintiff has the burden of proving the elements of an implied-in-fact contract by a preponderance of the evidence. The factfinder applies the objective standard to the totality of the evidence and resolves credibility disputes. If an implied contract is found, damages follow contract principles; if no contract is found but unjust enrichment is proven, restitutionary damages may be awarded.

Alternative pleading and election of remedies. Plaintiffs may plead in the alternative—asserting a contract claim and an unjust enrichment claim in the alternative—because at the pleading stage it may be unclear which theory will ultimately be supported by the evidence. However, if the evidence shows a contract, unjust enrichment is usually barred. Courts may dismiss unjust enrichment claims where they are duplicative of a contract claim and where the contract governs the relationship.

Choice of law and forum. In media and entertainment disputes that have cross-jurisdictional elements, choice-of-law analysis can affect whether a claim is characterized as contract or restitution and can shape enforceability of implied agreements. New York courts apply established choice-of-law tests; students should be alert to contractual choice-of-law clauses, the locus of negotiation and performance, and whether forum selection provisions exist that might influence enforcement.

Conclusion

Understanding implied-in-fact contracts under New York law requires attention to the practicalities of how courts infer assent, how consideration is evidenced by performance, and the degree of specificity required in pleadings for essential terms. The objective manifestation test and the requirement of reasonably definite terms constrain courts from enforcing vague bargains, while trade usage and course of dealing supply tools to interpret conduct. Distinguishing implied-in-fact contracts from unjust enrichment is doctrinally important because it dictates the applicable remedy and the appropriate allocation of burdens. In entertainment and media contexts—where informal dealings are common—the combination of heightened pleading expectations and careful factual proof makes early attention to documentary records, timelines, and industry practice especially important. Procedural posture shapes the litigation trajectory: what must be alleged, what must be proved in discovery, and what must be shown at summary judgment or trial. With these principles, a student will be prepared to read and analyze New York decisions that navigate the line between implied bargains and equitable relief.

Introduction

Fraudulent inducement is a central concept in New York commercial litigation. It arises where one party obtains an agreement or economic transaction by means of a false representation that the other party relies upon to its detriment. Because claims for fraud expose defendants to tort damages (and in some instances punitive awards or restitution), courts scrutinize the elements carefully and enforce pleading standards intended to separate genuine claims from opportunistic ones. New York also adheres to a distinctive approach to measuring compensatory damages for fraud—commonly described as the out-of-pocket rule—which shapes plaintiffs’ remedial choices and interacts with contractual claims and doctrines that limit duplicative recovery. This chapter sets out the doctrinal framework for fraudulent inducement in New York: the substantive elements (material misrepresentation, scienter, intent to induce, justifiable reliance, causation), the governing damages rules (out-of-pocket versus benefit-of-the-bargain, available consequential/incidental losses, restitutionary remedies), pleading requirements under Rule 9(b), and the interaction between fraud and contract claims to avoid double recovery.

# Material misrepresentation, scienter, intent, justifiable reliance, causation

Elements overview

A claim of fraudulent inducement in New York requires proof of several basic elements. Although different formulations exist, the core elements are that (1) the defendant made a material false representation of fact; (2) the defendant knew the representation was false or made it with reckless disregard or intent to deceive (scienter); (3) the representation was made with the intent that the plaintiff rely upon it; (4) the plaintiff did justifiably rely upon the representation; and (5) such reliance was the proximate cause of the plaintiff’s injury. The plaintiff bears the burden to prove each element by a preponderance of the evidence.

Material misrepresentation

Materiality is judged by whether the false statement would have been important to a reasonable person in deciding whether to enter into the transaction. A material misrepresentation must concern a past or existing fact or, in some instances, a present fact—statements of future intent generally are not actionable as fraud unless the speaker had no present intent to carry out the promise (i.e., the statement was a false statement of present intent cloaked as a promise about the future). Opinions can be actionable when they imply facts or are statements by a party with special knowledge that reasonably induce reliance. Puffery or mere sales talk is usually not material.

A key practical point is distinguishing representations that are part of the parties’ negotiated allocation of contractual risk from those that are collateral and deceptive. Courts will often treat as non-actionable representations that merely restate or summarize contractual terms, especially when the contract contains an integration clause and the representation is the kind of expectation legitimately hedged by the contract. Conversely, misrepresentations that relate to an independent fact, such as the past performance, financial solvency, ownership of assets, or the existence of third-party approvals, are more likely to be deemed materially actionable.

Scienter and intent to induce reliance

Scienter concerns the defendant’s state of mind: knowledge that the statement was false, reckless disregard for its truth, or, in some contexts, intent to deceive. New York law typically allows proof of scienter through direct or circumstantial evidence. Reckless indifference to the truth—making statements without knowing whether they were true and without caring—is sufficient to establish scienter. Mere negligence or failure to exercise reasonable care in forming a belief is generally insufficient to establish a fraud claim.

Intent to induce is distinct but related: the plaintiff must show that the misrepresentation was made with the purpose or objective that the plaintiff rely upon it and be induced into the transaction. Intent to induce can be inferred from the nature and circumstances of the statement, including timing, context, and the speaker’s position of influence.

Justifiable reliance

Reliance is an essential link between the misrepresentation and the injury. The plaintiff must have actually relied upon the false statement, and that reliance must have been justifiable. Actual reliance is demonstrated by proof that the misrepresentation was a motivating factor in the plaintiff’s decision to enter the transaction. The “justifiable” standard is measured objectively, but it is not a strict reasonable-person test: the plaintiff need not show that a reasonably prudent person would have relied; rather, reliance is unjustifiable only where the plaintiff’s conduct is so unreasonable that no reasonable person would have relied under the circumstances. Factors that undermine justifiable reliance include: clear and conspicuous contract terms that contradict the alleged representation, explicit integration/by-the-book disclaimers, obvious discrepancies or “red flags” that would put an ordinary person on notice, and when the plaintiff had equal access to the true facts and consciously chose not to investigate.

Causation

Causation requires that the reliance be the proximate cause of the plaintiff’s damages. Courts typically require both “but-for” causation (the loss would not have occurred but for the misrepresentation) and proximate causation (the loss was a reasonably foreseeable consequence of the reliance). The plaintiff must show that, had the truth been known, it would not have entered into the transaction (or would have negotiated differently). The measure of causation dovetails with the chosen remedy: a rescissory or restitutionary remedy focuses on returning the parties to the pre-contract position, while compensatory remedies measure the economic impact of the induced transaction.

# Out-of-pocket loss rule versus benefit-of-the-bargain damages

New York’s out-of-pocket rule

New York courts generally measure compensatory damages for common-law fraud by the out-of-pocket rule. Under that rule, the plaintiff’s recoverable damages are the actual losses sustained—the difference between what the plaintiff gave and what the plaintiff received because of the fraud. Practically, this is calculated as the amount the plaintiff paid or the value of the benefit conferred minus the actual value of what was received in return at the time of the transaction. The out-of-pocket measure is designed to compensate for the plaintiff’s economic loss rather than to punish the defendant or to award speculative gains the plaintiff might have expected.

This rule contrasts with the benefit-of-the-bargain measure, which awards the plaintiff the difference between the value represented (the bargain as promised) and the value actually received. Benefit-of-the-bargain damages aim to put the plaintiff in the position it would have occupied had the representation been true. New York courts are generally reluctant to award benefit-of-the-bargain damages for fraud claims. They view the out-of-pocket rule as better aligned with compensatory tort principles and as less susceptible to speculative calculation.

When benefit-of-the-bargain damages may be considered

Although the out-of-pocket rule is dominant, benefit-of-the-bargain damages may be invoked in some contexts outside the common-law framework. Certain statutory regimes, securities-law contexts, or specific contract-related statutory remedies may permit a different measure. Additionally, courts sometimes permit plaintiffs to present alternative measures of damages for the jury’s consideration where there is reason to believe that the out-of-pocket measure does not fully capture the economic harm, or where the contract or parties’ course of dealing supports a different expectation. Nevertheless, in New York’s typical common-law fraudulent inducement case, plaintiffs should expect the out-of-pocket formula to apply.

Practical consequences

The out-of-pocket rule affects litigation strategy. Plaintiffs seeking speculative future benefits or loss of bargain gains will find their recovery limited. Defendants can emphasize market valuations or objective measures showing that the plaintiff did receive value approximating what it paid. Plaintiffs must be prepared to prove the actual negative difference in value and to quantify their expenditures and the received value at the relevant time.

# Consequential and incidental losses cognizable in fraud

Beyond the primary out-of-pocket loss, New York law allows recovery of certain consequential and incidental damages arising from fraud when they are a natural and proximate result of the fraudulent inducement and were reasonably foreseeable to the defendant at the time of the misrepresentation.

Consequential damages

Consequential damages are secondary harms that flow from the fraud—lost profits, costs of remediation, increased financing costs, or other losses that were foreseeable when the misrepresentation was made. To recover consequential damages, the plaintiff must show that the losses were proximately caused by the fraud and that they were foreseeable in the sense that the defendant knew or should have known that such damages would likely follow from the misrepresentation. Where the fraud induced the plaintiff to enter a complex commercial transaction, lost profits claims require a reliable basis for computation; courts scrutinize speculative profit claims and often require industry-specific proof or historical performance data.

Incidental damages

Incidental damages cover reasonable costs incurred in dealing with the consequences of the fraud—for example, expenses of preservation, inspection, or disposal of defective goods, or costs of attempts to mitigate loss. Such losses are typically recoverable when they are tied directly to the fraud and provably documented.

Limits on consequential and incidental recovery

Courts will deny speculative or remote claims. Claims for consequential damages require careful proof of causation and foreseeability, and damages must be proved with a reasonable degree of certainty. Mitigation is relevant; plaintiffs are expected to take commercially reasonable steps to avoid or minimize harm after discovering the fraud, and failure to mitigate can reduce recoverable consequential damages. Where the plaintiff’s losses are primarily the kind of disappointment recoverable under contract law (loss of bargain), the out-of-pocket rule may limit recovery of consequential harms unless the fraud claim is sufficiently independent of the contract.

Restitutional and rescissory remedies

In addition to compensatory damages, plaintiffs may seek restitution—i.e., return of funds or recovery of the value of benefits conferred—when equity demands disgorgement or when rescission of the transaction is appropriate. Restitution aims to prevent enrichment of the defendant by the fruits of fraud. Remedies like rescission restore the pre-contract status quo and may be accompanied by restitutionary awards. Restitutionary measures can sometimes lead to recovery beyond the narrow out-of-pocket computation, particularly where the defendant has been unjustly enriched independent of measurable out-of-pocket loss.

Punitive damages and interest

Where the defendant’s conduct is particularly malicious, willful, or wanton, punitive damages may be awarded to punish and deter. Recovery of interest on awarded damages follows standard principles concerning prejudgment and postjudgment interest; courts will award interest where fairness requires compensating the plaintiff for the time value of its losses.

# Interaction with Rule 9(b) particularity pleading

Rule 9(b) standard

Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 9(b) requires that “[i]n alleging fraud or mistake, a party must state with particularity the circumstances constituting fraud.” New York’s state pleading standards are similarly exacting. The purpose of the particularity requirement is to provide defendants with fair notice of the precise misconduct alleged, to discourage strike suits, and to prevent fishing expeditions in discovery.

What particularity requires in fraud cases

To satisfy Rule 9(b), a complaint must generally identify the who, what, when, where, and how of the alleged misrepresentations: who made the representation, the time and place it was made, the specific content of the false statement, and why the statement was false. The plaintiff should identify the speaker, the recipient, the manner in which the statement was communicated, and the circumstances indicating scienter. Where multiple defendants are named, the complaint must specify which defendant made which statement.

Pleading scienter

Scienter must be pleaded with particularity. Conclusory assertions that a defendant “knew” or “intended” are insufficient; the complaint must plead facts that create a strong inference of fraudulent intent. This may be done by alleging facts that show motive and opportunity, a pattern of similar misrepresentations, internal documents or contemporaneous communications contradicting public statements, or circumstances making it highly likely that the defendant knew the representation was false.

Where particularity is relaxed

Rule 9(b) allows that “knowledge, intent, and other conditions of a person’s mind may be alleged generally.” Courts interpret this to mean that while the mere allegation of intent is permitted in form, the complaint must still include facts from which a reasonable inference of the requisite state of mind can be drawn. In cases where much of the relevant information is within the defendant’s exclusive control, courts may tolerate less factual specificity at the pleading stage but will allow discovery only upon a plausible, particularized showing.

Consequences of failing to plead with particularity

Failure to plead fraud with the required specificity can lead to dismissal of the fraud claim. Courts may grant leave to replead to correct deficiencies. Importantly, if the fraud claim is dismissed on pleading grounds, plaintiffs often try to preserve contract claims that do not require Rule 9(b)-level particularity.

# Economic loss and duplicative-claim concerns

Tension between tort and contract

A recurring issue is when a plaintiff’s losses arise from a failed commercial bargain—i.e., purely economic losses that could be characterized as either contract damages or tort damages for fraud. Courts are wary of allowing tort recovery for purely economic loss that stems from commercial expectations governed by contract law, because tort law traditionally remedies personal injury and property damage, whereas contract law governs allocation of commercial risk. The economic-loss doctrine expresses this policy by limiting recovery in tort when the loss is merely the disappointed expectation created by a contract.

When fraud survives alongside contract claims

Fraud claims that are collateral to the contract or that involve misrepresentations extraneous to the contractual obligations are more likely to coexist with contract claims without the risk of duplicative recovery. Examples include fraudulent misrepresentations that induced entry into the contract but were independent promises or false statements about pre-existing facts, representations about matters outside the scope of the contract, or affirmative concealment of defects. If the fraud claim is merely a restatement of the contract claim (i.e., the alleged misrepresentation is exactly the contractual promise or warranty), courts will often treat the fraud claim as duplicative and may limit or bar tort recovery.

Avoiding double recovery

New York law seeks to avoid double recovery. If a plaintiff simultaneously pursues contract and tort theories based on the same underlying injury, the court will craft remedies to prevent duplicative awards. This can happen in several ways: by requiring an election of remedies, by offsetting contract recovery against tort recovery, or by barring tort recovery when the contract provides an adequate remedy and the tort claim is not sufficiently independent. The plaintiff cannot obtain both full contract damages and full tort damages for the same losses.

Independent duty analysis

One way courts assess whether the tort claim may proceed is through the independent duty analysis: does the defendant owe a duty in tort that is separate from contractual obligations? If so, tort recovery may be permissible. This often turns on whether the defendant’s conduct violated a societal standard of conduct or involved a misrepresentation beyond the contract. Courts examine the nature of the duty breached, the parties’ relationship, and whether allowing recovery in tort would upset the contractual allocation of risk.

Election of remedies and remedies allocation

When both tort and contract claims are viable, the plaintiff may be required to elect a remedy in the sense of choosing the theory by which to recover, particularly where the remedies are inconsistent (e.g., rescission versus affirmance and damages). Where inconsistent remedies are not strictly mutually exclusive, courts will still guard against duplicative recovery by calculating the total damages award to reflect the single injury suffered. For example, if a plaintiff obtains rescission and also seeks damages, the court will credit the plaintiff for amounts already returned or recovered.

Practical litigation consequences

In practice, plaintiffs should plead fraud distinctly and show that the misrepresentation is collateral or extraneous to the contract. They should be prepared to quantify damages separately and explain why contract law is inadequate to compensate the harm. Defendants will frequently move to dismiss fraud counts as duplicative of contract claims or to strike damages claims that duplicate contract remedies. Hence, careful pleading and early identification of evidentiary distinctions between contractual breach and fraudulent inducement are essential.

# Conclusion

Understanding fraudulent inducement in New York requires mastering a set of interrelated doctrinal rules: the substantive elements of fraud (material misrepresentation, scienter, intent to induce, justifiable reliance, and causation); the state’s default out-of-pocket damages measure and the limited circumstances in which other measures may be available; the scope of consequential and incidental damages that may be recovered; the heightened pleading particularity required by Rule 9(b); and the ways courts prevent duplicative recovery when fraud and contract claims overlap. For a law student preparing to read judicial opinions on fraudulent inducement, it is critical to track how courts evaluate whether a representation is collateral to the contract, how they apply the justifiable-reliance standard in commercial settings, how they treat scienter in the pleadings, and how they compute damages in light of the out-of-pocket principle and potential restitutionary remedies. These themes recur in New York jurisprudence and drive both motion practice and trial strategy in fraud cases.

Introduction

When a federal court entertains a federal question — for example, a claim arising under the Copyright Act — plaintiffs frequently assert related state-law causes of action, such as breach of contract, misappropriation, or torts grounded in state law. Federal courts regularly decide those state-law claims alongside the federal claim. The choice of which state’s substantive law will govern those supplemental claims is not a matter of free-floating federal discretion: it is governed by a synthesis of principles drawn from supplemental-jurisdiction doctrine, Erie doctrine principles, federal procedural law, and the forum state’s conflict-of-laws rules. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework federal courts use before they read a judicial opinion that resolves these issues. The goal is to give a student the tools to determine (1) whether the federal court may hear the state claims at all, (2) which state’s substantive law a federal court will apply to those claims, (3) how enforceable contractual choice-of-law clauses perform in federal court, (4) how contract and tort claims are treated differently in the conflicts context, and (5) how the selection of governing law affects elements, defenses, and remedies.

# Supplemental jurisdiction over state claims in federal-question cases

Supplemental jurisdiction permits a federal court that has original jurisdiction over a federal claim to hear additional claims that do not independently qualify for federal jurisdiction but are part of the same case or controversy. The statutory foundation is 28 U.S.C. § 1367. The statute codifies preexisting doctrines (formerly known as pendent and ancillary jurisdiction) and sets both scope and boundaries.

Scope: Section 1367(a) grants jurisdiction over state-law claims that form part of the same case or controversy as the claim providing original jurisdiction. The usual articulation is that the state claim must arise out of the "same common nucleus of operative fact" such that a plaintiff would ordinarily expect to try them in one judicial proceeding. Courts decide this by comparing the factual allegations and determining whether resolution of the federal question would logically involve the same factual inquiry necessary to resolve the state claims.

Discretion and mandatory limits: Section 1367(b) and (c) impose limits. Section 1367(b) circumscribes supplemental jurisdiction in diversity cases, precluding federal jurisdiction over certain additional plaintiffs' claims that would destroy complete diversity. Section 1367(c) gives the district court discretion to decline supplemental jurisdiction in enumerated circumstances: when state claims raise novel or complex issues of state law, when state claims substantially predominate over federal claims, when the district court has dismissed the federal claims, or other compelling reasons. In practice, courts weigh judicial economy, convenience, fairness, and comity to decide whether to keep or dismiss supplemental state claims after the federal claims narrow or disappear. The posture matters: dismissal of the federal claim near the end of litigation often leads courts to dismiss the related state claims without prejudice so that they may be litigated in state court.

Practical implications for copyright suits: When a copyright claim gives rise to federal jurisdiction, related state contract and tort claims will normally be within § 1367(a) if they arise from the same transactions (e.g., negotiations, license terms, use of creative material). Yet courts may decline to exercise supplemental jurisdiction where the state-law claims involve novel state-law issues, when they would substantially predominate, or when the federal claims are dismissed before trial. Plaintiffs should be prepared that the decision to retain supplemental claims is discretionary and that litigation strategy (timing of dispositive motions, scope of discovery, settlement posture) can influence a court’s exercise of discretion.

# Erie doctrine and forum state choice-of-law rules

Erie and its progeny set the baseline constraint: a federal court sitting in diversity must apply state substantive law and federal procedural law. From this starting point, courts have developed tests to determine whether a particular rule is substantive (state law must be applied) or procedural (a federal rule may govern). Two central strands are important here: first, whether the federal court applies the forum state’s choice-of-law rules to determine which state’s substantive law controls; second, how to reconcile federal procedural rules with state substantive law under the Erie line.

Forum state choice-of-law rules and federal courts: The long-standing principle is that a federal court sitting in a forum state must apply that state’s choice-of-law rules when resolving conflict-of-law questions. The rationale is that allowing a federal court to apply different conflicts rules than a sister state court would create outcome-determinative differences that Erie seeks to avoid. Thus, when a federal court is presented with state-law claims, it must identify the controlling choice-of-law rules of the forum state and apply them to select the substantive law that governs each claim.

Erie’s substantive–procedural distinction: To decide whether to apply state or federal law to a particular issue, courts inquire whether a federal rule on point exists and whether adherence to federal law would lead to a substantially different outcome. The modern approach has two tracks.

If a federal rule (for example, a Federal Rule of Civil Procedure or a valid federal statute) is "on point" and conflicts with state law, then the court assesses whether the federal rule is valid under the Rules Enabling Act and constitutional constraints. If valid, the federal rule governs. This is the path courts follow for procedural matters governed by the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure.

If no federal rule is directly on point, courts apply an "outcome-determinative" inquiry, often coupled with Erie’s twin aims: discouraging forum shopping by asserting that the state rule is substantive if ignoring it would significantly affect litigation outcomes, and avoiding inequitable administration of the laws. A balancing approach (sometimes called the Byrd balancing) may also be used to weigh federal interests (for example, efficient, uniform administration of federal courts) against state interests. The result is that matters that are truly substantive (statutes of limitations, elements of state torts, property law definitions) are treated as state law, while pure procedural mechanics remain governed by federal rules.

Applying forum conflicts rules in federal-question cases: When the claim that gives rise to federal jurisdiction is federal (a federal-question case), the Erie framework still applies to state-law claims joined by supplemental jurisdiction: the federal court should apply the forum state’s conflicts rules to choose the substantive law that governs the state claims. The policy reasons are analogous to diversity litigation: to avoid creating different outcomes solely because a case is in federal court; to respect state substantive policies; and to ensure uniformity between state and federal adjudication in the forum. Therefore, the first step after establishing supplemental jurisdiction is to identify the forum state's choice-of-law methodology and follow it.

# Enforcement of contractual choice-of-law clauses

Parties frequently include choice-of-law clauses in contracts to allocate legal risks across jurisdictions. Federal courts hearing supplemental state-law contract claims must respect and enforce those clauses, subject to the same constraints that would apply in the forum state.

Validity and enforceability under forum law: Enforcement is determined by the substantive contract law of the forum as informed by its conflicts rules. A prevalent rule (derived from the Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws §187) is that a contractual choice-of-law provision will generally be honored if the chosen state has a substantial relation to the transaction or parties, or if the choice is reasonable. Even where the chosen state lacks any reasonable relation, the clause may be binding unless applying the chosen law would be contrary to a fundamental policy of a state with a materially greater interest in the issue. Some jurisdictions also use a "closely related" or "reasonable relation" test; others apply a governmental-interest analysis or the Restatement's "most significant relationship" approach. A federal court must apply the forum state's specific test.

Fraud, overreaching, and unconscionability: Choice clauses may be invalidated for the same defect that would invalidate any contractual term: fraud, duress, mistake, or unconscionability. Courts scrutinize bargaining power, notice, and whether the clause was a product of adhesion, particularly where its enforcement would deprive a party of access to remedies or defensible procedural protections under its home state.

Public policy and fundamental interests: A major exception is when the chosen law undermines a fundamental public policy of the forum or of a state with a materially greater interest. For example, if the chosen law would eliminate mandatory liability rules or remedies that protect consumers or employees in the forum state, the forum may decline to enforce the clause. The forum’s conflicts approach determines whether such a public-policy exception applies.

Mandatory federal law limits: A federal interest can also constrain enforcement. If the choice-of-law provision would produce application of a law inconsistent with a valid, overriding federal statute or policy, a federal court will not enforce the clause to the extent of conflict. This is particularly relevant in areas where Congress has created uniform national standards or preempted state regulation. The enforcement inquiry is therefore twofold: (1) Is the clause enforceable under the forum state’s contract/conflicts law? (2) Does federal law preempt or otherwise override the application of the chosen state’s law?

Practical consequences in federal copyright suits: Contractual license agreements often contain choice-of-law provisions specifying a jurisdiction to govern interpretation and remedies. A federal court hearing a state contract claim must honor those provisions if they are enforceable under the forum state’s rules and are not displaced by federal law. Thus, counsel should expect a court to scrutinize whether the chosen law has a reasonable connection to the parties or transaction and whether enforcement would contravene any fundamental policy or federal interest.

# Determining governing law for tort versus contract claims

Contracts and torts present different analytical starting points in conflicts-of-law doctrine, and many forums treat them differently. A federal court applying the forum state’s choice-of-law rules will therefore reach different governing-law conclusions depending on whether the supplemental claim sounds in contract or tort.

Contract claims: Many jurisdictions follow the Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws §187 for contractual disputes. Under §187, the parties’ choice of law is given deference: if the contract contains an express choice-of-law clause, that choice will be honored unless (a) the chosen law has no substantial relation to the parties or the transaction, or (b) application of the chosen law would be contrary to a fundamental policy of the forum state and the forum has a materially greater interest than the chosen state in the determination of the particular issue. Where there is no express clause, courts commonly apply the law of the state with the most significant relationship to the contract, determined by factors such as place of contracting, negotiation, performance, location of subject matter, and domicile or place of business of the parties.

Tort claims: Tort choice-of-law analyses often focus on the place of the injury (lex loci delicti), but many state courts have moved to more flexible approaches, such as the "most significant relationship" test (Restatement (Second) §145) or governmental-interest analysis. The test asks which jurisdiction has the most significant relationship to the occurrence and persons, taking into account connecting factors such as where the injury occurred, where the conduct causing the injury occurred, the domicile, residence or place of business of the parties, and where the relationship between the parties is centered. Some jurisdictions may treat particular tort elements (duty, breach, causation) differently in the conflict analysis.

Divergent results across claim types: Because contract-choice rules typically give determinative weight to the parties’ express agreement and to manifestation of parties’ intent, parties can exercise substantial autonomy over the governing law in contract disputes. By contrast, tort rules focus on the locus of the tort and policy considerations of safety, risk allocation, and protection of local citizens. This divergence means that in a multi-claim litigation arising from a single nucleus of operative fact — such as contractual negotiations that allegedly produced both an actionable breach and a tortious misrepresentation — a federal court may apply one state’s law to the contract claim and a different state’s law to the tort claim, depending on the forum’s conflicts methodology and the connecting facts.

Interaction between contract and tort claims (tort claims arising from contracts): Sometimes tort claims are pendent to contract disputes because the tort allegedly arises out of the contractual relationship (e.g., fraud in the inducement). Some jurisdictions apply the contract’s chosen law to tort claims that are so closely intertwined with the contract that applying different laws would undermine the parties’ expectations. Others treat tort claims separately: the tort law of the state with the most significant relationship to the tort governs. The forum’s doctrine controls this choice. Federal courts must follow the forum state in deciding whether the contract law governs related torts.

Forum-shopping and duplicative litigation risk: Different governing laws for different claims can create tactical incentives. Parties may seek federal adjudication to obtain application of particular forum conflicts rules or to leverage differences in available remedies. Erie-related doctrines and the forum state's approach to conflicts take these incentives into account; federal courts apply the forum state's conflicts rules to mitigate differences between state and federal forums that might encourage forum shopping.

# Effect on elements, defenses, and remedies

Once the governing substantive law is identified, it controls the elements of the claim, available defenses, and remedies — subject to constraints imposed by federal procedural rules and by preemption.

Elements and burdens of proof: The substantive law that the conflicts rules select determines the elements that the plaintiff must plead and prove to succeed. This includes the required factual predicates (e.g., duties, breach, causation in tort; offer, acceptance, consideration and breach in contract) and the burden of proof where that is deemed substantive by the forum. Federal courts must therefore be prepared to apply a foreign state's tests for causation, foreseeability, proximate cause, and other doctrinal contours if selected by the conflicts analysis.

Affirmative defenses: Defenses such as statute of limitations, waiver, estoppel, laches, and release are typically substantive and controlled by the law chosen under the forum’s conflicts rules. Yet the treatability of certain defenses as procedural (for example, the timing of motions) may allow federal procedure to shape their operation. Statutes of limitations are particularly sensitive: although they are often characterized as substantive, courts sometimes treat limitation-related rules such as tolling, discovery, or accrual as procedural. A federal court will look to the forum’s rule on whether limitation and tolling rules are deemed substantive and therefore selected by the conflicts rule. Where a federal rule directly governs (for instance, limitations embedded in federal statutes), the federal rule will take precedence.

Remedies and damages: Remedies — including damages, injunctive relief, punitive damages, and contract-specific remedies (specific performance, consequential damages) — are predominantly substantive. A federal court will use the substantive remedial framework of the selected state law in the absence of a controlling federal law or an applicable federal rule. However, federal courts retain equitable powers and may fashion equitable relief consistent with federal procedural standards. Additionally, federal courts must follow federal constitutional limitations (e.g., due process) and federal statutory caps or preemptive rules where relevant.

Interaction with federal procedural rules and evidence: Not all aspects of a state claim are substantive in the Erie sense. Federal procedural rules govern pleading, joinder, service, discovery, trial procedure, and evidentiary procedure, provided the federal rules are validly enacted and on point. Thus, a state-law claim tried in federal court will be pleaded, discovered, and tried under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and the Federal Rules of Evidence. For example, burdens over admissibility of evidence, hearsay exceptions, and motion practice will follow federal rules, although their operation might affect the ultimate factual development and thus the outcome under the chosen substantive law.

Preemption and federal policy constraints: Substantive state-law rules that conflict with federal policy or with a federal statute may be preempted. Where state-law defenses or remedies are inconsistent with federal law (for example if Congress has created exclusive remedies or has established uniform substantive standards), federal courts must apply federal law. In the copyright context, there is the additional layer that state-law doctrines may be preempted by federal copyright law if they attempt to provide equivalent rights within the subject matter of copyright; accordingly, whether a state-imposed remedy survives requires a preemption analysis under the controlling federal framework. Federal preemption is a substantive constraint that may invalidate a state rule that would otherwise have been selected by conflicts analysis.

Procedural posture and timing considerations: The stage of the litigation can materially shape the practical effect of choice-of-law. Early in litigation, choice-of-law determinations can be raised on motions to dismiss or for judgment on the pleadings to test whether the chosen law permits the plaintiff’s claim to proceed. At summary judgment, conflicting choice-of-law conclusions may collapse if material facts relevant to conflicts rules (e.g., where negotiations occurred, domicile of parties) are disputed. A court's later choice-of-law ruling near trial can reshape remedies sought and evidentiary strategies. Additionally, appellate review of conflict-of-law rulings is often treated as a mixed question of law and fact, so the appellate posture affects standards of review.

Conclusion

A federal court entertaining state-law contract or tort claims alongside a federal copyright claim must proceed in a structured sequence: first, determine whether supplemental jurisdiction properly reaches the state claims under § 1367; second, apply the forum state's choice-of-law rules (consistent with Erie and its progeny) to select the governing substantive law for each claim; third, enforce contractual choice-of-law provisions according to the forum's standards while policing for federal limits; fourth, recognize that tort and contract claims may point to different governing laws under the forum's conflict approach; and finally, apply the chosen substantive law to elements, defenses, and remedies while respecting controlling federal procedural rules and any preemptive federal law. Understanding each of these steps — and how variations in procedural posture and factual detail can tilt the analysis — is essential preparation before reading or critiquing judicial opinions that resolve state-law issues in federal copyright litigation.

Introduction

Fair use is the most important and elastic affirmative defense to copyright infringement. Under 17 U.S.C. § 107, courts weigh four statutory factors in light of the purposes of copyright to promote creative progress while allowing certain socially valuable reuses. Over decades of precedent, U.S. courts—especially the Supreme Court and the Second Circuit—have developed guiding principles about transformation, commerciality, market substitution, and procedural dispositions appropriate for resolving fair use disputes. This chapter surveys key precedents that shape modern doctrine: the Supreme Court’s treatment of parody and transformative purpose; the Second Circuit’s formulations for search and digital uses; how courts assess appropriation art by looking at the works themselves; the circumstances under which fair use can be decided on pleading or summary judgment; and the contemporary focus on market usurpation versus mere market harm. Understanding these doctrines and how judges apply them to fact patterns and procedural postures will prepare you to read and analyze judicial opinions that turn on fair use.

# The Four-Factor Framework and the Role of Transformation

Before discussing the specific precedents, recall the statutory four-factor framework that governs fair use analysis under 17 U.S.C. § 107. Courts must weigh:

1. The purpose and character of the use, including whether the use is of a commercial nature or is for nonprofit educational purposes.
2. The nature of the copyrighted work.
3. The amount and substantiality of the portion used in relation to the copyrighted work as a whole.
4. The effect of the use upon the potential market for or value of the copyrighted work.

These factors are not independent checklist items; courts perform a flexible, context-sensitive inquiry. In practice, factor one—especially the concept of transformativeness—often dominates the analysis, but that dominance does not render factors two through four irrelevant. Consideration of each factor depends on the particular facts, and the weight attached to any factor may vary.

Transformation as a doctrinal hinge. Since Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc., courts have read factor one to ask whether the new work adds something "new, with a further purpose or different character" that "changes the first with new expression, meaning, or message." Transformative uses—those that repurpose original material for a new communicative or aesthetic function—tend to favor fair use because they promote the constitutional purpose of copyright: encouraging creation and dissemination of knowledge and expression. Transformation is not binary; it is a spectrum. A use that merely republishes or otherwise supersedes the original's purpose (a true substitute) is less likely to be fair, while a use that alters the original to serve a different public interest (criticism, commentary, scholarship, search, parody, new aesthetic expression) weighs toward fair use.

Commerciality remains relevant but is not dispositive. Commercial character is one consideration within factor one; a use that is commercial does not automatically disqualify fair use. Courts ask whether the commerciality is merely incidental (and outweighed by transformation) or whether it corroborates a finding that the secondary use is a market substitute that usurps the original.

# Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc.: Parody, Commercial Use, and Factor-Four Analysis

Campbell is the Supreme Court’s touchstone on parody and the interpretive glue for the transformativeness inquiry. Two aspects of Campbell bear particular emphasis: how parody is treated, and how courts should approach market harm under factor four.

Parody as a paradigmatic transformative use. The Court recognized parody—use of a prior work to ridicule or comment upon it—as a classic example of transformation. Parody necessarily evokes the original to make its point; therefore, some degree of copying is often required to "conjure up" the original for a parody to be effective. The Court cautioned that assessing amount and substantiality (factor three) must account for the needs of parody: copying more than would otherwise be reasonable may be justified if it is necessary to the parodic purpose.

Commerciality not dispositive. Campbell rejected a rigid rule that commercial use presumptively defeats fair use. Instead, commerciality is a contextual element of factor one that is weighed along with transformativeness. A commercially exploited parody can nonetheless be transformative enough to be fair, depending on the other factors.

Market harm focused on substitution. Campbell clarified the analytical emphasis of factor four: the central concern is whether the secondary use supplants the market for the original or its derivatives—i.e., whether the secondary work functions as a market substitute. Criticism, commentary, or other transformative uses may depress demand for the original in some abstract sense, but such effects do not automatically translate into copyright market harm for purposes of the fair use inquiry unless the effect is substitutional. When evaluating market harm, courts look to both actual evidence and, where appropriate, reasonable inferences about likely effects on the original's markets (including potential licensing markets). The inquiry distinguishes harm arising because the secondary work fills the same market role from harm that arises incidentally from commentary or criticism.

Qualitative taking and necessity. Campbell stressed that factor three's focus is qualitative as well as quantitative. Even if a secondary user takes a small portion of the original, that portion might be the "heart" of the work and weigh against fair use unless justified by the secondary purpose (as with parody). Conversely, some extensive copying may be justified if it is necessary to accomplish a transformative purpose.

Burden allocation. While the statutory text does not fix burdens, Campbell and subsequent cases indicate that the putative infringer bears the burden of establishing fair use as an affirmative defense. Evidence demonstrating the secondary use’s transformative character and lack of substitutional market harm is typically central to that showing.

# Authors Guild v. Google, Inc.: Transformative Search and Non-Substitutive Uses

Authors Guild v. Google represents a major Second Circuit treatment of digitization and search functions, illustrating how courts apply the four-factor test to large-scale, nonconsumptive digital uses.

Transformation via informational purpose. The Second Circuit held that making searchable digital copies of books served a fundamentally different purpose than the expressive purpose of the original works: enabling text search and discovery is a utilitarian, informational purpose. Even though the defendant made verbatim copies of entire books, the court found the use transformative because the copies were not used to present the expressive content; instead, they were used to create a searchable index and to display only short "snippets" of text to users. The new function—facilitating access to information about the content of books—changed the use and purpose of the works.

Non-substitutive nature and limited display. Closely tied to transformation in the court’s analysis was the finding that the service did not serve as a meaningful substitute for the original books. The snippet displays were limited and did not provide the public a way to read the works in lieu of obtaining them; the system increased discoverability and thereby arguably stimulated demand for originals. Under factor four, courts examine whether the secondary use harms traditional or plausible derivative markets. A use that does not provide an adequate reading experience or otherwise fill the same market niche weighs against a finding of market substitution.

Commercial aspects. The Second Circuit considered commercial benefits but treated them in context: the defendant’s broader commercial strategy did not undercut the transformative, non-substitutive nature of the use when the direct public-facing aspect (search and snippets) did not replace the originals.

Importance of mitigations. The decision also highlights practical mitigations that courts consider—restricting full-text presentation, limiting access, or preserving library rights—when evaluating the scope of permissibility for large-scale digitization projects.

Implications for digital and informational uses. Authors Guild signals that courts will give significant weight to whether a digital use converts expressive works into information-serving functions, especially where the public benefit (research, discovery) and non-substitutive nature are evident.

# Cariou v. Prince: Appropriation Art and Examining the Works

Cariou v. Prince is a key Second Circuit case addressing appropriation art and instructs courts on assessing transformativeness by close, side-by-side comparison of the works.

Assessing transformation through visual and contextual change. In appropriation art disputes, courts must examine whether the secondary artworks altered the original photographs enough to present a new aesthetics, meaning, or message. Cariou emphasized that determining transformativeness often requires consideration of the works themselves—not just descriptions in pleadings or affidavits. Judges should evaluate the changes in medium, scale, colorization, cropping, collage, or additional imagery, and whether those changes yield a new expression or message distinct from the original.

No requirement that transformation be critical of the original. The Second Circuit rejected the notion that a secondary work must explicitly comment on the original or its creator to be transformative. Transformation can occur through aesthetic recontextualization, so long as the secondary work communicates a new meaning or message.

Piece-by-piece evaluation. Cariou teaches that transformation is not an all-or-nothing determination for a series of works. A district court should assess each secondary work individually; some may be fair uses while others may not. The degree and nature of alteration vary across works and so will the fair use outcome.

Interaction with factors three and four. When a secondary work significantly transforms the original appearance and purpose, factor three (amount and substantiality) is less problematic—even where the original is incorporated in whole—because the copying serves the new expressive purpose. For market effect (factor four), courts consider whether the secondary artworks function as market substitutes for the originals (e.g., whether collectors, galleries, or purchasers would use the secondary works in place of the original photographs). Artistic reuses often appeal to different markets; where that is so, factor four will weigh toward fair use.

Use of the works for adjudication. Cariou authorizes courts to resolve fair use in appropriate cases at summary judgment by directly examining the works. That approach limits wearisome discovery when the works themselves and public information suffice to determine transformativeness and substitution risk.

# TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum: Resolving Fair Use on the Pleadings

TCA Television Corp. v. McCollum addresses a procedural question: when is fair use amenable to resolution at the Rule 12(b)(6) (failure to state a claim) stage?

Fair use as a potentially dispositive legal question on the pleadings. The Second Circuit in TCA recognized that, in some cases, the complaint and incorporated materials can establish fair use as a matter of law. That outcome is possible when the allegations, if accepted, and any documents properly considered by the court (scripts, recordings, published works) show no plausible claim of infringement because the defendant’s use is indisputably fair.

Standards for deciding on pleadings. To grant a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal on fair use grounds, a court must conclude that, accepting all factual allegations in the complaint as true and drawing all reasonable inferences in the plaintiff’s favor, the pleaded facts and incorporated materials still establish the defense. This is a demanding standard for defendants because courts must avoid resolving genuine factual disputes at the pleading stage. However, where the alleged copying and the nature of the secondary use are clear from the face of the complaint and attached materials, dismissal can be proper.

Practical considerations. Early resolution on the pleadings saves time and expense, but courts remain cautious because factual development (intent, evidence of market harm, licensing practices) often bears heavily on factors one and four. Where such information is unnecessary because the nature of the use and public record are decisive, courts will consider dismissal; otherwise, summary judgment or trial is the appropriate forum.

Tension with factual determinations. TCA illustrates that fair use analyses sometimes involve mixed questions of law and fact. Courts will decide legal questions of statutory interpretation and apply those to undisputed factual matrices at the Rule 12(b)(6) or summary judgment stage; but if material facts are contested or require discovery, early disposition is improper.

# Hachette Book Group, Inc. v. Internet Archive: Market Usurpation Versus Harm from Criticism and the Flexible Inquiry

Hachette represents a contemporary, influential Second Circuit articulation emphasizing factor four’s focus on market usurpation and underscoring the flexible, context-specific nature of fair use.

Focus on usurpation of traditional or plausible markets. The court reaffirmed that factor four is primarily concerned with whether the secondary use usurps markets that copyright holders reasonably exploit or would exploit through licensing. Mere evidence of reputational harm or some diminution of demand caused by criticism or competing attention is insufficient; the inquiry asks whether the secondary work functions as a substitute for the original or an authorized derivative market.

Full copying with restricted access. Hachette confronted full-text copying of books and the defendant’s attempt to avoid substitution by imposing a controlled lending model. The court was skeptical of structural limitations that, while intended to mimic library lending, still created a free, widely accessible digital market that paralleled existing authorized markets for licensed digital lending or sales. Where the secondary service provides the public with the ability to access the full expressive content in a way that would otherwise be monetized, factor four will weigh heavily against fair use.

Contextual balancing and market evidence. Hachette illustrates that courts will analyze market harm with a realistic view of modern licensing markets. Courts consider whether publishers have developed business models (e.g., e-lending, controlled digital lending, subscription access) and whether the defendant’s activity would displace those markets. Empirical evidence, expert testimony, and reasonable inferences about market behavior can be critical. The Second Circuit in this line of cases emphasizes inquiry into plausible markets that rights holders either actually exploit or are likely to develop.

The flexible nature of fair use. Hachette reiterates that fair use remains a flexible doctrine; no single factor is determinative. Courts perform a holistic assessment. A use that might be transformative in some contexts can be problematic when it directly substitutes for the original in relevant market channels. Conversely, a use that would have negligible market impact may be fair even if it involves substantial copying. The flexibility allows the doctrine to adapt to technological change and evolving markets, but it also imposes fact-intensive analysis.

# How Courts Apply Rules to Facts: Tests, Elements, and Burdens in Practice

We now synthesize how courts operationalize the statutory framework and how procedural posture affects the analysis.

Burden and proof. Fair use is an affirmative defense; the defendant bears the burden of proving it. That burden includes showing, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the statutory factors, taken together, support a finding of fair use. Courts may consider extrinsic evidence (affidavits about intent, market data, licensing offers) and intrinsic evidence (the works themselves).

Typical itemized considerations by factor

- Factor one (purpose and character): Courts ask whether the secondary use is transformative—does it add new expression, meaning, or message? They consider whether the use is commercial (profit motive) and whether the use is nonprofit or educational. They also weigh whether the use is for criticism, commentary, news reporting, scholarship, research, or search/indexing—purposes historically favored as fair.

- Factor two (nature of the work): Works that are more factual or informational are more amenable to fair use than highly creative, fictional, or unpublished works. If the original is unpublished, courts have historically given more weight to the copyright owner’s right of first publication, although transformative uses may overcome that consideration.

- Factor three (amount and substantiality): Both quantitative and qualitative measures matter. The court asks how much of the original was used relative to the whole and whether the portion used constitutes the "heart" of the work. The analysis considers whether the amount taken was reasonable in light of the secondary purpose (e.g., necessary for parody, excerpted for criticism, copied for indexing).

- Factor four (market effect): Courts evaluate actual and potential market harm, including whether the secondary use supplants existing or likely licensing markets. Evidence of licensing offers, established markets for derivatives, or probable market development is persuasive. Mere abstract or speculative harms are given less weight than substitutional harms.

Interrelation and weighting. The four factors are interrelated; a strong showing under one factor can offset a weak showing under another. For instance, a highly transformative use (factor one) that is non-substitutive may outweigh a large quantitative taking (factor three) and even commercial exploitation. Conversely, perfect verbatim copying that creates a ready substitute for the original will likely be unfair even if the purpose is socially desirable.

Procedural posture and evidentiary implications

- Rule 12(b)(6): Courts may decide fair use on the pleadings only when no factual development could plausibly defeat the defense. When the complaint, materials incorporated into the complaint, and judicially noticeable facts make the defense obvious, dismissal is appropriate. But because fair use often implicates factual disputes—intent, market harm, licensing practices—courts typically hesitate to dispose of the case at this early stage.

- Summary judgment: More commonly, courts resolve fair use at summary judgment after discovery has produced factual records (market studies, expert reports, revenue data). Summary judgment is appropriate when the undisputed record supports a legal conclusion that the use is or is not fair.

- Trial: When genuine material facts remain in dispute—especially regarding market effects, consumer substitution, or the defendant's purpose—the case proceeds to trial. Fair use determinations at trial can involve complex testimony from market experts, comparisons of works, and consumer surveys.

Standards for reviewing factual findings on appeal. Appellate courts defer to trial courts’ factual findings unless clearly erroneous, but review mixed questions of law and fact de novo where the central issue is statutory interpretation (for example, the legal meaning of transformativeness) or where the record permits a legal determination.

# Practical Guidance for Analyzing Fair Use

Applying the precedents to fact patterns requires structured analysis:

1. Identify the precise use and the secondary purpose. Is the secondary use expressive, informational, critical, or commercial? Does it seek to repurpose the original or merely to exploit it?

2. Compare the works. For visually or auditorily based uses, examine the works side-by-side where possible. Ask whether the secondary work conveys a new message or is merely a different presentation of the same message.

3. Evaluate quantitative and qualitative taking. Determine what was taken and why. Was the amount necessary to achieve the secondary purpose?

4. Examine relevant markets. Identify actual markets the copyright owner exploits and potential licensing markets that the secondary use might supplant. Consider both existing markets and reasonable future markets.

5. Assess procedural posture. Decide whether the facts before the court permit disposition at the pleadings, at summary judgment, or require trial. Consider whether discovery will materially affect the analysis—especially on market impact.

6. Balance the factors holistically. No single factor is dispositive. Even a commercial, full-copy use may be fair if transformative and non-substitutive; conversely, a noncommercial full-copy use might be unfair if it supplants the original market.

# Concluding Observations

Landmark decisions like Campbell, Authors Guild, Cariou, and Hachette, together with procedural rulings like TCA, establish a doctrine that privileges transformation and focuses market-harm inquiry on substitution. The contours of fair use remain fluid and context-dependent, reflecting technological change and new business models. As a law student, focus on how courts articulate transformation, the interplay between commercial purpose and substitutional market harm, and the pragmatic procedural choices judges make about early resolution. When reading opinions, pay close attention to the court’s factual findings about the works and markets, its treatment of transformation as a legal standard, and how it balances the statutory factors to reach a context-sensitive result.

Introduction

In New York tort law, damages for fraudulent misrepresentation are governed by a distinctive remedial principle: the plaintiff ordinarily may recover only the actual pecuniary loss suffered as a result of the fraud—commonly called the out-of-pocket measure. This rule contrasts with the “benefit-of-the-bargain” or expectation measure that seeks to put the plaintiff in the position she would have occupied had the misrepresentation been true. For the practitioner and student, understanding the out-of-pocket rule is essential: it shapes pleading and evidentiary strategy, defines proof burdens, interacts with contract remedies, and governs remedial elections and setoffs. This chapter synthesizes the doctrinal landscape a New York court applies when fraud damages are asserted and offers practical guidance on how to allege and prove recoverable losses.

# Lama Holding Co. v. Smith Barney Inc.: out-of-pocket loss as the exclusive measure

Under longstanding New York authority, the proper measure of damages for intentional misrepresentation (fraudulent inducement) is the plaintiff’s out-of-pocket loss. The out-of-pocket rule ordinarily measures damages as the difference between the value of what the plaintiff gave and the value of what the plaintiff received because of the defendant’s fraudulent inducement. Stated differently, the plaintiff recovers the net pecuniary loss directly caused by the misrepresentation.

Conceptually, the out-of-pocket measure aligns fraud damages with restitutionary principles: it seeks to disgorge the defendant (or compensate the plaintiff) for the concrete loss actually suffered, rather than to approximate a hypothetical “world as promised.” Typical formulations of the rule include: the purchase price minus the actual value received, or the amount paid less the market value of the item acquired at the time of the transaction.

There are a few doctrinal points worth emphasizing about this rule:

- The rule applies to fraud claims: when a plaintiff alleges an intentional misrepresentation that induced a transaction or decision, New York courts generally restrict recovery to out-of-pocket loss rather than expectation damages.

- The measure is prospective-transaction neutral: whether the misrepresentation arises in the sale of securities, a business combination, a contract negotiation, or some other transaction, the out-of-pocket principle governs the fraud claim unless a distinct remedial theory or statutory rule applies.

- Computation requires valuation of both sides of the bargain: courts must determine the reasonable or market value of what the plaintiff paid and what the plaintiff received, at the relevant time(s), to arrive at the net loss.

# Prohibition on benefit-of-the-bargain in fraud claims

Under New York law, plaintiffs asserting fraud are generally precluded from recovering benefit-of-the-bargain (expectation) damages—that is, damages intended to give the plaintiff the benefit of the bargain had the misrepresentation been true. The expectation measure is characteristic of contract remedies, where damages aim to place the non-breaching party in the position it would have been in had the contract been performed. By contrast, fraud is remedialized to restore the victim’s actual financial position as a result of the deception.

Two commonly cited rationales explain the distinction. First, fraud remedies are rooted in tort and restitution; courts are reluctant to award speculative profit-based awards when those profits were premised on false information. Second, allowing benefit-of-the-bargain in fraud claims could produce windfalls: the promise might have never been performable in the real world, and expectation damages could overcompensate the plaintiff compared with the concrete loss actually caused.

Important qualifications and boundaries exist to this general rule:

- Contractual expectation damages remain available for breach of contract claims even if the underlying facts also support a fraud claim. Where separate contract claims are pleaded, a plaintiff may recover expectation damages on the contract claim. The fraud out-of-pocket measure does not convert a contract expectation claim into a tort-based recovery.

- In some circumstances, equitable relief such as rescission followed by restitution may produce results similar to expectation damages, but courts scrutinize the remedy to avoid overcompensation.

- New York courts have recognized that in limited factual situations—where the defendant’s misrepresentations were not only fraudulent but also led to the plaintiff reasonably and foreseeably suffering expectation-type losses—the plaintiff still must demonstrate pecuniary loss causally linked to the misrepresentation. The mere desire to be placed in the position of a fully performed contract is insufficient absent proof of an actual monetary loss.

- If several alternative remedies are available (contract damages, rescission, restitution), the plaintiff’s recovery may ultimately be shaped by election doctrines or setoff rules to prevent double recovery.

# Causation and proof of pecuniary loss

The out-of-pocket rule does not relieve the plaintiff of the usual tort-law requirements: a plaintiff alleging fraud must prove that the defendant made a material misrepresentation of fact; that the defendant knew the representation was false (or acted with reckless disregard for its falsity) and intended that the plaintiff rely on it; that the plaintiff justifiably relied on the misrepresentation; and that such reliance caused the plaintiff to suffer pecuniary harm. Causation and proof of pecuniary loss are integral to establishing entitlement to out-of-pocket damages.

Causation: factual and legal

- Factual causation ("but for"): The plaintiff must show that but for the misrepresentation she would not have taken the damaging action. For example, but for the false representation, the plaintiff would not have purchased or sold the asset or would have acted differently in a way that avoided the loss.

- Legal (proximate) causation: The plaintiff must show that the loss was a reasonably foreseeable result of the misrepresentation and not too remotely connected to the wrongdoing. Intervening acts that break the causal chain may preclude recovery.

Proof of pecuniary loss

- Valuation evidence: Because the remedy is focused on monetary loss, plaintiffs must present admissible evidence of values: the price paid, the market value of what was received at the relevant time, any directly related transaction costs (taxes, transfer fees), and any benefits received that should be offset against loss. Valuation frequently requires documentary proof—contracts, closing statements, invoices, appraisals, market quotations—and expert testimony when the relevant asset lacks a clear market price.

- Calculation models: Courts commonly accept a “before-and-after” valuation approach (value before transaction minus value after) or a purchase-price-minus-value-received approach. For example, where a plaintiff was induced to purchase securities, damages are often measured by the difference between the price paid and the fair market value of the securities at the time of purchase (or, depending on context, at discovery of the fraud). For sales, damages may be computed as the value lost on resale (if the sale was induced by fraud).

- Mitigation and offsets: Plaintiffs have a duty to mitigate losses where reasonable. Recoverable out-of-pocket damages are reduced by any benefits the plaintiff received from the transaction or by payments received from third parties. Likewise, collateral sources that restore economic value to the plaintiff (insurance, indemnities) are typically set off or credited against recoverable damages to avoid double recovery.

- Proximate estimation problems and damages can be reduced or denied where losses are speculative. The out-of-pocket rule constrains plaintiffs seeking intangible or speculative future profits. Courts will require a reasonable basis for measurement; when proof is uncertain, damages may be limited or measured conservatively.

Burdens and standards of proof

- Fraud is an intentional tort, so plaintiffs bear the burden of persuasion by a preponderance of the evidence on each element, including causal link and the amount of loss.

- When the amount of loss is disputed and involves market valuation, courts accept reasonable inferences where the essential elements are proven, but will deny speculative awards. Expert valuation testimony is common and often necessary.

# Interaction with contract remedies and election of remedies

The intersection of fraud (tort) claims and contract claims raises significant remedial and procedural questions. New York law allows plaintiffs to plead fraud and contract claims concurrently, but the remedies differ and courts apply rules to prevent duplicative or inconsistent recovery.

Concurrent pleading and duplicative recovery

- Pleading: It is common—and often strategically advisable—to plead both contract and fraud claims if the facts plausibly support each. A fraud claim may survive even where there is a contract, especially when the misrepresentation was collateral to the contract or induced assent to it.

- Remedies: Contract damages generally aim to put the plaintiff in the position of full contractual performance (expectation); tort damages for fraud are limited to out-of-pocket loss. If a plaintiff obtains a judgment on both theories for overlapping losses, courts will bar double recovery: the plaintiff cannot collect both the expectation measure for the contract claim and the out-of-pocket measure for the fraud claim to compensate the same loss. Courts will offset or require an election of remedies to eliminate duplication.

Election of remedies

- At what stage? New York courts have varied practices on the timing of election. Generally, a plaintiff need not elect remedies at the complaint stage; courts often permit alternative pleadings. Election typically occurs at the time of judgment or recovery, not at the pleading stage, unless a clear inconsistency would make allowance of both remedies unjust.

- Effect of election: Once a plaintiff makes an election between inconsistent remedies (e.g., rescission and affirmance with expectation damages), the election is decisive as to remedies. Election doctrines prevent plaintiffs from simultaneously retaining the contract and seeking to repudiate it for tort recovery.

Rescission and restitution

- Rescission annuls the contract and places parties in status quo by returning consideration. Rescission, if granted because of fraud, may entitle the plaintiff to restitution (return of what was paid), which functionally resembles out-of-pocket recovery.

- If rescission is sought and granted, the plaintiff usually must restore any benefits received; the remedy aims for return of consideration, not expectation.

- A plaintiff pursuing rescission must act promptly and cannot retain benefits inconsistent with rescission. Equitable defenses, laches, and unclean hands can limit rescission.

Setoff, contribution, and multiple defendants

- When there are multiple claims against multiple defendants (e.g., breaches and torts), courts will apportion liability and reduce damages for amounts already obtained from other defendants to prevent overcompensation.

- Where a contract provides liquidated damages or other agreed remedies, a court will consider whether those provisions displace tort remedies, although fraud claims remain viable unless the contractual scheme clearly forecloses them.

# Practical pleading strategies to allege recoverable losses

Pleading a fraud claim in New York involves both substantive and procedural considerations. Federal and state pleading practices require particularity for allegations of fraud, while the substantive damage theory (out-of-pocket) demands concrete description of monetary loss. The following strategies will maximize the plaintiff’s prospects for surviving dismissal and later proving recoverable damages.

1. Plead distinct causes of action in the alternative

- Plead contract causes of action and fraud claims in the alternative when facts permit both theories. Clearly label alternative theories and explain the factual nexus between representations and contractual commitments.

- Preserve both remedies: expectation damages on the contract claim; out-of-pocket damages on the fraud claim. If the facts support only one recovery, the court will sort the remedies later; but pleading both preserves the client’s options.

2. Comply with heightened pleading standards for fraud

- Under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 9(b) and similar state rules, allegations of fraud must be stated with particularity: describe the who, what, when, where, and how of the misrepresentation. Identify the specific statements or omissions alleged to be false, the speaker, the time and place, and the manner in which the plaintiff relied.

- When possible, attach the document(s) containing the misrepresentation (emails, prospectuses, offering memoranda). Incorporate those documents by exhibit to avoid questions about the existence or wording of the alleged representations.

3. Plead damages with precision and link them to the misrepresentation

- Do not plead damages in the abstract. State the specific out-of-pocket loss claimed and explain how it was calculated. If the plaintiff paid $X for an asset and received fair market value $Y for the asset at the time, plead the differential (X - Y) as the claimed loss.

- Where the plaintiff sold an asset at a loss because of fraudulent inducement, plead the purchase price, resale price, and any transactional costs, taxes, or fees that form part of the net loss. If the misrepresentation induced a sale that produced tax consequences, explain with specificity the tax computation and why it was caused by the fraudulent inducement.

- If a valuation requires expert proof, plead that expert valuation will show the value differential and identify the categories of evidence (appraisals, market prices, comparable transactions) the expert will use.

4. Provide the factual basis for causation and justifiable reliance

- Plead that but for the misrepresentation, the plaintiff would not have purchased, sold, or otherwise acted in the way that produced the loss. Describe contemporaneous documents or communications showing reliance (e.g., board minutes, emails approving the transaction, internal memoranda).

- Explain why the reliance was justifiable under the circumstances (e.g., absence of access to the information, complexity of the matter, no reason to doubt the specialist defendant’s statements).

5. Anticipate defendants’ affirmative defences

- Expect defendants to plead mitigation failure, offset for benefits received (e.g., proceeds of sale, tax benefits), and the speculative nature of profit claims. To head off these defenses, plead and, where available, attach documentation showing efforts to mitigate and record benefits received and how those have been accounted for.

- If the plaintiff seeks rescission, plead prompt election and restitution demands, and show that rescission is equitable (no laches, no unclean hands).

6. Quantify alternative damage calculations

- Because a court may permit alternative remedies, plead the principal out-of-pocket calculation and, where appropriate, a contract expectation number as an alternative. If possible, describe the assumptions underpinning each calculation. This preserves appellate positions and allows the factfinder to choose the legally appropriate measure.

7. Use documentary evidence in the complaint or supporting papers

- Attach the transaction documents, valuation reports, tax notices, closing statements, and correspondence reflecting the representations and the harm. Documentary proof minimizes disputes about what was said and can make the damages measurement less speculative.

8. For complex financial losses, include a damages schedule

- In complicated financial transactions, include a concise damages schedule: line items for purchase price, value received, transaction costs, taxes, mitigation proceeds, and net loss. This helps the court and adversary understand the precise monetary claim.

9. Preserve evidence and seek expedited discovery when appropriate

- Fraud claims often hinge on contemporaneous documentary evidence. Seek preservation letters and, when necessary and justified, expedited discovery to secure documents that might be destroyed or altered. Be prepared to show the court why expedited discovery is necessary for establishing the causal and valuation elements.

10. Prepare expert proof for valuation and causation

- Anticipate that valuation and causation will require expert testimony (economists, accountants, appraisers, tax experts). Plead the need for such experts and the factual bases for their forthcoming opinions, and be ready to justify their methods as reliable and appropriate.

# Additional doctrinal considerations

Mitigation, setoffs, and restitution

- Mitigation: Plaintiffs must act reasonably to mitigate losses. A failure to mitigate reduces recoverable out-of-pocket damages.

- Setoffs: Recovery is reduced by amounts the plaintiff received that partially restored losses or that represent benefits obtained through the challenged transaction. Courts apply setoffs to avoid overcompensation.

Punitive damages and emotional harm

- Punitive damages may be available in New York for fraud when the defendant’s conduct was malicious, wanton, or reckless, but courts require clear proof of the defendant’s culpability beyond the basic elements of fraud. Punitive awards are distinct from compensatory out-of-pocket recovery.

- Non-pecuniary harms (emotional distress, reputational harm) are not ordinarily recoverable as part of an out-of-pocket fraud claim unless they produce a measurable pecuniary loss and are pleaded and proven with specificity. New York courts resist awarding purely emotional damages in commercial fraud contexts.

Proof on summary judgment versus trial

- Evidence of value and causation is often amenable to resolution on summary judgment where documentary or expert proof establishes the absence of a triable issue. Conversely, where valuation or causation depends on disputed factual inferences or complex market data, summary judgment is often inappropriate.

- At trial, the plaintiff must present a coherent damages case with admissible evidence; the jury or judge then applies the out-of-pocket framework. If the defendant contests causation or measurement, the plaintiff’s evidence must provide a reasonable basis for the factfinder to use a particular calculation.

# Conclusion

New York’s out-of-pocket rule for fraud damages frames the plaintiff’s remedial universe: fraud claims recover actual pecuniary loss, not the hypothetical benefit of the bargain. This doctrinal posture mandates that plaintiffs carefully plead and document the monetary loss, link that loss causally to the misrepresentation, and anticipate interactions with contract remedies and setoff rules. For practitioners, the practical implications are twofold: (1) draft complaints that plead fraud with particularity and that state precise out-of-pocket calculations and supporting facts; and (2) build an evidentiary record—documentary and expert—sufficient to prove valuation, causation, and mitigation at summary judgment or trial. Mastery of these principles ensures that a fraud claim is both legally cognizable under New York law and positioned for the concrete proof courts require.
