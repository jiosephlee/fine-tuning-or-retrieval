Introduction

Expert testimony often decides complex technical disputes in litigation. Courts and juries rely on expert witnesses to translate specialist facts into legal conclusions—whether a patent claim is practiced, a product meets a regulatory standard, or a causal link exists. But not every expert statement is legally sufficient to sustain a verdict. When an expert’s testimony is internally inconsistent, rests on assumptions contradicted by the record, or fails to map to the legal elements that the plaintiff must prove, courts may conclude that no substantial evidence supports the jury’s finding. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework governing (1) when contradictions in expert testimony render it insufficient as a matter of law, (2) how courts and advocates attempt to reconcile conflicting statements, (3) how this analysis interacts with the “all-elements” rule (the requirement that each element of a claim or cause be proved), and (4) practical strategies for cross-examination and building a record to preserve and win post-trial motions or appeals.

# When contradictions render testimony insufficient as a matter of law

Standards of review and legal sufficiency

When a party moves for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) after a jury verdict, the judge asks whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, permits a reasonable jury to find for that party. The precise formulation varies by circuit, but federal practice commonly treats JMOL as proper when “substantial evidence” does not support the jury’s verdict and does so without weighing credibility. Substantial evidence generally means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion. On review, appellate courts apply the JMOL standard de novo as to legal questions and ask whether the district court correctly determined sufficiency under the regional circuit’s standard.

Contrast between contradictions that affect weight and contradictions that destroy sufficiency

Not every inconsistency in testimony defeats a verdict. Courts distinguish between contradictions that a jury is entitled to resolve (affecting the weight, credibility, or persuasiveness of testimony) and contradictions that are so fundamental or irreconcilable that the testimony cannot, as a matter of law, constitute substantial evidence for an essential element.

Contradictions that go to credibility are classic jury questions. If two experts disagree or an expert gives inconsistent answers, a jury may credit one version and discredit the other. JMOL is inappropriate where reasonable jurors could accept the testimony as internally coherent or could reconcile apparent tensions.

By contrast, contradictions destroy legal sufficiency when they make the expert’s testimony self-defeating on an essential point, or when the testimony relies on a premise that the record incontrovertibly disproves. Typical examples where courts have found insufficiency include:

- The expert’s testimony is the only evidence of an essential element, and the expert gives directly contradictory answers on that element such that no reasonable jury could find in favor of the proffered conclusion.

- The expert’s ultimate conclusion depends on a factual assumption that is demonstrably false in the record (e.g., the expert assumed a product included a component when the record shows the component was absent), and the expert offers no alternative ground tying the evidence to the element.

- The testimony is internally inconsistent such that the portions that support the verdict are negated by other portions; the positive assertions cannot be isolated from the contradictions without impermissibly reweighing or imputing credibility.

How courts analyze whether a contradiction is fatal

When evaluating whether a contradiction renders testimony insufficient, courts typically:

1. Identify the essential element(s) for which the expert supplies proof.

2. Determine whether the expert’s testimony (or combination of testimony and other record evidence) provides uncontradicted, non-speculative support for that element.

3. Decide whether any contradictions are matters of weight for the jury (resolvable by credibility determinations) or are so fundamental that resolving them would require weighing the same witness’s inconsistent statements—something JMOL forbids.

If the expert’s testimony is the sole evidence of an essential element and is internally contradictory on that element, courts often conclude that substantial evidence is lacking and grant JMOL.

# Reconciling conflicting expert statements

Principles favoring reconciliation

Courts are generally reluctant to throw out jury verdicts and will try to harmonize evidence where a reasonable construction permits it. Reconciling conflicting expert statements can preserve a verdict if the court can construe the testimony as describing different aspects of the same phenomenon, or as providing alternative but cumulative routes to the same legal conclusion.

Methods of reconciliation

Reconcilable conflicts are often treated through one or more of the following approaches:

- Contextual reading: Experts sometimes give abbreviated answers under trial pressure. Reading testimony in context—examining the question, the surrounding exchanges, and the underlying assumed hypotheticals—can show that a seemingly contradictory statement was limited in scope or meant to apply to a different scenario.

- Segmentation: Courts may isolate portions of testimony that are consistent with the record and treat contradictions as arguing only for diminished weight; if the consistent portions alone are sufficient to support the verdict, the contradiction is not fatal.

- Methodological correction: An expert may offer multiple independent bases for an opinion. If one basis is undermined, courts may accept an alternative basis that remains supported by the record, so long as the expert did not conceal the reliance on the alternative basis until appeal.

Limitations on reconciliation

Reconciling testimony has limits. A court cannot salvage a verdict by rewriting testimony or by attributing to the witness an unexpressed or invented view. Reconciliation is acceptable only where the construction is reasonable and does not require the court to credit or discredit competing statements—that is, it cannot involve the very credibility determinations JMOL forbids. If the testimony can only be reconciled by disbelieving parts of the same witness’s testimony (for example, treating one of two directly opposing statements as a misstatement), the court may not do so on JMOL grounds; the proper remedy may be a new trial if the contradictions are profound but juror discretion could have reasonably resolved them.

Reconciling across multiple experts

When the record contains multiple experts, courts perform a similar balancing. One expert’s unequivocal, well-founded testimony may suffice even if another expert contradicts it, because a jury may credit one and not the other. But if all expert testimony on an essential element is internally contradictory, or if all experts’ support depends on a common, unsupported assumption, then the aggregate evidence may still be legally insufficient.

# Interaction with the all-elements rule

The all-elements rule explained

In patent and many other areas of substantive law, the plaintiff must prove every element of a claim or cause of action. Under the “all-elements” (or “all limitations”) rule in patent law, for example, an infringement verdict or finding requires evidence that each claim element is present in the accused product or process. Similarly, for negligence or statutory claims, each statutory element must be proved. The all-elements rule imposes a structural constraint: failing proof on any single required element is fatal to the claim.

How contradictions interact with the all-elements rule

Contradictory expert testimony often raises problems under the all-elements rule in two main ways:

1. Failure to establish an element: If an expert is the sole source of evidence on a particular element, and their testimony is internally contradictory about that element, there is no legally sufficient proof of that element. Even if the expert gives persuasive testimony on other elements, lack of proof on one required element defeats the claim.

2. Gaps filled by speculation or assumption: Experts sometimes seek to bridge evidentiary gaps by assuming facts unsupported by the record. Where such assumptions are essential to an element, contradictions or absence of corroboration make the element unproven as a matter of law. Courts will not infer missing proof simply because other elements appear satisfied.

Mapping testimony to claim elements

To avoid sufficiency problems, expert testimony must do more than supply general support; it must be a reliable bridge between the record facts and each legal element. Effective expert opinions explicitly identify the evidence in the record that corresponds to each element and explain the reasoning that connects the two. The court will scrutinize whether the opinion provides a sufficiently particularized mapping and whether any contradictions undermine that mapping.

Common pitfalls that trigger all-elements failures

- Aggregated or generalized testimony: An expert who testifies in broad generalities without pinning testimony to discrete claim elements invites an all-elements failure. Juries cannot be left to infer that a general opinion covers every element.

- Inconsistent identifications: When an expert identifies alternative ways an element might be met but gives inconsistent accounts about which way actually applies, the uncertainty can make proof of that element legally inadequate.

- Reliance on disputed demonstrations: Opinions that lean heavily on demonstratives, simulations, or hypothesized reconstructions unsupported by admissible evidence are vulnerable if the underlying assumptions are contradicted.

# Strategic cross-examination and record-building

Objectives of strategic cross-examination

Cross-examination aims to test the accuracy, foundation, and consistency of an expert’s testimony, and to create a trial record that can be used in post-trial motions or on appeal. The defense or adverse party seeks to expose inconsistencies, force admissions about foundational assumptions, and establish that an opinion rests on speculation or erroneous facts. The proponent of the expert, in turn, must use direct examination and redirect to shore up foundations, elicit corroborating documentary or scientific support, and reconcile any apparent contradictions in a way that is recordable and repeatable.

Tactics for undermining self-contradictory testimony

1. Pin down the essential element(s). Ask the expert to identify precisely which record facts they rely on to prove each element. Force the witness to state whether a particular document, feature, or measurement is necessary to their conclusion. Short, focused questions make it harder for witnesses to improvise and produce contradictory sound bites.

2. Reduce hypotheticals to specifics. Translate hypothetical variations into concrete, record-substantiated scenarios. If an opinion depends on an assumption, ask whether the conclusion would change if that assumption were false and then introduce admissible evidence demonstrating the assumption’s falsity.

3. Use prior statements and reports. Cross-examine with depositions, prior expert reports, or public statements that conflict with the trial testimony. Having the prior statement in evidence and making the expert explain the discrepancy creates an explicit record of inconsistency.

4. Break complex conclusions into components. Experts often answer in long, compound propositions. Break the overall conclusion into sub-findings and attack each one. Expose any sub-finding that is unsupported or contradicted by other record evidence.

5. Highlight internal contradictions. If the expert makes a statement on direct that conflicts with an earlier answer, read both statements back and ask the witness to reconcile them. Get the witness to concede the inconsistency on the record. A clear admission that the expert said X in one place and Y in another creates a strong basis for JMOL or an appellate challenge.

6. Test methodology and alternatives. Show that the expert used an unreliable methodology or ignored more probative data. If the methodology would yield different answers under different reasonable assumptions, elicit that sensitivity on cross-examination.

Tactics for fortifying expert testimony on direct and redirect

1. Preempt contradictions by building a clear narrative. On direct, require the expert to map evidence to each legal element and to state explicitly which portions of the record support each mapping. Use demonstratives tied to identified exhibits and deposition testimony to make the linkage obvious.

2. Secure corroboration. Where possible, have the expert rely on documents, measurements, or admissions from adverse parties. Corroborative evidence reduces vulnerability to claims that an opinion rests on unsupported assumptions.

3. Limit reliance on broad hypotheticals. Encourage the expert to anchor conclusions in examined artifacts rather than abstract possibilities. If hypotheticals are necessary, have the expert spell out how the hypotheticals are grounded in the actual record.

4. Anticipate cross-examination points. During direct, raise and address anticipated challenges so that the expert can provide a coherent, reconciled explanation before cross-examination seizes the narrative.

Record-building to preserve JMOL or appellate relief

1. Make the essential statements explicit. A judge reviewing a sufficiency challenge will focus on whether the record contains competent evidence on each element. Asking the expert to declare on the record, with citation to exhibits and trial testimony, that each element is met reduces the chance the record will be characterized as ambiguous.

2. Preserve prior inconsistent statements. If you intend to rely on a witness’s inconsistency to argue insufficiency, ensure that the contradictory statements are admitted into the record—through deposition designations, admissions, or exhibit introduction—so the appellate court can review them.

3. Use proper objections and motions. If the opposing expert introduces speculative or legally insufficient opinions, contemporaneous objections (e.g., lack of foundation, improper legal conclusion) preserve the issue. File a timely JMOL or renewed JMOL (Rule 50(a) and (b) in federal practice) and a Rule 59 motion for a new trial if appropriate, explaining how the contradictions, when viewed as a whole, render the verdict unsupported by substantial evidence.

4. Elicit alternative testimony that the jury can rely on. If the expert offers multiple routes to a conclusion, get the witness to identify which route they prefer and to confirm that it is supported by specific exhibits. That creates a discrete basis for the jury’s verdict that may survive scrutiny even if other routes are undermined.

5. Create demonstratives tied to admissible evidence. Visuals can make element-by-element mappings clearer on the record. Ensure every demonstrative is tied to and supported by admitted exhibits to avoid appellate characterization of the demonstrative as speculative.

Preserving disputes about credibility versus sufficiency

Because appellate courts will not reweigh credibility, it is crucial to distinguish factual contradictions that are purely credibility issues from those that eliminate any legally sufficient evidence of an essential element. If the factual record includes admissible, non-contradicted evidence supporting the element independent of the witness’s inconsistency, the appellate argument that a jury could reasonably resolve the conflict is weaker. Conversely, if the contradicted testimony is the only evidence of an element, preserving a clear record of the contradiction and its centrality to the verdict strengthens a JMOL or appellate challenge.

# Conclusion

Self-contradictory expert testimony occupies a critical intersection between evidence, procedure, and substantive law. Courts will not sustain verdicts on testimony that is internally inconsistent in ways that defeat proof of essential elements, yet they will ordinarily leave to juries the resolution of conflicts that go to credibility. Lawyers must therefore build and preserve a record that maps expert reasoning to each legal element, expose and document any foundational falsehoods or contradictions, and know when to move for JMOL or a new trial. Trial judges and appellate courts will probe whether the contradictions are matters of weight for the jury or are so fundamental that permitting the verdict would transgress the requirement that every element be supported by substantial evidence. Understanding these doctrinal lines and employing strategic trial practices are essential to protecting a client’s rights at trial and on appeal.
