Introduction

This chapter explains the fundamental legal framework for analyzing patent infringement claims, with particular emphasis on proving literal infringement of method (process) claims. Before reading judicial opinions that apply these principles to particular facts, a student should understand the statutory baselines for infringement and the basic litigation burdens, the "all-elements" requirement for claim-by-claim proof, how courts distinguish literal infringement from infringement under the doctrine of equivalents, and the special considerations that arise when the asserted claims are method claims. This chapter also explains how the presumption of patent validity interacts with the parties’ burdens of proof.

# 35 U.S.C. § 271 and types of infringement

The principal statutory provisions that define what acts constitute infringement are found in 35 U.S.C. § 271. Section 271 sets out several distinct types of infringing conduct, each of which imposes liability under different factual patterns:

- Direct infringement of an apparatus, product, or manufacture typically occurs under § 271(a) when a party without authority makes, uses, sells, offers to sell, or imports a patented invention into the United States. For product and apparatus claims, the typical analysis asks whether the accused product embodies every element of the asserted claim as properly construed.

- Direct infringement of a method (process) claim is also governed by § 271(a). For method claims, liability arises when a party performs all of the claimed steps. The performance requirement is central to method-claim infringement and is discussed in detail later.

- Induced infringement is governed by § 271(b). A party may be liable if it actively induces another party to infringe a patent. Inducement requires an underlying act of direct infringement by someone other than the inducer; the inducement statute does not itself create liability unless there is direct infringement to be induced. Courts focus on whether the inducer knew of the patent and took affirmative steps to encourage infringement with the requisite intent.

- Contributory infringement is addressed by § 271(c). This provision makes it unlawful to sell or offer to sell a component of a patented machine, manufacture, combination or composition, or a material or apparatus for use in practicing a patented process, when the component is a material part of the invention and is not a staple article suitable for substantial noninfringing use, with knowledge that the component is especially made or adapted for use in infringement. Contributory infringement therefore targets suppliers of components that enable infringement.

These statutory categories are analytically distinct and sometimes interact. For example, inducement liability ordinarily depends on a showing that another party directly infringed the patent. Similarly, contributory infringement requires proof of the component’s special adaptation and the defendant’s knowledge. In all types, claim construction—the court’s interpretation of the patent claims—frames the question of whether the accused conduct falls within the claim scope.

# All-elements rule and element-by-element proof

The all-elements rule is a core doctrinal principle: to prove literal infringement of a claim, the patentee must show that the accused device, product, or practice contains every limitation (element or step) of the asserted claim as properly construed. This is often summarized as an element-by-element or limitation-by-limitation comparison: identify the claim limitations (after claim construction), determine their meaning, and then compare the accused instrumentality or conduct to each limitation. If any claim limitation is missing or not met, there is no literal infringement of that claim.

Several points about how courts apply the all-elements rule are important:

Claim construction precedes infringement analysis. Courts construe the claim terms to determine the metes and bounds of the claim’s limitations. Claim construction is typically a legal determination (subject to deference to certain subsidiary factual findings in some circumstances), and it shapes the element-by-element comparison. Once the court construes the claim terms, the finder of fact (judge when equitable; jury when factual disputes exist) compares the adjudicated facts to the construed claim elements to determine whether each limitation is present in the accused product or method.

Literal inclusion required for each limitation. Literal infringement requires that, for each limitation, the accused product or act literally meet the limitation as construed. If the accused device only performs something similar but not the same as a claim limitation, then literal infringement is not established for that limitation; the patentee may need to rely on doctrine of equivalents principles to cover insubstantial differences (discussed below).

Parties may attack or defend particular limitations. Litigation often focuses on disputed claim limitations—terms whose meaning is unclear or whose presence in the accused device is in dispute. A patentee must prove each such limitation by a preponderance of the evidence (discussed below). Defendants may concede certain limitations or contest them with technical arguments or expert testimony.

Dependent claims and alternative claim constructions. A claim may contain alternative or conjunctive limitations. For example, a claim may read "A device comprising A or B," or "A device comprising A and B." The all-elements rule operates on whatever the claim requires. Dependent claims carry additional limitations that narrow the independent claim; proving infringement of a dependent claim requires proving the additional limitations too.

The all-elements rule reflects the textual nature of patent rights: the patent grants a right to exclude corresponding to the language of the claims. Courts therefore rigorously apply the rule to avoid expanding patent scope beyond the claim language.

# Literal infringement vs. doctrine of equivalents (overview)

Courts recognize two principal ways a patentee can prevail on an infringement theory: literal infringement and infringement under the doctrine of equivalents. These are complementary but distinct doctrines.

Literal infringement. Literal infringement requires that the accused product or method contain each claim limitation exactly as construed. The all-elements rule is the governing principle. If the accused device or practice literally satisfies each limitation, infringement is established without recourse to equivalence analysis.

Doctrine of equivalents (DOE) — overview. The doctrine of equivalents provides a limited mechanism for a patentee to capture accused devices or practices that do not literally meet every claim limitation but nonetheless perform substantially the same function in substantially the same way to achieve substantially the same result, or otherwise are insubstantially different from the claimed invention. The function-way-result test is a common articulation of DOE analysis, though courts may apply other formulations such as the "insubstantial differences" test. DOE is a species of equitable relief that prevents an alleged infringer from escaping liability through insubstantial or colorable variations that copy the essence of the patented invention.

Key limitations on DOE. DOE is a narrow doctrine and courts have established several limits:

- Prosecution history estoppel. Statements, amendments, or disclaimers made during patent prosecution can limit the patentee’s ability to assert equivalent coverage for surrendered subject matter. If a patentee narrows a claim to obtain allowance, it may be estopped from later asserting equivalents that would encompass what was surrendered, although the precise scope of estoppel depends on the reasons for the amendment and other circumstances.

- Public notice and claims delineation. The patent system is premised on public notice—claim language should provide clear boundaries of what is claimed. DOE is applied sparingly to avoid eroding that notice function.

- Objective indicia. Courts consider whether applying DOE would vitiate an entire claim limitation or essentially read the claim out of the patent. DOE cannot be used to eliminate a claim element altogether.

Interaction in litigation. Procedurally, a patentee typically presents proof of literal infringement first, and if literal infringement is not shown, may present DOE as an alternative theory. Courts and juries often assess DOE only after claim construction establishes the literal boundaries. Because DOE is equitable in nature, judges retain significant control over whether DOE instructions are appropriate for juries and how to apply the doctrine in particular factual contexts.

# Direct infringement of method claims and performance of each step

Method or process claims present particular features in infringement analysis because their limitations are recited as steps to be performed rather than structural elements of a device. Under § 271(a), direct infringement of a method claim occurs when a single entity performs all the claimed steps. When analyzing a claim for infringement, courts apply the all-elements rule to steps: the patentee must prove that each claimed step was performed.

Single-actor rule. Traditionally, direct infringement of method claims requires that one party perform every step. If a single actor performs each step, whether sequentially or concurrently, that performance meets the literal infringement standard once each step has been proved to have occurred. Proof commonly depends on documentary evidence, logs, witness testimony, expert analysis, or other demonstrative evidence showing that the asserted steps were performed in the claimed manner and order (when order is recited and material to the claim).

Divided or multi-actor performance. Complications arise when multiple parties collectively perform steps of a claimed method. If no single actor performs every step, the patentee faces an evidentiary and legal hurdle: how to attribute others’ performance to a single liable entity. Courts have developed frameworks for attribution in such cases. The key legal question is whether one party can be held responsible for another party's performance so that, in law, a single entity is effectively performing all steps.

Common bases for attribution include agency, contract, or direction/control. For example, if the alleged infringer directs or controls the performance of others—by contracting with them to perform claimed steps, instructing them to act in a particular way, or establishing a joint enterprise—courts may treat the performance as attributable to the defendant for purposes of § 271(a). The specific doctrinal tests vary by jurisdiction and circumstance, but the core idea is that liability can attach where one party exercises control over the performance of steps by others or where the actions are attributable under general principles of law (agency, joint enterprise, or established contractual obligations).

Inducement and induced method infringement. When steps are performed by multiple actors who are not under the control of a single entity, the patentee may still pursue a claim for induced infringement under § 271(b) if the defendant knowingly induced others to perform the steps and had the requisite intent to encourage infringement. Inducement requires proof that (1) the defendant knew of the patent, (2) there was direct infringement by another (the steps were performed), and (3) the defendant took active steps to induce infringement with the requisite intent. The existence of directed actions, promotional materials, instructions, or other conduct encouraging third parties to perform steps can give rise to inducement liability.

Evidence and timing. Proving performance of method steps often turns on temporal and factual detail: when steps were taken, by whom, and how often. For claims that require particular sequences, timing evidence is critical. A patentee must show by a preponderance of the evidence that each step was performed in the claimed manner and order (if order is a limitation). Expert testimony frequently plays a role in establishing technical steps and translating business records or forensic data into a finding that the claim steps were performed.

# Burden of proof (preponderance) and presumption of validity (35 U.S.C. § 282)

Burden of proof on infringement. The patentee bears the burden of proving infringement. The standard of proof for infringement is the preponderance of the evidence: the patentee must prove it is more likely than not that the accused device or conduct meets every limitation of the asserted claim (or, alternatively, that it is equivalent under DOE). The preponderance standard applies to the fact-intensive element-by-element comparison, including proof that all steps of a method claim were performed by the accused infringer (or attributable to it).

Presumption of validity and burden for invalidity. At the same time, patents are accorded a statutory presumption of validity under 35 U.S.C. § 282. That statute states that a patent shall be presumed valid, and that the patent owner may enforce rights as set forth therein. In litigation, § 282 has been interpreted to mean that a party challenging a patent's validity bears the burden of proving invalidity. Historically, the Supreme Court has required the challenger to meet a heightened standard—clear and convincing evidence—to overcome the presumption of validity in district court litigation. Thus, many courts instruct that a defendant must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the asserted claims are invalid (for anticipation, obviousness, lack of enablement, lack of written description, indefiniteness, or other statutory grounds). The interplay between the presumption of validity and evidentiary standards can affect litigation strategy: a defendant seeking to avoid liability by proving invalidity faces a more demanding standard than the patentee faces when proving infringement.

Interaction of burdens in practice. The litigation posture often involves dual showings: the patentee must prove infringement by a preponderance, and the defendant may respond with a challenge to validity that must meet the clear and convincing standard. The net effect is that a patentee has a relatively easier task to show infringement than a defendant has to show invalidity. That said, certain procedural contexts—such as inter partes review (IPR) proceedings before the Patent Trial and Appeal Board—employ different standards and burdens; BIA and administrative proceedings use the administrative standard of preponderance of the evidence or other applicable standards. But in federal district court litigation, § 282’s presumption and the clear-and-convincing standard historically govern invalidity defenses.

Burden on inducement and intent. For indirect infringement claims, such as inducement under § 271(b), the plaintiff must prove the elements of inducement, including the defendant’s knowledge of the patent and intent to induce infringement. The standard for these factual predicates is typically preponderance of the evidence. However, establishing the required level of intent often depends on a defendant’s state of mind, which is difficult to prove and frequently inferred from circumstantial evidence such as promotional statements, instructions, or the design of a product that encourages infringing use.

Evidentiary implications for experts and fact witnesses. Because proving infringement (and rebutting invalidity) is a fact-driven exercise, expert testimony, technical demonstrations, documentary evidence, and clear factual narratives are critical. Experts explain claim terms, map claim elements to accused products or practices, and opine on whether substitutions are insubstantial for DOE. Fact witnesses provide the who/what/when detail for method step performance. The different standards of proof influence how parties present their evidence: defendants often aim to undermine the patentee’s element-by-element comparison while asserting affirmative invalidity defenses supported by prior art and forensic analysis intended to meet the clear-and-convincing threshold.

# Procedural posture and how it affects analysis

Procedural posture (e.g., summary judgment, bench trial, jury trial) significantly affects how courts handle infringement questions and the interplay of law and fact.

Claim construction as a preliminary legal step. Courts typically construe claims before conducting the infringement analysis. Claim construction is generally a matter of law for the court. The procedural posture matters because how claim construction is reached varies: a bench trial or summary judgment may involve a different posture for factual underpinnings of claim construction than a jury trial, but the ultimate claim interpretation is for the judge and governs the subsequent factual determination of infringement.

Summary judgment. On summary judgment, the movant bears the usual burden of showing no genuine dispute of material fact and that they are entitled to judgment as a matter of law. For infringement, a patentee seeking summary judgment of infringement must show that no reasonable factfinder could find the claimed elements unmet by the accused device; conversely, a defendant seeking summary judgment of noninfringement must show that even under the patentee’s favorable view of the evidence, no reasonable factfinder could find infringement. Courts are cautious to grant summary judgment on infringement or noninfringement when material factual disputes exist—particularly on issues of fact-intensive step performance, intent for inducement, or complex technical mappings between claims and accused devices.

Jury trial. When infringement questions are presented to a jury, the judge instructs the jury on claim construction as a matter of law, and the jury then decides whether the accused device meets the claims as construed. The jury applies the preponderance standard to determine infringement, and may also decide inducement or contributory infringement facts. Judges often retain control over DOE and over whether such theories may go to the jury given equitable considerations.

Bench trial. In a bench trial, the judge decides both claim construction and infringement. The judge applies the preponderance standard for factual matters, and equitable doctrines (such as DOE) are applied under the judge’s discretion.

Interplays with invalidity defenses. Where a defendant asserts invalidity, the doubly focused burden structure—preponderance for infringement, clear and convincing for invalidity—creates strategic dynamics. On a motion for summary judgment of invalidity, some courts may treat invalidity as raising material factual disputes requiring a trial if the challenger cannot establish clear and convincing evidence on the summary judgment record.

Procedural implications for method claims and multi-actor scenarios. The procedural vehicle can affect how courts allocate proof in multi-actor method claims. For example, on summary judgment a patentee may be denied relief if it cannot show evidence to attribute others’ performances to a single defendant; on the other hand, a defendant asserting divided performance as a defense may be unable to completely foreclose liability if a patentee presents evidence creating a factual dispute over direction or control.

Conclusion

Proving patent infringement is a structured exercise built atop claim construction, the all-elements rule, and statutory categories in § 271. For method claims, literal infringement requires proving by a preponderance of the evidence that a single entity performed each recited step, or alternatively that others’ performances can be legally attributed to the defendant or that the defendant induced the performances. The doctrine of equivalents offers a narrow equitable backstop for insubstantial differences, subject to limits such as prosecution history estoppel. The statutory presumption of validity under § 282 places the burden of proving invalidity on the challenger, historically by clear and convincing evidence, so litigation typically features simultaneous element-by-element infringement proof by the patentee and affirmative invalidity proof by the defendant under a higher evidentiary standard. Understanding how courts map facts to these legal principles is essential preparation for reading and applying judicial opinions in patent infringement disputes.
