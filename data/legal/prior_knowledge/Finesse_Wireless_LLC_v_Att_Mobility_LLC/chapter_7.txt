Introduction

Expert testimony is central to modern patent litigation. Technical complexity, specialized terminology, and the need to translate engineering or scientific features into legal conclusions make experts indispensable for proving infringement and establishing damages. Judges must balance the utility of expert evidence against the risk that unreliable or misleading testimony will distort the factfinder’s deliberations. That balance plays out through a combination of Federal Rules of Evidence (especially Rules 702, 703, and 705), Rule 26 expert-disclosure obligations, Daubert-style gatekeeping, and procedural tools used at summary judgment, in limine motions, during trial, and on appeal. This chapter explains the governing evidentiary rules and standards, describes how experts typically translate claim language into accusations against an accused product, discusses the use and limits of demonstratives and tutorials, and addresses how courts treat inconsistent or self-contradictory expert testimony. The emphasis is doctrinal: which legal tests apply, how courts apply them to facts, what burdens litigants bear, and how procedural posture affects outcomes.

# Federal Rules of Evidence 702 and Daubert reliability

Federal Rule of Evidence 702 governs the admissibility of expert testimony in federal courts. It requires that: (1) the witness is qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education; (2) the expert’s specialized knowledge will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or determine a fact in issue (helpfulness); (3) the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data; and (4) the testimony is the product of reliable principles and methods reliably applied to the facts of the case.

Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals and its progeny interpret Rule 702 to impose a trial-court gatekeeping function. The gatekeeper’s role is not to choose between competing expert opinions, but to ensure that the proffered opinion rests on a reliable foundation and is relevant to the issues the jury must decide. The analysis has two overarching dimensions: reliability and relevance.

Reliability. Courts examine whether the expert used a reliable methodology. The Daubert line supplied nonexclusive factors courts may consider: testability or falsifiability of the underlying theory or technique; whether the theory or method has been subjected to peer review and publication; known or potential error rate and the existence of standards controlling the technique’s operation; and general acceptance within the relevant scientific community. The Supreme Court later confirmed that the Daubert inquiry applies not only to “scientific” testimony but also to technical and other specialized knowledge (Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael). The pertinent considerations are therefore pragmatic and flexible: the inquiry focuses on whether the reasoning or methodology underlying the testimony is scientifically or technically valid and whether it can be applied reliably to the facts.

Relevance. Even a reliable methodology must be tied to the facts of the case. An expert’s opinion must assist the trier of fact by relating to matters that are within the jury’s purview. Opinions that address purely legal questions for the court (e.g., construing claim terms) or that are untethered to the record may be excluded as unhelpful. The relevance inquiry also looks at the fit between the expert’s opinion and the factual issues—whether the expert’s reasoning logically advances the factfinder’s understanding of contested questions.

Application to patent litigation. In patent cases, courts apply Daubert gatekeeping to a variety of opinions: technical infringement opinions, claim-construction-related analyses, and economic damages opinions. For technical experts, courts scrutinize the expert’s testing protocols, reverse-engineering practices, sampling and measurement procedures, and the chain of reasoning by which particular product features are found to satisfy specific claim limitations. For damages experts, courts often focus on the validity of the hypothetical negotiation framework, the selection and comparability of license agreements or comparable products, apportionment of value to the patented features, and the robustness of any regression or economic modeling.

Burden and standard of review. The proponent of the expert testimony bears the burden of establishing admissibility by a preponderance of the evidence. On appeal, a district court’s Daubert rulings are reviewed for abuse of discretion, reflecting the gatekeeping role that trial courts exercise over expert evidence.

Procedural posture. Daubert motions may be filed pretrial as standalone motions or combined with motions in limine. Decisions are often made before trial to shape the admissible scope of evidence, but Rule 702 may be invoked at summary judgment and at trial. When an opinion is excluded at the gatekeeping stage, the proponent may seek to cure defects (e.g., via additional testing or supplementation) or present other evidence. If an expert’s methodology is merely weak, not fatally unreliable, courts may admit it and leave the issue to cross-examination and the factfinder.

# Rule 703: reliance on technical materials and company documents

Rule 703 governs the facts or data an expert may rely upon in forming an opinion. It states that an expert may base an opinion on facts or data that the expert has been made aware of or personally observed. These underlying facts need not be admissible for the opinion to be admitted, provided they are of the type reasonably relied upon by experts in the particular field. If the underlying facts or data would otherwise be inadmissible, the court may exclude their disclosure to the jury if the probative value of the underlying facts is substantially outweighed by the risk of undue prejudice, confusion, or a waste of time.

Reasonable reliance. The “reasonably relied upon” standard is both field-specific and context-specific. Courts will consider whether experts in the discipline commonly rely on similar sources—testing data, vendor specifications, code review, internal company documents, software output, business records, or hearsay statements. For example, a software engineer might reasonably rely on source code, compiled binaries, execution traces, or vendor documentation. An electrical engineer might reasonably rely on schematic diagrams, test bench results, or component datasheets. Rule 703 permits experts to base their opinions on such materials even if those materials would be hearsay if offered for the truth of the matter asserted.

Disclosure and limits. Rule 705 interacts with Rule 703 on disclosure. Under Rule 705, an expert may give an opinion without first disclosing the underlying facts or data. The expert must, however, disclose such facts and data on cross-examination if requested. In practice, disclosure obligations under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure—particularly Rule 26(a)(2), which requires expert reports signed by the witness containing a complete statement of all opinions and the basis and reasons therefor—functionally require greater transparency than the FRE alone. Rule 26 requires identification of the facts and data considered, the methodology used, and often production of the underlying materials.

Company documents and business records. Internal company documents commonly serve as the factual foundation for both infringement and damages opinions. Rule 703 permits experts to rely on these documents, and many company documents may independently qualify under hearsay exceptions (e.g., the business-records exception). Even when a document is hearsay and not independently admissible, the expert may still rely on it. The court, however, must decide whether the circumstances of the document’s creation and the expert’s evaluation render the reliance reasonable. For instance, cherry-picked or self-serving internal documents that do not withstand scrutiny may be attacked via cross-examination, demonstrations of selective reliance, or motions to exclude where the reliance is so deficient as to render the opinion unreliable.

Authentication and weight. Reliance under Rule 703 does not relieve the proponent of the need to authenticate documents properly when they are offered into evidence for their truth. If an opinion depends on a company document that is not authenticated or is excluded under another evidentiary rule, the expert’s opinion may survive but will be vulnerable on cross-examination and may carry limited weight.

# Mapping claim limitations to accused products

Proving infringement requires mapping each asserted claim limitation to features of the accused product or process and demonstrating that the accused instrumentality meets every limitation of the claim as properly construed. This task typically falls to technical experts working within the boundaries of claim construction established by the court.

Claim construction and the expert’s role. Claim construction is a legal determination for the court, not the jury. That said, experts play an essential role in assisting the court and jury to understand the technical meaning of claim terms and to map claims to products. Experts often prepare claim charts that set out, line-by-line, each claim limitation alongside evidence tying that limitation to specific product structures, code snippets, processes, tests, screenshots, or documentation. Because claim construction may change after a report is produced, experts must carefully state assumptions tied to particular constructions and be prepared to adjust analyses when the court construes terms.

Literal infringement and the doctrine of equivalents. There are two primary modes of proving infringement: literal infringement (the accused product contains every claim limitation exactly as written) and infringement under the doctrine of equivalents (the accused product performs substantially the same function in substantially the same way to achieve substantially the same result, thereby equivalent to a claimed element). Experts must construct separate analyses for each theory. For literal infringement, experts typically identify specific structures or steps that correspond to each claim element. For equivalents, experts must craft a reasoned technical explanation of how an accused feature performs the same function in substantially the same way to achieve the same result, often addressing the particular limitations requiring an equivalence analysis.

Stepwise mapping and claim charts. Well-structured claim charts are the common method for mapping limitations to accused products. Each limitation is restated; the expert cites evidence showing where in the accused product the limitation exists; and the expert explains the basis for the mapping (e.g., test results, source code lines, product manuals, reverse-engineering reports). The mapping must be specific: conclusory assertions that “the product meets this limitation” without an evidentiary trail are weak and vulnerable to exclusion or summary-judgment attack. When addressing complex multi-component systems, an expert should identify which component, subroutine, or module corresponds to each claim element and, for method claims, which steps or operations are performed and in what sequence.

Testing, sampling, and reverse engineering. Mapping often depends on testing and reverse engineering. Courts evaluate the reliability of the testing methodology under Daubert: Was the testing repeatable? Were tests conducted under conditions that reflect normal operation? Was the sampling sufficient to overcome variability across product versions or configurations? For software, experts may examine source code, compiled binaries, API traces, log files, and runtime behavior. They may also rely on vendor documentation and third-party specs. When testing or reverse engineering is the factual basis, experts must preserve and document the chain of custody and the procedures employed. Where an accused product exists in many configurations, experts must explain why the tested configuration reliably represents the accused product in the marketplace.

Joint infringement and multi-actor systems. Mapping claim limitations becomes particularly challenging in cases alleging divided performance or joint infringement (where multiple actors together perform all claim steps). Experts must carefully identify who or what performs each step and explain legal theories for attributing steps performed by others to the accused party (e.g., agency, direction and control, contractual arrangements, or single-entity control). Courts scrutinize such mapping because the legal attribution of steps to a single actor has complex doctrinal constraints.

Burden of proof. The patentee bears the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence. The expert’s mapping must therefore be sufficient to persuade a reasonable factfinder that it is more likely than not that each claim limitation is met. On summary judgment, the absence of a genuine dispute about any limitation—or the presence of reliable contrary expert evidence from the accused infringer—can defeat the infringement claim. On motions to exclude, the proponent of the expert must prove that the methodology by which the mapping was performed is reliable.

# Use of demonstratives and tutorials

Demonstratives and tutorials are common tools for conveying complex technical concepts to judges and juries. They can simplify intricate mechanisms, show comparative diagrams, animate processes over time, or present step-by-step deconstructions of code or circuitry. While these tools can be persuasive, courts treat them as special species of evidence and apply both evidentiary and pragmatic constraints.

Substantive versus illustrative. Demonstratives that merely illustrate an expert’s admissible opinion are generally permissible. Examples include diagrams, animations, timelines, and simplified schematics. However, demonstratives that introduce new substantive facts or novel analysis not disclosed in the expert report are problematic. If a demonstrative goes beyond what the expert disclosed in accordance with Rule 26(a)(2) or mischaracterizes the record, it may be excluded or struck.

Foundation and fidelity. A demonstrative must be founded in the record. If an exhibit claims to show a particular operation or a specific line of code performing a function, the proponent must be able to point to the underlying evidence (e.g., test results, code excerpts, product documentation) supporting the demonstrative. Courts will exclude demonstratives that misrepresent the underlying evidence or are unfaithful to the record’s content. The more a demonstrative simplifies or abstracts technical detail, the more important it is that the simplification not change the substantive meaning.

Prejudice and confusion. Under Rule 403, a demonstrative may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, or misleading the jury. Animated simulations and colorful graphics can be powerful; judges will exclude or limit demonstratives that overstate evidence, use inflammatory imagery, or otherwise distort the jury’s view.

Tutorials for the court. Judges commonly permit tutorials—extended presentations to teach the court technical background. Tutorials are not evidence but help the court and attorneys frame issues for claim construction and trial. Effective tutorials are neutral, comprehensible, and grounded in the disciplines at issue. Parties should avoid using tutorials as surreptitious advocacy; a tutorial’s primary purpose should be education, not persuasion.

Timing and disclosure. Demonstratives are typically exchanged in pretrial disclosures and often subject to motions in limine. Courts require counsel to share demonstratives sufficiently in advance to allow objections and to prevent surprises. When demonstratives contain demonstrable factual assertions or calculations, those parts must be supported by the expert reports and underlying data. Failure to disclose substantive demonstratives may result in preclusion under Rule 37 or exclusion under the FRE.

Use at bench and jury phases. During the bench phase (e.g., claim construction or pretrial Daubert hearings) demonstratives can aid the court’s comprehension. At trial, demonstratives can be used in opening statements and during expert testimony, but counsel must be careful to distinguish argument from demonstrative evidence. Judges regularly require counsel to provide copies of demonstratives ahead of time and may require consent or rulings on their admissibility before use in opening.

# Handling inconsistencies and the effect of self-contradictory testimony

In patent litigation, experts may provide testimony that is inconsistent either across time (e.g., expert report vs. deposition vs. trial testimony), internally within a single report, or in tension with other evidence (including company documents). Handling inconsistencies raises questions of admissibility, impeachment, credibility, and procedural sanctions.

Rule 702 and Daubert implications. An internal contradiction or a clear conflict between an expert’s methodology and the facts can affect admissibility. If an expert’s opinion is rendered unreliable because the expert ignored data that fatally undermines the methodology, the court may exclude the opinion under Rule 702. For example, if an expert claims to rely on testing but the tests, properly interpreted, contradict the expert’s conclusions, the court may find the methodology insufficiently reliable. However, not all inconsistencies warrant exclusion. Many conflicts go to weight, not admissibility; they can be explored on cross-examination and resolved by the trier of fact.

Prior inconsistent statements and impeachment. When an expert’s deposition, report, or earlier statement contradicts later testimony, opposing counsel may use prior inconsistent statements to impeach credibility. Impeachment can be highly effective with juries and may be used to undermine critical linkages in claim charts or the foundations of damages models. Courts permit use of prior inconsistent expert statements at trial, subject to rules governing refreshing recollection, prior inconsistent statements as substantive evidence (depending on jurisdiction and circumstances), and the requirements for preserving objections.

Limitations of impeachment. While impeachment affects credibility, it does not automatically remove an expert’s opinions from evidence. The court will generally not exclude testimony solely because it is contested or because the expert’s conclusions are disputed. Exclusion is more likely when inconsistencies reveal that the opinion is not grounded in any reliable methodology or when the expert consciously manipulates data in a way that undermines the foundations required by Rule 702.

Self-contradictory testimony. Testimony that contradicts itself within a single expert report or between the expert’s report and deposition raises the question whether the opinion is sufficiently reliable. Courts distinguish between: (1) a genuine change in view that is explained and supported by additional analysis, and (2) an unexplained or internally inconsistent opinion that suggests the expert’s methods were not applied reliably. The former may be admissible, perhaps accompanied by an explanation; the latter may justify exclusion.

Procedural remedies for inconsistency. Courts have several procedural tools to address inconsistent or late-changing expert positions. If an expert’s testimony departs materially from an expert report and no supplemental disclosure was made as required by Rule 26(e), the opposing party may move to exclude the new testimony, strike the report, or obtain fees and costs for the extra discovery burden under Rule 37(c)(1). Courts may also permit limited re-deposition, require supplementation, or impose other cures short of exclusion. In extreme cases—where the inconsistency appears to be strategic gamesmanship or a deliberate attempt to mislead—the court may impose sanctions, preclude testimony, or draw adverse inferences.

Dealing with inconsistent company documents. When an expert’s opinion relies on company documents that are themselves inconsistent with the expert’s narrative, the opponent may attack the expert’s use of the documents (selective reliance) and seek to place contradictory documents before the jury. Rule 703 allows reliance on such documents, but the expert must be prepared to explain why some documents are more probative or trustworthy than others. If the discrepancies reveal a methodological defect—such as ignoring contrary data—courts may find the opinion unreliable.

Appellate posture. On appeal, challenges to the admissibility of expert testimony are reviewed deferentially for abuse of discretion. Appellate courts will examine whether the district court engaged in the proper gatekeeping analysis under Rule 702 and Daubert and whether the court’s evidentiary rulings were supported by the record. The fact-intensive nature of the inquiry explains why outcomes vary with procedural posture, the quality of opposing expert work, and the particular facts at hand.

# Practical guidance and concluding points

Preparation and disclosure. Parties preparing expert testimony should ensure that expert reports comply fully with Rule 26(a)(2): opinions should be clearly stated, the bases and reasons disclosed, and the underlying data produced. Experts should document methods carefully, retain raw data, and be transparent about assumptions and limitations. For damages experts, the hypothetical negotiation, selection of comparable licenses, and apportionment reasoning must be explained in sufficient detail to satisfy Daubert scrutiny.

Responding to challenges. When attacking adversary experts, counsel should focus on methodological flaws, lack of fit between methodology and case facts, failure to consider contrary evidence, and unsupported extrapolations. Daubert motions should marshal record evidence showing the expert’s methods are unreliable as applied. Motions in limine can address demonstratives and tutorials that go beyond disclosed opinions.

Trial advocacy. Use demonstratives and tutorials to clarify without overstating. Anticipate and prepare for cross-examination on any assumptions, untested inferences, or selective reliance. Impeachment strategies for prior inconsistent statements are powerful, but counsel should take care to preserve foundation and aims consistent with the rules governing prior statements.

Damage models and apportionment. Damages experts must connect the patent’s value to the patented features and not attribute the whole market value to the patent unless a strong nexus exists (the Entire Market Value Rule’s narrow application). Apportionment is a frequent ground for Daubert exclusions of damages opinions.

Final observation. Expert testimony in patent cases exists at the intersection of law, technology, and economics. Courts exercise a gatekeeping role to ensure that only reliable and relevant expert evidence reaches juries, but they are cautious not to usurp the jury’s role in resolving genuine disputes. Understanding the interplay of Rules 702 and 703, claim-mapping techniques, the use of demonstratives, and the consequences of inconsistencies equips a litigator or student to evaluate, craft, or challenge expert evidence effectively.
