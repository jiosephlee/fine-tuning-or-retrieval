Introduction

Appellate courts do not merely express legal opinions; they affect the substantive and procedural posture of cases by granting specific forms of relief. Understanding the forms of appellate relief—reversal, vacatur, and remand—and the practical consequences each has for liability and damages is essential for effective appellate advocacy and for anticipating the case’s future course. Appellate remedies determine whether a trial court’s ruling remains enforceable, whether factual questions must be reexamined, and whether a party obtains a judgment as a matter of law or is entitled to a new trial. They also frame who ultimately bears the costs of appeal. This chapter explains the governing concepts, standards, and typical doctrinal applications that shape appellate remedies.

# Reversal vs. vacatur vs. remand

The three principal devices appellate courts use to change the trial-court result are reversal, vacatur, and remand. Though related and often used in combination, each has a distinct legal effect.

Reversal is an appellate court’s substantive correction of an error in the trial court’s judgment. When an appellate court reverses, it concludes that the judgment below was legally incorrect. A reversal may be total—setting aside the entire judgment—or partial—setting aside only portions tied to specific issues. Reversal generally changes the parties’ rights as specified by the appellate court: it may order that judgment be entered for an appellant, or it may require further proceedings consistent with the appellate court’s legal conclusions.

Vacatur is an act that nullifies a lower-court order or judgment, rendering it void for future preclusive effect. Vacatur is often sought where the lower-court decision should not stand as precedent or have collateral-estoppel or claim-preclusive consequences—commonly because the case became moot on appeal or the judgment was procured by settlement or procedural irregularity. Vacatur does not itself resolve the merits; it simply erases the judgment’s legal effect as if it had not been rendered. Vacatur is an equitable remedy, so courts apply principles of equity and sound discretion in deciding whether to vacate a judgment.

Remand is the act of sending a case back to the trial court for further proceedings consistent with the appellate court’s disposition. A remand may be ministerial—directly giving effect to appellate findings and instructing the trial court to enter judgment—or it may require additional proceedings, such as a retrial limited to certain issues (e.g., damages), additional fact-finding, or consideration of issues the trial court did not reach in light of new legal guidance. A remand is constrained by the scope of the appellate mandate; the trial court must act consistent with the appellate court’s legal rulings and any instructions about scope and procedure.

The remedies interact. An appellate court may reverse a trial court’s legal ruling, vacate its judgment, and remand for further proceedings. Alternatively, an appellate court may reverse and order entry of judgment as a matter of law for the prevailing party, obviating remand. Whether reversal alone is adequate or whether vacatur and remand are also necessary depends on the nature of the error, the posture of the case, and equitable considerations.

# Effect of reversing liability on damages awards

Liability and damages are analytically distinct but practically entwined. When an appellate court reverses a trial court’s finding of liability, the natural consequence for a damages award is that it cannot stand: damages rest on a subsisting legal basis—liability—and if liability is extinguished, the damages award typically lacks legal support and must be vacated. Conversely, if an appellate court affirms liability but finds error in the damages computation or admission of evidence affecting damages, the court may affirm liability and remit or vacate the damages award either in whole or in part.

There are several common configurations and procedural implications:

- Complete reversal of liability: If the appellate court reverses the judgment that imposed liability, the damages award is ordinarily vacated because there is no underlying obligation to justify damages. The appellate court may then order entry of judgment for the defendant or direct dismissal of claims. The plaintiff’s damages claim cannot survive absent liability in a way that would produce an inconsistent result.

- Reversal of some liability theories, affirmation of others: Where liability was predicated on multiple theories or claims and the appellate court sustains at least one legal basis for liability, the damages award may survive in whole or in part. The key question becomes whether the surviving liability theory supports the damages awarded and whether the record permits allocation among different theories. If the damages award is indivisible among theories that are partially reversed, the appellate court must determine whether to remand for apportionment or to vacate and order a new determination consistent with the surviving liability. Courts often remand when the record does not permit an accurate apportionment.

- Remand for retrial limited to damages: If liability is upheld but errors taint the damages proceedings (for example, improper admission of expert evidence affecting damages, incorrect jury instructions on damages, or erroneous legal standards governing damages), the appellate court may remand for a new trial limited to damages. The remand will generally specify what is to be reconsidered and whether the trial court should receive supplemental instruction.

- Appellate reduction or remittitur: In some systems, an appellate court may reduce an excessive damages award without ordering a new trial by finding the award unsupported by the evidence. Alternatively, the appellate court may offer remittitur—requesting that the plaintiff accept a reduced award or face a new trial on damages. The availability of remittitur and the standards for reduction versus new trial vary by jurisdiction and are constrained by constitutional limits on judicial interference with jury factfinding (e.g., protections against denying a jury’s role absent clear error).

- Compensatory vs. punitive damages: Reversal of liability typically eliminates reckless or punitive-damage awards tied to that liability. But cases may raise separate appellate considerations for punitive damages—constitutional limits, the need for specific findings, or bifurcation rules—so appellate orders may separately address their validity and proper treatment on remand.

Throughout, appellate courts apply standards of review that govern the disposition. Questions of law (e.g., whether a claim is cognizable) are reviewed de novo; factual findings are reviewed for clear error or substantial evidence depending on the context; discretionary rulings (e.g., admission of evidence) for abuse of discretion. Where an appellate court concludes that errors affected the damages outcome, it must decide whether the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt or whether reversal and remand are necessary to ensure a fair determination.

# Scope of remand instructions

A remand is not a blank slate. The appellate mandate defines the parameters of the trial court’s subsequent proceedings, and the trial court must adhere to the appellate court’s legal conclusions and any mandated procedural steps. The scope of remand instructions can be characterized along several dimensions: the substantive issues left for reconsideration, evidentiary and procedural limits, deference to trial-court factfinding, and preservation of appellate rulings.

An appellate court typically specifies whether the remand is for entry of a specific judgment, further factfinding, additional proceedings on limited issues, or a new trial. The language of the mandate and accompanying opinion shapes the trial court’s authority.

- Mandate-driven review: The trial court is bound by the appellate court’s legal rulings. The law of the case doctrine ordinarily prevents relitigation of issues decided on appeal unless exceptional circumstances exist. Thus, on remand, the trial court applies the appellate court’s holding as the governing law and resolves remaining factual questions consistent with that law.

- Limited remand versus plenary remand: A limited remand confines the trial court to a narrow set of issues (e.g., recalculating damages, applying a particular legal standard, or making additional factual findings about causation). A plenary remand permits broader proceedings, possibly including reconsideration of matters not previously addressed. Courts will describe the permitted scope; absent explicit limitation, courts assume the remand allows all proceedings consistent with the appellate disposition, but trial courts should seek clarification when the scope is unclear.

- Remand with specific instructions: Appellate courts sometimes mandate precise procedural actions—for example, to enter judgment for a particular party, to permit a new evidentiary hearing on a specified issue, or to apply a particular legal test. Such instructions bind the trial court and limit its discretion. If the appellate court has directed specific findings, the trial court may implement them without relitigating settled points.

- Factfinding and new evidence: When remand asks for further factfinding, courts must consider whether new evidence can be received. The appellate court may permit new evidence if it is necessary to decide matters left open, but it may also instruct the trial court to proceed with the existing record if further factfinding is not appropriate. If new trials are ordered, rules governing evidence, discovery, and admissibility apply.

- Preservation of issues and procedural posture: The appellate court’s disposition may preserve or foreclose issues on remand. For example, if the appellate court decides a legal question in a party’s favor, the opposing party cannot relitigate that question on remand. However, parties may raise preserved factual or procedural issues that the appellate court did not finally decide. Trial courts must carefully apply the appellate mandate and avoid expansion beyond the appellate court’s holding.

- Time and enforcement: The mandate often contains procedural directives about deadlines for renewed motions, the entry of judgment, and the timing of further proceedings. The trial court must follow these timelines, subject to reasonable motions for extension or clarification addressed to the appellate court when necessary.

In sum, the remand operates under the appellate court’s control. Clear remand instructions minimize uncertainty and sharpen the focus of further proceedings; lack of clarity can generate additional appeals or motions for clarification of the mandate.

# Entry of judgment as a matter of law vs. ordering a new trial

When errors are found in a trial, an appellate court can either direct that judgment be entered in favor of a party as a matter of law or order a new trial. Choosing between these two remedies depends on the nature of the error, the sufficiency of the evidence, and equitable considerations. The procedural posture—whether the appellant preserved appropriate motions at trial—often governs the available relief.

Entry of judgment as a matter of law is appropriate when, taking the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, no reasonable jury could have found for that party on the contested issue(s). In federal practice, this doctrine often appears as the renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law under procedures that require a timely motion at trial (commonly termed a JMOL or motion for directed verdict) and a renewed motion after the verdict to preserve the right to appeal. On appeal, a court applying this standard conducts de novo review of whether the trial record suffices to support the verdict. If the court concludes that reasonable minds could not reach the verdict, it may reverse and render judgment for the movant, thereby short-circuiting the need for retrial and resolving the dispute as a matter of law.

Ordering a new trial is appropriate when the verdict is against the clear weight of the evidence, but the record nevertheless permits reasonable minds to differ, or when trial errors are of such a nature that retrial is necessary to secure a fair proceeding. The new-trial standard differs from JMOL in important respects. A motion for new trial challenges the correctness of the result in light of the whole trial, often invoking errors of procedure, improper admission of evidence, incorrect jury instructions, or verdicts that shock the conscience. The standard of review for an appellate court determining whether to order a new trial frequently gives the trial court broad discretion because the trial judge is in the best position to judge witness credibility and the fairness of proceedings. Appellate courts apply an abuse-of-discretion standard to the trial court’s ruling on new-trial motions. On direct appellate review, some courts will independently evaluate whether a new trial is warranted, but they typically do so with deference to the trial judge’s superior position.

Several procedural rules and practical considerations affect the choice between entry of judgment and a new trial:

- Preservation requirements: Many jurisdictions require parties to move for directed verdict or judgment as a matter of law at trial and to renew such motions after verdict to preserve the right to appellate judgment. Failure to make or renew these motions can limit the appellate remedy to a new trial rather than entry of judgment.

- Sufficiency versus weight of evidence: Entry of judgment resolves sufficiency questions; a new trial addresses the weight of the evidence and procedural fairness. If the appellate court finds the evidence insufficient as a matter of law, rendering judgment is appropriate. If the evidence is sufficient but tainted by error or unfairness, retrial is the usual remedy.

- Harmless error and prejudice: Even if error exists, appellate courts will not order a new trial if the error was harmless—i.e., it did not affect substantial rights or the outcome. The party seeking reversal bears the burden of showing prejudice unless the error is structural, which may warrant automatic reversal.

- Practical and equitable implications: Appellate courts consider whether rendition (entry of judgment) or retrial will serve justice. For example, if key witnesses are no longer available or evidence has materially changed, a retrial may be unfair; yet if the record conclusively favors one party, rendition promotes finality and efficiency. Courts exercise equitable judgment in choosing the remedy.

- Partial retrial: Courts may order a new trial as to specific issues only, preserving other parts of the judgment. For instance, they may order retrial on damages while allowing liability findings to stand. Whether bifurcated retrial is appropriate depends on whether the errors are separable and whether limiting retrial would preserve the parties’ rights.

In practice, appellate courts strive to avoid making factual determinations that should be the province of a jury unless the record makes those determinations inevitable. They also respect trial-court discretion in managing trials and correcting procedural errors. The interplay between standards of review and preservation rules strongly shapes whether the appellate remedy will be rendition or remand for a new trial.

# Allocation of costs on appeal

Appellate litigation generates costs—court fees, transcripts, and attorney’s fees in certain cases—that must be allocated among the parties. The baseline principle is that appellate costs are taxable to the losing party unless a statute, rule, or equitable considerations allocate them otherwise. Specific rules govern what costs are recoverable, how they are taxed, and when appellate courts may award fees.

Taxable costs typically include items listed by rule—filing and docketing fees, costs of reproducing the record or transcript, and certain copying expenses—subject to statutory ceilings and local rules. Appellate courts disallow costs that are not authorized by statute or rule. The procedure for recovering costs usually requires the prevailing party to file a bill of costs within a short time after the mandate issues, and the opposing party may contest specific items.

Attorney’s fees are distinct from taxable costs. Under the American Rule, each party ordinarily bears its own attorney’s fees unless a statute, contract, or equitable doctrine shifts fees. Many federal and state statutes (civil-rights laws, consumer-protection laws, fee-shifting provisions, and contractual fee provisions) authorize prevailing-party attorney’s fees on appeal; courts applying such statutes determine prevailing-party status, calculate reasonable fees using accepted methods (e.g., lodestar multiplied by adjustment factors), and award fees consistent with the statute’s scope (which may be discretionary or mandatory).

Other considerations affecting allocation include:

- Offers of judgment and sanctions: Offer-of-judgment rules can alter cost and fee allocation by shifting costs or fees against a party who rejects a reasonable settlement and fares worse at judgment. Appellate outcomes influence whether the offer was successful in practical effect and thus whether the offer’s cost-shifting provisions apply. Sanctions for frivolous appeals or misconduct also may require payment of costs, fees, or fines.

- Partial success and apportionment: When the appeal yields mixed results, courts must apportion costs and fees equitably. Prevailing party calculations become more complex if a party wins on some claims but loses others; the court must determine whether the party achieved prevailing-party status overall or only with respect to specific claims and allocate costs proportionately.

- Security for costs and supersedeas bonds: A party seeking to stay enforcement of a judgment pending appeal may be required to post a bond, which covers potential costs and damages. The bond’s amount and the requirements for posting relate to the nature of the judgment and jurisdictional rules. If the appellant fails to post adequate security and loses on appeal, the appellee may recover enforcement costs tied to the delay.

- Taxation timeline and enforcement: Costs on appeal are normally taxed and collected after the mandate issues. Parties should observe rules about filing a bill of costs, objections, and motions for review in the trial court. The trial court normally determines taxable costs; appellate courts seldom award costs themselves except when specifically authorized.

- Equity and discretion: Appellate courts retain discretion to depart from strict cost awards in exceptional circumstances. For instance, if an appeal raises important unsettled law, even an unsuccessful appellant might not be taxed heavy costs as a matter of equitable fairness. Conversely, frivolous appeals or those filed for dilatory purposes may result in punitive cost awards.

Understanding the allocation of costs is strategic: it influences settlement decisions, the calculus of whether to appeal, decisions about requesting a stay, and how to frame fee-shifting claims on appeal.

Conclusion

Appellate remedies determine not only whether trial-court error is corrected but also the practical consequences for the parties’ liabilities, damages, and exposure to future litigation. Reversal, vacatur, and remand are distinct tools: reversal corrects legal error and may change outcomes; vacatur removes a judgment’s legal effect; and remand sends matters back with specific instructions. The effect of reversing liability on damages is usually to vacate the damages award unless liability survives on some basis; remand instructions must be followed closely and limit the trial court’s discretion in important ways. The choice between entering judgment as a matter of law and ordering a new trial hinges on evidentiary sufficiency, preservation of motions, and the balance of fairness and finality. Finally, costs on appeal are allocated according to rules and equitable considerations, with taxable costs and attorney’s fees governed by different regimes. Mastery of these doctrines arms the appellate advocate to request the appropriate remedy and to anticipate the implications of appellate relief for the case’s future.
