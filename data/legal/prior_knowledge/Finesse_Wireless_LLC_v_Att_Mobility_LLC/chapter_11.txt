Introduction

Proving patent infringement requires a careful mapping of each asserted claim element to an accused product or process. Technical documentation—engineering diagrams, block charts, schematics, source-code comments, test procedures, and product specifications—often supplies the most direct route to that mapping. But treating such documents as proof demands attention to evidentiary foundations, proper interpretation, and corroboration with objective operational proof. This chapter explains how courts and litigants authenticate and interpret engineering materials, translate the nomenclature used by engineers into claim terms, use mathematical notation and signal labels as evidentiary proof, avoid common misreads of ambiguous legends or labels, and connect design documentation to the device’s actual operation. The objective is to equip you with the doctrinal tools and practical habits necessary to convert technical documents into admissible, persuasive evidence of infringement while anticipating and avoiding common defenses.

# Authenticating and interpreting engineering diagrams and block charts

Authentication and foundational requirements

Before an engineering diagram or block chart can be admitted as evidence, a proponent must satisfy basic authentication rules: show that the document is what the proponent claims it to be. Typical means of authentication in technical cases include testimony from a witness with personal knowledge (the author, an engineer involved in the design, or a custodian of records), metadata and file histories, version-control records, manufacturing or release records that reference the document, and corroborating emails or meeting minutes that discuss the document.

When a document is internal to the accused infringer, a sponsoring witness from that company (preferably the author or a knowledgeable custodian) can establish authorship, date, purpose, and distribution. When an internal witness is unavailable, authentication may rely on a business-records foundation—showing regular practice of maintaining such documents and the trustworthiness of the record-keeping process—or on a combination of metadata (timestamps, file paths, repository logs) and third-party corroboration (e.g., a customer or supplier email referring to the diagram). Be alert to chain-of-custody issues for physical plans and printed diagrams; for digital files, preserve original files and extract metadata in a forensically sound manner.

Interpretation: how courts treat diagrams

Courts treat diagrams and block charts as documentary evidence of what a product was designed to do and how its components are intended to interconnect. But diagrams rarely speak for themselves. A diagram may function as intrinsic evidence of design intent, but it often requires expert testimony to translate engineering conventions for a factfinder. Interpretive tasks include identifying component symbols, tracing signal and data flows, and explaining timing relationships or control logic implied by arrows, dashed lines, or layering in the diagram.

Two important interpretive considerations bear emphasis.

First, diagrams are interpreted in context. That includes the caption, legend, titles on adjacent pages, associated specification text, revision notes, and related documents (data sheets, interface control documents, and source code) that clarify ambiguous symbols. A block labeled “FFT Block,” for example, might be a hardware accelerator, a software library, or a conceptual step in a design flow. Contextual documents help identify which.

Second, diagrams may be conceptual rather than literal. High-level block diagrams often represent functional relationships without specifying physical implementation. When the claim requires a particular structure (e.g., a “hardware module” vs. “software routine” or a distinct memory), you must show that the diagram depicts an implemented component rather than an abstract function. That may require linking the block to fabrication drawings, bill-of-materials, firmware images, or test logs demonstrating the block’s physical instantiation.

Best practices for preparing diagrams for trial

When relying on diagrams, litigators and testifying experts should prepare a clear evidentiary chain: identify the specific version and date; obtain witness foundation for authorship and intended meaning; prepare a demonstrative that highlights the parts corresponding to claim elements; and assemble corroborating documents to address any ambiguities. When possible, preserve native files to retain layers and callouts that may explain relationships obscured in flattened images.

# Correlating nomenclature in documents to claim terms

The gap between an engineer’s language and a patent claim’s terms is a frequent battleground. Engineers use product-specific jargon, acronyms, and internal names; patent claims use legal terms of art formed during prosecution and claim construction. Bridging that gap requires methodical correlation, grounded in claim construction and the intrinsic record, supplemented by extrinsic evidence as needed.

Start with claim construction

Before mapping, you must know the judicially construed meaning of each claim term. Claim construction defines the universe of admissible mappings by identifying the precise legal elements that must be proved. A term construed to require a specific structure, result, or function constrains how nomenclature in documents can be correlated. For example, if a claim term is construed as a “single integrated processor” rather than “any processor or processor cluster,” internal documents that use the plural “CPUs” will require additional proof to show they function as a single integrated processor.

Direct naming and labeling

The most straightforward mapping occurs when an internal document uses the same or synonymous term as the claim. A specification sheet labeled “signal comparator” can be matched to a claim element “comparator” if the document’s description and context show the same functionality. But beware literal name matches that mislead: engineers sometimes use marketing labels that don’t reflect the technical function relevant to the claim. Corroboration is necessary to ensure the named component actually performs the claimed operation.

Functional equivalence and translation of jargon

Engineers often describe a claim element using different words. To bridge this, the proponent must show functional equivalence: that the component described in the document performs the same role or achieves the same result specified by the claim. Demonstrate this by parsing the document’s description of inputs, outputs, timing, and algorithms and mapping them onto the claim’s required limitations. When a claim uses functional language (e.g., “means for doing X” or a functional limitation under applicable statutory provisions), showing that the disclosed component performs the stated function and corresponds to a structure known in the art is critical.

Interpreting acronyms and versioned names

Internal artifacts change names across revisions. Always trace nomenclature across document versions and repositories. An acronym introduced in a design overview should be defined somewhere; if it is not, search related documents and commit logs for the earliest introduction and definitions. Preserve an evidentiary trail that connects the acronym to the technical function and, ultimately, the claim term. If a term is ambiguous, use other artifacts—source code identifiers, test case names, email threads—to demonstrate consistent usage linking the engineer’s term to the claimed concept.

Handling means-plus-function and structural claims

When claims are in means-plus-function format or otherwise specify structure, the mapping must focus on corresponding structure disclosed in the patent or known equivalents. Correlate internal structure names (component identifiers, module numbers, part numbers) to the structure identified or required by claim construction. Where a claim recites a specific algorithm (e.g., a control law), the correspondence must show that the internal component implements that algorithm or its equivalent. Documentation that merely states an intention to implement a function without providing structure is weak proof unless complemented by implementation artifacts.

Burdens and the role of expert witnesses

The patentee bears the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence. Expert witnesses play a central role in translating nomenclature and showing technical equivalence. Expert testimony should explain the engineer’s language, map signals and components to claim limitations, and reference other documents and testing that corroborate the mapping in terms that a factfinder can understand. Experts must avoid conveying mere ipse dixit; their opinions should be grounded in demonstrable document trails, reproducible tests, and standard engineering practices.

# Mathematical notation and signal labels as evidentiary proof

Mathematical formulas, parameter tables, and signal labels frequently appear in technical docs and can powerfully show that an accused product meets quantitative claim limitations. They must, however, be tied to measurable quantities and interpreted reliably.

Mathematical notation: what it proves and the foundation required

A formula in a design document that defines a threshold, filter coefficient, or timing relationship can, if authenticated and properly interpreted, provide direct evidence that a system satisfies a numerical claim limitation (e.g., “less than 10 ms,” “attenuation of at least 30 dB,” or a particular mathematical transformation). To be probative, the proponent should show:

- The formula’s provenance and applicability: that the formula governs the accused product’s implementation and is not merely a theoretical note or an offhand calculation.
- The units and constants: mathematical expressions are meaningless without units and defined variables. A symbol such as $T$ must be tied to a measurable time interval, and constants (e.g., sampling rate $f_s$) must be shown by document or measurement.
- Tolerances and implementation approximations: engineering practice often uses approximations. Explain how approximations affect whether the claimed threshold is met—e.g., whether rounding or component tolerances could defeat a literal numerical limitation.

Signal labels and waveform evidence

Signal names and labels on schematics (e.g., “CLK_OUT,” “DATA_IN[7:0],” “reset_n”), together with timing diagrams and annotated waveforms, can demonstrate how signals propagate and whether particular asserted timing or logical relationships are present. To use such evidence:

- Show the mapping from the signal label to the physical pin, net, or variable in firmware/source code.
- Produce timing diagrams or logic analyzer captures from an actual device showing the labeled signals behaving as the documentation predicts.
- Explain how signal naming conventions correspond to functionality rather than merely indicating design intent; corroborate with test logs or source-level identifiers.

Admissibility and hearsay concerns

Mathematical calculations or tabulated values created by engineers are admissible as business records if the foundation is satisfied, but they may also be hearsay if introduced to prove the truth of the matter asserted without a proper exception. Often the safest route is to have an expert adopt the calculation, re-create the computation independently, and explain the methodology and its relevance. Alternatively, the author of the calculations may testify and be cross-examined about assumptions and methodology.

Using simulations and derived calculations

When equations in documentation are used to derive a claim-limiting result (for example, showing that a design achieves a certain signal-to-noise ratio), supporting evidence should include input parameters drawn from authenticated documents and either lab measurements or a reproducible simulation. Document the simulation environment, seed inputs, and the toolchain used so opposing experts can replicate results. Where the formula depends on idealized conditions, be prepared to address how real-world non-idealities affect compliance with the claim limitation.

# Avoiding misreads and clarifying ambiguous legends or labels

Ambiguities in legends, labels, or diagram conventions are common sources of dispute. The proponent must anticipate likely alternate readings and eliminate them through careful corroboration.

Common sources of misreads

Ambiguity often arises from undefined abbreviations, generic boxes in block diagrams, dashed versus solid lines, unlabeled arrows, overlapping layers in multi-sheet designs, and revisions with inconsistent labeling. Draft annotations, redline comments, or placeholders (e.g., “TODO: define interface”) may be mistaken for final specification.

Strategies to disambiguate

1. Seek contemporaneous defining artifacts. Look for documents that define the contested label—design requirement documents, interface control documents, data sheets, codebase comments, or meeting minutes where the term was introduced and explained. A contemporaneous definition is stronger than a later explanation.

2. Use the author or a custodian to clarify. If available, obtain a witness who participated in creating or using the document to explain the intended meaning and whether the document is a schematic of an as-built structure or a conceptual sketch.

3. Cross-check multiple documents. Corroborate the meaning through other artifacts: part numbers in the bill of materials, schematic netlists, firmware variables, test names, and produced hardware. Inconsistencies between documents require explanation; courts are more likely to credit an interpretation that explains all materials coherently.

4. Avoid reliance on a single ambiguous figure. If an argument rests solely on one unclear legend, opposing counsel will exploit the ambiguity. Strengthen the case by combining the diagram with operational evidence (measurements, logs), code that references the label, or physical inspection.

5. Explain conventional meanings. Where a symbol follows standard engineering conventions (for example, a grounded triangle indicating common reference or a dashed arrow denoting optional connection), an expert can explain the conventional reading. This is persuasive, but beware of situations where the designer intentionally repurposed symbols.

Handling revisions and conflicting versions

Design documentation evolves. When confronted with multiple versions that use different labels or legends, map the evolution: identify which version was implemented, which versions were draft or superseded, and whether changes were cosmetic or substantive. Present a timeline supported by metadata and release notes to show which terminology governed the released product. Courts will generally credit the version tied to production and release, not an early conceptual sketch.

Dealing with redactions and redlines

Redactions and redlines complicate interpretation. Preserve copies before redaction where possible; otherwise ensure the unredacted portions suffice to make the mapping. If redactions hide critical definitions, seek discovery or judicial intervention; but be prepared to show that sufficient corroborating evidence exists despite the gaps.

# Linking documents to actual operation, not just design goals

Documents are more persuasive when tied to proof of actual operation. Proof of infringement requires showing how the accused product actually functions in practice, not merely how designers intended it to function.

Design intent versus usage

Design documentation often states goals or nominal operation. Intentional or unintentional deviations can occur in manufacturing, firmware updates, or configurational options. Courts distinguish between what the designers planned and what the shipped product does. Thus, documentation showing an intended feature is helpful but typically insufficient alone. The patentee should tie documentation to as-built evidence.

Connecting documentation to implementation

There are several classes of evidence that bridge from design documents to actual operation:

- Source code and firmware: Comments, variable names, and function modules that implement the design are direct evidence. Demonstrate how code implements the claimed function, and where possible, produce build artifacts showing the code version in the deployed firmware.

- Manufacturing artifacts: Bill of materials, assembly instructions, configuration files, and release notes show that specific components and firmware were assembled and shipped.

- Test reports and quality-control logs: Factory test logs, in-circuit test outputs, and final test reports show that the shipped unit exhibited the behavior described in the documentation.

- Field logs and telemetry: Devices that phone home or store logs can produce real-world evidence of signals, events, or state changes corresponding to claim elements.

- Physical inspection and measurements: Lab testing (component-level and system-level measurements) validates performance claims and timing characteristics. Use calibrated instruments and document the test setup to preclude methodological attacks.

- Demonstrations and reproductions: Controlled reproductions of the accused process or demonstrations of a unit operating under representative conditions can connect the documentation to operation. Preserve and authenticate the demo recordings and test conditions.

Addressing configurability and optional features

Many products have optional features, modes, or configurations that can enable or disable functionality. To prove infringement, show that (1) the feature is enabled in the accused product as shipped, (2) it is enabled by default, (3) it can be easily enabled by the user, or (4) enabling it is inevitable or foreseeable in normal use. Documentation that only shows a feature is possible but turned off by default is weaker; confirm whether firmware updates or user action are required and whether those actions were taken.

Temporal and conditional behavior

Claims may require behavior under particular conditions (e.g., “upon detection of event E, the system performs X”). Documents may state conditional flows; but to prove infringement you need operational proof that those conditions obtain and cause the claimed response. Combine logs capturing the triggering event and the subsequent response with diagrams showing the control flow.

Doctrinal limits: cannot prove infringement by intent alone

A design goal or marketing promise does not, by itself, prove that the product performed as claimed. Consequently, while documents showing intent can inform the reasonable inferences a factfinder may draw, they must be buttressed by operational evidence to carry the preponderance burden. Similarly, for doctrines that depend on equivalence rather than literal structure, corporate intent may be relevant but not dispositive; the expert analysis must show technical equivalence in structure or function.

# Procedural posture and strategic considerations

How documents are used varies with procedural posture—claim construction, summary judgment, or trial.

- Claim construction stage: Documents may be offered as intrinsic or extrinsic evidence to inform claim meaning, but they must be carefully framed so that they do not purport to prove infringement. Experts may explain technical usage of terms in the art but should avoid opining on infringement before construction.

- Summary judgment: At summary judgment, documentary evidence must create genuine disputes of material fact or, conversely, show lack of disputed facts. Documents that demonstrate literal correspondence between claim elements and product components can support or defeat summary judgment, but the foundation for the documents and an expert interpretation are often necessary to show that reasonable jurors could not disagree.

- Trial: At trial, the proponent must produce authenticated documents, supporting witness testimony, demonstratives, and corroborating operational proof to persuade the factfinder. Be prepared for Daubert-style challenges to expert methodology and for cross-examination on document provenance and meaning.

Privileged or protected materials

Engineers’ notes, internal deliberations, and pre-decisional documents may be subject to privilege or protection. When assembling technical documentation, ensure proper waiver analysis and consider whether redaction, privilege logs, or court-ordered in camera review are necessary. Also plan for protective orders governing highly technical trade secrets.

Practical checklist for litigators and experts

- Preserve native files with metadata; obtain version histories and release notes.
- Identify authors and custodians early; seek deposition testimony explaining document meaning.
- Corroborate ambiguous labels with code, tests, BOMs, and logs.
- Recreate mathematical calculations independently; document simulations and instrument calibration.
- Produce demonstrative evidence that traces document elements to physical pins, firmware routines, or test points.
- Address alternative explanations: be ready to show why alternate readings of a diagram are unlikely or inconsistent with other evidence.
- Anticipate and preempt hearsay or authentication objections by having authors testify or by qualifying documents as business records where appropriate.
- Keep in mind claim construction constraints; avoid mapping that conflicts with a court’s construction.

Conclusion

Technical documentation is indispensable in patent infringement litigation, but it is not self-proving. Effective use of diagrams, nomenclature, equations, and signal labels requires careful authentication, rigorous interpretation, corroboration with implementation evidence, and an awareness of procedural and evidentiary pitfalls. By methodically linking documents to actual operation and by preparing experts to explain the technical record in legally cognizable terms, litigators can convert engineering artifacts into the convincing, admissible proof needed to establish infringement.
