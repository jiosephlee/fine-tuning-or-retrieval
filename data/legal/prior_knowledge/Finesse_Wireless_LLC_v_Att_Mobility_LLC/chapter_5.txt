Introduction

Claim construction is the process by which a court determines the legal meaning and scope of the language used in a patent's claims. It is a threshold and indispensable step for nearly every substantive dispute that follows: whether an accused product or method infringes, whether claims are anticipated or obvious, and whether they are indefinite. The task is interpretive and hybrid in character: courts apply principles of statutory-like construction to technical language and often resolve contested factual disputes about how a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA) would understand the claim terms. Because claim construction narrows the universe of permissible factual disputes at trial and frames the legal issues on appeal, understanding its doctrinal mechanics is essential before reading any opinion that turns on claim meaning.

This chapter explains the core tools courts use when construing claims, the limits on outside evidence, the procedural vehicle for resolving disputes (Markman hearings), the role of subsidiary factual findings in construction and their appellate review, and special considerations for structural versus functional claim language, including clauses introduced by “wherein.” The exposition gives emphasis to legal standards, the typical order of analysis, burdens of proof, and how courts translate evidence into interpretive outcomes.

# Phillips hierarchy: claim language, specification, prosecution history

When courts construe claims they do so within a hierarchy of intrinsic evidence: first and foremost the claim language itself; next the specification (the written description and drawings); and finally the prosecution history (the file wrapper). This hierarchy governs the relative weight of these sources, and courts move among them in order to resolve ambiguity or confirm a plain meaning.

The claim language. The claims define the metes and bounds of the invention and therefore receive primary attention. Courts begin with the claim as written because the text is the most direct indicator of the patentee’s public notice. Ordinary words are generally given their ordinary and customary meaning, which is the meaning that a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA) would ascribe to the claim term at the time of the invention. Courts focus on how the claim term is used in context—both within the claim that contains it and across the other claims, because claim sets form an integrated claim language (e.g., a term used consistently in dependent and independent claims will be read consistently unless there is a clear reason to treat usages differently).

The specification. If claim language is ambiguous or admits multiple plausible meanings, courts consult the specification to resolve that ambiguity. The specification is particularly important for two reasons. First, it is the inventor’s own description of the invention and so provides reliable evidence about how the inventor conceived of claim terms and what embodiments were intended to fall within the claims. Second, the specification may provide definitions, examples, or structures that clarify functional limitations.

However, the specification cannot be used to rewrite the claim or to act as a dictionary that limits claim terms to preferred embodiments unless the patentee clearly intended to do so. Two corollaries follow. If the specification defines a term explicitly—either expressly or by clear implication—courts will typically adopt that definition. Conversely, if the specification merely describes a preferred embodiment without clearly disclaiming other possible embodiments, courts should avoid reading a limitation into the claims that would exclude other embodiments described in ordinary meaning.

The prosecution history. The file history of the patent application provides objective evidence of what the applicant said to obtain allowance, including amendments, arguments, and responses to examiner rejections. Prosecution history can be used to confirm a construction suggested by the claims and specification or to disavow claim scope where the patentee made clear and unmistakable statements limiting the claims to obtain allowance. Because the prosecution history is a written record of negotiations with the Patent Office, it is less reliable than the specification as evidence of the inventor’s technical understanding (it may be shaped by strategic bargaining). Courts therefore use it last in the intrinsic-evidence hierarchy, giving it weight when it contains clear statements bearing on the meaning of disputed claim language.

Interplay and canons. The court’s task is to determine the meaning that a POSITA would ascribe in light of all intrinsic evidence. That requires balancing canons of interpretation: give ordinary meaning to words; read claims in light of the specification but not to import limitations; use prosecution history to illuminate, not to enlarge or shrink the claims beyond what the claim language reasonably supports. Procedurally, the judge explains why intrinsic evidence supports the chosen construction and why alternative readings are inconsistent with that intrinsic record.

# Role and limits of extrinsic evidence (e.g., inventor testimony, textbooks)

Extrinsic evidence consists of materials external to the patent document and its prosecution file: expert testimony about technical meaning, inventor declarations or testimony, treatises and textbooks, dictionaries, industry standards, and demonstrative exhibits. Courts may consider extrinsic evidence to aid the court’s understanding of the technology and to confirm the meaning given by intrinsic evidence; but extrinsic evidence is subordinate to intrinsic evidence and cannot be used to contradict or override the intrinsic record.

Permitted uses. Extrinsic evidence is useful for background, context, and clarification. Expert declarations can explain how a POSITA would understand technical terms at the relevant time, provide definitions used in the field, and translate complex technologies for a court. Technical treatises and dictionaries can corroborate a particular meaning if consistent with the patent’s internal evidence. Inventor testimony may be admissible to explain technical aspects of the inventions, demonstrate what the inventor considered the invention to be, and to shed light on ambiguous claim language.

Limits and restrictions. Courts routinely limit extrinsic evidence in several ways. First, extrinsic evidence cannot be used to contradict an unambiguous claim meaning that is supported by the specification and prosecution history. Second, dictionaries and treatises are given only as much weight as they deserve; dictionary definitions are not dispositive if inconsistent with the intrinsic record. Third, inventor testimony is treated cautiously: testimony by the patentee or inventors about claim meaning can be self-serving, and courts will evaluate such testimony in light of the intrinsic record. Courts are wary of allowing inventors to expand or narrow claim scope through after-the-fact testimony that conflicts with the written disclosure or the prosecution record.

Standards for evaluating expert evidence. A court will assess extrinsic expert testimony for reliability and relevance. Reliable expert testimony explains how a POSITA would read particular claim terms at the time of invention and grounds its opinions in recognized technical sources and methodologies. Courts may exclude or discount expert testimony that amounts to ipse dixit, that relies on definitions contrary to the specification, or that postdates the invention and thus does not reflect the contemporary understanding.

Practical takeaway. When building a claim construction record, parties should prioritize intrinsic evidence and use extrinsic evidence for context and corroboration. Inventor testimony and dictionaries are persuasive only to the extent they align with the specification and prosecution history. A successful construction argument demonstrates how extrinsic materials fit into and reinforce the intrinsic record rather than trying to replace it.

# Markman hearings and timing relative to trial

A Markman hearing (commonly called a claim construction hearing) is the judicial proceeding in which parties present arguments and evidence about disputed claim terms and the court issues constructions. The hearing is a procedural mechanism by which the court resolves claim meaning before or during trial, because claim construction significantly narrows the issues remaining for the finder of fact.

Purpose and structure. The markman hearing’s core purpose is to allow the court to determine the legal meaning of claim terms as a matter of law. The hearing typically involves written briefing, submission of intrinsic and extrinsic evidence (e.g., patents, specification excerpts, prosecution history, expert declarations, dictionaries), and oral argument. Parties may present testimony and cross-examination on factual matters relevant to construction, though many courts decide constructions from the papers and live argument without calling witnesses.

Timing relative to trial. Claim construction often occurs before the jury trial during pretrial proceedings because it shapes the claim scope that the jury must apply in infringement and invalidity findings. Early construction promotes efficiency by resolving determinative legal issues and focusing discovery and trial. That said, construction can also be raised or refined during trial in response to newly developed factual record, and courts retain discretion to revisit a prior construction if material new evidence emerges.

Interlocutory nature and preclusion. Because claim construction is an issue of law (with subsidiary factual components), the court’s construction is binding on the parties for purposes of the litigation, subject to revision only in limited circumstances. Judges generally strive for final constructions before trial to avoid confusion, but they also possess flexibility to amend constructions when necessary to prevent manifest injustice.

Procedural posture and strategic implications. The moment of construction affects litigation strategy. Parties prefer early claim construction to clarify litigation risks and to shape settlement expectations. Conversely, the party asserting infringement may seek to postpone construction until the factual record is better developed to provide context for disputed terms. Practically, claim construction deadlines are often set in the case scheduling order, and aggressive parties use claim construction disputes to obtain early leverage (e.g., by seeking summary judgment of noninfringement based on a narrow construction).

# Teva: factual underpinnings of claim construction and appellate review

Claim construction is typically described as a question of law, but many constructions depend on subsidiary factual findings—such as what a POSITA would have understood a term to mean, whether a term has a well-established definition in the art, or whether the specification clearly disavowed a particular meaning. Handling these factual matters has important appellate implications: appellate courts review legal conclusions de novo but review factual findings under a deferential clear-error standard.

Subsidiary factual findings. When a district court resolves an issue like the meaning of a term in light of expert testimony or determines whether a patentee unambiguously disavowed claim scope during prosecution, the court is making factual determinations. Examples include assessments of credibility (is the inventor’s testimony believable?), determinations about the level of skill in the art at the relevant time, or findings about whether a technical term had a special meaning in the art.

Effect on appellate review. Because claim construction rests on mixed fact-law questions, appellate courts apply a dual standard. Pure legal determinations—such as the application of canons of claim interpretation or the ultimate claim construction when it follows from undisputed facts—are reviewed de novo. Numerical or subsidiary fact determinations (e.g., what a POSITA would have understood) are reviewed for clear error. If a district court’s factual finding is plausible in light of the record, a court of appeals must defer and cannot overturn it absent a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made.

Practical consequences. Litigants should appreciate that developing a strong factual record at the district court level matters. If claim construction depends on factual inputs, those findings will likely survive appellate scrutiny unless they are clearly erroneous. Accordingly, parties should present comprehensive, credible evidence (declarations, deposition testimony, contemporaneous technical literature) during the Markman proceeding to support favorable factual findings. Conversely, a party who seeks to preserve de novo appellate review will try to frame disputes as legal questions amenable to pure legal analysis and, where possible, limit the need for subsidiary factual findings.

Standard-of-proof and notice. The court’s role in distinguishing law from fact also affects burdens. For instance, if a party argues that a claim term is indefinite because it lacks clear meaning, that is often a legal determination informed by facts; the party challenging indefiniteness must present persuasive evidence. Courts will explain the basis of their factual findings in the claim construction order to facilitate proper appellate review.

# Construing structural vs. functional language; ‘wherein’ clauses

Claim language can be structural (defining parts, elements, and their relationships) or functional (defining an element by what it does rather than what it is). Different rules and statutory provisions apply to these categories, and courts treat “wherein” clauses—which often express functional limitations or describe relationships—carefully to decide whether they limit claim scope or merely state use or result.

Structural language. Structural claim language recites tangible parts, their arrangement, and physical interconnections. Such claims are generally construed by reference to the structure disclosed in the specification and, when relevant, the ordinary meaning those structures have in the art. When a claim recites a specific structure, courts look for corresponding description in the specification to determine whether the patentee intended that structure broadly or in a narrow sense.

Functional language and means-plus-function. Functional language describes what an element does. Under certain statutory provisions, most prominently 35 U.S.C. 112(f) (formerly 112 ¶6), claim language expressed as a “means for” performing a specified function is treated as a “means-plus-function” limitation. When a claim invokes the means-plus-function format, the claim element is construed to cover the structure, material, or acts described in the specification as corresponding to that function, and equivalents thereof. If the specification fails to disclose sufficient corresponding structure, the limitation may be indefinite.

Identifying means-plus-function. The presence of “means for” language creates a presumption that §112(f) applies, whereas the absence of the literal phrase can create dispute. Courts analyze whether the claim term recites sufficiently definite structure or whether it is merely a functional description without structural details. If a claim uses functional language but does not employ the “means for” phrasing, §112(f) may still apply if the claim term fails to recite structure and the element is otherwise purely functional. The key inquiry is whether a person of ordinary skill would recognize the term as denoting structure or merely a function.

Consequences of functional claiming. When an element is construed under means-plus-function, the patentee is limited to the structure disclosed in the specification and equivalents—this can significantly narrow claim scope. For elements expressed functionally but not captured under §112(f), courts nonetheless attempt to give content to the term by reference to the specification and common meaning in the art. Courts are cautious not to read functional language so broadly as to encompass the entire principle of operation; that risk raises issues of overbreadth and potential indefiniteness.

“Wherein” clauses. “Wherein” clauses often appear in dependent and independent claims to link components or to state a relationship or result achieved by the claimed combination. Courts ask whether a “wherein” clause imposes a limiting structural or functional requirement on the claim element or whether it merely describes an intended use or optional result.

Limiting versus non-limiting language. Courts look to the claim drafter’s intent as revealed in the claim set and specification to determine whether a “wherein” clause is limiting. If the clause provides a necessary structural or operational constraint (e.g., a specific relationship among parts required for the claimed invention to function), it will be treated as a limitation. By contrast, if the clause merely states an intended benefit, result, or preferred operation without narrowing the claimed elements, the court may read it as non-limiting.

Antecedent basis and clarity. A common source of dispute with “wherein” clauses is lack of clear antecedent basis (i.e., the clause uses pronouns or refers to elements not introduced earlier). Proper claim drafting provides clear antecedent references and language tying the “wherein” clause to previously recited elements. A court may construe an ambiguous “wherein” clause against the drafter under the doctrine of claim ambiguity, potentially finding indefiniteness if the clause renders the scope unclear.

Interplay with functional limitations. Because “wherein” clauses frequently express relationships or results, they can functionally limit the claim. If a “wherein” clause results in a new functional requirement that lacks disclosed corresponding structure, the clause may raise §112 issues. Alternatively, a “wherein” clause that sets forth a functional relationship but that is supported by detailed disclosure in the specification can be an effective way to define claim scope.

Practical drafting and litigating implications. Drafters should be careful when using functional language and “wherein” clauses: provide sufficient structural detail in the specification for any functional recitation and ensure clear antecedent basis. Litigators should probe whether a contested functional term invokes §112(f), whether the specification supplies sufficient corresponding structure, and whether a “wherein” clause imposes a real limitation. During claim construction, parties should explain whether a disputed functional limitation requires a structural correlate and, if so, point to specific portions of the specification that qualify.

Conclusion

Claim construction is both a legal and evidentiary exercise grounded in the intrinsic record, supplemented where necessary by extrinsic evidence. The interpretive hierarchy—claims first, specification second, prosecution history third—frames the analysis, and extrinsic sources play a subordinate but useful role. Procedurally, Markman hearings resolve claim meaning and shape the remainder of the litigation, and factual findings made in support of construction receive deferential appellate review. Special care is required when claims use functional language, means-plus-function formats, or “wherein” clauses, because these features can dramatically affect scope and potential indefiniteness. For the law student preparing to read judicial decisions, a solid command of these fundamentals will illuminate how courts translate technical language into adjudicable legal rules.
