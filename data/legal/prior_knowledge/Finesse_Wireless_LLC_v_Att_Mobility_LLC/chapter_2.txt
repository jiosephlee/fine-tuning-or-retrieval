Introduction

Standards of review are the scaffolding of appellate decisionmaking. They determine how an appellate court treats the district court’s factual findings, legal conclusions, discretionary rulings, and jury verdicts. In patent law, where mixed questions of law and fact are routine (infringement and invalidity often turn on technical facts and claim meaning), understanding which standard governs a given ruling is essential both to preserving issues at trial and to framing appellate briefs. This chapter surveys the principal standards of review that structure patent appeals: de novo review of judgments as a matter of law (JMOL) under regional circuit law, substantial-evidence review of jury findings on infringement and damages, the hybrid Teva-style framework for claim construction (legal review with clear-error review of subsidiary facts), abuse-of-discretion review for new-trial orders, and harmless error principles that limit reversal even when mistakes occurred. For each standard the chapter explains the legal definition, who bears the burdens, how courts apply the rule to patent themes (infringement, invalidity, damages, claim construction), and how the procedural posture (motions, timing, preservation) affects appellate analysis.

# De novo review of JMOL decisions under regional circuit law

A judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) challenges the sufficiency of the opponent’s evidentiary showing. In practice, JMOL is typically a two-step process: the defendant (or non-moving party) moves for JMOL at the close of the plaintiff’s case or at the close of all evidence in the district court; if the motion is denied and a jury returns a verdict, the losing party may renew the JMOL motion (often called a renewed JMOL). On appeal the reviewing court asks whether the district court properly granted or denied the motion based on the sufficiency of the evidence.

When appellate review is said to be de novo for JMOL decisions, the appellate court gives no deference to the district court’s legal conclusion about sufficiency; it independently decides whether the record contains legally sufficient evidence to sustain the verdict. But the precise formulation of de novo sufficiency review is shaped by evidentiary presumptions and the applicable regional procedural law. In some procedural contexts the relevant regional circuit’s precedent governs the standard of review. That matters in patent appeals because the Federal Circuit’s procedural law on certain topics (like JMOL) historically has sometimes yielded to the law of the regional circuit that would have had jurisdiction but for the specialized appellate route. The practical consequence is that counsel must be alert both to the controlling standard of review and to the way that standard is articulated in that regional circuit’s precedents.

What de novo sufficiency review typically means in practice

- Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prevailing party. Even under de novo review, appellate courts generally do not reweigh evidence or judge witness credibility. The court accepts the jury’s credibility determinations and draws all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party. The question is whether, on that favorable view, a reasonable jury could have found for the verdict winner.
- No deference to the district court’s ultimate legal determination. The appellate court independently decides whether the legal standard for sufficiency has been met.
- Legal articulation: the reviewing court often asks whether there is “substantial evidence” (or “legally sufficient evidence”) supporting the verdict. When regional circuit law governs, the appellate court applies that circuit’s formulation of “substantial” or “sufficiency” review; the result is typically similar but nuanced differences in phrasing or burden allocation can matter. For example, some circuits emphasize that the evidence must be such that reasonable jurors could fairly conclude the disputed fact, while others articulate a slightly different test; these differences can influence the outcome in close cases.

Special considerations in patent contexts

- Technical fact disputes. JMOL in patent cases often turns on complex technical testimony about infringement (e.g., whether an accused device includes each claim limitation) or about damages (e.g., causation and reasonable royalty calculations). De novo review does not permit the appellate court to substitute its own factual determinations for the jury’s reasonable inferences from expert testimony, but it does permit reevaluation of whether any reasonable jury could have drawn the necessary inferences.
- Claim construction interplay. Because claim construction can be a legal determination, it may be decided de novo by the appellate court and then used as a fixed premise for JMOL review. When claim construction rests on subsidiary factual findings, the standard interplay with JMOL becomes more complex (see the Teva framework below).
- Motion timing and preservation. Appellate courts expect proper preservation of JMOL issues: a party typically must move for JMOL in the district court before the case is submitted to the jury and must renew the motion after verdict to preserve the issue for appeal. Failure to preserve can limit appellate relief and may shift review to plain-error standards.

# Substantial evidence review for jury findings of infringement and damages

Substantial evidence review is the appellate standard applied to factual findings by a jury or by the district court sitting as factfinder when those factual findings are challenged on appeal. The standard asks whether a reasonable mind might accept the evidence as adequate to support the conclusion reached. It is a deferential standard: it does not permit reweighing of evidence, nor does it permit overturning credibility determinations made by the trier of fact.

Core definition and mechanics

- The inquiry. The appellate court determines whether the record contains such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support the jury’s factual finding. If so, the finding stands; if not, the appellate court may reverse or remand.
- Scope. Substantial evidence review looks only at the evidence that supports the verdict; it does not consider evidence favorable to the unsuccessful party that the jury reasonably could have rejected.
- Inferences. The court must respect reasonable inferences drawn by the jury; it does not supplant those inferences with alternative inferences more compelling to the appellate court.
- Credibility. Determinations about witness credibility are for the trier of fact; appellate courts do not reassess credibility under the substantial-evidence standard.

Application to infringement findings

- Element-by-element. Infringement findings require that the accused product or process meets every limitation of the asserted claims. Where the jury finds infringement, an appellant challenging that finding must show that no reasonable jury could have found the necessary fact or inference for one or more claim elements.
- Ultimate facts and mixed questions. Some infringement questions are mixed law-and-fact (e.g., whether the properly construed claim, applied to the accused device, is met). When claim construction is treated as a legal issue, that construction is fixed for the factual sufficiency calculus. If claim construction involves subsidiary facts (for example, the import of extrinsic evidence), those underlying facts will be evaluated for clear error while the ultimate infringement application may still be reviewed for substantial evidence.
- Expert testimony. Much of infringement proof uses expert testimony. Appellate courts applying substantial-evidence review will assess whether the expert’s testimony, combined with demonstrative evidence and other record materials, suffices for a reasonable jury to conclude infringement. Cross-examination and contrary expert testimony are grounds for the jury to reject an expert, and appellate courts defer to that jury choice.

Application to damages findings

- Causation and apportionment. Jury awards of damages require proof of causation—linking the accused product’s use of the patented invention to the harm—and proper apportionment between patented and unpatented features. Appellate review asks whether reasonable jurors had sufficient evidence to find both the amount and the causal relationship.
- Reasonable royalty analysis. Reasonable royalty determinations rely on hypothetical negotiations, comparable licenses, and expert economic analysis. Substantial-evidence review asks whether the record offers reliable, relevant support for the jury’s royalty figure, recognizing that there can be a range of reasonable outcomes and that jurors may choose any figure within that range.
- Jury instructions and legal gateway issues. If legal error (for example, an erroneous instruction defining the damages concept) infected the jury’s damages determination, the appellate court addresses that legal error independently; but the ultimate question of whether record evidence supports an award is one of substantial evidence.

Practical consequences

- Appeal strategy. Under the substantial-evidence standard, an appellant must identify specific gaps in the record rather than urging a wholesale reweighing of evidence. Demonstrating that a crucial fact rests on speculative inferences, or that the necessary evidentiary predicate is absent, is the path to reversal.
- Jury verdicts often stand. Because of its deference, the substantial-evidence standard makes it harder to overturn jury findings than to overturn purely legal rulings.

# De novo review of claim construction with clear-error review for subsidiary fact findings (Teva framework)

Claim construction historically sat at the intersection of law and fact. The modern controlling framework treats claim construction as ultimately a question of law subject to de novo review, but it recognizes that district courts may make subsidiary factual findings in the course of construing claims (for example, findings about the meaning of technical terms to a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA) or about the content of extrinsic evidence). Those subsidiary factual findings are reviewed for clear error; the court’s ultimate legal construction is reviewed de novo.

Structure of the two-tiered inquiry

- The legal question. The core question—what the claim means and how it applies to the accused device—is a matter of law. That legal component is reviewed de novo on appeal. De novo review means the appellate court independently construes the claim language, prosecution history, and intrinsic record to determine meaning.
- Subsidiary factual findings. In the process of construction, district courts may resolve factual disputes: e.g., which definition of a technical term is supported by expert extrinsic evidence, whether a particular teaching in the patent specification would be noticed by a POSITA, or whether a dictionary definition accurately reflects usage in the art. These are factual determinations and the appellate court reviews them for clear error.
- Interaction on appeal. The appellate court starts by reviewing the district court’s factual findings for clear error. If clear error is found in a factual predicate, that can change the legal construction. If no clear error is found, the appellate court then independently construes the claims based on those facts and the intrinsic evidence.

What constitutes clear error in this context

- Definition. Clear error is a highly deferential standard. The appellate court will only set aside the district court’s factual finding if, after reviewing the entire evidence, it is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made.
- Practical application. Because claims commonly turn on technical background and expert evidence, clear-error review preserves the district court’s advantage in hearing live testimony and assessing witness credibility. Appellate reversal on these subsidiary findings is therefore uncommon unless the district court’s factual findings are unsupported by the record or are internally inconsistent.

How courts apply the framework to common claim-construction issues

- Intrinsic vs. extrinsic evidence. Intrinsic evidence (the claim language, the specification, and the prosecution history) is the primary source for claim meaning. Extrinsic evidence (dictionaries, treatises, expert testimony) may inform subsidiary factual issues (e.g., ordinary meaning in the field). Since extrinsic evidence often underpins factual determinations, clear-error review protects the district court’s credibility assessments about such evidence.
- Claim terms of art and POSITA understanding. When experts disagree about a term’s meaning in the art, the district court may credit one expert and make a factual determination about the POSITA’s understanding. That factual finding is reviewed for clear error, while the ultimate claim construction remains legal.
- Disputed factual predicates and legal conflict. If an appellant can show that a claimed factual finding has no support in the record, the appellate court may set it aside and then perform de novo claim construction. The result can change whether asserted claims are interpreted to cover accused products or processes.

Implications for motions and appellate briefing

- Preservation. Parties must develop the factual record at the district court, including through expert testimony and contested exhibits, because appellate review of those facts is deferential. Weak factual presentations in the district court are unlikely to be cured on appeal.
- Focus of appellate briefs. On appeal, counsel must separate arguments: challenge factual findings by showing clear error (pointing to insufficient or contradictory evidence); challenge the legal construction de novo by offering alternative readings grounded in the intrinsic record and proper legal canons of construction.
- Timing of JMOL and summary judgment. Claim construction rulings often occur pretrial (summary judgment or Markman hearings) and shape JMOL motions. Because claim construction is legal on appeal, parties should preserve claim-construction challenges both in the district court and on appeal.

# Abuse of discretion review for new-trial rulings

A new trial is an equitable remedy in which the district court, exercising its discretion, sets aside a judgment and orders a retrial because of trial error, injustice, or other appropriate reasons. Appellate courts review a district court’s order granting or denying a new trial for abuse of discretion. This review affords substantial deference to the district court’s judgment about whether the trial’s fairness or integrity was compromised.

What abuse of discretion means

- Deference to district judge’s judgment. Abuse of discretion occurs when the district court’s decision is based on an erroneous view of the law, a clearly erroneous assessment of the evidence, or a manifestly unreasonable application of the law to the facts. The reviewing court does not substitute its own judgment unless the lower court’s choice falls outside the range of reasonable alternatives.
- Context matters. Where the district court’s new-trial decision is premised on a legal conclusion (for example, an incorrect understanding of what constitutes reversibility), appellate review may be mixed: the legal premise may be reviewed de novo, while the ultimate exercise of discretion remains reviewed for abuse.
- Standard degrees. Abuse-of-discretion review is deferential, but the strength of deference may vary depending on whether the district court weighed credibility and demeanor (more deference) or made a legal ruling about admissibility or instruction (less deference concerning legal questions).

Grounds for new-trial motions in patent cases

- Verdict against the weight of the evidence. A party may seek a new trial on the ground that the jury’s verdict is against the manifest weight of the evidence. District courts are permitted to order a new trial when they conclude that the verdict is seriously erroneous even though some evidence supports it. Appellate courts seldom reverse such discretionary determinations.
- Errors of law during trial. Significant legal errors—erroneous evidentiary rulings, misleading jury instructions, or improper conduct by counsel that materially affected the verdict—can be grounds for a new trial. On appeal, the correctness of the underlying law is reviewed de novo; if the legal error is established, the appellate court then evaluates whether the district court abused its discretion in denying or granting a new trial.
- Procedural irregularities and jury misconduct. Where irregularities or misconduct undermined the jury’s deliberative process, a new trial may be ordered. Appellate review again is abuse of discretion, mindful of the district court’s ability to evaluate candor, credibility, and immediate remedial steps.

Specific considerations in patent litigation

- Complex technical and damages evidence. District courts are often better positioned to assess whether a jury—presented with dense technical and economic testimony—reached a reliable verdict or whether confusion and misapplication of law warrant a retrial.
- Balancing fairness and finality. Patent cases can be expensive and protracted. District courts weigh both the fairness of a possible retrial and the burden on the parties. Appellate courts respect that balancing unless it is unreasonable.
- Relationship to JMOL. A grant of JMOL and a denial of a new trial present different bases for post-trial relief. A district court denying JMOL but granting a new trial is exercising discretion because it finds the verdict legally sufficient but unfair or unreliable. Appellate review will respect that nuanced judgment unless it is outside reasonable bounds.

Practical advice

- Preserve objections and move timely. To preserve new-trial issues, object contemporaneously at trial and, if seeking a new trial, file the post-trial motion under the applicable rule and within the deadline. Failure to do so can curtail appellate relief.
- Develop the record. Because appellate courts give considerable weight to the district court’s view of trial dynamics, thorough record development and post-trial briefing are crucial to persuading the trial court—and later the appellate court—of the need for a new trial.

# Harmless error principles

Harmless error doctrine governs whether an appellate court must reverse a judgment when trial error is established. The doctrine reflects the policy that not all mistakes justify reversal; errors that did not affect substantial rights or the outcome are to be left undisturbed. In patent appeals, harmless error analysis can decide whether an erroneous evidentiary ruling, instruction, or procedural mistake requires a new trial or whether the verdict stands notwithstanding the mistake.

The general test for harmless error

- Prejudice requirement. An error is harmless if the appellate court is convinced beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict (in some contexts) or, more generally in civil cases, did not affect the substantial rights of the parties. In civil appeals the inquiry is typically whether the error had a substantial and injurious effect or influence on the jury’s verdict.
- Burden of persuasion. The party benefitting from the error often bears the burden of showing that the error was harmless, though in some formulations the appellant must demonstrate that the error was not harmless. The allocation of burden can depend on whether the error was preserved or plain.
- Preserved vs. plain error. When a party preserved the objection at trial, appellate review assesses harmlessness under the preserved-error standard; when an issue was not preserved, plain-error review limits reversal to particularly egregious mistakes affecting substantial rights and the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.

Applying harmless-error analysis in patent contexts

- Erroneous claim construction. If a court applied an incorrect claim construction at trial, appellate courts first determine whether the error was preserved and then whether it was prejudicial—i.e., whether a correct construction would have changed the outcome on infringement or invalidity. If the record shows that the same evidence would have produced the same result under the correct construction, the error may be deemed harmless.
- Evidentiary rulings. The admission of improperly admitted expert testimony or the exclusion of critical evidence requires analysis of whether the misstep materially affected the jury’s verdict or the district court’s decision. If the erroneous evidence was cumulative or minor relative to the overwhelming contrary proof, it may be harmless.
- Jury instructions. Incorrect instructions are analyzed to determine whether they likely led the jury to a different result. Appellate courts consider the instructions as a whole and whether, despite the error, the jury necessarily would have returned the same verdict.
- Interplay with substantial-evidence review. Even when an error is found, substantial-evidence support for the verdict can lead to a finding of harmless error: if the record contains ample support for the verdict independent of the error, appellate courts may affirm.

Special tools and practical points

- Harmless beyond a reasonable doubt vs. substantial-rights test. Criminal and some constitutional contexts use the “harmless beyond a reasonable doubt” phrasing; civil patent appeals typically use a “substantial rights” or “substantial and injurious effect” formulation. Counsel must frame arguments under the correct standard.
- Nonconstitutional vs. constitutional errors. Constitutional errors (for example, Seventh Amendment or due process problems) may trigger a stricter harmlessness test; ordinary evidentiary mistakes are usually reviewed under the civil harmless-error formulation.
- Cumulative errors. Multiple minor errors that individually might be harmless can, in aggregate, have a prejudicial effect and warrant reversal or remand; appellate courts will assess whether the cumulative impact undermines confidence in the outcome.
- Remedial options. If an error is reversible, appellate remedies may include remanding for a new trial on limited issues, remanding for additional factfinding (especially where subsidiary factual findings are implicated), or entering judgment as a matter of law if the record compels a particular outcome.

Conclusion

Standards of review are decisive in patent appeals because they govern how much deference an appellate court affords district-court factfinding, legal rulings, and discretionary choices. De novo review of JMOL claims permits independent assessment of evidentiary sufficiency but still respects the jury’s reasonable inferences; substantial-evidence review is a deferential test that preserves jury determinations unless no reasonable evidence supports them; the Teva framework reconciles legal and factual components in claim construction by applying de novo review to ultimate legal construction and clear-error review to subsidiary factual findings; abuse-of-discretion review protects the district court’s judgment about new trials except where that judgment is unreasonable; and harmless error principles limit reversal to errors that actually or substantially affected the outcome. For trial counsel and appellate advocates alike, the practical lesson is straightforward: develop the record on contested facts; preserve objections; articulate legal grounds clearly at the district level; and tailor appellate arguments to the controlling standard of review, showing either that the record lacks any reasonable basis for the district court’s or jury’s decision or that, even if an error occurred, it was harmless.
