Introduction

Post-trial motions that challenge a jury verdict serve several distinct but related functions. They allow the trial judge to correct errors that occurred during trial, to resolve persistent legal questions about the sufficiency of the evidence, and to reduce or avoid unnecessary appeals by either directing judgment in favor of a party or ordering a new trial. In the federal system, the principal post-verdict mechanisms are Rule 50 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (judgment as a matter of law, commonly called JMOL and historically called "JNOV" or "directed verdict"/"judgment notwithstanding the verdict") and Rule 59 (new trial). Successful use of these procedural devices requires precise timing, careful preservation at trial, and strategic judgment about whether to seek relief under Rule 50, Rule 59, or both. Courts apply different legal standards and review doctrines to Rule 50 and Rule 59 motions, and appellate remedies differ depending on what relief the district court grants or denies. This chapter explains the governing rules, the typical tests and burdens, the consequences of failing to preserve objections, and the appellate framework for reviewing JMOL and new-trial decisions.

# Rule 50(a) pre-verdict motions and issue preservation

Rule 50(a) governs the pre-verdict motion for judgment as a matter of law. In substance, a party may move for judgment as a matter of law during trial, and it must do so before the case is submitted to the jury if the party believes there is insufficient legally sufficient evidence to support a claim or defense. The rule has two core purposes. First, it prevents a case that cannot legally succeed from going to the jury, preserving judicial resources and protecting parties from irrelevant deliberation. Second, it preserves the sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge so that the party may later renew that challenge after the verdict under Rule 50(b).

Timing and form. The critical procedural requirement of Rule 50(a) is temporal: the motion must be made at a point when the court can grant the motion without a jury verdict in hand—generally after the opposing party has presented its evidence and before the case is submitted to the jury. In practice, that often means the defendant moves after the plaintiff rests, and the plaintiff must move after the defendant rests if the plaintiff bears the burden of proof. A Rule 50(a) motion must specify the grounds on which the movant contends that judgment should be directed. Although Rule 50(a) does not require a particular length or detailed argument, the motion must adequately identify the legal theory of insufficiency (for example, lack of evidentiary support for a required element) so the court and opposing party know what is at issue.

Preservation function. Rule 50(a) serves as an indispensable preservation device. Failure to make a proper Rule 50(a) motion generally forecloses the movant from raising sufficiency of the evidence on appeal or renewing that challenge in a post-verdict Rule 50(b) motion. The rule thus forces litigants to crystallize the sufficiency argument at trial so the court and opposing party have a chance to correct the perceived inadequacy in presentation or proof and ensures that the trial judge has the opportunity to take the case from the jury before the verdict. The preservation requirement is strict: the motion must fairly present the grounds for the challenge. A general, conclusory, or late remark that does not assert a basis recognized by Rule 50(a) may fail to preserve the claim.

Substantive sufficiency standard. When a party makes a Rule 50(a) motion, the judge must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party and draw all reasonable inferences in that party's favor. The motion should be granted only if a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for the nonmoving party on the issue. Ordinarily courts do not weigh the evidence, judge credibility, or resolve conflicts in the testimony at that stage; those functions are reserved to the jury. The focus is solely whether the nonmoving party presented evidence on which a reasonable juror could rely to find for that party as to the particular claim or defense.

Practical tips. Practitioners should make Rule 50(a) motions every time there is a genuine argument that the opponent has failed to present legally sufficient evidence, even if the relief is unlikely. A properly made Rule 50(a) motion preserves the issue for a renewed motion under Rule 50(b) and for appeal. The motion should clearly identify the elements (or legal theory) on which the evidence is insufficient and, where relevant, cite the record, evidence omissions, absence of essential testimony, or legal deficiencies.

# Rule 50(b) renewed JMOL requirements and scope

Rule 50(b) governs the renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law made after a jury verdict. It is often called a "renewed JMOL" and is the modern analogue of the historical JNOV motion. A successful Rule 50(b) motion results in the entry of judgment in favor of the moving party notwithstanding the jury’s verdict; the district court may also grant partial JMOL as to particular issues.

Timing and procedural prerequisites. A renewed JMOL under Rule 50(b) cannot be made de novo for the first time after verdict; its availability is grounded in the earlier Rule 50(a) motion. Thus, the central prerequisite is that the moving party actually made a timely Rule 50(a) motion and preserved the same issue. Absent that, Rule 50(b) is typically unavailable. When the Rule 50(a) motion has been made, the Rule 50(b) motion must be filed no later than 28 days after the entry of judgment. The 28-day deadline is jurisdictional in practice: failure to file within that period usually bars the motion.

Scope of the renewed JMOL. The scope of a Rule 50(b) motion is limited to the grounds asserted in the pre-verdict Rule 50(a) motion. The renewed motion may not raise new arguments that were not presented in the Rule 50(a) motion, because one function of the Rule 50(a) motion is to give the trial court and opposing counsel notice and the opportunity to correct deficiencies before submission to the jury. Thus, the renewed motion must be confined to the legal theories and factual bases that were reasonably included in the earlier motion. If a party wishes to press additional arguments based on trial developments, it must ensure those arguments were preserved in Rule 50(a) or risk forfeiture.

Standard of review at the district and appellate levels. On a Rule 50(b) motion, the district court applies the same sufficiency standard it would have applied on a Rule 50(a) motion: viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party and drawing all reasonable inferences for that party, the court asks whether a reasonable jury could find for the nonmoving party. If the court concludes no reasonable jury could do so, it must direct judgment as a matter of law. However, if it finds that reasonable jurors could differ, it must deny the renewed motion.

On appeal, when an appellant challenges the district court's denial of a Rule 50(b) motion, the appellate court reviews de novo whether the evidence presented at trial was legally sufficient to support the jury verdict on the preserved question. De novo review means the appellate court reexamines the record without deference to the district court’s legal conclusion about sufficiency. The appellate court still applies the familiar standard of viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict winner and drawing all reasonable inferences in that party's favor, but the appellate court decides the sufficiency question anew.

Partial JMOL and alternative relief. Rule 50(b) permits a district court to grant judgment as a matter of law on some issues while submitting others to the jury or leaving certain claims intact. A court may also conditionally grant JMOL in situations where legal entitlement is present but mistrial or remittitur may be appropriate. When JMOL is granted post-verdict, the court must enter judgment accordingly; when it is denied, the party can seek a new trial under Rule 59 or appeal the denial after judgment.

# Rule 59 motions for new trial: grounds and standards

Rule 59 provides the mechanism for seeking a new trial or to alter or amend a judgment. Unlike Rule 50, which focuses on legal sufficiency, Rule 59 empowers the court to grant a new trial on a broad range of grounds, including but not limited to errors of law, errors in the admission or exclusion of evidence, juror misconduct, irregularities in the proceedings that prevented a fair trial, verdicts against the weight of the evidence, and excessive or inadequate damages. Rule 59 is remedial and discretionary in character: it gives the trial court latitude to correct not just necessarily erroneous legal conclusions but also trial errors or verdicts that are substantially unfair.

Common grounds for new trial. Courts regularly consider several nonexclusive grounds when evaluating Rule 59 motions:

- Errors of law. If evidentiary rulings, jury instructions, or procedural errors so infected the trial that they prejudiced a party’s substantial rights, a new trial may be warranted. The key inquiry is whether the error was prejudicial—that is, whether it affected the outcome in a meaningful way.

- Verdict contrary to weight of the evidence. Unlike Rule 50, which focuses on legal insufficiency and does not permit reweighing evidence, Rule 59 allows the judge to conclude that the jury's verdict is against the clear weight of the evidence. This ground often arises when the judge believes the jury reached an unreasonable result despite the legally sufficient record. When a judge grants a new trial on this basis, the court has effectively determined it would not have reached the same conclusion as the jury considering all the evidence and with the trial judge’s view of credibility and weight.

- Substantial trial irregularities or misconduct. Juror misconduct, attorney misconduct, failure to follow court orders, or extraneous influence on the jury can justify a new trial.

- Newly discovered evidence. If material evidence emerges after trial that could not reasonably have been discovered before trial and would probably produce a different result, a new trial may be ordered to consider that evidence.

- Excessive or inadequate damages. Where the damages award is clearly excessive or inadequate as a matter of fact, a judge may grant a new trial on damages. Federal courts also use remittitur (reducing an excessive award and offering the plaintiff the choice between accepting the reduced amount or undergoing a new trial) or additur (in some circuits, adding to an inadequate award) as alternatives.

Standards of decision. The standard a district court uses in deciding a Rule 59 motion is generally broad discretion and a flexible balancing of factors. The Court should not grant a new trial lightly; the decision reflects the trial court’s superior vantage point for judging the fairness of the trial, assessing credibility, and weighing evidence. Appellate courts review a district court’s Rule 59 decisions for abuse of discretion. That is, reversal is appropriate only when the district court’s decision is grounded in a clear error of judgment or where the court applied incorrect legal standards.

Distinction from JMOL. The conceptual distinction between Rule 50 and Rule 59 is important. Rule 50 addresses legal sufficiency and prevents unjustified jury findings; it does not permit the court to reweigh evidence or substitute the judge's view for the jury’s unless no reasonable jury could have found as it did. Rule 59, by contrast, permits a district judge to grant a new trial when a verdict is against the weight of the evidence or when other trial errors produced an unjust result. For that reason, litigants sometimes file both a renewed Rule 50(b) motion and a Rule 59 motion: the 50(b) argues the verdict cannot stand as a matter of law; the 59 motion asks the court to exercise its equitable discretion to order a new trial if the 50(b) is denied.

Procedural details and timing. A Rule 59 motion must be filed no later than 28 days after the entry of judgment. Timely filing preserves a number of appellate rights and tolls the time for taking an appeal in many circumstances. The Rule 59 motion should articulate specific grounds; boilerplate assertions are less persuasive. When a Rule 59 motion and a Rule 50(b) motion are both asserted, courts typically consider the Rule 50(b) renewed motion first because a grant of JMOL may obviate the need for a new trial.

# Waiver and forfeiture tied to Rule 50 timing

Because Rule 50(a) is the preservation device for a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence, failure to make a proper Rule 50(a) motion typically results in waiver or forfeiture of that argument. Courts treat the preservation requirement strictly for several reasons: it affords the trial court an opportunity to resolve evidentiary or legal insufficiency before the jury decides the case; it gives opposing counsel notice and the opportunity to correct gaps in proof; and it preserves the trial record for meaningful appellate review.

Distinguishing waiver from forfeiture. Different doctrines (waiver, forfeiture) are invoked depending on whether the party intentionally relinquished a known right (waiver) or failed to take timely action (forfeiture). Under both labels, however, the practical consequence is similar: an appellate court will often refuse to consider a sufficiency challenge that was not raised under Rule 50(a) before trial submission. The federal rules leave little room for raising sufficiency arguments for the first time on appeal, except under narrow plain-error review. Plain-error review is exceptional and requires showing that an obvious error affected substantial rights and that the error seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the judicial proceedings. Courts rarely grant such relief for failure to file Rule 50(a), because Rule 50(a) exists to allow the trial court to fix issues without recourse to appellate intervention.

What constitutes a proper Rule 50(a) motion for preservation? For a Rule 50(a) motion to preserve an argument, it must be timely and must articulate a legal ground that reasonably apprises the court and opposing counsel of the insufficiency claim. Vague or generalized motions that fail to connect the deficiency to a particular element or legal principle may not preserve the issue. Likewise, asserting only evidentiary objections (for example, contemporaneous objections to questions or exhibits) does not necessarily preserve a sufficiency argument under Rule 50 unless those objections explicitly request that judgment be directed because of the insufficiency of proof.

Exceptions and interplay with other rules. There are limited circumstances in which an appellate court may consider the sufficiency of the evidence despite the absence of a timely Rule 50(a) motion—principally via plain-error review or other exceptional doctrines such as fundamental miscarriage of justice. Additionally, in bench trials the preservation rule is different: Rule 52(b) permits a party to file motion to amend findings of fact and conclusions of law, and appellate courts generally review sufficiency de novo in nonjury trials because there is no jury verdict to which to defer. But in jury trials under Rule 50, failure to preserve the issue at trial is a critical procedural mistake.

Strategic implications. Given the harsh consequences of failing to preserve, litigators should make well-crafted Rule 50(a) motions whenever a sufficiency argument exists, even if the motion seems unlikely to succeed. The motion should be specific, state the precise basis for insufficiency (which element lacks proof), and be timely. Because Rule 50(b) depends on Rule 50(a), preservation serves not just immediate trial strategy but also appellate strategy.

# Appellate remedies after JMOL and new-trial rulings

The appellate consequences of district court rulings on Rule 50(b) and Rule 59 motions differ depending on whether the district court grants or denies relief and on what form of relief is granted. Appellate courts apply distinct standards of review and may fashion remedies that reflect the interplay between legal and equitable power.

Appellate review after denial of renewed JMOL. When a district court denies a Rule 50(b) renewed motion, the movant may appeal from the final judgment. On appeal, the sufficiency-of-the-evidence question is reviewed de novo, with the appellate court viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict winner and drawing all reasonable inferences in that party’s favor. If the appellate court concludes the evidence was insufficient as a matter of law, it may reverse the judgment and enter judgment for the moving party. That is, the appellate court may grant judgment as a matter of law in place of the jury verdict. In some cases, an appellate court that finds insufficiency will remand with instructions to enter judgment for the moving party; in other cases, the appellate court may order a new trial limited to certain issues if the record reveals disputed factual issues that must be resolved or if equitable considerations counsel against ordering entry of judgment.

Appellate review after grant of renewed JMOL. If the district court grants JMOL under Rule 50(b), the losing party may appeal that judgment. The appellate court reviews the sufficiency determination de novo. If the appellate court agrees that no reasonable jury could have found for the verdict winner, it will affirm the judgment. If the appellate court disagrees and concludes the evidence could support the jury verdict, it will typically reverse and remand for entry of judgment in favor of the previous verdict winner or for a new trial as appropriate.

Appellate review after denial of new trial. When a district court denies a Rule 59 motion for a new trial, the appellate court generally reviews the denial for abuse of discretion. That is a deferential standard: the appellate court will affirm unless the district court committed a clear error of judgment (for example, applied an incorrect legal standard, or reached a conclusion that no reasonable judge could reach). The underlying legal issues that formed the basis for the Rule 59 motion—such as alleged errors in jury instructions or the admission of evidence—may also be reviewed on appeal; depending on preservation and the type of error, appellate review may be de novo or for harmless error. But as to the new-trial exercise of discretion—whether to grant a new trial because the verdict is against the weight of the evidence—appellate courts give substantial deference to the trial judge's superior position.

Appellate review after grant of new trial. When a district court grants a new trial under Rule 59, appellate review is limited because there is no final judgment for the prior trial; the grant of a new trial typically produces a new trial and resumption of the litigation in the district court. In some contexts, a grant of a new trial on certain issues may be treated as an appealable order if it has the characteristics of a final decision or if the appellate court finds an interlocutory path. But generally, the grant of a new trial is not appealable until a final judgment is entered after retrial, though there are exceptions under the collateral order doctrine or other jurisdictional principles that rarely apply.

Conditional rulings and alternative remedies. District courts sometimes act conditionally: for example, after denying renewal of JMOL they may grant a new trial. Conversely, after granting JMOL a court may allow the defeated party some relief in the form of a remittitur or conditionally grant a new trial on damages. On appeal, the existence of conditional rulings can complicate review: an appellate court must determine which interlocutory or final orders are reviewable and how to treat alternative grounds for relief. Appellate courts will also consider whether the district court acted within its equitable powers and whether any remedial ordering (such as remittitur) respects constitutional rights (e.g., Seventh Amendment concerns in civil cases).

Interplay between Rule 50(b) and Rule 59 on appeal. Because a litigant often files both a renewed JMOL and a Rule 59 motion, understanding appellate timing is crucial. If a district court grants a new trial under Rule 59 after denying Rule 50(b), there may be questions about whether the denying order is appealable, since the grant of a new trial generally moots the judgment. If the district court denies both the Rule 50(b) and Rule 59 motions and the moving party appeals, the appellate court will review the sufficiency question de novo (from Rule 50(b) denial) and will review the denial of Rule 59 for abuse of discretion. The availability of these multiple standards often leads litigants to pursue both forms of relief because they preserve different theories and review pathways for appellate consideration.

Practical appellate strategies. Litigators should bear in mind that Rule 50(b) is the vehicle to preserve legal sufficiency claims for appellate de novo review. If that relief is denied, a Rule 59 motion gives the trial judge the opportunity to order a new trial, and denial of the Rule 59 will be reviewed deferentially. On appeal, plaintiffs and defendants should frame the issues to highlight the correct standard of review: errors of law and sufficiency-of-the-evidence questions invite de novo review, while discretionary decisions about ordering a new trial typically attract abuse-of-discretion review.

Conclusion

Rule 50 and Rule 59 are powerful tools for litigants who believe a jury verdict is legally or factually unsupportable. Effective use of these rules depends on careful preservation at the proper times, precise articulation of the legal deficiencies or trial errors, and a realistic assessment of the range of remedies available. Rule 50(a) is the critical preservation mechanism that enables a renewed JMOL under Rule 50(b) and de novo appellate review of sufficiency claims. Rule 59 grants a district court equitable discretion to order a new trial for a broad array of reasons, but appellate review of those discretionary decisions is deferential. Failure to make timely, specific Rule 50(a) motions or to meet the procedural demands of Rule 50(b) and Rule 59 often results in waiver and limits appellate options. Mastery of these doctrines will allow a practitioner to preserve issues effectively, to choose the most advantageous post-trial motion strategy, and to anticipate how appellate courts will treat the district court’s rulings.
