Introduction

In patent litigation, judges and juries resolve fact-intensive questions about whether an accused product or process meets the elements of a patent claim, whether prior art anticipates or renders obvious the claimed invention, and what damages, if any, are appropriate. Appellate courts reviewing the sufficiency of the evidence supporting a jury verdict apply the substantial-evidence standard, a deferential test that asks whether the record contains relevant evidence such that a reasonable jury could have reached the verdict. Because patent disputes routinely involve complex technical subject matter, the courts focus less on the intuitiveness of a particular inference and more on whether the party who bore a burden of proof produced a logical, evidentiary bridge from the basic facts in the record to the ultimate claim-related conclusions.

This chapter explains the doctrine and practice of evidence sufficiency review in patent cases. It begins by defining substantial evidence and the reasonable-jury test. It then explains how documentary and testimonial proof operate in complex-technology disputes, stressing the role of expert testimony and reliable demonstratives. The chapter develops the concept of the inferential gap and why courts require clear mappings from evidence to claim elements. It describes how contradictions, speculation, and conclusory assertions affect sufficiency analysis. Finally, it treats the interplay between sufficiency review and motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), including timing, standards, and the relationship between trial and appellate review.

# Defining substantial evidence and the reasonable-jury test

Substantial evidence is the foundational standard for appellate review of jury factfindings. The phrase captures a deferential approach: an appellate court asks whether the trial record contains relevant evidence that a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support the jury’s conclusions. The inquiry is not whether the appellate court itself would have weighed the evidence differently; nor does it involve reweighing credibility determinations that the jury was entitled to make. Rather, the court must view the record in the light most favorable to the verdict winner and determine whether there is more than a mere scintilla of evidence linking the record to the essential facts underlying the verdict.

Two related features distinguish substantial-evidence review. First, the standard looks to relevant evidence, not to whether the evidence was compelling. Evidence that, taken together, yields a reasonable inference supporting the verdict suffices even if reasonable minds could disagree. Second, the standard is claim-specific and burden-sensitive. The amount and quality of evidence necessary to sustain a finding depend on which party bore the burden of proof and the degree of persuasion required. For example, factual findings that the patentee carried by a preponderance of the evidence need less in the way of corroboration than findings of invalidity that some doctrines require to be proved by clear and convincing evidence.

The reasonable-jury test operationalizes substantial evidence. When the jury returns a verdict on contested factual questions—e.g., that an accused product meets each limitation of a claim, or that certain prior-art references, taken together, make the claim obvious—the appellate court asks whether a reasonable juror, drawing permissible inferences from the evidence, could reach the same conclusion. If reasonable jurors could, the verdict stands; if not, the court must reverse or remand. The test implicates rules of admissibility and relevance only insofar as they affect whether the evidence in the record is proper for consideration. For that reason, evidentiary rulings that exclude or admit evidence can have significant sufficiency implications.

Different substantive contexts impose different burdens of proof, which feed into the sufficiency analysis. For infringement, liability for damages, and most affirmative claims by the patentee, the patentee has the burden of proving elements by a preponderance of the evidence. For affirmative defenses attacking the patent’s validity—anticipation, obviousness, enablement, written description—the defendant ordinarily must prove invalidity by clear and convincing evidence (although recent developments and special circumstances can affect the applicable standard). Because of this discrepancy, the sufficiency of evidence for a verdict finding validity may be judged against the higher clear-and-convincing threshold on appeal: the appellate court asks whether substantial evidence supports the finding given the required degree of persuasion.

# Documentary vs. testimonial proof in complex technology cases

Patent cases often hinge on technical facts: structural or functional aspects of an accused device, the content of prior art, the operation of an algorithm, or the state of ordinary skill in the art. These matters can be proved through documents (patents, prior publications, product manuals, source code, laboratory data, design logs), demonstratives and physical exhibits (models, schematics, samples), and testimony (lay witnesses, inventor testimony, fact witnesses, and most importantly, expert witnesses). The admissibility, sufficiency, and weight of these different forms of proof interact in defining whether there is substantial evidence to sustain a verdict.

Documentary evidence has particular strengths. Contemporaneous documents authored during the development of technology or prior-art references themselves can be powerful because they directly show the content of the art, design choices, or the physical features of an accused product. A well-preserved document can allow a jury to compare alleged features of a product to claim limitations without extensive interpretive leaps. Source code and schematics, if authenticated and explained, can demonstrate the existence (or absence) of structures and functions corresponding to claim elements.

Testimonial proof, especially expert testimony, is often necessary to translate complex documents and physical exhibits into jury-applicable findings. Experts explain terminology, interpret technical disclosures, opine on how a product operates, and synthesize multiple documents into an obviousness or enablement theory. Courts scrutinize expert testimony for its fit to the facts and for adequate foundation: an expert must explain the reasoning and methodology that underlie the opinion, and cannot simply assert legal conclusions. When testimony is the principal basis for a verdict, appellate courts examine whether the testimony, viewed in the record context and taking into account the jury’s credibility determinations, was sufficiently concrete and tied to admissible facts.

The relationship between documentary and testimonial evidence is symbiotic. Documents without testimony that explains how the document bears on claim elements may be inscrutable to jurors. Conversely, expert assertions divorced from evidentiary anchors—the documents, the product, or demonstrable test results—risk being viewed as ipse dixit. A robust trial presentation in a technology case typically offers both primary documentary or physical data and expert testimony that maps those materials to the legal elements the jury must decide.

Because jurors are lay factfinders, courts permit expert testimony to bridge technical gaps, but they require that the expert’s reasoning be disclosed and show a reliable basis. Where an expert’s testimony is cumulative of documentary proof, the combination strengthens a verdict; where both expert testimony and documentary proof are weak or speculative, the combination will not meet substantial evidence requirements.

# Bridging the inferential gap: need for clear mappings and explanations

A recurring theme in sufficiency review is the “inferential gap”: the distance between the basic facts in evidence and the ultimate fact the jury must find to apply the law. In patent cases, the inferential gap often appears when a party contends that some piece of evidence (a document, a component, a test result) corresponds to a claim limitation. Courts require a clear mapping from the admissible evidence to each claim element. If the party’s theory requires multiple inferential steps that are speculative or unsupported, appellate courts may hold that the evidence is not substantial.

Bridging the inferential gap typically requires three elements. First, the underlying facts must be in evidence: the document, the component, the operation logs, the test results. Second, there must be reliable testimony or explanation that ties those facts to the technical concept embodied in the claim limitation—this is often provided by an expert who explains the basis for the identification. Third, the chain of inference must be reasonable: each step must be supported by evidence, and the cumulative inference must not rely on speculation or mere possibility.

For example, to prove that an accused product contains a structure that performs a claimed function, a patentee needs evidence that the component in question actually performs that function and testimony explaining why the presence and operation of that component satisfy the claim language. Demonstratives and labels alone will not suffice; the jury needs admissible evidence showing both the presence and the operational effect. Similarly, when proving anticipation by a prior reference, the party must show that the reference discloses each claim limitation, not merely that the reference shares some general concept. The mapping requires showings that technical language corresponds, that the reference would have been understood by a person of ordinary skill in the art as disclosing the limitation, and that any claim terms resolved in claim construction match the reference’s disclosure.

Courts are attentive to whether the proof substitutes a chain of inference for direct evidence. A chain of inferences may be adequate if each link is supported, but courts will overturn findings based on unsupported inference stacks. The need to bridge the inferential gap is especially acute where the legal standard for the issue carries a higher burden (e.g., clear and convincing proof of invalidity) because the evidentiary foundation must be commensurately firmer.

# Impact of contradictions, speculation, and conclusory assertions

Not all evidence is created equal. Contradictory evidence, speculative testimony, and conclusory assertions can undermine the sufficiency of a record. The jury is charged with resolving conflicts in testimony and may accept one party’s version over another, but when contradictions are so fundamental that no reasonable juror could reconcile them into a coherent finding, an appellate court may conclude that the verdict lacks substantial evidence.

Contradictions in the record can take many forms. Two witnesses may give inconsistent descriptions of a process, or documents may present content that undermines a party’s claim about what was known or what was performed. A single expert may present one theory in a written report and another at trial. If a reasonable juror could treat these contradictions as resolved by credibility findings or plausible reconciliations, the verdict can stand. If resolution would require speculative leaps or ignore plainly contradictory documentary proof, the verdict cannot be sustained.

Speculation occurs when a party asks the jury to accept a factual proposition without sufficient evidentiary support. For example, inferring that a component that looks similar to a claimed structure necessarily works in the same way can be speculative absent testing, internal documentation, or reliable expert demonstration. Courts do not permit verdicts based on speculation that the accused device “must” operate in a particular manner when the record affords no reliable basis for that inference. Expert witnesses must ground their opinions in facts or data; pure conjecture is inadmissible and cannot be the basis for substantial evidence.

Conclusory assertions are another frequent shortcoming. A witness who opines that “the accused product meets the claim” without explaining how each claim limitation is satisfied provides no bridge from the record to the ultimate legal conclusion. Similarly, labeling a document as “teaching” a claim limitation without explaining the mapping—terminology alignment, functional correspondence, or structural equivalence—does not aid a jury in forming a reasoned inference. Courts expect witnesses, especially experts, to “connect the dots.” When testimony is conclusory, appellate courts are likely to view it as insufficient for substantial-evidence purposes.

The interaction between contradictions, speculation, and credibility determinations is central. Appellate courts do not substitute their judgment for reasonable credibility choices by the jury. However, where the only plausible interpretation of the record is one that contradicts the verdict, or where the record rests on speculation and conclusory testimony without evidentiary support, the substantial-evidence standard is not satisfied.

# Interplay between sufficiency review and JMOL

Judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), known in some courts and contexts as a Rule 50 motion, functions at trial and shapes the appellate sufficiency inquiry. A party may move for JMOL during trial after the opponent has presented its evidence and again after the verdict; failure to move precludes preserving sufficiency-related arguments for appeal in some jurisdictions. The standard for granting JMOL at trial is that a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for the nonmoving party on that issue.

At the JMOL stage, the trial judge determines whether the evidence presented, viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, could reasonably support a verdict for that party. The judge cannot weigh credibility or substitute her own view of the evidence; but if the evidence is legally insufficient—no reasonable jury could find for the nonmoving party—the judge must enter judgment for the moving party. A renewed JMOL after the verdict gives the trial court one last opportunity to correct a verdict the court finds unsupported by the evidence.

The appellate review of a JMOL denial or grant most commonly follows the substantial-evidence framework for underlying factual findings. When reviewing a denial of JMOL on factual issues, an appellate court typically applies the same deferential test: whether substantial evidence supports the jury’s verdict. The standard is effectively the same substantive question that the trial court confronted, though the appellate court reviews the trial court’s legal ruling de novo (i.e., without deference) when it comes to the legal standard applied to the sufficiency question. If the trial court granted JMOL, the appellate court reviews de novo whether the record contained sufficient evidence.

Procedural posture matters. A successful JMOL motion at the close of the opponent’s case is an early admission by the moving party that the evidence presented so far is legally insufficient. Denial of that motion preserves the factual dispute for resolution by the jury. A post-verdict JMOL motion allows the trial judge to set aside a verdict that the judge determines no reasonable jury could have reached. On appeal, courts look at the same record and the same evidentiary submissions, but they also consider whether the litigants preserved issues properly and whether any legal standards (such as the required burden of proof) were correctly applied at trial.

The practical import is that parties must present, at trial, a legally sufficient evidentiary record. Relying solely on inflammatory argument, demonstratives without evidentiary foundation, or conclusory expert assertions invites a JMOL ruling and, if a verdict nevertheless issues, a successful sufficiency challenge on appeal. Conversely, the denial of JMOL is not a legal rubber stamp; appellate courts will set aside jury verdicts that are unsupported even after a trial judge denied renewed JMOL, but they will do so only when the record as a whole lacks substantial evidence.

Taken together, the doctrines encourage litigants to produce evidence that directly ties to each element the jury must find, to preserve objections and JMOL motions to protect appellate review, and to recognize that the standard of persuasion (preponderance or clear and convincing) governs assessment of whether the evidence meets the sufficiency threshold.

Conclusion

Sufficiency review in patent cases balances deference to jury factfinding with the judiciary’s gatekeeping role against verdicts unsupported by a rational evidentiary foundation. Substantial evidence requires more than labels and conclusory assertions; it requires a reasonable chain of inference grounded in admissible facts, documentary proof, and reliable expert explanation. Documentary and testimonial proofs are complementary: documents provide raw materials while testimony interprets them for lay jurors. Courts will sustain a verdict when reasonable jurors could find for the prevailing party based on permissible inferences; they will set it aside when the record rests on speculation, contradictions that cannot be reconciled, or unexplained inferential leaps. Motions for JMOL are the procedural mechanism at trial that tests sufficiency, and their disposition frames the scope of appellate review. Understanding these principles prepares a reader to assess how courts analyze the sufficiency of evidence in complex patent disputes.
