Introduction

Federal appellate review of patent cases is governed by a specialized jurisdictional regime: appeals from district court decisions involving patents generally go to the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit (the Federal Circuit) rather than to the regional circuit where the district court sits. That statutory allocation centralizes patent law development in a single appellate tribunal, but it does not mean the Federal Circuit writes on a clean slate. The Federal Circuit must apply its own distinctive body of patent law on substantive patent issues while often applying regional-circuit law to many procedural and non-patent questions. Mastery of the interplay between statutory jurisdiction, the final judgment rule, allocation of governing law, standards of review, and the rules for awarding costs and fees on appeal is essential for predicting outcomes and planning appellate strategy in patent litigation.

This chapter explains the scope of the Federal Circuit’s statutory jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), the significance of the final judgment rule for appealability, how the Federal Circuit and regional circuits divide authority over procedural and substantive issues on appeal (with particular attention to motions such as judgment as a matter of law and new trials), the contours of Federal Circuit law for patent-specific questions, and the standards and statutes that govern awards of costs and appellate fees. The treatment emphasizes legal reasoning: how courts frame issues, define applicable law, apply standards of review, and account for procedural posture when resolving appeals.

# 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) and the scope of Federal Circuit jurisdiction

Statutory allocation and scope

28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) creates the Federal Circuit’s appellate jurisdiction over civil actions “arising under” the patent laws and certain other federal statutes by providing that appeals in specified categories from district courts shall be taken to the Federal Circuit. Practically, this means that final decisions by a district court in cases involving patent validity, infringement, and related claims are generally reviewable by the Federal Circuit, rather than by the geographically organized regional courts of appeals.

Determining whether an appeal “arises under” the patent laws turns on the complaint’s original character and the issues actually litigated. When patent claims are essential to the plaintiff’s right to relief, or when the resolution of patent law questions is central to the disposition, the Federal Circuit will have jurisdiction. Jurisdiction under § 1295(a)(1) is thus linked to the federal nature and centrality of patent issues in the litigation. The statutory grant is broad in patent disputes but not unlimited: where the patent law question is incidental, hypothetical, or asserted defensively but not central to the parties’ claims and remedies, jurisdictional questions can arise.

Interplay with other statutes and appeals

The Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction under § 1295(a)(1) operates alongside the usual federal appellate jurisdiction statutes, including the statute that creates appeals from final decisions of the district courts. There are statutory exceptions and special provisions—such as the availability of interlocutory review in certain limited circumstances under other subsections of § 1295 and other statutes—which can affect the timing and scope of appeals. Appellate jurisdiction over patent matters is thus statutory and can be defined both by the content of the issues on appeal and by the specific appellate mechanism invoked.

Jurisdictional posture and preservation

The timing and manner of presenting issues at the district court affect whether an issue is ripe for Federal Circuit review. A party that fails to preserve a claim or defense properly in the district court may face procedural bars on appeal regardless of the jurisdictional reach of § 1295(a)(1). In addition, the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction does not automatically authorize review of interlocutory rulings unless a statutory pathway is available; absent an interlocutory statute, a party generally must await a final judgment and then appeal.

# Final judgment rule and appealability

The final judgment rule: appealable order requirement

The final judgment rule is the basic threshold for appellate jurisdiction in federal courts. Under the final judgment doctrine, an appeal may be taken only from “final decisions” of district courts—orders that dispose of all claims against all parties, leaving nothing further for the trial court to do on the merits. This rule promotes efficient and orderly appellate review and prevents piecemeal appeals.

Finality in multi-claim or multi-party cases

Finality is measured by whether the district court’s order finally resolves the rights and liabilities of the parties with respect to the claims and parties encompassed by the appeal. In multi-claim or multi-party litigation, finality can be achieved through an order disposing of the last claim or by the district court’s entry of final judgment as to fewer than all claims or parties when the requirements of the controlling statute (e.g., a certification under rules that permit partial final judgment) are satisfied. Otherwise, an appeal that seeks to challenge only an interlocutory ruling or the resolution of fewer than all claims or parties will typically be premature.

Interlocutory appeals and statutory exceptions

There are well-defined statutory exceptions to the final judgment rule. Certain interlocutory orders may be immediately appealable if a statute authorizes it—examples include certain injunctions, collateral orders meeting the narrow collateral order doctrine (orders that conclusively determine important rights separate from the merits and are effectively unreviewable on appeal from final judgment), and certification for interlocutory appeal under statutes allowing interlocutory review. In the patent context, statutory provisions may allow interlocutory appeals in some circumstances (for example, appeals from decisions on claim construction in some limited contexts heard under specific statutory paths), but the ordinary rule is that the Federal Circuit hears appeals after final judgment.

Effect of post-judgment motions

Post-judgment motions can affect appealability. A timely post-trial or post-judgment motion that affects the judgment may either toll the running of time to appeal or suspend finality until the motion is resolved. Examples include motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), motions for a new trial, motions to alter or amend a judgment, or motions for relief from judgment. The invocation of such motions can alter the procedural posture in which an appellate court assesses the district court’s rulings, and appellate courts frequently require that these preservation mechanisms be used to challenge factual sufficiency or procedural errors that arise during or immediately after trial.

Practical consequences for litigants

For practitioners, the final judgment rule means careful planning. Parties must identify which issues are susceptible to interlocutory review and which must be preserved and renewed through post-trial motions if immediate appeal is sought. Strategic use of post-trial motions not only preserves issues for appeal but also may change the standard of review an appellate court applies, as the appellate court may review the denial of certain motions like JMOL de novo (or under other well-established standards) while treating other findings as final.

# Applying regional circuit law to procedural issues (e.g., JMOL, new trial)

The general choice-of-law principle

When the Federal Circuit hears an appeal from a district court in a patent case, it applies federal law to substantive patent issues but often looks to the law of the regional circuit that decided the case for many procedural questions. This is a choice-of-law principle recognizing that a district court operates within the regional circuit’s procedural and interlocutory framework, and that a uniform approach to federal procedure is desirable. Therefore, for procedural matters that are not uniquely attached to patent law, the Federal Circuit commonly applies the regional circuit’s law, including its precedents interpreting the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and standards for preservation, waiver, or procedural motions.

Scope of “procedural issues”

Procedural issues to which regional circuit law commonly applies include, but are not limited to, preservation rules, standards governing waiver and forfeiture, evidentiary rulings on matters of admissibility where the regional circuit has specific doctrines, and the particulars of motions such as judgment as a matter of law and new trial motions. The idea is that these legal questions are not patent-specific and are better resolved consistently with the regional circuit that oversees the trial court’s ordinary practice.

Standards for JMOL (judgment as a matter of law)

Judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) (sometimes called judgment notwithstanding the verdict or JNOV in prior terminology) challenges the sufficiency of the evidence supporting the jury’s verdict. The controlling standard for JMOL and the timing for making such a motion are set by federal procedural rules, but courts’ interpretation of those rules—particularly what constitutes sufficient evidence, how to view the evidence on review, and whether an appellate court examines the record anew—can vary across circuits in detail. The Federal Circuit typically adopts the regional circuit’s interpretation of the standard (for example, the test of whether a reasonable jury could have found for the non-moving party) and follows the regional circuit’s guidance on such matters as whether the judge may weigh evidence or assess witness credibility.

Standards for new trial motions

New trial motions, which ask the district court to set aside a jury verdict and order another trial, are equitable and discretionary. The standard for granting a new trial—whether because of the verdict’s against the weight of the evidence, errors in the admission or exclusion of evidence, prejudicial conduct, or other trial irregularities—derives from the district court’s local practice and regional precedent. The Federal Circuit defers to the regional circuit’s approach to these discretionary determinations, respecting the trial court’s fact-finding and judgment unless the regional circuit’s law would permit reversal.

Preservation, waiver, and forfeiture

Whether a litigant preserved an objection for appeal often controls appellate reviewability and standard of review. The regional circuit’s doctrines on preservation, waiver, and forfeiture therefore apply. For example, some regional circuits adhere strictly to contemporaneous-objection requirements for evidentiary errors, while others allow more flexibility; the Federal Circuit follows the regional circuit law that governed the trial court when assessing whether an appellant waived or preserved an issue.

Interaction with standards of review

Because the regional circuits may differ in how they apply preservation doctrines or in the intensity of review for discretionary rulings, the choice to apply regional circuit law can materially affect the outcome of an appeal. For instance, under one regional approach, the failure to make a particular motion at trial might result in plain-error review on appeal, making reversal difficult; under another approach, the issue might be considered preserved for more searching review. The Federal Circuit thus pays attention to the procedural posture and to the regional circuit framework that governed the district court’s proceedings before drawing its appellate conclusions.

# Applying Federal Circuit law to patent-specific issues

Substantive patent law as Federal Circuit law

The Federal Circuit is the authoritative national appellate forum for substantive patent law questions. This includes, but is not limited to, claim construction, infringement analysis (literal infringement and doctrine of equivalents), validity questions such as anticipation and obviousness, enablement and written description, indefiniteness, and patentability determinations tied directly to substantive patent doctrine. The rationale is that uniformity in substantive patent law across the nation is desirable to reduce forum shopping and to promote predictable patent standards for inventors, competitors, and the public.

Claim construction: law versus fact

Claim construction—interpreting what the patent claims mean—has both legal and factual components. The overarching principle is that ultimate claim construction is a legal determination reviewed on appeal as a question of law, but subsidiary factual findings that inform claim construction (for example, factual findings about how a person of ordinary skill in the art would understand a term or objective evidence from prosecution history or extrinsic evidence) are reviewed for clear error. Thus, on appeal, legal aspects of claim interpretation are reviewed de novo, while embedded factual determinations receive deference.

Standards for obviousness and anticipation

Obviousness and anticipation revolve around mixed questions of law and fact. The factual components typically include the scope and content of the prior art, differences between the prior art and the claimed invention, the level of ordinary skill in the art, and objective indicia of nonobviousness (such as commercial success, long-felt need, or failure of others). Those factual findings are reviewed for clear error. The ultimate question—whether a person of ordinary skill in the art would have found the claimed invention obvious in view of the prior art—is treated as a legal conclusion based on those facts and is reviewed de novo. For anticipation, factual determinations about whether a single prior-art reference discloses every claim limitation are reviewed for clear error; the ultimate legal conclusion of anticipation extends from those facts.

Infringement (literal and doctrine of equivalents)

Literal infringement analysis applies the claim construction framework—if properly construed claim terms are literally met by the accused device or method, infringement may be found. The Federal Circuit reviews claim construction de novo (with deference to subsidiary facts), and then applies those constructions to the accused product as a matter of law. The doctrine of equivalents, by contrast, is fact-intensive and typically involves questions about the function-way-result and whether prosecution history estoppel bars equivalents. Appellate courts review underlying factual determinations (e.g., whether an element is insubstantially different) for clear error and legal conclusions de novo.

Patent invalidity defenses and burdens of proof

In a civil action, the party asserting invalidity bears the burden of persuasion by clear and convincing evidence when the patent is presumed valid. That burden is a matter of substantive patent law and thus governed uniformly by the Federal Circuit’s treatment of the standard. When validity issues are tried to a jury, the jury’s factual findings are given the usual appellate deference (clear error for factual findings), and the Federal Circuit applies its own law to the legal aspects. If the district court later grants a JMOL on invalidity, appellate review will carefully distinguish between whether the evidence could support the jury’s view and whether legal conclusions derived from facts were correct.

Administrative decisions and appeals (e.g., patent office proceedings)

Where patent validity is determined in administrative proceedings before the patent office (such as post-grant review or inter partes review), the interplay between administrative adjudication and district-court litigation creates doctrinal nuances. The Federal Circuit reviews administrative decisions under administrative-law standards (de novo review of legal questions, substantial evidence review of factual findings, and Chevron/Skidmore-type deference where statutory interpretation is at issue). While this topic bridges administrative law and patent law, the Federal Circuit’s patent expertise increasingly informs its review of both district court and administrative agency determinations.

# Standards for granting costs on appeal

Distinguishing taxable costs and fee-shifting

Appellate costs fall into two broad categories: taxable costs (statutorily enumerated expenses that the prevailing party can recover under 28 U.S.C. § 1920 and related authorities) and attorneys’ fees or other non-taxable costs recoverable only through specific fee-shifting statutes (e.g., 35 U.S.C. § 285 for patent exceptional cases), contractual fee-shifting clauses, or rule-based sanctions (e.g., appellate rules authorizing sanctions for frivolous appeals).

Taxable costs under § 1920 and rules

Taxable costs include specific categories such as fees of the clerk and marshal, fees for transcripts necessarily obtained for use in the case, printing and witnesses, copying costs (subject to limits), and docket fees. On appeal, a prevailing party may file a bill of costs requesting taxable items, and the appellate rules set timelines and formality for such requests. The appellate court applies statutory and rule-based limits to what may be taxed, and the standard is narrower than for broader fee awards—taxable costs are generally awarded unless the court finds a reason to deny them (e.g., prevailing party is not entitled to costs under relevant rules, the costs were not necessary, or equitable considerations counsel denial).

Appellate sanctions and frivolous appeals

Appellate rules provide mechanisms to penalize frivolous appeals. A court may impose sanctions when an appeal is frivolous, vexatious, or taken for improper purposes. The standard typically requires a showing that the appeal has no reasonable basis in law or fact or is pursued in bad faith. When sanctions are warranted, a court may impose monetary penalties, award fees to the other party, or enter other sanctions tailored to deter similar conduct. Courts often require a fairly high threshold for sanctions because appellate advocacy can involve difficult or novel legal arguments that, while ultimately unsuccessful, are not frivolous.

35 U.S.C. § 285 and “exceptional case” fee shifting

In patent litigation, 35 U.S.C. § 285 authorizes a district court to award reasonable attorneys’ fees to the prevailing party in “exceptional cases.” The standard for an exceptional case traditionally has included situations of bad-faith litigation, willful infringement, frivolous claims, or litigation conduct falling outside the norm. On appeal, the appellate court may review awards under § 285 for abuse of discretion as to the decision to award fees and for correctness of legal interpretations of what constitutes an exceptional case. Appellate review of the amount awarded involves assessment of reasonableness (lodestar, multiplier, and related considerations), which the appellate court reviews for abuse of discretion with deference to the district court’s factual findings about the scope and necessity of the work.

Standards for appellate fee awards

When an appellate court contemplates awarding fees or costs arising from the appeal itself, it considers both the statutory authority invoked and the conduct of the parties on appeal. If the appellant’s arguments are frivolous or the appeal was taken in bad faith, the appellate court may impose sanctions under its rulemaking authority or under federal statutes. When appellate fees are sought pursuant to a substantive statute (such as § 285), the appellate court will evaluate whether the district court properly applied the statute and whether the facts supporting an exceptionality finding were clearly erroneous.

Procedural posture and timing of fee requests

The timely filing and proper preservation of fee requests matter. Fee petitions often must be filed in the district court initially (for § 285 awards) and are then reviewable on appeal. Taxable costs on appeal are typically requested in the appellate court under the appellate rules. Whether the appellate court will entertain a fee request or will remand to the district court for a factual determination depends on the statutory basis and the record; appellate courts often remand if the record lacks findings necessary to resolve disputed factual questions about exceptionality or reasonableness.

Equitable considerations and discretion

Even where statutory authority exists, fee awards are discretionary and equitable. Courts weigh the totality of the circumstances—including the litigation’s substantive merit, the parties’ conduct, the necessity and reasonableness of the incurred fees, and considerations of fairness—before awarding fees or denying them. The appellate court respects the district court’s superior vantage to assess many of these factual and discretionary elements but will correct legal errors or abuses of discretion.

Conclusion

Understanding the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction and choice-of-law framework is crucial for effective appellate practice in patent litigation. The statutory grant in 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) centralizes substantive patent law in the Federal Circuit, but the final judgment rule, preservation doctrines, and regional-circuit procedural law shape what is appealable and how appeals are reviewed. Procedural motions such as JMOL and motions for a new trial are typically governed by regional-circuit law for questions of procedure and preservation, while substantive patent questions—claim construction, infringement, validity, and other patent doctrines—are resolved under Federal Circuit law with an established mix of de novo review for legal conclusions and clear-error review for subsidiary factual findings. Finally, awards of costs and attorneys’ fees on appeal require careful attention to whether the costs are taxable or fee-shifting, the statutory standard invoked (e.g., § 1920 versus § 285), and the discretionary and equitable nature of such awards. Mastery of these allocation rules and standards will arm a law student or practitioner with the framework necessary to analyze appellate briefing, preserve issues in the trial court, and predict how appellate courts will approach the parties’ disputes.

Introduction

Standards of review are the scaffolding of appellate decisionmaking. They determine how an appellate court treats the district court’s factual findings, legal conclusions, discretionary rulings, and jury verdicts. In patent law, where mixed questions of law and fact are routine (infringement and invalidity often turn on technical facts and claim meaning), understanding which standard governs a given ruling is essential both to preserving issues at trial and to framing appellate briefs. This chapter surveys the principal standards of review that structure patent appeals: de novo review of judgments as a matter of law (JMOL) under regional circuit law, substantial-evidence review of jury findings on infringement and damages, the hybrid Teva-style framework for claim construction (legal review with clear-error review of subsidiary facts), abuse-of-discretion review for new-trial orders, and harmless error principles that limit reversal even when mistakes occurred. For each standard the chapter explains the legal definition, who bears the burdens, how courts apply the rule to patent themes (infringement, invalidity, damages, claim construction), and how the procedural posture (motions, timing, preservation) affects appellate analysis.

# De novo review of JMOL decisions under regional circuit law

A judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) challenges the sufficiency of the opponent’s evidentiary showing. In practice, JMOL is typically a two-step process: the defendant (or non-moving party) moves for JMOL at the close of the plaintiff’s case or at the close of all evidence in the district court; if the motion is denied and a jury returns a verdict, the losing party may renew the JMOL motion (often called a renewed JMOL). On appeal the reviewing court asks whether the district court properly granted or denied the motion based on the sufficiency of the evidence.

When appellate review is said to be de novo for JMOL decisions, the appellate court gives no deference to the district court’s legal conclusion about sufficiency; it independently decides whether the record contains legally sufficient evidence to sustain the verdict. But the precise formulation of de novo sufficiency review is shaped by evidentiary presumptions and the applicable regional procedural law. In some procedural contexts the relevant regional circuit’s precedent governs the standard of review. That matters in patent appeals because the Federal Circuit’s procedural law on certain topics (like JMOL) historically has sometimes yielded to the law of the regional circuit that would have had jurisdiction but for the specialized appellate route. The practical consequence is that counsel must be alert both to the controlling standard of review and to the way that standard is articulated in that regional circuit’s precedents.

What de novo sufficiency review typically means in practice

- Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prevailing party. Even under de novo review, appellate courts generally do not reweigh evidence or judge witness credibility. The court accepts the jury’s credibility determinations and draws all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party. The question is whether, on that favorable view, a reasonable jury could have found for the verdict winner.
- No deference to the district court’s ultimate legal determination. The appellate court independently decides whether the legal standard for sufficiency has been met.
- Legal articulation: the reviewing court often asks whether there is “substantial evidence” (or “legally sufficient evidence”) supporting the verdict. When regional circuit law governs, the appellate court applies that circuit’s formulation of “substantial” or “sufficiency” review; the result is typically similar but nuanced differences in phrasing or burden allocation can matter. For example, some circuits emphasize that the evidence must be such that reasonable jurors could fairly conclude the disputed fact, while others articulate a slightly different test; these differences can influence the outcome in close cases.

Special considerations in patent contexts

- Technical fact disputes. JMOL in patent cases often turns on complex technical testimony about infringement (e.g., whether an accused device includes each claim limitation) or about damages (e.g., causation and reasonable royalty calculations). De novo review does not permit the appellate court to substitute its own factual determinations for the jury’s reasonable inferences from expert testimony, but it does permit reevaluation of whether any reasonable jury could have drawn the necessary inferences.
- Claim construction interplay. Because claim construction can be a legal determination, it may be decided de novo by the appellate court and then used as a fixed premise for JMOL review. When claim construction rests on subsidiary factual findings, the standard interplay with JMOL becomes more complex (see the Teva framework below).
- Motion timing and preservation. Appellate courts expect proper preservation of JMOL issues: a party typically must move for JMOL in the district court before the case is submitted to the jury and must renew the motion after verdict to preserve the issue for appeal. Failure to preserve can limit appellate relief and may shift review to plain-error standards.

# Substantial evidence review for jury findings of infringement and damages

Substantial evidence review is the appellate standard applied to factual findings by a jury or by the district court sitting as factfinder when those factual findings are challenged on appeal. The standard asks whether a reasonable mind might accept the evidence as adequate to support the conclusion reached. It is a deferential standard: it does not permit reweighing of evidence, nor does it permit overturning credibility determinations made by the trier of fact.

Core definition and mechanics

- The inquiry. The appellate court determines whether the record contains such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support the jury’s factual finding. If so, the finding stands; if not, the appellate court may reverse or remand.
- Scope. Substantial evidence review looks only at the evidence that supports the verdict; it does not consider evidence favorable to the unsuccessful party that the jury reasonably could have rejected.
- Inferences. The court must respect reasonable inferences drawn by the jury; it does not supplant those inferences with alternative inferences more compelling to the appellate court.
- Credibility. Determinations about witness credibility are for the trier of fact; appellate courts do not reassess credibility under the substantial-evidence standard.

Application to infringement findings

- Element-by-element. Infringement findings require that the accused product or process meets every limitation of the asserted claims. Where the jury finds infringement, an appellant challenging that finding must show that no reasonable jury could have found the necessary fact or inference for one or more claim elements.
- Ultimate facts and mixed questions. Some infringement questions are mixed law-and-fact (e.g., whether the properly construed claim, applied to the accused device, is met). When claim construction is treated as a legal issue, that construction is fixed for the factual sufficiency calculus. If claim construction involves subsidiary facts (for example, the import of extrinsic evidence), those underlying facts will be evaluated for clear error while the ultimate infringement application may still be reviewed for substantial evidence.
- Expert testimony. Much of infringement proof uses expert testimony. Appellate courts applying substantial-evidence review will assess whether the expert’s testimony, combined with demonstrative evidence and other record materials, suffices for a reasonable jury to conclude infringement. Cross-examination and contrary expert testimony are grounds for the jury to reject an expert, and appellate courts defer to that jury choice.

Application to damages findings

- Causation and apportionment. Jury awards of damages require proof of causation—linking the accused product’s use of the patented invention to the harm—and proper apportionment between patented and unpatented features. Appellate review asks whether reasonable jurors had sufficient evidence to find both the amount and the causal relationship.
- Reasonable royalty analysis. Reasonable royalty determinations rely on hypothetical negotiations, comparable licenses, and expert economic analysis. Substantial-evidence review asks whether the record offers reliable, relevant support for the jury’s royalty figure, recognizing that there can be a range of reasonable outcomes and that jurors may choose any figure within that range.
- Jury instructions and legal gateway issues. If legal error (for example, an erroneous instruction defining the damages concept) infected the jury’s damages determination, the appellate court addresses that legal error independently; but the ultimate question of whether record evidence supports an award is one of substantial evidence.

Practical consequences

- Appeal strategy. Under the substantial-evidence standard, an appellant must identify specific gaps in the record rather than urging a wholesale reweighing of evidence. Demonstrating that a crucial fact rests on speculative inferences, or that the necessary evidentiary predicate is absent, is the path to reversal.
- Jury verdicts often stand. Because of its deference, the substantial-evidence standard makes it harder to overturn jury findings than to overturn purely legal rulings.

# De novo review of claim construction with clear-error review for subsidiary fact findings (Teva framework)

Claim construction historically sat at the intersection of law and fact. The modern controlling framework treats claim construction as ultimately a question of law subject to de novo review, but it recognizes that district courts may make subsidiary factual findings in the course of construing claims (for example, findings about the meaning of technical terms to a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA) or about the content of extrinsic evidence). Those subsidiary factual findings are reviewed for clear error; the court’s ultimate legal construction is reviewed de novo.

Structure of the two-tiered inquiry

- The legal question. The core question—what the claim means and how it applies to the accused device—is a matter of law. That legal component is reviewed de novo on appeal. De novo review means the appellate court independently construes the claim language, prosecution history, and intrinsic record to determine meaning.
- Subsidiary factual findings. In the process of construction, district courts may resolve factual disputes: e.g., which definition of a technical term is supported by expert extrinsic evidence, whether a particular teaching in the patent specification would be noticed by a POSITA, or whether a dictionary definition accurately reflects usage in the art. These are factual determinations and the appellate court reviews them for clear error.
- Interaction on appeal. The appellate court starts by reviewing the district court’s factual findings for clear error. If clear error is found in a factual predicate, that can change the legal construction. If no clear error is found, the appellate court then independently construes the claims based on those facts and the intrinsic evidence.

What constitutes clear error in this context

- Definition. Clear error is a highly deferential standard. The appellate court will only set aside the district court’s factual finding if, after reviewing the entire evidence, it is left with the definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made.
- Practical application. Because claims commonly turn on technical background and expert evidence, clear-error review preserves the district court’s advantage in hearing live testimony and assessing witness credibility. Appellate reversal on these subsidiary findings is therefore uncommon unless the district court’s factual findings are unsupported by the record or are internally inconsistent.

How courts apply the framework to common claim-construction issues

- Intrinsic vs. extrinsic evidence. Intrinsic evidence (the claim language, the specification, and the prosecution history) is the primary source for claim meaning. Extrinsic evidence (dictionaries, treatises, expert testimony) may inform subsidiary factual issues (e.g., ordinary meaning in the field). Since extrinsic evidence often underpins factual determinations, clear-error review protects the district court’s credibility assessments about such evidence.
- Claim terms of art and POSITA understanding. When experts disagree about a term’s meaning in the art, the district court may credit one expert and make a factual determination about the POSITA’s understanding. That factual finding is reviewed for clear error, while the ultimate claim construction remains legal.
- Disputed factual predicates and legal conflict. If an appellant can show that a claimed factual finding has no support in the record, the appellate court may set it aside and then perform de novo claim construction. The result can change whether asserted claims are interpreted to cover accused products or processes.

Implications for motions and appellate briefing

- Preservation. Parties must develop the factual record at the district court, including through expert testimony and contested exhibits, because appellate review of those facts is deferential. Weak factual presentations in the district court are unlikely to be cured on appeal.
- Focus of appellate briefs. On appeal, counsel must separate arguments: challenge factual findings by showing clear error (pointing to insufficient or contradictory evidence); challenge the legal construction de novo by offering alternative readings grounded in the intrinsic record and proper legal canons of construction.
- Timing of JMOL and summary judgment. Claim construction rulings often occur pretrial (summary judgment or Markman hearings) and shape JMOL motions. Because claim construction is legal on appeal, parties should preserve claim-construction challenges both in the district court and on appeal.

# Abuse of discretion review for new-trial rulings

A new trial is an equitable remedy in which the district court, exercising its discretion, sets aside a judgment and orders a retrial because of trial error, injustice, or other appropriate reasons. Appellate courts review a district court’s order granting or denying a new trial for abuse of discretion. This review affords substantial deference to the district court’s judgment about whether the trial’s fairness or integrity was compromised.

What abuse of discretion means

- Deference to district judge’s judgment. Abuse of discretion occurs when the district court’s decision is based on an erroneous view of the law, a clearly erroneous assessment of the evidence, or a manifestly unreasonable application of the law to the facts. The reviewing court does not substitute its own judgment unless the lower court’s choice falls outside the range of reasonable alternatives.
- Context matters. Where the district court’s new-trial decision is premised on a legal conclusion (for example, an incorrect understanding of what constitutes reversibility), appellate review may be mixed: the legal premise may be reviewed de novo, while the ultimate exercise of discretion remains reviewed for abuse.
- Standard degrees. Abuse-of-discretion review is deferential, but the strength of deference may vary depending on whether the district court weighed credibility and demeanor (more deference) or made a legal ruling about admissibility or instruction (less deference concerning legal questions).

Grounds for new-trial motions in patent cases

- Verdict against the weight of the evidence. A party may seek a new trial on the ground that the jury’s verdict is against the manifest weight of the evidence. District courts are permitted to order a new trial when they conclude that the verdict is seriously erroneous even though some evidence supports it. Appellate courts seldom reverse such discretionary determinations.
- Errors of law during trial. Significant legal errors—erroneous evidentiary rulings, misleading jury instructions, or improper conduct by counsel that materially affected the verdict—can be grounds for a new trial. On appeal, the correctness of the underlying law is reviewed de novo; if the legal error is established, the appellate court then evaluates whether the district court abused its discretion in denying or granting a new trial.
- Procedural irregularities and jury misconduct. Where irregularities or misconduct undermined the jury’s deliberative process, a new trial may be ordered. Appellate review again is abuse of discretion, mindful of the district court’s ability to evaluate candor, credibility, and immediate remedial steps.

Specific considerations in patent litigation

- Complex technical and damages evidence. District courts are often better positioned to assess whether a jury—presented with dense technical and economic testimony—reached a reliable verdict or whether confusion and misapplication of law warrant a retrial.
- Balancing fairness and finality. Patent cases can be expensive and protracted. District courts weigh both the fairness of a possible retrial and the burden on the parties. Appellate courts respect that balancing unless it is unreasonable.
- Relationship to JMOL. A grant of JMOL and a denial of a new trial present different bases for post-trial relief. A district court denying JMOL but granting a new trial is exercising discretion because it finds the verdict legally sufficient but unfair or unreliable. Appellate review will respect that nuanced judgment unless it is outside reasonable bounds.

Practical advice

- Preserve objections and move timely. To preserve new-trial issues, object contemporaneously at trial and, if seeking a new trial, file the post-trial motion under the applicable rule and within the deadline. Failure to do so can curtail appellate relief.
- Develop the record. Because appellate courts give considerable weight to the district court’s view of trial dynamics, thorough record development and post-trial briefing are crucial to persuading the trial court—and later the appellate court—of the need for a new trial.

# Harmless error principles

Harmless error doctrine governs whether an appellate court must reverse a judgment when trial error is established. The doctrine reflects the policy that not all mistakes justify reversal; errors that did not affect substantial rights or the outcome are to be left undisturbed. In patent appeals, harmless error analysis can decide whether an erroneous evidentiary ruling, instruction, or procedural mistake requires a new trial or whether the verdict stands notwithstanding the mistake.

The general test for harmless error

- Prejudice requirement. An error is harmless if the appellate court is convinced beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the verdict (in some contexts) or, more generally in civil cases, did not affect the substantial rights of the parties. In civil appeals the inquiry is typically whether the error had a substantial and injurious effect or influence on the jury’s verdict.
- Burden of persuasion. The party benefitting from the error often bears the burden of showing that the error was harmless, though in some formulations the appellant must demonstrate that the error was not harmless. The allocation of burden can depend on whether the error was preserved or plain.
- Preserved vs. plain error. When a party preserved the objection at trial, appellate review assesses harmlessness under the preserved-error standard; when an issue was not preserved, plain-error review limits reversal to particularly egregious mistakes affecting substantial rights and the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings.

Applying harmless-error analysis in patent contexts

- Erroneous claim construction. If a court applied an incorrect claim construction at trial, appellate courts first determine whether the error was preserved and then whether it was prejudicial—i.e., whether a correct construction would have changed the outcome on infringement or invalidity. If the record shows that the same evidence would have produced the same result under the correct construction, the error may be deemed harmless.
- Evidentiary rulings. The admission of improperly admitted expert testimony or the exclusion of critical evidence requires analysis of whether the misstep materially affected the jury’s verdict or the district court’s decision. If the erroneous evidence was cumulative or minor relative to the overwhelming contrary proof, it may be harmless.
- Jury instructions. Incorrect instructions are analyzed to determine whether they likely led the jury to a different result. Appellate courts consider the instructions as a whole and whether, despite the error, the jury necessarily would have returned the same verdict.
- Interplay with substantial-evidence review. Even when an error is found, substantial-evidence support for the verdict can lead to a finding of harmless error: if the record contains ample support for the verdict independent of the error, appellate courts may affirm.

Special tools and practical points

- Harmless beyond a reasonable doubt vs. substantial-rights test. Criminal and some constitutional contexts use the “harmless beyond a reasonable doubt” phrasing; civil patent appeals typically use a “substantial rights” or “substantial and injurious effect” formulation. Counsel must frame arguments under the correct standard.
- Nonconstitutional vs. constitutional errors. Constitutional errors (for example, Seventh Amendment or due process problems) may trigger a stricter harmlessness test; ordinary evidentiary mistakes are usually reviewed under the civil harmless-error formulation.
- Cumulative errors. Multiple minor errors that individually might be harmless can, in aggregate, have a prejudicial effect and warrant reversal or remand; appellate courts will assess whether the cumulative impact undermines confidence in the outcome.
- Remedial options. If an error is reversible, appellate remedies may include remanding for a new trial on limited issues, remanding for additional factfinding (especially where subsidiary factual findings are implicated), or entering judgment as a matter of law if the record compels a particular outcome.

Conclusion

Standards of review are decisive in patent appeals because they govern how much deference an appellate court affords district-court factfinding, legal rulings, and discretionary choices. De novo review of JMOL claims permits independent assessment of evidentiary sufficiency but still respects the jury’s reasonable inferences; substantial-evidence review is a deferential test that preserves jury determinations unless no reasonable evidence supports them; the Teva framework reconciles legal and factual components in claim construction by applying de novo review to ultimate legal construction and clear-error review to subsidiary factual findings; abuse-of-discretion review protects the district court’s judgment about new trials except where that judgment is unreasonable; and harmless error principles limit reversal to errors that actually or substantially affected the outcome. For trial counsel and appellate advocates alike, the practical lesson is straightforward: develop the record on contested facts; preserve objections; articulate legal grounds clearly at the district level; and tailor appellate arguments to the controlling standard of review, showing either that the record lacks any reasonable basis for the district court’s or jury’s decision or that, even if an error occurred, it was harmless.

Introduction

Post-trial motions that challenge a jury verdict serve several distinct but related functions. They allow the trial judge to correct errors that occurred during trial, to resolve persistent legal questions about the sufficiency of the evidence, and to reduce or avoid unnecessary appeals by either directing judgment in favor of a party or ordering a new trial. In the federal system, the principal post-verdict mechanisms are Rule 50 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure (judgment as a matter of law, commonly called JMOL and historically called "JNOV" or "directed verdict"/"judgment notwithstanding the verdict") and Rule 59 (new trial). Successful use of these procedural devices requires precise timing, careful preservation at trial, and strategic judgment about whether to seek relief under Rule 50, Rule 59, or both. Courts apply different legal standards and review doctrines to Rule 50 and Rule 59 motions, and appellate remedies differ depending on what relief the district court grants or denies. This chapter explains the governing rules, the typical tests and burdens, the consequences of failing to preserve objections, and the appellate framework for reviewing JMOL and new-trial decisions.

# Rule 50(a) pre-verdict motions and issue preservation

Rule 50(a) governs the pre-verdict motion for judgment as a matter of law. In substance, a party may move for judgment as a matter of law during trial, and it must do so before the case is submitted to the jury if the party believes there is insufficient legally sufficient evidence to support a claim or defense. The rule has two core purposes. First, it prevents a case that cannot legally succeed from going to the jury, preserving judicial resources and protecting parties from irrelevant deliberation. Second, it preserves the sufficiency-of-the-evidence challenge so that the party may later renew that challenge after the verdict under Rule 50(b).

Timing and form. The critical procedural requirement of Rule 50(a) is temporal: the motion must be made at a point when the court can grant the motion without a jury verdict in hand—generally after the opposing party has presented its evidence and before the case is submitted to the jury. In practice, that often means the defendant moves after the plaintiff rests, and the plaintiff must move after the defendant rests if the plaintiff bears the burden of proof. A Rule 50(a) motion must specify the grounds on which the movant contends that judgment should be directed. Although Rule 50(a) does not require a particular length or detailed argument, the motion must adequately identify the legal theory of insufficiency (for example, lack of evidentiary support for a required element) so the court and opposing party know what is at issue.

Preservation function. Rule 50(a) serves as an indispensable preservation device. Failure to make a proper Rule 50(a) motion generally forecloses the movant from raising sufficiency of the evidence on appeal or renewing that challenge in a post-verdict Rule 50(b) motion. The rule thus forces litigants to crystallize the sufficiency argument at trial so the court and opposing party have a chance to correct the perceived inadequacy in presentation or proof and ensures that the trial judge has the opportunity to take the case from the jury before the verdict. The preservation requirement is strict: the motion must fairly present the grounds for the challenge. A general, conclusory, or late remark that does not assert a basis recognized by Rule 50(a) may fail to preserve the claim.

Substantive sufficiency standard. When a party makes a Rule 50(a) motion, the judge must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party and draw all reasonable inferences in that party's favor. The motion should be granted only if a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for the nonmoving party on the issue. Ordinarily courts do not weigh the evidence, judge credibility, or resolve conflicts in the testimony at that stage; those functions are reserved to the jury. The focus is solely whether the nonmoving party presented evidence on which a reasonable juror could rely to find for that party as to the particular claim or defense.

Practical tips. Practitioners should make Rule 50(a) motions every time there is a genuine argument that the opponent has failed to present legally sufficient evidence, even if the relief is unlikely. A properly made Rule 50(a) motion preserves the issue for a renewed motion under Rule 50(b) and for appeal. The motion should clearly identify the elements (or legal theory) on which the evidence is insufficient and, where relevant, cite the record, evidence omissions, absence of essential testimony, or legal deficiencies.

# Rule 50(b) renewed JMOL requirements and scope

Rule 50(b) governs the renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law made after a jury verdict. It is often called a "renewed JMOL" and is the modern analogue of the historical JNOV motion. A successful Rule 50(b) motion results in the entry of judgment in favor of the moving party notwithstanding the jury’s verdict; the district court may also grant partial JMOL as to particular issues.

Timing and procedural prerequisites. A renewed JMOL under Rule 50(b) cannot be made de novo for the first time after verdict; its availability is grounded in the earlier Rule 50(a) motion. Thus, the central prerequisite is that the moving party actually made a timely Rule 50(a) motion and preserved the same issue. Absent that, Rule 50(b) is typically unavailable. When the Rule 50(a) motion has been made, the Rule 50(b) motion must be filed no later than 28 days after the entry of judgment. The 28-day deadline is jurisdictional in practice: failure to file within that period usually bars the motion.

Scope of the renewed JMOL. The scope of a Rule 50(b) motion is limited to the grounds asserted in the pre-verdict Rule 50(a) motion. The renewed motion may not raise new arguments that were not presented in the Rule 50(a) motion, because one function of the Rule 50(a) motion is to give the trial court and opposing counsel notice and the opportunity to correct deficiencies before submission to the jury. Thus, the renewed motion must be confined to the legal theories and factual bases that were reasonably included in the earlier motion. If a party wishes to press additional arguments based on trial developments, it must ensure those arguments were preserved in Rule 50(a) or risk forfeiture.

Standard of review at the district and appellate levels. On a Rule 50(b) motion, the district court applies the same sufficiency standard it would have applied on a Rule 50(a) motion: viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party and drawing all reasonable inferences for that party, the court asks whether a reasonable jury could find for the nonmoving party. If the court concludes no reasonable jury could do so, it must direct judgment as a matter of law. However, if it finds that reasonable jurors could differ, it must deny the renewed motion.

On appeal, when an appellant challenges the district court's denial of a Rule 50(b) motion, the appellate court reviews de novo whether the evidence presented at trial was legally sufficient to support the jury verdict on the preserved question. De novo review means the appellate court reexamines the record without deference to the district court’s legal conclusion about sufficiency. The appellate court still applies the familiar standard of viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict winner and drawing all reasonable inferences in that party's favor, but the appellate court decides the sufficiency question anew.

Partial JMOL and alternative relief. Rule 50(b) permits a district court to grant judgment as a matter of law on some issues while submitting others to the jury or leaving certain claims intact. A court may also conditionally grant JMOL in situations where legal entitlement is present but mistrial or remittitur may be appropriate. When JMOL is granted post-verdict, the court must enter judgment accordingly; when it is denied, the party can seek a new trial under Rule 59 or appeal the denial after judgment.

# Rule 59 motions for new trial: grounds and standards

Rule 59 provides the mechanism for seeking a new trial or to alter or amend a judgment. Unlike Rule 50, which focuses on legal sufficiency, Rule 59 empowers the court to grant a new trial on a broad range of grounds, including but not limited to errors of law, errors in the admission or exclusion of evidence, juror misconduct, irregularities in the proceedings that prevented a fair trial, verdicts against the weight of the evidence, and excessive or inadequate damages. Rule 59 is remedial and discretionary in character: it gives the trial court latitude to correct not just necessarily erroneous legal conclusions but also trial errors or verdicts that are substantially unfair.

Common grounds for new trial. Courts regularly consider several nonexclusive grounds when evaluating Rule 59 motions:

- Errors of law. If evidentiary rulings, jury instructions, or procedural errors so infected the trial that they prejudiced a party’s substantial rights, a new trial may be warranted. The key inquiry is whether the error was prejudicial—that is, whether it affected the outcome in a meaningful way.

- Verdict contrary to weight of the evidence. Unlike Rule 50, which focuses on legal insufficiency and does not permit reweighing evidence, Rule 59 allows the judge to conclude that the jury's verdict is against the clear weight of the evidence. This ground often arises when the judge believes the jury reached an unreasonable result despite the legally sufficient record. When a judge grants a new trial on this basis, the court has effectively determined it would not have reached the same conclusion as the jury considering all the evidence and with the trial judge’s view of credibility and weight.

- Substantial trial irregularities or misconduct. Juror misconduct, attorney misconduct, failure to follow court orders, or extraneous influence on the jury can justify a new trial.

- Newly discovered evidence. If material evidence emerges after trial that could not reasonably have been discovered before trial and would probably produce a different result, a new trial may be ordered to consider that evidence.

- Excessive or inadequate damages. Where the damages award is clearly excessive or inadequate as a matter of fact, a judge may grant a new trial on damages. Federal courts also use remittitur (reducing an excessive award and offering the plaintiff the choice between accepting the reduced amount or undergoing a new trial) or additur (in some circuits, adding to an inadequate award) as alternatives.

Standards of decision. The standard a district court uses in deciding a Rule 59 motion is generally broad discretion and a flexible balancing of factors. The Court should not grant a new trial lightly; the decision reflects the trial court’s superior vantage point for judging the fairness of the trial, assessing credibility, and weighing evidence. Appellate courts review a district court’s Rule 59 decisions for abuse of discretion. That is, reversal is appropriate only when the district court’s decision is grounded in a clear error of judgment or where the court applied incorrect legal standards.

Distinction from JMOL. The conceptual distinction between Rule 50 and Rule 59 is important. Rule 50 addresses legal sufficiency and prevents unjustified jury findings; it does not permit the court to reweigh evidence or substitute the judge's view for the jury’s unless no reasonable jury could have found as it did. Rule 59, by contrast, permits a district judge to grant a new trial when a verdict is against the weight of the evidence or when other trial errors produced an unjust result. For that reason, litigants sometimes file both a renewed Rule 50(b) motion and a Rule 59 motion: the 50(b) argues the verdict cannot stand as a matter of law; the 59 motion asks the court to exercise its equitable discretion to order a new trial if the 50(b) is denied.

Procedural details and timing. A Rule 59 motion must be filed no later than 28 days after the entry of judgment. Timely filing preserves a number of appellate rights and tolls the time for taking an appeal in many circumstances. The Rule 59 motion should articulate specific grounds; boilerplate assertions are less persuasive. When a Rule 59 motion and a Rule 50(b) motion are both asserted, courts typically consider the Rule 50(b) renewed motion first because a grant of JMOL may obviate the need for a new trial.

# Waiver and forfeiture tied to Rule 50 timing

Because Rule 50(a) is the preservation device for a challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence, failure to make a proper Rule 50(a) motion typically results in waiver or forfeiture of that argument. Courts treat the preservation requirement strictly for several reasons: it affords the trial court an opportunity to resolve evidentiary or legal insufficiency before the jury decides the case; it gives opposing counsel notice and the opportunity to correct gaps in proof; and it preserves the trial record for meaningful appellate review.

Distinguishing waiver from forfeiture. Different doctrines (waiver, forfeiture) are invoked depending on whether the party intentionally relinquished a known right (waiver) or failed to take timely action (forfeiture). Under both labels, however, the practical consequence is similar: an appellate court will often refuse to consider a sufficiency challenge that was not raised under Rule 50(a) before trial submission. The federal rules leave little room for raising sufficiency arguments for the first time on appeal, except under narrow plain-error review. Plain-error review is exceptional and requires showing that an obvious error affected substantial rights and that the error seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the judicial proceedings. Courts rarely grant such relief for failure to file Rule 50(a), because Rule 50(a) exists to allow the trial court to fix issues without recourse to appellate intervention.

What constitutes a proper Rule 50(a) motion for preservation? For a Rule 50(a) motion to preserve an argument, it must be timely and must articulate a legal ground that reasonably apprises the court and opposing counsel of the insufficiency claim. Vague or generalized motions that fail to connect the deficiency to a particular element or legal principle may not preserve the issue. Likewise, asserting only evidentiary objections (for example, contemporaneous objections to questions or exhibits) does not necessarily preserve a sufficiency argument under Rule 50 unless those objections explicitly request that judgment be directed because of the insufficiency of proof.

Exceptions and interplay with other rules. There are limited circumstances in which an appellate court may consider the sufficiency of the evidence despite the absence of a timely Rule 50(a) motion—principally via plain-error review or other exceptional doctrines such as fundamental miscarriage of justice. Additionally, in bench trials the preservation rule is different: Rule 52(b) permits a party to file motion to amend findings of fact and conclusions of law, and appellate courts generally review sufficiency de novo in nonjury trials because there is no jury verdict to which to defer. But in jury trials under Rule 50, failure to preserve the issue at trial is a critical procedural mistake.

Strategic implications. Given the harsh consequences of failing to preserve, litigators should make well-crafted Rule 50(a) motions whenever a sufficiency argument exists, even if the motion seems unlikely to succeed. The motion should be specific, state the precise basis for insufficiency (which element lacks proof), and be timely. Because Rule 50(b) depends on Rule 50(a), preservation serves not just immediate trial strategy but also appellate strategy.

# Appellate remedies after JMOL and new-trial rulings

The appellate consequences of district court rulings on Rule 50(b) and Rule 59 motions differ depending on whether the district court grants or denies relief and on what form of relief is granted. Appellate courts apply distinct standards of review and may fashion remedies that reflect the interplay between legal and equitable power.

Appellate review after denial of renewed JMOL. When a district court denies a Rule 50(b) renewed motion, the movant may appeal from the final judgment. On appeal, the sufficiency-of-the-evidence question is reviewed de novo, with the appellate court viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict winner and drawing all reasonable inferences in that party’s favor. If the appellate court concludes the evidence was insufficient as a matter of law, it may reverse the judgment and enter judgment for the moving party. That is, the appellate court may grant judgment as a matter of law in place of the jury verdict. In some cases, an appellate court that finds insufficiency will remand with instructions to enter judgment for the moving party; in other cases, the appellate court may order a new trial limited to certain issues if the record reveals disputed factual issues that must be resolved or if equitable considerations counsel against ordering entry of judgment.

Appellate review after grant of renewed JMOL. If the district court grants JMOL under Rule 50(b), the losing party may appeal that judgment. The appellate court reviews the sufficiency determination de novo. If the appellate court agrees that no reasonable jury could have found for the verdict winner, it will affirm the judgment. If the appellate court disagrees and concludes the evidence could support the jury verdict, it will typically reverse and remand for entry of judgment in favor of the previous verdict winner or for a new trial as appropriate.

Appellate review after denial of new trial. When a district court denies a Rule 59 motion for a new trial, the appellate court generally reviews the denial for abuse of discretion. That is a deferential standard: the appellate court will affirm unless the district court committed a clear error of judgment (for example, applied an incorrect legal standard, or reached a conclusion that no reasonable judge could reach). The underlying legal issues that formed the basis for the Rule 59 motion—such as alleged errors in jury instructions or the admission of evidence—may also be reviewed on appeal; depending on preservation and the type of error, appellate review may be de novo or for harmless error. But as to the new-trial exercise of discretion—whether to grant a new trial because the verdict is against the weight of the evidence—appellate courts give substantial deference to the trial judge's superior position.

Appellate review after grant of new trial. When a district court grants a new trial under Rule 59, appellate review is limited because there is no final judgment for the prior trial; the grant of a new trial typically produces a new trial and resumption of the litigation in the district court. In some contexts, a grant of a new trial on certain issues may be treated as an appealable order if it has the characteristics of a final decision or if the appellate court finds an interlocutory path. But generally, the grant of a new trial is not appealable until a final judgment is entered after retrial, though there are exceptions under the collateral order doctrine or other jurisdictional principles that rarely apply.

Conditional rulings and alternative remedies. District courts sometimes act conditionally: for example, after denying renewal of JMOL they may grant a new trial. Conversely, after granting JMOL a court may allow the defeated party some relief in the form of a remittitur or conditionally grant a new trial on damages. On appeal, the existence of conditional rulings can complicate review: an appellate court must determine which interlocutory or final orders are reviewable and how to treat alternative grounds for relief. Appellate courts will also consider whether the district court acted within its equitable powers and whether any remedial ordering (such as remittitur) respects constitutional rights (e.g., Seventh Amendment concerns in civil cases).

Interplay between Rule 50(b) and Rule 59 on appeal. Because a litigant often files both a renewed JMOL and a Rule 59 motion, understanding appellate timing is crucial. If a district court grants a new trial under Rule 59 after denying Rule 50(b), there may be questions about whether the denying order is appealable, since the grant of a new trial generally moots the judgment. If the district court denies both the Rule 50(b) and Rule 59 motions and the moving party appeals, the appellate court will review the sufficiency question de novo (from Rule 50(b) denial) and will review the denial of Rule 59 for abuse of discretion. The availability of these multiple standards often leads litigants to pursue both forms of relief because they preserve different theories and review pathways for appellate consideration.

Practical appellate strategies. Litigators should bear in mind that Rule 50(b) is the vehicle to preserve legal sufficiency claims for appellate de novo review. If that relief is denied, a Rule 59 motion gives the trial judge the opportunity to order a new trial, and denial of the Rule 59 will be reviewed deferentially. On appeal, plaintiffs and defendants should frame the issues to highlight the correct standard of review: errors of law and sufficiency-of-the-evidence questions invite de novo review, while discretionary decisions about ordering a new trial typically attract abuse-of-discretion review.

Conclusion

Rule 50 and Rule 59 are powerful tools for litigants who believe a jury verdict is legally or factually unsupportable. Effective use of these rules depends on careful preservation at the proper times, precise articulation of the legal deficiencies or trial errors, and a realistic assessment of the range of remedies available. Rule 50(a) is the critical preservation mechanism that enables a renewed JMOL under Rule 50(b) and de novo appellate review of sufficiency claims. Rule 59 grants a district court equitable discretion to order a new trial for a broad array of reasons, but appellate review of those discretionary decisions is deferential. Failure to make timely, specific Rule 50(a) motions or to meet the procedural demands of Rule 50(b) and Rule 59 often results in waiver and limits appellate options. Mastery of these doctrines will allow a practitioner to preserve issues effectively, to choose the most advantageous post-trial motion strategy, and to anticipate how appellate courts will treat the district court’s rulings.

Introduction

This chapter explains the fundamental legal framework for analyzing patent infringement claims, with particular emphasis on proving literal infringement of method (process) claims. Before reading judicial opinions that apply these principles to particular facts, a student should understand the statutory baselines for infringement and the basic litigation burdens, the "all-elements" requirement for claim-by-claim proof, how courts distinguish literal infringement from infringement under the doctrine of equivalents, and the special considerations that arise when the asserted claims are method claims. This chapter also explains how the presumption of patent validity interacts with the parties’ burdens of proof.

# 35 U.S.C. § 271 and types of infringement

The principal statutory provisions that define what acts constitute infringement are found in 35 U.S.C. § 271. Section 271 sets out several distinct types of infringing conduct, each of which imposes liability under different factual patterns:

- Direct infringement of an apparatus, product, or manufacture typically occurs under § 271(a) when a party without authority makes, uses, sells, offers to sell, or imports a patented invention into the United States. For product and apparatus claims, the typical analysis asks whether the accused product embodies every element of the asserted claim as properly construed.

- Direct infringement of a method (process) claim is also governed by § 271(a). For method claims, liability arises when a party performs all of the claimed steps. The performance requirement is central to method-claim infringement and is discussed in detail later.

- Induced infringement is governed by § 271(b). A party may be liable if it actively induces another party to infringe a patent. Inducement requires an underlying act of direct infringement by someone other than the inducer; the inducement statute does not itself create liability unless there is direct infringement to be induced. Courts focus on whether the inducer knew of the patent and took affirmative steps to encourage infringement with the requisite intent.

- Contributory infringement is addressed by § 271(c). This provision makes it unlawful to sell or offer to sell a component of a patented machine, manufacture, combination or composition, or a material or apparatus for use in practicing a patented process, when the component is a material part of the invention and is not a staple article suitable for substantial noninfringing use, with knowledge that the component is especially made or adapted for use in infringement. Contributory infringement therefore targets suppliers of components that enable infringement.

These statutory categories are analytically distinct and sometimes interact. For example, inducement liability ordinarily depends on a showing that another party directly infringed the patent. Similarly, contributory infringement requires proof of the component’s special adaptation and the defendant’s knowledge. In all types, claim construction—the court’s interpretation of the patent claims—frames the question of whether the accused conduct falls within the claim scope.

# All-elements rule and element-by-element proof

The all-elements rule is a core doctrinal principle: to prove literal infringement of a claim, the patentee must show that the accused device, product, or practice contains every limitation (element or step) of the asserted claim as properly construed. This is often summarized as an element-by-element or limitation-by-limitation comparison: identify the claim limitations (after claim construction), determine their meaning, and then compare the accused instrumentality or conduct to each limitation. If any claim limitation is missing or not met, there is no literal infringement of that claim.

Several points about how courts apply the all-elements rule are important:

Claim construction precedes infringement analysis. Courts construe the claim terms to determine the metes and bounds of the claim’s limitations. Claim construction is typically a legal determination (subject to deference to certain subsidiary factual findings in some circumstances), and it shapes the element-by-element comparison. Once the court construes the claim terms, the finder of fact (judge when equitable; jury when factual disputes exist) compares the adjudicated facts to the construed claim elements to determine whether each limitation is present in the accused product or method.

Literal inclusion required for each limitation. Literal infringement requires that, for each limitation, the accused product or act literally meet the limitation as construed. If the accused device only performs something similar but not the same as a claim limitation, then literal infringement is not established for that limitation; the patentee may need to rely on doctrine of equivalents principles to cover insubstantial differences (discussed below).

Parties may attack or defend particular limitations. Litigation often focuses on disputed claim limitations—terms whose meaning is unclear or whose presence in the accused device is in dispute. A patentee must prove each such limitation by a preponderance of the evidence (discussed below). Defendants may concede certain limitations or contest them with technical arguments or expert testimony.

Dependent claims and alternative claim constructions. A claim may contain alternative or conjunctive limitations. For example, a claim may read "A device comprising A or B," or "A device comprising A and B." The all-elements rule operates on whatever the claim requires. Dependent claims carry additional limitations that narrow the independent claim; proving infringement of a dependent claim requires proving the additional limitations too.

The all-elements rule reflects the textual nature of patent rights: the patent grants a right to exclude corresponding to the language of the claims. Courts therefore rigorously apply the rule to avoid expanding patent scope beyond the claim language.

# Literal infringement vs. doctrine of equivalents (overview)

Courts recognize two principal ways a patentee can prevail on an infringement theory: literal infringement and infringement under the doctrine of equivalents. These are complementary but distinct doctrines.

Literal infringement. Literal infringement requires that the accused product or method contain each claim limitation exactly as construed. The all-elements rule is the governing principle. If the accused device or practice literally satisfies each limitation, infringement is established without recourse to equivalence analysis.

Doctrine of equivalents (DOE) — overview. The doctrine of equivalents provides a limited mechanism for a patentee to capture accused devices or practices that do not literally meet every claim limitation but nonetheless perform substantially the same function in substantially the same way to achieve substantially the same result, or otherwise are insubstantially different from the claimed invention. The function-way-result test is a common articulation of DOE analysis, though courts may apply other formulations such as the "insubstantial differences" test. DOE is a species of equitable relief that prevents an alleged infringer from escaping liability through insubstantial or colorable variations that copy the essence of the patented invention.

Key limitations on DOE. DOE is a narrow doctrine and courts have established several limits:

- Prosecution history estoppel. Statements, amendments, or disclaimers made during patent prosecution can limit the patentee’s ability to assert equivalent coverage for surrendered subject matter. If a patentee narrows a claim to obtain allowance, it may be estopped from later asserting equivalents that would encompass what was surrendered, although the precise scope of estoppel depends on the reasons for the amendment and other circumstances.

- Public notice and claims delineation. The patent system is premised on public notice—claim language should provide clear boundaries of what is claimed. DOE is applied sparingly to avoid eroding that notice function.

- Objective indicia. Courts consider whether applying DOE would vitiate an entire claim limitation or essentially read the claim out of the patent. DOE cannot be used to eliminate a claim element altogether.

Interaction in litigation. Procedurally, a patentee typically presents proof of literal infringement first, and if literal infringement is not shown, may present DOE as an alternative theory. Courts and juries often assess DOE only after claim construction establishes the literal boundaries. Because DOE is equitable in nature, judges retain significant control over whether DOE instructions are appropriate for juries and how to apply the doctrine in particular factual contexts.

# Direct infringement of method claims and performance of each step

Method or process claims present particular features in infringement analysis because their limitations are recited as steps to be performed rather than structural elements of a device. Under § 271(a), direct infringement of a method claim occurs when a single entity performs all the claimed steps. When analyzing a claim for infringement, courts apply the all-elements rule to steps: the patentee must prove that each claimed step was performed.

Single-actor rule. Traditionally, direct infringement of method claims requires that one party perform every step. If a single actor performs each step, whether sequentially or concurrently, that performance meets the literal infringement standard once each step has been proved to have occurred. Proof commonly depends on documentary evidence, logs, witness testimony, expert analysis, or other demonstrative evidence showing that the asserted steps were performed in the claimed manner and order (when order is recited and material to the claim).

Divided or multi-actor performance. Complications arise when multiple parties collectively perform steps of a claimed method. If no single actor performs every step, the patentee faces an evidentiary and legal hurdle: how to attribute others’ performance to a single liable entity. Courts have developed frameworks for attribution in such cases. The key legal question is whether one party can be held responsible for another party's performance so that, in law, a single entity is effectively performing all steps.

Common bases for attribution include agency, contract, or direction/control. For example, if the alleged infringer directs or controls the performance of others—by contracting with them to perform claimed steps, instructing them to act in a particular way, or establishing a joint enterprise—courts may treat the performance as attributable to the defendant for purposes of § 271(a). The specific doctrinal tests vary by jurisdiction and circumstance, but the core idea is that liability can attach where one party exercises control over the performance of steps by others or where the actions are attributable under general principles of law (agency, joint enterprise, or established contractual obligations).

Inducement and induced method infringement. When steps are performed by multiple actors who are not under the control of a single entity, the patentee may still pursue a claim for induced infringement under § 271(b) if the defendant knowingly induced others to perform the steps and had the requisite intent to encourage infringement. Inducement requires proof that (1) the defendant knew of the patent, (2) there was direct infringement by another (the steps were performed), and (3) the defendant took active steps to induce infringement with the requisite intent. The existence of directed actions, promotional materials, instructions, or other conduct encouraging third parties to perform steps can give rise to inducement liability.

Evidence and timing. Proving performance of method steps often turns on temporal and factual detail: when steps were taken, by whom, and how often. For claims that require particular sequences, timing evidence is critical. A patentee must show by a preponderance of the evidence that each step was performed in the claimed manner and order (if order is a limitation). Expert testimony frequently plays a role in establishing technical steps and translating business records or forensic data into a finding that the claim steps were performed.

# Burden of proof (preponderance) and presumption of validity (35 U.S.C. § 282)

Burden of proof on infringement. The patentee bears the burden of proving infringement. The standard of proof for infringement is the preponderance of the evidence: the patentee must prove it is more likely than not that the accused device or conduct meets every limitation of the asserted claim (or, alternatively, that it is equivalent under DOE). The preponderance standard applies to the fact-intensive element-by-element comparison, including proof that all steps of a method claim were performed by the accused infringer (or attributable to it).

Presumption of validity and burden for invalidity. At the same time, patents are accorded a statutory presumption of validity under 35 U.S.C. § 282. That statute states that a patent shall be presumed valid, and that the patent owner may enforce rights as set forth therein. In litigation, § 282 has been interpreted to mean that a party challenging a patent's validity bears the burden of proving invalidity. Historically, the Supreme Court has required the challenger to meet a heightened standard—clear and convincing evidence—to overcome the presumption of validity in district court litigation. Thus, many courts instruct that a defendant must prove by clear and convincing evidence that the asserted claims are invalid (for anticipation, obviousness, lack of enablement, lack of written description, indefiniteness, or other statutory grounds). The interplay between the presumption of validity and evidentiary standards can affect litigation strategy: a defendant seeking to avoid liability by proving invalidity faces a more demanding standard than the patentee faces when proving infringement.

Interaction of burdens in practice. The litigation posture often involves dual showings: the patentee must prove infringement by a preponderance, and the defendant may respond with a challenge to validity that must meet the clear and convincing standard. The net effect is that a patentee has a relatively easier task to show infringement than a defendant has to show invalidity. That said, certain procedural contexts—such as inter partes review (IPR) proceedings before the Patent Trial and Appeal Board—employ different standards and burdens; BIA and administrative proceedings use the administrative standard of preponderance of the evidence or other applicable standards. But in federal district court litigation, § 282’s presumption and the clear-and-convincing standard historically govern invalidity defenses.

Burden on inducement and intent. For indirect infringement claims, such as inducement under § 271(b), the plaintiff must prove the elements of inducement, including the defendant’s knowledge of the patent and intent to induce infringement. The standard for these factual predicates is typically preponderance of the evidence. However, establishing the required level of intent often depends on a defendant’s state of mind, which is difficult to prove and frequently inferred from circumstantial evidence such as promotional statements, instructions, or the design of a product that encourages infringing use.

Evidentiary implications for experts and fact witnesses. Because proving infringement (and rebutting invalidity) is a fact-driven exercise, expert testimony, technical demonstrations, documentary evidence, and clear factual narratives are critical. Experts explain claim terms, map claim elements to accused products or practices, and opine on whether substitutions are insubstantial for DOE. Fact witnesses provide the who/what/when detail for method step performance. The different standards of proof influence how parties present their evidence: defendants often aim to undermine the patentee’s element-by-element comparison while asserting affirmative invalidity defenses supported by prior art and forensic analysis intended to meet the clear-and-convincing threshold.

# Procedural posture and how it affects analysis

Procedural posture (e.g., summary judgment, bench trial, jury trial) significantly affects how courts handle infringement questions and the interplay of law and fact.

Claim construction as a preliminary legal step. Courts typically construe claims before conducting the infringement analysis. Claim construction is generally a matter of law for the court. The procedural posture matters because how claim construction is reached varies: a bench trial or summary judgment may involve a different posture for factual underpinnings of claim construction than a jury trial, but the ultimate claim interpretation is for the judge and governs the subsequent factual determination of infringement.

Summary judgment. On summary judgment, the movant bears the usual burden of showing no genuine dispute of material fact and that they are entitled to judgment as a matter of law. For infringement, a patentee seeking summary judgment of infringement must show that no reasonable factfinder could find the claimed elements unmet by the accused device; conversely, a defendant seeking summary judgment of noninfringement must show that even under the patentee’s favorable view of the evidence, no reasonable factfinder could find infringement. Courts are cautious to grant summary judgment on infringement or noninfringement when material factual disputes exist—particularly on issues of fact-intensive step performance, intent for inducement, or complex technical mappings between claims and accused devices.

Jury trial. When infringement questions are presented to a jury, the judge instructs the jury on claim construction as a matter of law, and the jury then decides whether the accused device meets the claims as construed. The jury applies the preponderance standard to determine infringement, and may also decide inducement or contributory infringement facts. Judges often retain control over DOE and over whether such theories may go to the jury given equitable considerations.

Bench trial. In a bench trial, the judge decides both claim construction and infringement. The judge applies the preponderance standard for factual matters, and equitable doctrines (such as DOE) are applied under the judge’s discretion.

Interplays with invalidity defenses. Where a defendant asserts invalidity, the doubly focused burden structure—preponderance for infringement, clear and convincing for invalidity—creates strategic dynamics. On a motion for summary judgment of invalidity, some courts may treat invalidity as raising material factual disputes requiring a trial if the challenger cannot establish clear and convincing evidence on the summary judgment record.

Procedural implications for method claims and multi-actor scenarios. The procedural vehicle can affect how courts allocate proof in multi-actor method claims. For example, on summary judgment a patentee may be denied relief if it cannot show evidence to attribute others’ performances to a single defendant; on the other hand, a defendant asserting divided performance as a defense may be unable to completely foreclose liability if a patentee presents evidence creating a factual dispute over direction or control.

Conclusion

Proving patent infringement is a structured exercise built atop claim construction, the all-elements rule, and statutory categories in § 271. For method claims, literal infringement requires proving by a preponderance of the evidence that a single entity performed each recited step, or alternatively that others’ performances can be legally attributed to the defendant or that the defendant induced the performances. The doctrine of equivalents offers a narrow equitable backstop for insubstantial differences, subject to limits such as prosecution history estoppel. The statutory presumption of validity under § 282 places the burden of proving invalidity on the challenger, historically by clear and convincing evidence, so litigation typically features simultaneous element-by-element infringement proof by the patentee and affirmative invalidity proof by the defendant under a higher evidentiary standard. Understanding how courts map facts to these legal principles is essential preparation for reading and applying judicial opinions in patent infringement disputes.

Introduction

Claim construction is the process by which a court determines the legal meaning and scope of the language used in a patent's claims. It is a threshold and indispensable step for nearly every substantive dispute that follows: whether an accused product or method infringes, whether claims are anticipated or obvious, and whether they are indefinite. The task is interpretive and hybrid in character: courts apply principles of statutory-like construction to technical language and often resolve contested factual disputes about how a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA) would understand the claim terms. Because claim construction narrows the universe of permissible factual disputes at trial and frames the legal issues on appeal, understanding its doctrinal mechanics is essential before reading any opinion that turns on claim meaning.

This chapter explains the core tools courts use when construing claims, the limits on outside evidence, the procedural vehicle for resolving disputes (Markman hearings), the role of subsidiary factual findings in construction and their appellate review, and special considerations for structural versus functional claim language, including clauses introduced by “wherein.” The exposition gives emphasis to legal standards, the typical order of analysis, burdens of proof, and how courts translate evidence into interpretive outcomes.

# Phillips hierarchy: claim language, specification, prosecution history

When courts construe claims they do so within a hierarchy of intrinsic evidence: first and foremost the claim language itself; next the specification (the written description and drawings); and finally the prosecution history (the file wrapper). This hierarchy governs the relative weight of these sources, and courts move among them in order to resolve ambiguity or confirm a plain meaning.

The claim language. The claims define the metes and bounds of the invention and therefore receive primary attention. Courts begin with the claim as written because the text is the most direct indicator of the patentee’s public notice. Ordinary words are generally given their ordinary and customary meaning, which is the meaning that a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA) would ascribe to the claim term at the time of the invention. Courts focus on how the claim term is used in context—both within the claim that contains it and across the other claims, because claim sets form an integrated claim language (e.g., a term used consistently in dependent and independent claims will be read consistently unless there is a clear reason to treat usages differently).

The specification. If claim language is ambiguous or admits multiple plausible meanings, courts consult the specification to resolve that ambiguity. The specification is particularly important for two reasons. First, it is the inventor’s own description of the invention and so provides reliable evidence about how the inventor conceived of claim terms and what embodiments were intended to fall within the claims. Second, the specification may provide definitions, examples, or structures that clarify functional limitations.

However, the specification cannot be used to rewrite the claim or to act as a dictionary that limits claim terms to preferred embodiments unless the patentee clearly intended to do so. Two corollaries follow. If the specification defines a term explicitly—either expressly or by clear implication—courts will typically adopt that definition. Conversely, if the specification merely describes a preferred embodiment without clearly disclaiming other possible embodiments, courts should avoid reading a limitation into the claims that would exclude other embodiments described in ordinary meaning.

The prosecution history. The file history of the patent application provides objective evidence of what the applicant said to obtain allowance, including amendments, arguments, and responses to examiner rejections. Prosecution history can be used to confirm a construction suggested by the claims and specification or to disavow claim scope where the patentee made clear and unmistakable statements limiting the claims to obtain allowance. Because the prosecution history is a written record of negotiations with the Patent Office, it is less reliable than the specification as evidence of the inventor’s technical understanding (it may be shaped by strategic bargaining). Courts therefore use it last in the intrinsic-evidence hierarchy, giving it weight when it contains clear statements bearing on the meaning of disputed claim language.

Interplay and canons. The court’s task is to determine the meaning that a POSITA would ascribe in light of all intrinsic evidence. That requires balancing canons of interpretation: give ordinary meaning to words; read claims in light of the specification but not to import limitations; use prosecution history to illuminate, not to enlarge or shrink the claims beyond what the claim language reasonably supports. Procedurally, the judge explains why intrinsic evidence supports the chosen construction and why alternative readings are inconsistent with that intrinsic record.

# Role and limits of extrinsic evidence (e.g., inventor testimony, textbooks)

Extrinsic evidence consists of materials external to the patent document and its prosecution file: expert testimony about technical meaning, inventor declarations or testimony, treatises and textbooks, dictionaries, industry standards, and demonstrative exhibits. Courts may consider extrinsic evidence to aid the court’s understanding of the technology and to confirm the meaning given by intrinsic evidence; but extrinsic evidence is subordinate to intrinsic evidence and cannot be used to contradict or override the intrinsic record.

Permitted uses. Extrinsic evidence is useful for background, context, and clarification. Expert declarations can explain how a POSITA would understand technical terms at the relevant time, provide definitions used in the field, and translate complex technologies for a court. Technical treatises and dictionaries can corroborate a particular meaning if consistent with the patent’s internal evidence. Inventor testimony may be admissible to explain technical aspects of the inventions, demonstrate what the inventor considered the invention to be, and to shed light on ambiguous claim language.

Limits and restrictions. Courts routinely limit extrinsic evidence in several ways. First, extrinsic evidence cannot be used to contradict an unambiguous claim meaning that is supported by the specification and prosecution history. Second, dictionaries and treatises are given only as much weight as they deserve; dictionary definitions are not dispositive if inconsistent with the intrinsic record. Third, inventor testimony is treated cautiously: testimony by the patentee or inventors about claim meaning can be self-serving, and courts will evaluate such testimony in light of the intrinsic record. Courts are wary of allowing inventors to expand or narrow claim scope through after-the-fact testimony that conflicts with the written disclosure or the prosecution record.

Standards for evaluating expert evidence. A court will assess extrinsic expert testimony for reliability and relevance. Reliable expert testimony explains how a POSITA would read particular claim terms at the time of invention and grounds its opinions in recognized technical sources and methodologies. Courts may exclude or discount expert testimony that amounts to ipse dixit, that relies on definitions contrary to the specification, or that postdates the invention and thus does not reflect the contemporary understanding.

Practical takeaway. When building a claim construction record, parties should prioritize intrinsic evidence and use extrinsic evidence for context and corroboration. Inventor testimony and dictionaries are persuasive only to the extent they align with the specification and prosecution history. A successful construction argument demonstrates how extrinsic materials fit into and reinforce the intrinsic record rather than trying to replace it.

# Markman hearings and timing relative to trial

A Markman hearing (commonly called a claim construction hearing) is the judicial proceeding in which parties present arguments and evidence about disputed claim terms and the court issues constructions. The hearing is a procedural mechanism by which the court resolves claim meaning before or during trial, because claim construction significantly narrows the issues remaining for the finder of fact.

Purpose and structure. The markman hearing’s core purpose is to allow the court to determine the legal meaning of claim terms as a matter of law. The hearing typically involves written briefing, submission of intrinsic and extrinsic evidence (e.g., patents, specification excerpts, prosecution history, expert declarations, dictionaries), and oral argument. Parties may present testimony and cross-examination on factual matters relevant to construction, though many courts decide constructions from the papers and live argument without calling witnesses.

Timing relative to trial. Claim construction often occurs before the jury trial during pretrial proceedings because it shapes the claim scope that the jury must apply in infringement and invalidity findings. Early construction promotes efficiency by resolving determinative legal issues and focusing discovery and trial. That said, construction can also be raised or refined during trial in response to newly developed factual record, and courts retain discretion to revisit a prior construction if material new evidence emerges.

Interlocutory nature and preclusion. Because claim construction is an issue of law (with subsidiary factual components), the court’s construction is binding on the parties for purposes of the litigation, subject to revision only in limited circumstances. Judges generally strive for final constructions before trial to avoid confusion, but they also possess flexibility to amend constructions when necessary to prevent manifest injustice.

Procedural posture and strategic implications. The moment of construction affects litigation strategy. Parties prefer early claim construction to clarify litigation risks and to shape settlement expectations. Conversely, the party asserting infringement may seek to postpone construction until the factual record is better developed to provide context for disputed terms. Practically, claim construction deadlines are often set in the case scheduling order, and aggressive parties use claim construction disputes to obtain early leverage (e.g., by seeking summary judgment of noninfringement based on a narrow construction).

# Teva: factual underpinnings of claim construction and appellate review

Claim construction is typically described as a question of law, but many constructions depend on subsidiary factual findings—such as what a POSITA would have understood a term to mean, whether a term has a well-established definition in the art, or whether the specification clearly disavowed a particular meaning. Handling these factual matters has important appellate implications: appellate courts review legal conclusions de novo but review factual findings under a deferential clear-error standard.

Subsidiary factual findings. When a district court resolves an issue like the meaning of a term in light of expert testimony or determines whether a patentee unambiguously disavowed claim scope during prosecution, the court is making factual determinations. Examples include assessments of credibility (is the inventor’s testimony believable?), determinations about the level of skill in the art at the relevant time, or findings about whether a technical term had a special meaning in the art.

Effect on appellate review. Because claim construction rests on mixed fact-law questions, appellate courts apply a dual standard. Pure legal determinations—such as the application of canons of claim interpretation or the ultimate claim construction when it follows from undisputed facts—are reviewed de novo. Numerical or subsidiary fact determinations (e.g., what a POSITA would have understood) are reviewed for clear error. If a district court’s factual finding is plausible in light of the record, a court of appeals must defer and cannot overturn it absent a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made.

Practical consequences. Litigants should appreciate that developing a strong factual record at the district court level matters. If claim construction depends on factual inputs, those findings will likely survive appellate scrutiny unless they are clearly erroneous. Accordingly, parties should present comprehensive, credible evidence (declarations, deposition testimony, contemporaneous technical literature) during the Markman proceeding to support favorable factual findings. Conversely, a party who seeks to preserve de novo appellate review will try to frame disputes as legal questions amenable to pure legal analysis and, where possible, limit the need for subsidiary factual findings.

Standard-of-proof and notice. The court’s role in distinguishing law from fact also affects burdens. For instance, if a party argues that a claim term is indefinite because it lacks clear meaning, that is often a legal determination informed by facts; the party challenging indefiniteness must present persuasive evidence. Courts will explain the basis of their factual findings in the claim construction order to facilitate proper appellate review.

# Construing structural vs. functional language; ‘wherein’ clauses

Claim language can be structural (defining parts, elements, and their relationships) or functional (defining an element by what it does rather than what it is). Different rules and statutory provisions apply to these categories, and courts treat “wherein” clauses—which often express functional limitations or describe relationships—carefully to decide whether they limit claim scope or merely state use or result.

Structural language. Structural claim language recites tangible parts, their arrangement, and physical interconnections. Such claims are generally construed by reference to the structure disclosed in the specification and, when relevant, the ordinary meaning those structures have in the art. When a claim recites a specific structure, courts look for corresponding description in the specification to determine whether the patentee intended that structure broadly or in a narrow sense.

Functional language and means-plus-function. Functional language describes what an element does. Under certain statutory provisions, most prominently 35 U.S.C. 112(f) (formerly 112 ¶6), claim language expressed as a “means for” performing a specified function is treated as a “means-plus-function” limitation. When a claim invokes the means-plus-function format, the claim element is construed to cover the structure, material, or acts described in the specification as corresponding to that function, and equivalents thereof. If the specification fails to disclose sufficient corresponding structure, the limitation may be indefinite.

Identifying means-plus-function. The presence of “means for” language creates a presumption that §112(f) applies, whereas the absence of the literal phrase can create dispute. Courts analyze whether the claim term recites sufficiently definite structure or whether it is merely a functional description without structural details. If a claim uses functional language but does not employ the “means for” phrasing, §112(f) may still apply if the claim term fails to recite structure and the element is otherwise purely functional. The key inquiry is whether a person of ordinary skill would recognize the term as denoting structure or merely a function.

Consequences of functional claiming. When an element is construed under means-plus-function, the patentee is limited to the structure disclosed in the specification and equivalents—this can significantly narrow claim scope. For elements expressed functionally but not captured under §112(f), courts nonetheless attempt to give content to the term by reference to the specification and common meaning in the art. Courts are cautious not to read functional language so broadly as to encompass the entire principle of operation; that risk raises issues of overbreadth and potential indefiniteness.

“Wherein” clauses. “Wherein” clauses often appear in dependent and independent claims to link components or to state a relationship or result achieved by the claimed combination. Courts ask whether a “wherein” clause imposes a limiting structural or functional requirement on the claim element or whether it merely describes an intended use or optional result.

Limiting versus non-limiting language. Courts look to the claim drafter’s intent as revealed in the claim set and specification to determine whether a “wherein” clause is limiting. If the clause provides a necessary structural or operational constraint (e.g., a specific relationship among parts required for the claimed invention to function), it will be treated as a limitation. By contrast, if the clause merely states an intended benefit, result, or preferred operation without narrowing the claimed elements, the court may read it as non-limiting.

Antecedent basis and clarity. A common source of dispute with “wherein” clauses is lack of clear antecedent basis (i.e., the clause uses pronouns or refers to elements not introduced earlier). Proper claim drafting provides clear antecedent references and language tying the “wherein” clause to previously recited elements. A court may construe an ambiguous “wherein” clause against the drafter under the doctrine of claim ambiguity, potentially finding indefiniteness if the clause renders the scope unclear.

Interplay with functional limitations. Because “wherein” clauses frequently express relationships or results, they can functionally limit the claim. If a “wherein” clause results in a new functional requirement that lacks disclosed corresponding structure, the clause may raise §112 issues. Alternatively, a “wherein” clause that sets forth a functional relationship but that is supported by detailed disclosure in the specification can be an effective way to define claim scope.

Practical drafting and litigating implications. Drafters should be careful when using functional language and “wherein” clauses: provide sufficient structural detail in the specification for any functional recitation and ensure clear antecedent basis. Litigators should probe whether a contested functional term invokes §112(f), whether the specification supplies sufficient corresponding structure, and whether a “wherein” clause imposes a real limitation. During claim construction, parties should explain whether a disputed functional limitation requires a structural correlate and, if so, point to specific portions of the specification that qualify.

Conclusion

Claim construction is both a legal and evidentiary exercise grounded in the intrinsic record, supplemented where necessary by extrinsic evidence. The interpretive hierarchy—claims first, specification second, prosecution history third—frames the analysis, and extrinsic sources play a subordinate but useful role. Procedurally, Markman hearings resolve claim meaning and shape the remainder of the litigation, and factual findings made in support of construction receive deferential appellate review. Special care is required when claims use functional language, means-plus-function formats, or “wherein” clauses, because these features can dramatically affect scope and potential indefiniteness. For the law student preparing to read judicial decisions, a solid command of these fundamentals will illuminate how courts translate technical language into adjudicable legal rules.

Introduction

Prosecution history, the written record of the interactions between the patent applicant and the patent office during examination, plays a central role in defining the scope of a granted patent. Statements made by the applicant and changes made to the claims during prosecution can both restrict what the patentee can later claim as its exclusive property. Two related but distinct doctrines govern those effects: prosecution disclaimer (sometimes called disavowal) and prosecution history estoppel. Both doctrines prevent a patentee from expanding claimed subject matter in litigation beyond what was relinquished during prosecution, but they operate differently and apply in different contexts. Understanding how courts treat applicant statements, claim amendments, examiner comments, and the interaction between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence is essential before reading judicial opinions that resolve claim scope disputes or equivalence issues.

This chapter explains the doctrine of prosecution disclaimer/disavowal in claim construction, the evidentiary standard for invoking a disclaimer, the distinction between disclaimer and prosecution history estoppel (especially in the doctrine of equivalents context), how courts weigh statements from applicants versus statements from examiners, and the relative roles of intrinsic and extrinsic evidence when assessing whether a limitation has been surrendered.

# Prosecution disclaimer/disavowal in claim construction

Prosecution disclaimer, or disavowal, is a constraining principle in claim construction. Courts construe patent claims to give reasonable meaning to the words the patentee used, in light of the specification and the prosecution history. When an applicant makes clear and unambiguous statements during prosecution that describe the invention in a way that excludes particular subject matter, the patentee is bound by those statements: the claims will be construed to exclude what was disclaimed.

The doctrine rests on the objective-public-record principle. Patent claims are public notice of the patentee’s rights, and the prosecution history is part of the public record that informs that notice. When an applicant tells the patent office that the invention does not include certain embodiments or that certain claim language should be interpreted narrowly, that communication is relied on by the examiner and becomes part of what the public may rely on. Consequently, courts will not allow the patentee to argue for a claim construction in litigation that is inconsistent with those earlier statements.

In practical terms, during claim construction the court examines the prosecution history to determine whether the applicant made statements—oral or written—that would lead a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA), reading the record, to understand that the patentee disavowed a particular meaning of a claim term or excluded particular subject matter. If such a disavowal exists, the claim term is construed to incorporate the limitation reflected by the disavowal. Thus, prosecution disclaimer functions as a boundary-setting tool: it narrows claim scope to conform to representations the applicant made to obtain the patent.

# Requirement of clear and unambiguous statements to limit scope

Not every prosecutorial remark will work a disclaimer. Courts uniformly require that statements that trigger prosecution disclaimer be clear and unambiguous. The threshold is demanding because claim construction is a task of interpretation that should not lightly impute limiting meanings to ordinary claim language. The rule is designed to preserve predictability and fairness: patentees should not be bound by a later-found ambiguous snippet of the prosecution record that could have multiple readings.

The legal standard typically has two components. First, there must be an express statement or unequivocal representation by the applicant that the claimed subject matter excludes the disputed meaning. Vague, ambiguous, or qualified statements—such as tentative negotiations, isolated admissions not tied to claim scope, or equivocal descriptions—do not ordinarily suffice. Second, the statement must be reasonably interpreted by a POSITA as a deliberate disavowal of the broader claim scope in favor of the narrower meaning urged by the patentee during litigation.

Examples of statements that satisfy the standard are: direct admissions that a term should be limited to a particular structure or material; statements disclaiming whole categories of embodiments in order to secure allowance; and explicit arguments distinguishing prior art by asserting that the claimed invention lacks a particular feature. Conversely, statements that merely describe a preferred embodiment, state an objective or result, or provide background that happens to be inconsistent with later litigation positions are unlikely to be treated as clear disclaimers.

When assessing clarity and ambiguity courts consider the context: the language used, the examiner’s responses, the reason for claim amendments, and other prosecution documents. Importantly, an ordinarily skilled reader’s understanding is key. The test is not whether a court or litigant can find some plausible reading of an ambiguous comment that would constitute a disclaimer, but whether the record, as read by a POSITA, unambiguously reflects a surrender of claim scope.

# Distinguishing disclaimer from prosecution history estoppel (DOE context)

Although both prosecution disclaimer and prosecution history estoppel relate to the prosecution record, they operate differently and apply to different legal inquiries.

Prosecution disclaimer is a claim-construction doctrine. It is used when a court determines the proper ordinary and customary meaning of claim language. If the prosecution record shows that the patentee limited the meaning of a term, the court incorporates that limitation into the construed claim. The practical effect is to narrow the literal scope of the claims.

Prosecution history estoppel, by contrast, is an equitable limitation on the doctrine of equivalents (DOE). The DOE allows an accused infringer’s product or process to infringe even though it does not fall within the literal claim language, provided it is equivalent to the claimed element. Prosecution history estoppel prevents a patentee from using the DOE to recapture subject matter surrendered during prosecution by amending claims for reasons related to patentability. In other words, when a patentee narrows a claim to obtain allowance, prosecution history estoppel can bar the patentee from asserting that the surrendered territory nevertheless infringes under the DOE.

The two doctrines thus apply at different stages. Disclaimer is about how to read and construe claims; estoppel is about whether the patentee can assert equivalents beyond those literal claims. They are related because both look to what was surrendered during prosecution, but they impose different burdens and produce different results. A narrowing amendment or an argument made for patentability may both create estoppel with respect to certain equivalents even if they do not produce a formal disclaimer that narrows the literal claim language. Conversely, a clear disavowal in the prosecution history yields a narrowing construction and obviates the need to analyze DOE for the disclaimed subject matter: the term, as construed, excludes it.

In practice, courts proceed carefully to avoid double-counting: a statement that operates as a clear disclaimer will narrow the claim and thereby make an equivalent analysis unnecessary for the disclaimed subject matter. But a narrowing amendment that lacks the requisite clarity for a disclaimer may nonetheless give rise to estoppel against asserting equivalents that fall within the scope of the surrendered value. Thus, prosecution history may produce either or both effects depending on the nature of the statements and amendments.

# Weight of examiner statements vs. applicant statements

Not all statements in the prosecution file are equally binding. Two actors appear in the file: the patent applicant (and the applicant’s agents or counsel) and examiners employed by the patent office. The law treats their communications differently.

Applicant statements are generally given controlling weight when assessing whether the patentee disavowed claim scope. The rationale is straightforward: the applicant is the party seeking protection; its representations about what the claims mean or what the invention excludes are statements about its intent and the scope it is willing to assert. Moreover, the applicant’s arguments and amendments are the reasons the examiner allows or rejects claim language, and they are relied upon by the public to understand claim boundaries. Therefore, a clear, unambiguous statement by the applicant during prosecution can bind the patentee.

Examiner statements, by contrast, are typically given less weight in determining limits on the patentee’s rights. An examiner’s rejection or comment reflects the examiner’s view but does not represent an admission by the patentee about the meaning of the claims. In claim construction, courts focus on what the patentee said about claim scope. Examiner comments may be considered as part of the context—particularly if the applicant agreed with or adopted the examiner’s characterization—but the examiner’s private assessment does not itself disavow claim scope.

Two important practical points follow. First, if the applicant expressly agrees with or adopts an examiner’s characterization—e.g., by stating “we agree with the examiner that X means Y” or by amending claims in direct response to an examiner’s characterization—that mutual exchange can result in a binding limitation. Second, when the prosecution file contains only examiner statements narrowing the scope (without any adoptive statements by the applicant), courts are typically reluctant to construe the claims more narrowly solely on the basis of the examiner’s views.

The identities of the speakers are also relevant. Statements by the applicant’s attorney or agent matter because they act as the applicant’s representatives. Similarly, statements by the inventor that are later ratified by counsel or the applicant can be binding. Courts will look to who made the statement and whether it was part of the record as a position taken by the applicant to overcome an examiner’s rejection or to secure allowance.

# Reliance on intrinsic vs. extrinsic evidence in assessing disclaimer

In claim construction disputes and in determining the effect of prosecution statements, courts distinguish between intrinsic and extrinsic evidence and give them different weights. Intrinsic evidence consists of the patent claims, the specification, and the prosecution history. Extrinsic evidence includes expert testimony, dictionaries, treatises, and other external sources.

The leading principle is that intrinsic evidence is primary and generally dispositive. The claims define the scope of the invention; the specification provides context for interpreting the claims and may serve as a lexicographer’s definition or a source of disavowal; and the prosecution history illuminates what the patentee said to obtain the patent. When courts assess whether a prosecution statement amounts to a disclaimer, they analyze the intrinsic record to determine whether the statement objectively and unambiguously conveys a surrender of claim scope. Thus, intrinsic evidence governs the analysis.

Extrinsic evidence has a subsidiary role. It can be useful to help a court understand technical aspects or to determine how a POSITA would have understood the prosecution record. Expert testimony is commonly used to explain the meaning of technical terms in the specification or prosecution history and to interpret whether an ordinary skilled artisan would have read a particular statement as a disavowal. Dictionaries and treatises can shed light on ordinary meaning, but they cannot override clear intrinsic evidence; they only inform the court’s understanding in the absence of a clear intrinsic directive.

The court’s approach is typically sequential. First the court interprets the claim language using intrinsic evidence. If intrinsic evidence is ambiguous or silent on a disputed issue, extrinsic evidence may be considered to assist interpretation. But extrinsic evidence cannot be used to contradict a clear disavowal in the prosecution history or a clear specification definition. Put another way, extrinsic evidence cannot create a broader claim construction if the intrinsic evidence shows that the applicant clearly limited the claim.

When assessing disclaimer, courts ask whether the intrinsic record, as reasonably understood by a POSITA, contains the clear and unambiguous statement necessary to limit claim scope. If the intrinsic record is ambiguous, courts may cautiously consider extrinsic evidence to resolve that ambiguity, but with the understanding that extrinsic sources are subordinate. In litigation, parties will commonly present both intrinsic highlights and extrinsic experts; judges must weigh them within this hierarchy.

# Procedural posture and evidentiary burdens

How prosecution history issues are raised and decided depends on the procedural posture of the case. Claim construction is typically a legal determination, often decided before trial (e.g., during a Markman hearing or its equivalent). When a party seeks summary judgment of noninfringement or invalidity and relies on a prosecution disclaimer, the court construes the claim in light of the prosecution history. Because claim construction is a matter of law, appellate review of claim construction varies by jurisdiction, but it often involves de novo review of the court’s legal conclusions and clear-error review of factual findings drawn from the intrinsic and extrinsic record.

In the doctrine of equivalents context, the patentee ordinarily bears the burden to prove equivalence. When prosecution history estoppel is at issue, the party asserting estoppel need not always bear the entire burden; instead, estoppel is often raised as an affirmative defense by the accused infringer. The accused infringer must identify the amendment or argument and the reason given for it (e.g., to avoid prior art). The patentee then may attempt to rebut the presumption of estoppel by showing one of the recognized exceptions (for example, that the reason for the amendment is unrelated to patentability, that the particular equivalent was unforeseeable at the time of amendment, or that the amendment was only tangentially related to the equivalent in question). The allocation of burdens can depend on the governing rules of civil procedure and substantive patent doctrine, but the general flow is that the accused infringer points to the prosecution record to establish the surrender, and the patentee attempts to show a reason to preserve certain equivalents despite the surrender.

Because the doctrines operate at different stages, the remedies also differ. A claim construction that incorporates a prosecution disclaimer is applied to the infringement analysis for literal infringement. Prosecution history estoppel limits the patentee’s ability to rely on the DOE and can foreclose assertions of infringement based on equivalents. A court may rule on these issues in pretrial motions; sometimes, factual disputes about what a POSITA would understand or about the factual circumstances of the amendment prevent resolution on summary judgment, carrying the issue to trial.

# Practical effects and strategic considerations

For applicants and litigants, the practical implications are significant. During prosecution, careful drafting and measured commentary can preserve flexibility in later enforcement. Applicants should avoid making sweeping, unqualified statements about what the claims do or do not cover unless they intend to narrow claim scope. When responding to rejections, counsel should be precise when stating the reasons for amendments or arguments; if the narrowing is necessary only to clarify language rather than to overcome prior art, stating the reason explicitly may help avoid estoppel in the DOE context.

For litigators, identifying prosecution statements that create binding limits requires meticulous review of the prosecution file, including examiner communications and applicant responses. Effective arguments will demonstrate that a given statement is objectively unambiguous and that a POSITA would have read it as a disavowal or as a surrender made for patentability reasons. Conversely, defenders will emphasize ambiguity in the prosecution record and argue that examiner statements alone cannot bind the patentee.

Conclusion

Prosecution disclaimer and prosecution history estoppel are distinct doctrines that constrain how a patentee may define and later enforce its patent rights. The core rule is that clear and unambiguous statements or amendments made during prosecution can limit claim scope or bar assertions of equivalence, but the standard requires an objective showing that a POSITA would perceive a deliberate surrender. Applicant statements carry primary weight; examiner statements are relevant mainly to the extent the applicant adopts them. Intrinsic evidence governs the analysis, with extrinsic evidence available only to clarify ambiguous technical matters. Understanding these principles allows a student to appreciate how courts read the prosecution record and apply legal tests to resolve disputes over claim scope and the boundaries of patent rights.

Introduction

Expert testimony is central to modern patent litigation. Technical complexity, specialized terminology, and the need to translate engineering or scientific features into legal conclusions make experts indispensable for proving infringement and establishing damages. Judges must balance the utility of expert evidence against the risk that unreliable or misleading testimony will distort the factfinder’s deliberations. That balance plays out through a combination of Federal Rules of Evidence (especially Rules 702, 703, and 705), Rule 26 expert-disclosure obligations, Daubert-style gatekeeping, and procedural tools used at summary judgment, in limine motions, during trial, and on appeal. This chapter explains the governing evidentiary rules and standards, describes how experts typically translate claim language into accusations against an accused product, discusses the use and limits of demonstratives and tutorials, and addresses how courts treat inconsistent or self-contradictory expert testimony. The emphasis is doctrinal: which legal tests apply, how courts apply them to facts, what burdens litigants bear, and how procedural posture affects outcomes.

# Federal Rules of Evidence 702 and Daubert reliability

Federal Rule of Evidence 702 governs the admissibility of expert testimony in federal courts. It requires that: (1) the witness is qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education; (2) the expert’s specialized knowledge will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or determine a fact in issue (helpfulness); (3) the testimony is based on sufficient facts or data; and (4) the testimony is the product of reliable principles and methods reliably applied to the facts of the case.

Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals and its progeny interpret Rule 702 to impose a trial-court gatekeeping function. The gatekeeper’s role is not to choose between competing expert opinions, but to ensure that the proffered opinion rests on a reliable foundation and is relevant to the issues the jury must decide. The analysis has two overarching dimensions: reliability and relevance.

Reliability. Courts examine whether the expert used a reliable methodology. The Daubert line supplied nonexclusive factors courts may consider: testability or falsifiability of the underlying theory or technique; whether the theory or method has been subjected to peer review and publication; known or potential error rate and the existence of standards controlling the technique’s operation; and general acceptance within the relevant scientific community. The Supreme Court later confirmed that the Daubert inquiry applies not only to “scientific” testimony but also to technical and other specialized knowledge (Kumho Tire Co. v. Carmichael). The pertinent considerations are therefore pragmatic and flexible: the inquiry focuses on whether the reasoning or methodology underlying the testimony is scientifically or technically valid and whether it can be applied reliably to the facts.

Relevance. Even a reliable methodology must be tied to the facts of the case. An expert’s opinion must assist the trier of fact by relating to matters that are within the jury’s purview. Opinions that address purely legal questions for the court (e.g., construing claim terms) or that are untethered to the record may be excluded as unhelpful. The relevance inquiry also looks at the fit between the expert’s opinion and the factual issues—whether the expert’s reasoning logically advances the factfinder’s understanding of contested questions.

Application to patent litigation. In patent cases, courts apply Daubert gatekeeping to a variety of opinions: technical infringement opinions, claim-construction-related analyses, and economic damages opinions. For technical experts, courts scrutinize the expert’s testing protocols, reverse-engineering practices, sampling and measurement procedures, and the chain of reasoning by which particular product features are found to satisfy specific claim limitations. For damages experts, courts often focus on the validity of the hypothetical negotiation framework, the selection and comparability of license agreements or comparable products, apportionment of value to the patented features, and the robustness of any regression or economic modeling.

Burden and standard of review. The proponent of the expert testimony bears the burden of establishing admissibility by a preponderance of the evidence. On appeal, a district court’s Daubert rulings are reviewed for abuse of discretion, reflecting the gatekeeping role that trial courts exercise over expert evidence.

Procedural posture. Daubert motions may be filed pretrial as standalone motions or combined with motions in limine. Decisions are often made before trial to shape the admissible scope of evidence, but Rule 702 may be invoked at summary judgment and at trial. When an opinion is excluded at the gatekeeping stage, the proponent may seek to cure defects (e.g., via additional testing or supplementation) or present other evidence. If an expert’s methodology is merely weak, not fatally unreliable, courts may admit it and leave the issue to cross-examination and the factfinder.

# Rule 703: reliance on technical materials and company documents

Rule 703 governs the facts or data an expert may rely upon in forming an opinion. It states that an expert may base an opinion on facts or data that the expert has been made aware of or personally observed. These underlying facts need not be admissible for the opinion to be admitted, provided they are of the type reasonably relied upon by experts in the particular field. If the underlying facts or data would otherwise be inadmissible, the court may exclude their disclosure to the jury if the probative value of the underlying facts is substantially outweighed by the risk of undue prejudice, confusion, or a waste of time.

Reasonable reliance. The “reasonably relied upon” standard is both field-specific and context-specific. Courts will consider whether experts in the discipline commonly rely on similar sources—testing data, vendor specifications, code review, internal company documents, software output, business records, or hearsay statements. For example, a software engineer might reasonably rely on source code, compiled binaries, execution traces, or vendor documentation. An electrical engineer might reasonably rely on schematic diagrams, test bench results, or component datasheets. Rule 703 permits experts to base their opinions on such materials even if those materials would be hearsay if offered for the truth of the matter asserted.

Disclosure and limits. Rule 705 interacts with Rule 703 on disclosure. Under Rule 705, an expert may give an opinion without first disclosing the underlying facts or data. The expert must, however, disclose such facts and data on cross-examination if requested. In practice, disclosure obligations under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure—particularly Rule 26(a)(2), which requires expert reports signed by the witness containing a complete statement of all opinions and the basis and reasons therefor—functionally require greater transparency than the FRE alone. Rule 26 requires identification of the facts and data considered, the methodology used, and often production of the underlying materials.

Company documents and business records. Internal company documents commonly serve as the factual foundation for both infringement and damages opinions. Rule 703 permits experts to rely on these documents, and many company documents may independently qualify under hearsay exceptions (e.g., the business-records exception). Even when a document is hearsay and not independently admissible, the expert may still rely on it. The court, however, must decide whether the circumstances of the document’s creation and the expert’s evaluation render the reliance reasonable. For instance, cherry-picked or self-serving internal documents that do not withstand scrutiny may be attacked via cross-examination, demonstrations of selective reliance, or motions to exclude where the reliance is so deficient as to render the opinion unreliable.

Authentication and weight. Reliance under Rule 703 does not relieve the proponent of the need to authenticate documents properly when they are offered into evidence for their truth. If an opinion depends on a company document that is not authenticated or is excluded under another evidentiary rule, the expert’s opinion may survive but will be vulnerable on cross-examination and may carry limited weight.

# Mapping claim limitations to accused products

Proving infringement requires mapping each asserted claim limitation to features of the accused product or process and demonstrating that the accused instrumentality meets every limitation of the claim as properly construed. This task typically falls to technical experts working within the boundaries of claim construction established by the court.

Claim construction and the expert’s role. Claim construction is a legal determination for the court, not the jury. That said, experts play an essential role in assisting the court and jury to understand the technical meaning of claim terms and to map claims to products. Experts often prepare claim charts that set out, line-by-line, each claim limitation alongside evidence tying that limitation to specific product structures, code snippets, processes, tests, screenshots, or documentation. Because claim construction may change after a report is produced, experts must carefully state assumptions tied to particular constructions and be prepared to adjust analyses when the court construes terms.

Literal infringement and the doctrine of equivalents. There are two primary modes of proving infringement: literal infringement (the accused product contains every claim limitation exactly as written) and infringement under the doctrine of equivalents (the accused product performs substantially the same function in substantially the same way to achieve substantially the same result, thereby equivalent to a claimed element). Experts must construct separate analyses for each theory. For literal infringement, experts typically identify specific structures or steps that correspond to each claim element. For equivalents, experts must craft a reasoned technical explanation of how an accused feature performs the same function in substantially the same way to achieve the same result, often addressing the particular limitations requiring an equivalence analysis.

Stepwise mapping and claim charts. Well-structured claim charts are the common method for mapping limitations to accused products. Each limitation is restated; the expert cites evidence showing where in the accused product the limitation exists; and the expert explains the basis for the mapping (e.g., test results, source code lines, product manuals, reverse-engineering reports). The mapping must be specific: conclusory assertions that “the product meets this limitation” without an evidentiary trail are weak and vulnerable to exclusion or summary-judgment attack. When addressing complex multi-component systems, an expert should identify which component, subroutine, or module corresponds to each claim element and, for method claims, which steps or operations are performed and in what sequence.

Testing, sampling, and reverse engineering. Mapping often depends on testing and reverse engineering. Courts evaluate the reliability of the testing methodology under Daubert: Was the testing repeatable? Were tests conducted under conditions that reflect normal operation? Was the sampling sufficient to overcome variability across product versions or configurations? For software, experts may examine source code, compiled binaries, API traces, log files, and runtime behavior. They may also rely on vendor documentation and third-party specs. When testing or reverse engineering is the factual basis, experts must preserve and document the chain of custody and the procedures employed. Where an accused product exists in many configurations, experts must explain why the tested configuration reliably represents the accused product in the marketplace.

Joint infringement and multi-actor systems. Mapping claim limitations becomes particularly challenging in cases alleging divided performance or joint infringement (where multiple actors together perform all claim steps). Experts must carefully identify who or what performs each step and explain legal theories for attributing steps performed by others to the accused party (e.g., agency, direction and control, contractual arrangements, or single-entity control). Courts scrutinize such mapping because the legal attribution of steps to a single actor has complex doctrinal constraints.

Burden of proof. The patentee bears the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence. The expert’s mapping must therefore be sufficient to persuade a reasonable factfinder that it is more likely than not that each claim limitation is met. On summary judgment, the absence of a genuine dispute about any limitation—or the presence of reliable contrary expert evidence from the accused infringer—can defeat the infringement claim. On motions to exclude, the proponent of the expert must prove that the methodology by which the mapping was performed is reliable.

# Use of demonstratives and tutorials

Demonstratives and tutorials are common tools for conveying complex technical concepts to judges and juries. They can simplify intricate mechanisms, show comparative diagrams, animate processes over time, or present step-by-step deconstructions of code or circuitry. While these tools can be persuasive, courts treat them as special species of evidence and apply both evidentiary and pragmatic constraints.

Substantive versus illustrative. Demonstratives that merely illustrate an expert’s admissible opinion are generally permissible. Examples include diagrams, animations, timelines, and simplified schematics. However, demonstratives that introduce new substantive facts or novel analysis not disclosed in the expert report are problematic. If a demonstrative goes beyond what the expert disclosed in accordance with Rule 26(a)(2) or mischaracterizes the record, it may be excluded or struck.

Foundation and fidelity. A demonstrative must be founded in the record. If an exhibit claims to show a particular operation or a specific line of code performing a function, the proponent must be able to point to the underlying evidence (e.g., test results, code excerpts, product documentation) supporting the demonstrative. Courts will exclude demonstratives that misrepresent the underlying evidence or are unfaithful to the record’s content. The more a demonstrative simplifies or abstracts technical detail, the more important it is that the simplification not change the substantive meaning.

Prejudice and confusion. Under Rule 403, a demonstrative may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusing the issues, or misleading the jury. Animated simulations and colorful graphics can be powerful; judges will exclude or limit demonstratives that overstate evidence, use inflammatory imagery, or otherwise distort the jury’s view.

Tutorials for the court. Judges commonly permit tutorials—extended presentations to teach the court technical background. Tutorials are not evidence but help the court and attorneys frame issues for claim construction and trial. Effective tutorials are neutral, comprehensible, and grounded in the disciplines at issue. Parties should avoid using tutorials as surreptitious advocacy; a tutorial’s primary purpose should be education, not persuasion.

Timing and disclosure. Demonstratives are typically exchanged in pretrial disclosures and often subject to motions in limine. Courts require counsel to share demonstratives sufficiently in advance to allow objections and to prevent surprises. When demonstratives contain demonstrable factual assertions or calculations, those parts must be supported by the expert reports and underlying data. Failure to disclose substantive demonstratives may result in preclusion under Rule 37 or exclusion under the FRE.

Use at bench and jury phases. During the bench phase (e.g., claim construction or pretrial Daubert hearings) demonstratives can aid the court’s comprehension. At trial, demonstratives can be used in opening statements and during expert testimony, but counsel must be careful to distinguish argument from demonstrative evidence. Judges regularly require counsel to provide copies of demonstratives ahead of time and may require consent or rulings on their admissibility before use in opening.

# Handling inconsistencies and the effect of self-contradictory testimony

In patent litigation, experts may provide testimony that is inconsistent either across time (e.g., expert report vs. deposition vs. trial testimony), internally within a single report, or in tension with other evidence (including company documents). Handling inconsistencies raises questions of admissibility, impeachment, credibility, and procedural sanctions.

Rule 702 and Daubert implications. An internal contradiction or a clear conflict between an expert’s methodology and the facts can affect admissibility. If an expert’s opinion is rendered unreliable because the expert ignored data that fatally undermines the methodology, the court may exclude the opinion under Rule 702. For example, if an expert claims to rely on testing but the tests, properly interpreted, contradict the expert’s conclusions, the court may find the methodology insufficiently reliable. However, not all inconsistencies warrant exclusion. Many conflicts go to weight, not admissibility; they can be explored on cross-examination and resolved by the trier of fact.

Prior inconsistent statements and impeachment. When an expert’s deposition, report, or earlier statement contradicts later testimony, opposing counsel may use prior inconsistent statements to impeach credibility. Impeachment can be highly effective with juries and may be used to undermine critical linkages in claim charts or the foundations of damages models. Courts permit use of prior inconsistent expert statements at trial, subject to rules governing refreshing recollection, prior inconsistent statements as substantive evidence (depending on jurisdiction and circumstances), and the requirements for preserving objections.

Limitations of impeachment. While impeachment affects credibility, it does not automatically remove an expert’s opinions from evidence. The court will generally not exclude testimony solely because it is contested or because the expert’s conclusions are disputed. Exclusion is more likely when inconsistencies reveal that the opinion is not grounded in any reliable methodology or when the expert consciously manipulates data in a way that undermines the foundations required by Rule 702.

Self-contradictory testimony. Testimony that contradicts itself within a single expert report or between the expert’s report and deposition raises the question whether the opinion is sufficiently reliable. Courts distinguish between: (1) a genuine change in view that is explained and supported by additional analysis, and (2) an unexplained or internally inconsistent opinion that suggests the expert’s methods were not applied reliably. The former may be admissible, perhaps accompanied by an explanation; the latter may justify exclusion.

Procedural remedies for inconsistency. Courts have several procedural tools to address inconsistent or late-changing expert positions. If an expert’s testimony departs materially from an expert report and no supplemental disclosure was made as required by Rule 26(e), the opposing party may move to exclude the new testimony, strike the report, or obtain fees and costs for the extra discovery burden under Rule 37(c)(1). Courts may also permit limited re-deposition, require supplementation, or impose other cures short of exclusion. In extreme cases—where the inconsistency appears to be strategic gamesmanship or a deliberate attempt to mislead—the court may impose sanctions, preclude testimony, or draw adverse inferences.

Dealing with inconsistent company documents. When an expert’s opinion relies on company documents that are themselves inconsistent with the expert’s narrative, the opponent may attack the expert’s use of the documents (selective reliance) and seek to place contradictory documents before the jury. Rule 703 allows reliance on such documents, but the expert must be prepared to explain why some documents are more probative or trustworthy than others. If the discrepancies reveal a methodological defect—such as ignoring contrary data—courts may find the opinion unreliable.

Appellate posture. On appeal, challenges to the admissibility of expert testimony are reviewed deferentially for abuse of discretion. Appellate courts will examine whether the district court engaged in the proper gatekeeping analysis under Rule 702 and Daubert and whether the court’s evidentiary rulings were supported by the record. The fact-intensive nature of the inquiry explains why outcomes vary with procedural posture, the quality of opposing expert work, and the particular facts at hand.

# Practical guidance and concluding points

Preparation and disclosure. Parties preparing expert testimony should ensure that expert reports comply fully with Rule 26(a)(2): opinions should be clearly stated, the bases and reasons disclosed, and the underlying data produced. Experts should document methods carefully, retain raw data, and be transparent about assumptions and limitations. For damages experts, the hypothetical negotiation, selection of comparable licenses, and apportionment reasoning must be explained in sufficient detail to satisfy Daubert scrutiny.

Responding to challenges. When attacking adversary experts, counsel should focus on methodological flaws, lack of fit between methodology and case facts, failure to consider contrary evidence, and unsupported extrapolations. Daubert motions should marshal record evidence showing the expert’s methods are unreliable as applied. Motions in limine can address demonstratives and tutorials that go beyond disclosed opinions.

Trial advocacy. Use demonstratives and tutorials to clarify without overstating. Anticipate and prepare for cross-examination on any assumptions, untested inferences, or selective reliance. Impeachment strategies for prior inconsistent statements are powerful, but counsel should take care to preserve foundation and aims consistent with the rules governing prior statements.

Damage models and apportionment. Damages experts must connect the patent’s value to the patented features and not attribute the whole market value to the patent unless a strong nexus exists (the Entire Market Value Rule’s narrow application). Apportionment is a frequent ground for Daubert exclusions of damages opinions.

Final observation. Expert testimony in patent cases exists at the intersection of law, technology, and economics. Courts exercise a gatekeeping role to ensure that only reliable and relevant expert evidence reaches juries, but they are cautious not to usurp the jury’s role in resolving genuine disputes. Understanding the interplay of Rules 702 and 703, claim-mapping techniques, the use of demonstratives, and the consequences of inconsistencies equips a litigator or student to evaluate, craft, or challenge expert evidence effectively.

Introduction

In patent litigation, judges and juries resolve fact-intensive questions about whether an accused product or process meets the elements of a patent claim, whether prior art anticipates or renders obvious the claimed invention, and what damages, if any, are appropriate. Appellate courts reviewing the sufficiency of the evidence supporting a jury verdict apply the substantial-evidence standard, a deferential test that asks whether the record contains relevant evidence such that a reasonable jury could have reached the verdict. Because patent disputes routinely involve complex technical subject matter, the courts focus less on the intuitiveness of a particular inference and more on whether the party who bore a burden of proof produced a logical, evidentiary bridge from the basic facts in the record to the ultimate claim-related conclusions.

This chapter explains the doctrine and practice of evidence sufficiency review in patent cases. It begins by defining substantial evidence and the reasonable-jury test. It then explains how documentary and testimonial proof operate in complex-technology disputes, stressing the role of expert testimony and reliable demonstratives. The chapter develops the concept of the inferential gap and why courts require clear mappings from evidence to claim elements. It describes how contradictions, speculation, and conclusory assertions affect sufficiency analysis. Finally, it treats the interplay between sufficiency review and motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), including timing, standards, and the relationship between trial and appellate review.

# Defining substantial evidence and the reasonable-jury test

Substantial evidence is the foundational standard for appellate review of jury factfindings. The phrase captures a deferential approach: an appellate court asks whether the trial record contains relevant evidence that a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support the jury’s conclusions. The inquiry is not whether the appellate court itself would have weighed the evidence differently; nor does it involve reweighing credibility determinations that the jury was entitled to make. Rather, the court must view the record in the light most favorable to the verdict winner and determine whether there is more than a mere scintilla of evidence linking the record to the essential facts underlying the verdict.

Two related features distinguish substantial-evidence review. First, the standard looks to relevant evidence, not to whether the evidence was compelling. Evidence that, taken together, yields a reasonable inference supporting the verdict suffices even if reasonable minds could disagree. Second, the standard is claim-specific and burden-sensitive. The amount and quality of evidence necessary to sustain a finding depend on which party bore the burden of proof and the degree of persuasion required. For example, factual findings that the patentee carried by a preponderance of the evidence need less in the way of corroboration than findings of invalidity that some doctrines require to be proved by clear and convincing evidence.

The reasonable-jury test operationalizes substantial evidence. When the jury returns a verdict on contested factual questions—e.g., that an accused product meets each limitation of a claim, or that certain prior-art references, taken together, make the claim obvious—the appellate court asks whether a reasonable juror, drawing permissible inferences from the evidence, could reach the same conclusion. If reasonable jurors could, the verdict stands; if not, the court must reverse or remand. The test implicates rules of admissibility and relevance only insofar as they affect whether the evidence in the record is proper for consideration. For that reason, evidentiary rulings that exclude or admit evidence can have significant sufficiency implications.

Different substantive contexts impose different burdens of proof, which feed into the sufficiency analysis. For infringement, liability for damages, and most affirmative claims by the patentee, the patentee has the burden of proving elements by a preponderance of the evidence. For affirmative defenses attacking the patent’s validity—anticipation, obviousness, enablement, written description—the defendant ordinarily must prove invalidity by clear and convincing evidence (although recent developments and special circumstances can affect the applicable standard). Because of this discrepancy, the sufficiency of evidence for a verdict finding validity may be judged against the higher clear-and-convincing threshold on appeal: the appellate court asks whether substantial evidence supports the finding given the required degree of persuasion.

# Documentary vs. testimonial proof in complex technology cases

Patent cases often hinge on technical facts: structural or functional aspects of an accused device, the content of prior art, the operation of an algorithm, or the state of ordinary skill in the art. These matters can be proved through documents (patents, prior publications, product manuals, source code, laboratory data, design logs), demonstratives and physical exhibits (models, schematics, samples), and testimony (lay witnesses, inventor testimony, fact witnesses, and most importantly, expert witnesses). The admissibility, sufficiency, and weight of these different forms of proof interact in defining whether there is substantial evidence to sustain a verdict.

Documentary evidence has particular strengths. Contemporaneous documents authored during the development of technology or prior-art references themselves can be powerful because they directly show the content of the art, design choices, or the physical features of an accused product. A well-preserved document can allow a jury to compare alleged features of a product to claim limitations without extensive interpretive leaps. Source code and schematics, if authenticated and explained, can demonstrate the existence (or absence) of structures and functions corresponding to claim elements.

Testimonial proof, especially expert testimony, is often necessary to translate complex documents and physical exhibits into jury-applicable findings. Experts explain terminology, interpret technical disclosures, opine on how a product operates, and synthesize multiple documents into an obviousness or enablement theory. Courts scrutinize expert testimony for its fit to the facts and for adequate foundation: an expert must explain the reasoning and methodology that underlie the opinion, and cannot simply assert legal conclusions. When testimony is the principal basis for a verdict, appellate courts examine whether the testimony, viewed in the record context and taking into account the jury’s credibility determinations, was sufficiently concrete and tied to admissible facts.

The relationship between documentary and testimonial evidence is symbiotic. Documents without testimony that explains how the document bears on claim elements may be inscrutable to jurors. Conversely, expert assertions divorced from evidentiary anchors—the documents, the product, or demonstrable test results—risk being viewed as ipse dixit. A robust trial presentation in a technology case typically offers both primary documentary or physical data and expert testimony that maps those materials to the legal elements the jury must decide.

Because jurors are lay factfinders, courts permit expert testimony to bridge technical gaps, but they require that the expert’s reasoning be disclosed and show a reliable basis. Where an expert’s testimony is cumulative of documentary proof, the combination strengthens a verdict; where both expert testimony and documentary proof are weak or speculative, the combination will not meet substantial evidence requirements.

# Bridging the inferential gap: need for clear mappings and explanations

A recurring theme in sufficiency review is the “inferential gap”: the distance between the basic facts in evidence and the ultimate fact the jury must find to apply the law. In patent cases, the inferential gap often appears when a party contends that some piece of evidence (a document, a component, a test result) corresponds to a claim limitation. Courts require a clear mapping from the admissible evidence to each claim element. If the party’s theory requires multiple inferential steps that are speculative or unsupported, appellate courts may hold that the evidence is not substantial.

Bridging the inferential gap typically requires three elements. First, the underlying facts must be in evidence: the document, the component, the operation logs, the test results. Second, there must be reliable testimony or explanation that ties those facts to the technical concept embodied in the claim limitation—this is often provided by an expert who explains the basis for the identification. Third, the chain of inference must be reasonable: each step must be supported by evidence, and the cumulative inference must not rely on speculation or mere possibility.

For example, to prove that an accused product contains a structure that performs a claimed function, a patentee needs evidence that the component in question actually performs that function and testimony explaining why the presence and operation of that component satisfy the claim language. Demonstratives and labels alone will not suffice; the jury needs admissible evidence showing both the presence and the operational effect. Similarly, when proving anticipation by a prior reference, the party must show that the reference discloses each claim limitation, not merely that the reference shares some general concept. The mapping requires showings that technical language corresponds, that the reference would have been understood by a person of ordinary skill in the art as disclosing the limitation, and that any claim terms resolved in claim construction match the reference’s disclosure.

Courts are attentive to whether the proof substitutes a chain of inference for direct evidence. A chain of inferences may be adequate if each link is supported, but courts will overturn findings based on unsupported inference stacks. The need to bridge the inferential gap is especially acute where the legal standard for the issue carries a higher burden (e.g., clear and convincing proof of invalidity) because the evidentiary foundation must be commensurately firmer.

# Impact of contradictions, speculation, and conclusory assertions

Not all evidence is created equal. Contradictory evidence, speculative testimony, and conclusory assertions can undermine the sufficiency of a record. The jury is charged with resolving conflicts in testimony and may accept one party’s version over another, but when contradictions are so fundamental that no reasonable juror could reconcile them into a coherent finding, an appellate court may conclude that the verdict lacks substantial evidence.

Contradictions in the record can take many forms. Two witnesses may give inconsistent descriptions of a process, or documents may present content that undermines a party’s claim about what was known or what was performed. A single expert may present one theory in a written report and another at trial. If a reasonable juror could treat these contradictions as resolved by credibility findings or plausible reconciliations, the verdict can stand. If resolution would require speculative leaps or ignore plainly contradictory documentary proof, the verdict cannot be sustained.

Speculation occurs when a party asks the jury to accept a factual proposition without sufficient evidentiary support. For example, inferring that a component that looks similar to a claimed structure necessarily works in the same way can be speculative absent testing, internal documentation, or reliable expert demonstration. Courts do not permit verdicts based on speculation that the accused device “must” operate in a particular manner when the record affords no reliable basis for that inference. Expert witnesses must ground their opinions in facts or data; pure conjecture is inadmissible and cannot be the basis for substantial evidence.

Conclusory assertions are another frequent shortcoming. A witness who opines that “the accused product meets the claim” without explaining how each claim limitation is satisfied provides no bridge from the record to the ultimate legal conclusion. Similarly, labeling a document as “teaching” a claim limitation without explaining the mapping—terminology alignment, functional correspondence, or structural equivalence—does not aid a jury in forming a reasoned inference. Courts expect witnesses, especially experts, to “connect the dots.” When testimony is conclusory, appellate courts are likely to view it as insufficient for substantial-evidence purposes.

The interaction between contradictions, speculation, and credibility determinations is central. Appellate courts do not substitute their judgment for reasonable credibility choices by the jury. However, where the only plausible interpretation of the record is one that contradicts the verdict, or where the record rests on speculation and conclusory testimony without evidentiary support, the substantial-evidence standard is not satisfied.

# Interplay between sufficiency review and JMOL

Judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), known in some courts and contexts as a Rule 50 motion, functions at trial and shapes the appellate sufficiency inquiry. A party may move for JMOL during trial after the opponent has presented its evidence and again after the verdict; failure to move precludes preserving sufficiency-related arguments for appeal in some jurisdictions. The standard for granting JMOL at trial is that a reasonable jury would not have a legally sufficient evidentiary basis to find for the nonmoving party on that issue.

At the JMOL stage, the trial judge determines whether the evidence presented, viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, could reasonably support a verdict for that party. The judge cannot weigh credibility or substitute her own view of the evidence; but if the evidence is legally insufficient—no reasonable jury could find for the nonmoving party—the judge must enter judgment for the moving party. A renewed JMOL after the verdict gives the trial court one last opportunity to correct a verdict the court finds unsupported by the evidence.

The appellate review of a JMOL denial or grant most commonly follows the substantial-evidence framework for underlying factual findings. When reviewing a denial of JMOL on factual issues, an appellate court typically applies the same deferential test: whether substantial evidence supports the jury’s verdict. The standard is effectively the same substantive question that the trial court confronted, though the appellate court reviews the trial court’s legal ruling de novo (i.e., without deference) when it comes to the legal standard applied to the sufficiency question. If the trial court granted JMOL, the appellate court reviews de novo whether the record contained sufficient evidence.

Procedural posture matters. A successful JMOL motion at the close of the opponent’s case is an early admission by the moving party that the evidence presented so far is legally insufficient. Denial of that motion preserves the factual dispute for resolution by the jury. A post-verdict JMOL motion allows the trial judge to set aside a verdict that the judge determines no reasonable jury could have reached. On appeal, courts look at the same record and the same evidentiary submissions, but they also consider whether the litigants preserved issues properly and whether any legal standards (such as the required burden of proof) were correctly applied at trial.

The practical import is that parties must present, at trial, a legally sufficient evidentiary record. Relying solely on inflammatory argument, demonstratives without evidentiary foundation, or conclusory expert assertions invites a JMOL ruling and, if a verdict nevertheless issues, a successful sufficiency challenge on appeal. Conversely, the denial of JMOL is not a legal rubber stamp; appellate courts will set aside jury verdicts that are unsupported even after a trial judge denied renewed JMOL, but they will do so only when the record as a whole lacks substantial evidence.

Taken together, the doctrines encourage litigants to produce evidence that directly ties to each element the jury must find, to preserve objections and JMOL motions to protect appellate review, and to recognize that the standard of persuasion (preponderance or clear and convincing) governs assessment of whether the evidence meets the sufficiency threshold.

Conclusion

Sufficiency review in patent cases balances deference to jury factfinding with the judiciary’s gatekeeping role against verdicts unsupported by a rational evidentiary foundation. Substantial evidence requires more than labels and conclusory assertions; it requires a reasonable chain of inference grounded in admissible facts, documentary proof, and reliable expert explanation. Documentary and testimonial proofs are complementary: documents provide raw materials while testimony interprets them for lay jurors. Courts will sustain a verdict when reasonable jurors could find for the prevailing party based on permissible inferences; they will set it aside when the record rests on speculation, contradictions that cannot be reconciled, or unexplained inferential leaps. Motions for JMOL are the procedural mechanism at trial that tests sufficiency, and their disposition frames the scope of appellate review. Understanding these principles prepares a reader to assess how courts analyze the sufficiency of evidence in complex patent disputes.

Introduction

Patent damages serve two essential purposes: to compensate the patent holder for infringement and to deter future violations. Determining the correct measure and amount of damages is frequently one of the most fact-intensive and contested portions of a patent trial. Courts apply a mixture of statutory command, settled doctrines, and equitable considerations to translate past infringement into an award in money. This chapter sets out the legal framework that governs damages in U.S. patent law, the familiar methodologies used by courts and experts, common evidentiary pitfalls, and the standards courts apply when a damages verdict is challenged or when liability is later disturbed on appeal. The goal is to equip the reader with the doctrinal tools necessary to understand how courts reason about compensatory patent awards and the practical limits on proof.

# 35 U.S.C. § 284 and the reasonable royalty floor

35 U.S.C. § 284 is the statutory anchor for patent damages. It provides that when a patent is infringed the court "may increase the damages up to three times the amount found or assessed" for willful infringement, and otherwise awards "damages adequate to compensate for the infringement, but in no event less than a reasonable royalty for the use made of the invention by the infringer." The statute imposes three central constraints or signals.

First, damages must be adequate to compensate the patentee for the infringement. That is a compensatory principle: the award should make the patentee whole for the economic harm caused by the unlawful use.

Second, the statutory minimum is a reasonable royalty. Even if a patentee cannot prove lost profits, or proves them only in part, the minimum recovery is a reasonable royalty measured by what the infringer would have paid in a hypothetical voluntary license for the infringement period. The reasonable royalty functions as a floor that ensures some recovery when causation or market proof of lost sales is incomplete.

Third, § 284 contemplates enhanced damages for willfulness, but the analysis of willfulness and enhancement follows separate doctrines (for example, the scope of culpability for enhanced damages and related procedural standards). The key point here is that § 284 requires courts first to measure compensatory damages (including reasonable royalties) before considering discretionary enhancement.

Two additional implications of § 284 are frequently litigated. One is whether a patentee who proves only a technical or minimal economic injury may nevertheless recover a reasonable royalty as a floor; courts uniformly allow it so long as the royalty is supported by competent proof. The second is that the royalty must be causal and tied to the patented invention's value; § 284 does not authorize windfalls that are unrelated to the infringement.

The patentee bears the burden of proving damages by a preponderance of the evidence. Damages must be proved with reasonable certainty, not speculative guesswork. Courts are willing to accept reasonable approximations so long as they are based on sound methodology and admissible evidence; however, awards resting on puro conjecture or untethered to the patented contribution typically fail.

# Georgia-Pacific factors and the hypothetical negotiation

When courts assess a reasonable royalty, they normally employ a hypothetical negotiation construct: what royalty rate would a willing licensee and willing licensor have agreed to just before the defendant began infringing, given the patent's value and the parties' economic positions? This framework focuses on ex ante bargaining and uses the circumstances that would have informed that hypothetical negotiation to fix a royalty for past infringement.

The most widely cited articulation of the factors that can shape that hypothetical negotiation are the Georgia-Pacific factors. These are a non-exhaustive list of considerations that courts may weigh in arriving at a reasonable royalty. They include, among others: established royalties for the patent or comparable patents; rates paid by the licensee for prior patents; the nature and scope of the license; the profitability and competitive relationship of the parties; the utility and advantages of the patent; the patent's contribution to the product selling price; the commercial success and long-felt need associated with the patented invention; the availability and cost of noninfringing alternatives; the portion of profit that would be allocated to the patent as opposed to other components; and the opinion testimony of qualified experts.

It is important to emphasize several doctrinal points about Georgia-Pacific and the hypothetical negotiation.

- The factors are neither a rigid checklist nor a mandatory balancing formula. A court need not address every factor so long as the ultimate royalty is supported by sound reasoning and evidence. Some factors may be inapplicable or overlapping; the admissibility and weight of evidence under each factor remains governed by the usual rules of evidence and relevance.

- The hypothetical negotiation is backward-looking in that it seeks to replicate what would have happened before infringement started. That constrains the parties' knowledge: neither side in the hypothetical possesses the benefit of hindsight about actual litigation outcomes or the infringer’s conduct after infringement began. However, parties could consider known marketplace facts and the perceived risks at the time of the putative license.

- Apportionment is a central subtheme of the Georgia-Pacific analysis. Several of the Georgia-Pacific factors directly address the need to tie any royalty to the value of the patented feature: for example, the patent's contribution to the product, the portion of profits attributable to the patented invention, and commercial success due to the patented feature. Courts require that a reasonable royalty compensate for the "use made of the invention," not for added value stemming entirely from unpatented features, brand, or other reasons.

- Evidence that anchors the hypothetical negotiation can come from prior licenses, comparable transactions, negotiated settlements, or internal documents showing the parties' willingness to pay for the patented technology. While prior licenses are often persuasive, they must be sufficiently comparable in technology, market, and timing, and properly adjusted to reflect differences.

- Experts play a major role in applying Georgia-Pacific. Their models should articulate how the facts translate into a royalty rate. Critically, expert testimony must grapple with apportionment, admissibility of comparables, and appropriate time value adjustments for royalties spanning many years.

# Lump-sum vs. running royalties; convoyed sales and apportionment

Two basic royalty structures commonly arise in patent damages: lump-sum (fixed) royalties and running (per-unit or percentage) royalties. Each has different implications for proof, apportionment, and the mechanics of damages computation.

A running royalty ties payment to units sold or revenues earned from products practicing the invention. Damage calculation is straightforward in principle: determine the infringing unit quantity or revenue for the infringement period, multiply by an agreed per-unit royalty or percentage rate, and adjust for offsets, credits, or licensed periods. Running royalties are particularly appropriate when the relationship between the patented feature and each unit sold is clear, and when the patent accounts for a measurable share of unit value.

Lump-sum royalties entail a single negotiated payment covering a period or scope of use. In a damages context, courts will attempt to disaggregate a lump-sum to the relevant infringement period and to apportion the payment among different uses or products if necessary. The valuation of a lump-sum requires present-value analysis and careful allocation of the sum across time, products, and geographic markets. Because a lump-sum can reflect bargaining over risk allocation, uncertainty about future sales, or coverage of multiple patented and unpatented features, courts scrutinize whether a lump-sum offered as comparable is sufficiently analogous to the hypothetical license in question.

Convoyed sales (sometimes called "related sales") occur where a patented feature is sold together with unpatented goods or services as part of a bundle. Typical examples include sales of an entire product that contains both patented and unpatented components (for example, a smartphone containing a patented chip) or a manufactured item sold together with complementary items. When assessing damages, the patentee must isolate (apportion) the value attributable to the patented component from the value of the convoyed, nonpatented elements. Failure to apportion properly risks awarding damages that compensate for value not created by the patented invention.

Apportionment requires experts and courts to distinguish between value derived from the patented features and value stemming from brand, marketing, design, or ancillary improvements. Legal doctrine requires that a patentee’s royalty base reflect the economic footprint of the invention, not the price of an entire multi-feature product unless the patented feature itself drives demand for the whole product. Several methodologies are used for apportionment: estimating the price premium attributable to the claimed feature, using cost-based measures for the patented component, comparing the accused product to a noninfringing alternative, or allocating profits according to comparable license splits. Whatever method is used, it must be logically tied to the facts and supported by evidence.

Key practical considerations when handling lump-sum vs. running royalties and convoyed sales:

- When experts propose using a lump-sum comparable to support a per-unit royalty, they must explain the conversion: how much of the lump-sum would have been allocated to the infringing uses, over what time horizon, and at what discount rate.

- Running royalties require accurate accounting of units and revenue. Disputes often arise over what constitutes a "unit" (e.g., software downloads, subscriptions, or bundled devices) and over that unit’s relation to the claimed invention.

- Convoyed sale situations commonly require elimination of value attributable to unpatented components. Experts must explain why the chosen apportionment methodology produces a reasonable estimate of the patented invention's contribution.

- The court will reject royalty theories that rely on overall product price where the patented feature is a small component and no evidence shows the patented feature drove consumer demand for the entire product.

# Entire market value rule and smallest saleable unit

Two closely related doctrines impose limits on using the entire value of an accused product as the damages base: the Entire Market Value Rule (EMVR) and the Smallest Saleable Unit (SSU) concept.

The Entire Market Value Rule allows a patentee to recover damages based on the entire market value (i.e., whole product price) only in the narrow circumstance where the patented feature is the basis for customer demand for the entire product. In other words, the EMVR applies if the patent-related feature is what causes consumers to buy the whole product. If other features or the product as a whole drive demand, then using the entire product value as the royalty base overcompensates the patentee. Courts require affirmative proof that the patented feature creates the demand for the complete product before permitting an EMVR-based royalty.

Because patentees often seek to base royalties on the full device price (for example, in complex products with many features), courts have developed the SSU idea as a practical constraint. The smallest saleable unit is the least complex, salable item practicing the claimed invention for which sales are recorded. Using the SSU as the royalty base limits the risk of inflating damages by including revenue from unrelated components. The SSU is not a doctrinal substitute for EMVR; rather, it is an evidentiary baseline that helps ensure apportionment. If the patentee can show the patented feature drives demand for the entire product, the SSU limitation may not foreclose using the entire device price; otherwise the SSU or some apportionment of the product price should govern.

Courts recognize that modern products can be complex and modular (e.g., hardware with software and services). In such cases, practical application of SSU involves:

- Identifying what is the smallest component that is sold and practices the claim. For software, this may be an instantiation or module; for hardware, a sub-assembly.

- Evaluating whether that unit is sold in isolation or only as part of a larger assembly. If the SSU is never sold separately, courts focus on whether the patented feature on its own justifies capturing the entire assembled product's value.

- Considering whether the patented feature is a technical improvement that meaningfully contributes to demand. Courts look to consumer surveys, external statements, or market data showing the feature's influence on purchase decisions.

The overarching principle is that damages must be proportionate to the economic contribution of the patent. EMVR allows whole-product damages only as an exception; SSU and apportionment doctrines operationalize the limitation that damages be tied to the patented invention's value.

# Standards for new trial on damages and vacatur when liability is reversed

Because damages derive from mixed legal and factual determinations, post-trial challenges to damages verdicts are common. Two principal procedural remedies exist at the trial-court level and on appeal: motions for a new trial (or remittitur) and vacatur or remand when liability is disturbed on appeal.

Standards for new trial and remittitur

A defendant dissatisfied with a jury's damages award can move for a new trial under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure or seek remittitur—that is, an alternative to a new trial where the court orders a reduced award instead. The standard for granting a new trial on damages typically requires a showing that the verdict is against the clear weight of the evidence, that the damages award is excessive or inadequate, or that the trial was marred by legal error affecting the damages determination. Trial courts have broad discretion in deciding motions for new trial, and appellate courts review those decisions under deferential standards: abuse of discretion is the norm for most aspects of damage-related gatekeeping.

Appellate courts will review sufficiency of the evidence challenges to determine whether the damages award is supported by substantial evidence. When damages are challenged on legal grounds—such as failure to apportion, misapplication of EMVR, or use of speculative assumptions—appellate review may apply de novo review to legal errors but will generally leave factual determinations undisturbed absent clear error.

Remittitur is an equitable mechanism whereby the court reduces an excessive jury award to an amount the court deems supported by the evidence and gives the prevailing party a choice between accepting the reduced award or undergoing a new trial on damages. Courts apply remittitur cautiously because it implicates the jury's role and the Seventh Amendment; however, it is appropriate where the court finds the jury's award inconsistent with the evidence or law.

Key points regarding motions for new trial and remittitur:

- The moving party bears a heavy burden to show that the jury’s damages award lacks evidentiary support or is legally defective.

- Courts will vacate or order remittitur where expert testimony is inadmissible or legally insufficient to support the award (for example, when an expert fails to apply apportionment or relies on inadmissible comparables).

- Procedural timing matters: a party must preserve objections at trial and move timely for a new trial or judgment as a matter of law to preserve appellate review.

Vacatur when liability is reversed on appeal

When an appellate court reverses liability wholly or in part, the fate of any damages award hinges on the scope of reversal. If liability is reversed in toto (no infringement), any damages awarded for the reversed infringement period typically cannot stand because there is no underlying liability to support them. The appellate decision will generally vacate the damages award and remand for further proceedings as necessary—this may include retrial on damages if some infringement liability remains, or entry of judgment for the defendant when the reversal is complete and no further proceedings are required.

If liability is reversed only in part (for example, some asserted claims are invalidated or some accused products are found not to infringe), courts must determine whether the remaining liability supports the previously entered damages award. This often requires parsing the award to allocate damages to particular claims and products. If the trial record supports a division of damages by claim or product, a remand for recalculation or a revised judgment can often cure the error. If the damages award is inextricably conflated across claims or products, vacatur and a new trial limited to damages or liability may be required.

Practical rules that guide vacatur and remand:

- When liability is reversed entirely, damages are vacated because the legal predicate for any compensation no longer exists.

- When liability is partially reversed, appellate courts try to preserve well-founded portions of the damages award, but will vacate or remand where damages cannot be reliably apportioned among valid and invalid bases.

- Appellate courts may also remand with instructions to the trial court to consider whether a new trial on damages is necessary or whether the existing record permits recalculation consistent with the appellate holdings.

- The standards for vacating damages are legal in nature when they depend on a legal defect (e.g., erroneous admission of certain evidence that was essential to the award) but may involve further factfinding when apportionment or recalculation is possible.

- If the appellate reversal implicates willfulness or enhanced damages, those remedial aspects may likewise require reconsideration or vacatur since enhancement presupposes underlying culpable infringement.

# Common evidentiary pitfalls and best practices

Proof of damages is as much about admissibility and method as it is about numbers. Several recurrent pitfalls can undermine a damages case.

Unreliable comparables. Prior licenses and settlements can be powerful evidence for reasonable royalty determinations, but they are probative only if sufficiently comparable. Courts will exclude or discount comparables that differ materially in technology, scope, market conditions, or bargaining posture. Experts should explain adjustments for timing, market size, and covered patents.

Failure to apportion. Perhaps the most critical and frequent error is failing to tie the royalty base to the patented invention. Experts must show how their methodology isolates the patent's economic contribution. Just taking the entire product price without proof that the patent drives demand will typically be rejected.

Hindsight bias in the hypothetical negotiation. Experts and fact witnesses sometimes rely on knowledge obtained after infringement began (including litigation outcomes) to justify a particular negotiated rate. The hypothetical negotiation must mimic the ex ante positions of a willing licensor and licensee; reliance on post-infringement knowledge must be carefully justified or avoided.

Improper use of the entire market value. Treat EMVR as an exception: courts will require compelling proof that the patent created demand for the whole product. Absent that, start from the SSU or otherwise apportion.

Discounting and present value errors. When damages span multiple years—particularly with lump-sum conversions—experts must correctly account for time value of money and appropriate discount rates. Mistakes in discounting can materially distort present-value calculations.

Speculative lost profits. Lost profits require proof of but-for sales, causation, and foreseeability, including proof that the patentee would have made the sales absent infringement and that sufficient market demand existed. Courts will reject lost-profit claims that rest on speculative assumptions about demand or customer behaviors.

Unsubstantiated assumptions about market share or profitability. Using overall corporate profit margins to justify a share attributable to the patent is risky absent specific evidence linking profitability to the patented feature.

To avoid these pitfalls:

- Tie every step of the damages model to admissible evidence in the record.

- Explicitly address apportionment early and show how the chosen royalty base reflects only patented value.

- When using comparables, carefully identify similarities and differences and explain any adjustments.

- Preserve objections at trial and be prepared to defend expert methodologies under Daubert or similar standards.

- Use sensitivity analyses to show how conclusions vary with changes in key assumptions; this increases transparency and helps courts assess the reasonableness of estimates.

Conclusion

Calculating patent damages is a hybrid legal-economic exercise that requires aligning statutory commands, doctrinal constraints, and careful factual proof. 35 U.S.C. § 284 mandates compensation at least as large as a reasonable royalty; the hypothetical negotiation and Georgia-Pacific factors provide a flexible but structured way to fix a royalty; and doctrines such as apportionment, the EMVR, and the SSU protect against awards untethered to the patented contribution. The form of royalty—lump-sum or running—affects valuation mechanics and evidentiary needs, and convoyed sales heighten the need for apportionment. Trial courts possess broad discretion in awarding damages and in granting new trials or remittitur, but appellate courts will intervene where legal errors or insufficient evidence undermine an award. Understanding these doctrines and common evidentiary traps is essential for litigants, experts, and judges to produce damages awards that are compensatory, proportional, and legally sustainable.

Introduction

Method claims in the realm of communication and signal-processing technologies present a distinctive set of doctrinal and practical issues. These claims often recite sequences of mathematical and algorithmic steps (sampling, filtering, Fourier transforms, complex multiplication, modulus operations), but they also implicate hardware architectures (FPGAs, ASICs, DSPs), firmware, and software implementations. Before reading judicial opinions that interpret or apply such claims, a student must understand (1) how method claims are typically drafted in this technical field; (2) how courts construe mathematical operations and algorithmic steps; (3) how different implementation media affect claim scope and infringement analysis; (4) how to distinguish actual performance of steps from mere capability; (5) how single-actor and divided-performance issues arise; and (6) how parties prove that the claimed method was performed (or anticipated) using technical schematics, flowcharts, code, and experimental data. This chapter presents those topics in doctrinal and practical detail, focusing on legal reasoning, elements and tests, burdens of proof, and common evidentiary approaches.

# Common claim structures for sampling, filtering, and digital multiplication

Method claims in signal-processing patents are usually structured as a sequence of steps that transform a signal from one representation to another. Typical high-level steps include acquiring or sampling an analog waveform, converting it to a digital representation, applying time-domain or frequency-domain filters, performing arithmetic or linear algebraic operations (including complex arithmetic), and outputting or storing transformed samples. Drafting patterns and claim-construction issues flow from how those steps are described.

A representative structural pattern is: (1) receive an input signal; (2) sample or digitize the signal at a specified rate or under specified conditions; (3) perform a filtering or windowing operation described by a transfer function, kernel, or coefficient set; (4) perform a multiplication, convolution, correlation, or transform (e.g., FFT/IFFT); (5) compute a derived quantity (e.g., magnitude, phase, power, channel estimate); and (6) take an output action (e.g., transmit, store, apply to demodulation). Each step can be further qualified by parameters (sampling rate, filter coefficients, FFT size), conditions (synchronization, time-locked acquisition), or intended use (navigation, demodulation, spectral analysis).

From a legal perspective, method claim analysis begins with claim construction. Courts construe claim terms in light of intrinsic evidence (the claim language, specification, prosecution history) and may consult extrinsic technical dictionaries or expert testimony. For mathematical operations and algorithmic steps, courts assess whether the claim language is describing a mathematical abstraction (which raises patent-eligibility concerns under doctrines that ask whether the claim is directed to an abstract idea) or whether the claim ties the mathematics to a specific practical application or technological improvement. Even when eligibility is not at issue, construction clarifies what each step requires for infringement and validity analyses.

Two drafting nuances commonly arise and have distinct doctrinal consequences. First, whether a claimed step recites a functional result (e.g., “filtering to reduce interference”) or a structural process (e.g., “apply a band-pass filter with impulse response h[n] = ...”) affects definiteness and enablement inquiries. Vague functional language invites challenges that the claim fails to particularly point out and distinctly claim the invention. Second, specifying numerical parameters—sampling rate, filter cutoffs, window length—can narrow claim scope and provide definite boundaries for infringement and prior-art comparison. But overly narrow numerical limits can make validity vulnerable to anticipation or obviousness over similar parameter choices.

In claims involving sampling, courts often focus on whether the sampling rate or quantization is an essential part of the claimed invention. For filtering, they examine whether the claim requires a particular architecture (FIR vs. IIR), coefficient values, or simply the abstract notion of “filtering.” For multiplication and other arithmetic steps, claim language may recite scalar multiplication, complex multiplication, element-wise vector multiplication, or matrix multiplication. The distinction matters: convolution, pointwise multiplication in the frequency domain (via the convolution theorem), and matrix-vector multiplications each have different technical meanings that affect infringement and anticipation tests.

# Hardware/firmware/software implementations (e.g., FPGAs, DSPs)

Method claims in signal processing can be implemented in many media: general-purpose processors running software, specialized DSP cores, FPGAs programmed with HDL, ASICs with hardwired datapaths, or combinations thereof. The chosen implementation medium has legal implications for claim construction, enablement, means-plus-function treatment, and infringement.

Claim construction. If the claim language expressly recites a device or apparatus (e.g., “a digital signal processor configured to perform steps A–E”), courts will construe the claim against that recitation. Pure method claims that do not recite a particular hardware platform are broader and may cover any means of performing the recited steps. However, when the specification repeatedly emphasizes a particular hardware embodiment (for example, an FPGA architecture) without disclaiming other means, courts may interpret claim terms in light of those embodiments, particularly when terms are ambiguous. The doctrine of claim differentiation and the specification’s stated field of invention may guide whether hardware-specific limitations should be imported into the claim.

Enablement and written-description. Complex signal-processing methods often require disclosure of algorithmic detail or hardware constraints to enable a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA) to practice the invention across the full scope of the claims. Implementations on FPGAs and DSPs entail different programming and timing considerations; a specification that teaches only a high-level algorithm but does not disclose how to map the algorithm onto hardware resources (e.g., pipeline stages, fixed-point wordlengths, coefficient quantization) may be vulnerable to enablement challenges if the claim scope covers those unaddressed variations. Courts apply the enablement standard: the specification must teach how to make and use the claimed invention without undue experimentation. For software implementations, source code, pseudo-code, and flowcharts can supply enablement; for hardware, schematics, timing diagrams, and HDL examples may be necessary.

Means-plus-function claims. When claims use functional language tied to a “means for” or equivalent phrasing, they may be interpreted under 35 U.S.C. § 112(f) (or equivalent case law). Under that provision, the claim covers the corresponding structure described in the specification and equivalents, rather than all structures that perform the function. For instance, a “means for computing a fast Fourier transform” could be limited to the FFT structures disclosed (a radix-2 iterative algorithm implemented on a specific pipeline) and equivalents. Courts will look to the specification for adequate structure. Conversely, if the claim recites specific structural language (e.g., “FFT engine comprising a butterfly unit, bit-reversal unit, and twiddle-factor memory”), it is not a means-plus-function claim.

Infringement analysis by implementation medium. For method claims, infringement occurs when the accused party performs each claimed step. When an accused system is software on a general-purpose processor, direct infringement requires that an actor cause the processor to perform each step, often by executing software instructions. For firmware in FPGAs or microcontrollers, the executed bitstream or microcode effects the steps. Distinctions arise in reverse engineering and evidence. For software implementations, source code, binaries with symbol tables, and runtime logs are probative. For FPGA or ASIC implementations, bitstreams, RTL, gate-level netlists, and hardware test vectors are relevant.

Performance characteristics (timing, precision, parallelism) also affect infringement. A claim that requires “real-time filtering with latency less than $T$ ms” depends on the actual implementation’s timing behavior; performance measured on a simulator or on-the-bench hardware may be required to prove infringement or invalidity. Implementations that achieve the same mathematical result but via different operational timing or parallelization still may infringe if the claim does not state timing constraints.

Interoperability and standards. Communication systems often interact with standardized protocols. Implementations may be accused of infringing even if the patented method is performed only in some devices within a standard. Conversely, standard-essentiality, licensing commitments, or patent exhaustion issues can arise. Courts will evaluate whether the claim covers necessary protocol steps and whether an accused device necessarily performs them when complying with the standard.

# Interpreting mathematical operations (e.g., modulus, conjugates) in claims

Mathematical and complex-number operations are central to signal processing claims. Understanding how courts treat such operations is essential for claim construction, patent-eligibility, and infringement/invalidity analyses. The key points are: what the claim means by a mathematical operation; whether the operation is an abstract idea; and how to translate such operations into actionable steps or structures.

Plain-language construction. Courts often interpret mathematical terms according to their ordinary meaning in the field unless the specification defines them otherwise. Terms like “modulus,” “conjugate,” “real part,” or “imaginary part” have well-understood definitions in electrical engineering and applied mathematics. For example, for a complex number $z = a + jb$, where $j$ is the imaginary unit, “conjugate” normally means $\overline{z} = a - jb$, and “modulus” means $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$. Courts will typically allow a POSITA’s understanding to inform construction and may admit expert testimony to clarify subtleties (for example, when the claim uses a different notation or when the field uses $i$ or $j$ for the imaginary unit).

Algorithmic vs. structural descriptions. When a claim recites a mathematical operation without tying it to a particular machine or improvement, it may be susceptible to a challenge that it is an abstract idea or purely mathematical algorithm. The Federal Circuit and Supreme Court jurisprudence emphasize that recitation of mathematical formulas or abstract operations can be patent-ineligible unless the claim is directed to their application in a practical technological environment. Thus, claim drafters and courts often seek a connecting thread: Does the claimed operation solve a technological problem in a specific way (e.g., by reducing computational complexity, enabling hardware efficiency, or improving signal fidelity under defined constraints)? If so, the claim is more likely to be seen as directed to a practical application.

Discrete vs. continuous formulations. Many signal-processing claims operate on discrete-time samples (indices $n$) or discrete-frequency bins ($k$) while mathematical texts may use continuous variables ($t$, $f$). Construction must reconcile these formulations. For instance, a claim that recites “compute the FFT of the sampled input” implicitly assumes discrete samples and may require conversion of continuous-time signals to samples at some rate. The court will consider the specification and context to decide whether the claim requires a certain sampling theorem compliance (e.g., Nyquist criteria) or whether sampling is simply a step whose particulars are left to the POSITA.

Approximation, precision, and numeric representations. Practical implementations use finite precision representations (fixed point, floating point) and quantized coefficients. Claims that recite an operation like “compute $|z| = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$” do not always address whether approximations or mathematical identities (e.g., a magnitude approximation avoiding square root) infringe. Courts typically construe such claims to require performance of the claimed mathematical result as a person of skill in the art would understand it, and proof can consider whether approximations are equivalent under an objective standard. Disputes often turn on whether an approximation provides results that fall within a tolerable error bound relative to the claim’s purpose. If the claim explicitly states a tolerance or approximation threshold, that controls.

Order and grouping of operations. In signal processing, associative, commutative, and distributive properties may allow different computational orders to yield the same result. A claim that prescribes a specific order (e.g., “first multiply, then filter”) may be narrower than one that requires only the eventual outcome. Infringement analysis examines whether a different order nevertheless performs the same claimed step or produces an equivalent result. Claim language matters: “perform steps A then B” is typically interpreted as requiring that sequence. Conversely, claims that do not specify order may permit any order that accomplishes the end result.

Mathematical expressions as claim limitations. Courts will treat explicit mathematical expressions in claims as definite limitations. If a claim recites an equation, proving infringement requires showing that the accused process computes that equation (within any stated tolerances). When the claim language is silent, courts may not import mathematical detail beyond what a POSITA would necessarily understand. Therefore, the precise wording of mathematical limitations is determinative.

# Direct performance vs. capability; single-actor performance of steps

Methods claims require performance of each claimed step. The doctrinal distinction between actual performance and capability, and the questions about single-actor performance (direct infringement) versus divided performance (multiple actors), are critical in signal-processing contexts where systems are distributed, cloud-based, or involve multiple cooperating entities.

Direct infringement and step performance. Direct infringement of a method claim occurs when a single actor performs every step of the claimed method. For an accused system, this means that the entity must be responsible for each step: the actor itself must perform them or control/induce their performance. The evidentiary focus is on who executes the steps in fact, regardless of whether the claim is implemented in hardware or software.

Capability vs. actual performance. Some claims can be asserted against devices or systems that are merely capable of performing the claimed steps (e.g., a programmable radio that can be configured to execute the claimed algorithm). Courts have grappled with whether mere capability suffices for infringement or whether an accused infringer must actually configure and operate the device to perform the method. The prevailing rule is that mere capability alone, without more, typically does not constitute direct infringement; there must be proof that the device was used to perform the claim or that the defendant vicariously controlled or directed another to perform the steps. However, capability evidence can be relevant to inducement claims or to argue that a reasonable inference of use exists when combined with circumstantial evidence (e.g., marketing documents, default configurations, routine operations).

Single-actor requirement and divided performance. When steps of a claimed method are performed by multiple parties, the single-actor requirement can prevent finding direct infringement. Courts analyze whether there is a single entity that directs or controls the entire process, either by contract, agency, or by conditioning participation and providing instructions that lead to the performance of the method. The “direction or control” test asks whether the defendant conditions participation in an activity or service on performing certain steps and establishes the manner or timing of that performance. If such direction exists, a court may aggregate multiple parties’ actions to attribute all steps to a single actor.

Examples particular to signal processing. Communication systems often split processing across transmitters, receivers, servers, and network elements. A transmitter may sample and pre-filter signals, a base station may perform channel equalization, and a handset may compute demodulation. When a claim recites a method covering steps that naturally fall on different devices, plaintiffs must show that a single entity performed all steps or that the defendant controlled others who performed missing steps. Courts scrutinize the claim language—if claim steps are directed to operations on a single device (e.g., “the receiver performs X, Y, Z”), and the accused system distributes steps, the plaintiff faces a challenge.

Induced infringement and contributory infringement. Where multiple actors perform different steps but a defendant induces the combined behavior, inducement (under 35 U.S.C. § 271(b) in the U.S. regime) may be invoked. Inducement requires evidence that the defendant knowingly induced infringement and possessed specific intent that the induced acts constitute infringement. Marketing materials, instructions, software downloads, or configuration scripts that encourage the practice of the claimed method may support inducement. Contributory infringement can arise when a defendant supplies a component that has no substantial non-infringing use and is material to practicing the invention; in signal processing, this could encompass specialized hardware or code modules.

Joint enterprise or agency. Liability can attach when actors operate under agency or joint-enterprise principles such that their combined actions are attributed to a single legal actor. Courts consider whether an arrangement involved mutual benefit, common plan, and shared control; these facts may permit treating multiple performers as a single actor for infringement purposes.

# Proving operation using technical schematics and algorithm descriptions

Proof of infringement or invalidity in signal-processing cases routinely depends on technical documents: block diagrams, flowcharts, HDL or C code, timing diagrams, test vectors, specification tables, and experimental results. Understanding the relevant types of evidence and how courts treat them is essential.

Types of evidence

- Source code and binaries. Source code is direct evidence of algorithmic steps and control flow. It can show that a device executes specific routines corresponding to claim steps. Binaries, decompiled code, or symbol-stripped executables may require expert analysis to map to claimed steps. Courts often permit in camera review of proprietary code.

- Flowcharts and pseudo-code. These are used to demonstrate logical structure and the order of operations. Pseudo-code in a specification or internal documents can show the invention’s computation and often bridges between abstract math and implementable steps.

- Hardware schematics and HDL. For FPGA or ASIC implementations, schematics, register-transfer-level (RTL) code, netlists, and bitstreams show parallel datapaths, pipelining, and resource utilization. These are useful to demonstrate whether a claimed operation is implemented in hardware and whether any structural claim limitations are met.

- Timing diagrams and test vectors. These documents show the temporal behavior of a system and can prove performance constraints (throughput, latency) or that inputs produce claimed outputs. Logic analyzers and oscilloscopes provide empirical runtime traces that can be matched against claim steps.

- Data sets and experimental results. Replaying real or synthetic signal traces through an accused system and observing outputs is direct experimental evidence. Controlled lab experiments often strengthen the link between claimed operations and accused products.

- Manuals, datasheets, and marketing materials. These can be circumstantial evidence of capability, default operation, or intended use. Statements that a device “implements FFT-based spectral analysis” or “supports complex multiplication in the frequency domain” may be used to infer that steps are performed in practice.

- Expert testimony. Because claims involve technical methods, courts allow experts to interpret complex evidence, map code and hardware to claim elements, and explain equivalence and tolerances. Experts may also provide comparisons between claimed mathematical operations and approximations used in practice.

Standards of proof and burdens. The plaintiff bears the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence in civil litigation. For invalidity defenses (anticipation, obviousness), the accused infringer bears the burden of proof by clear and convincing evidence (in U.S. practice), although that standard can vary by jurisdiction. Expert witnesses typically present claim charts that map claim steps to accused product elements, providing the factual basis for the court’s verdict.

Claim charts and element-by-element analysis. Courts and litigants often use claim charts to compare each claim limitation with evidence from the accused product or prior art. The chart identifies where each element is found in source code, hardware, or documentation. For mathematical steps, the chart should show how inputs map to outputs and include tolerances where applicable.

Dealing with black-box devices. When the accused product’s internal workings are not publicly available, litigants rely on reverse engineering, import records, teardown analyses, firmware extraction, and behavioral testing. Demonstrating that a black-box device performs a claimed method may require setting up controlled input-output experiments and using statistical analysis to show that outputs align with expected behavior for the claimed processing. Courts treat circumstantial behavioral evidence as admissible but subject to scrutiny about alternative explanations.

Equivalence and approximation evidence. Implementations often use approximations (e.g., magnitude squared approximations to avoid square roots). To prove that such implementations meet claim limitations, the party must demonstrate equivalence—either literal equivalence (the same mathematical operation) or equivalence under the doctrine of equivalents, which requires showing that differences are insubstantial. Experts typically quantify error bounds and demonstrate that approximations achieve the claimed function in substantially the same way to yield substantially the same result, considering the claim’s purpose.

Chain-of-custody and reproducibility. When experimental tests are central, courts expect rigorous documentation and reproducible procedures. Test setups, signal sources, measurement tools, and software versions must be recorded. Opposing experts will probe experimental controls, sampling adequacy, and whether tests actually exercise the claimed steps.

Timing and conditional steps. For claims that include conditional branches (e.g., “if SNR < X then perform step A else perform step B”) proving infringement requires showing the accused system reached the relevant condition and executed the corresponding branch. Evidence must therefore capture stateful behavior or configuration patterns that trigger distinct processing paths.

# Conclusion

Method claims in communication and signal-processing technologies intertwine legal doctrine with technical precision. Law students should appreciate that claim construction, enablement, and infringement questions all depend on how algorithmic steps, mathematical operations, and hardware implementations are described and evidenced. Courts will parse claim language for sequential and conditional step requirements, reconcile mathematical abstractions with concrete technological applications, and require proof that a single actor performed all steps (or that others acted under that actor’s direction). Proving the performance of claimed steps relies heavily on technical artifacts—code, schematics, flowcharts, timing diagrams, and experimental data—interpreted through expert testimony. Mastery of these doctrinal principles and evidentiary methods will prepare a reader to follow judicial analyses of method claims in signal-processing contexts and to anticipate the issues likely to determine claim scope and enforcement.

Introduction

Proving patent infringement requires a careful mapping of each asserted claim element to an accused product or process. Technical documentation—engineering diagrams, block charts, schematics, source-code comments, test procedures, and product specifications—often supplies the most direct route to that mapping. But treating such documents as proof demands attention to evidentiary foundations, proper interpretation, and corroboration with objective operational proof. This chapter explains how courts and litigants authenticate and interpret engineering materials, translate the nomenclature used by engineers into claim terms, use mathematical notation and signal labels as evidentiary proof, avoid common misreads of ambiguous legends or labels, and connect design documentation to the device’s actual operation. The objective is to equip you with the doctrinal tools and practical habits necessary to convert technical documents into admissible, persuasive evidence of infringement while anticipating and avoiding common defenses.

# Authenticating and interpreting engineering diagrams and block charts

Authentication and foundational requirements

Before an engineering diagram or block chart can be admitted as evidence, a proponent must satisfy basic authentication rules: show that the document is what the proponent claims it to be. Typical means of authentication in technical cases include testimony from a witness with personal knowledge (the author, an engineer involved in the design, or a custodian of records), metadata and file histories, version-control records, manufacturing or release records that reference the document, and corroborating emails or meeting minutes that discuss the document.

When a document is internal to the accused infringer, a sponsoring witness from that company (preferably the author or a knowledgeable custodian) can establish authorship, date, purpose, and distribution. When an internal witness is unavailable, authentication may rely on a business-records foundation—showing regular practice of maintaining such documents and the trustworthiness of the record-keeping process—or on a combination of metadata (timestamps, file paths, repository logs) and third-party corroboration (e.g., a customer or supplier email referring to the diagram). Be alert to chain-of-custody issues for physical plans and printed diagrams; for digital files, preserve original files and extract metadata in a forensically sound manner.

Interpretation: how courts treat diagrams

Courts treat diagrams and block charts as documentary evidence of what a product was designed to do and how its components are intended to interconnect. But diagrams rarely speak for themselves. A diagram may function as intrinsic evidence of design intent, but it often requires expert testimony to translate engineering conventions for a factfinder. Interpretive tasks include identifying component symbols, tracing signal and data flows, and explaining timing relationships or control logic implied by arrows, dashed lines, or layering in the diagram.

Two important interpretive considerations bear emphasis.

First, diagrams are interpreted in context. That includes the caption, legend, titles on adjacent pages, associated specification text, revision notes, and related documents (data sheets, interface control documents, and source code) that clarify ambiguous symbols. A block labeled “FFT Block,” for example, might be a hardware accelerator, a software library, or a conceptual step in a design flow. Contextual documents help identify which.

Second, diagrams may be conceptual rather than literal. High-level block diagrams often represent functional relationships without specifying physical implementation. When the claim requires a particular structure (e.g., a “hardware module” vs. “software routine” or a distinct memory), you must show that the diagram depicts an implemented component rather than an abstract function. That may require linking the block to fabrication drawings, bill-of-materials, firmware images, or test logs demonstrating the block’s physical instantiation.

Best practices for preparing diagrams for trial

When relying on diagrams, litigators and testifying experts should prepare a clear evidentiary chain: identify the specific version and date; obtain witness foundation for authorship and intended meaning; prepare a demonstrative that highlights the parts corresponding to claim elements; and assemble corroborating documents to address any ambiguities. When possible, preserve native files to retain layers and callouts that may explain relationships obscured in flattened images.

# Correlating nomenclature in documents to claim terms

The gap between an engineer’s language and a patent claim’s terms is a frequent battleground. Engineers use product-specific jargon, acronyms, and internal names; patent claims use legal terms of art formed during prosecution and claim construction. Bridging that gap requires methodical correlation, grounded in claim construction and the intrinsic record, supplemented by extrinsic evidence as needed.

Start with claim construction

Before mapping, you must know the judicially construed meaning of each claim term. Claim construction defines the universe of admissible mappings by identifying the precise legal elements that must be proved. A term construed to require a specific structure, result, or function constrains how nomenclature in documents can be correlated. For example, if a claim term is construed as a “single integrated processor” rather than “any processor or processor cluster,” internal documents that use the plural “CPUs” will require additional proof to show they function as a single integrated processor.

Direct naming and labeling

The most straightforward mapping occurs when an internal document uses the same or synonymous term as the claim. A specification sheet labeled “signal comparator” can be matched to a claim element “comparator” if the document’s description and context show the same functionality. But beware literal name matches that mislead: engineers sometimes use marketing labels that don’t reflect the technical function relevant to the claim. Corroboration is necessary to ensure the named component actually performs the claimed operation.

Functional equivalence and translation of jargon

Engineers often describe a claim element using different words. To bridge this, the proponent must show functional equivalence: that the component described in the document performs the same role or achieves the same result specified by the claim. Demonstrate this by parsing the document’s description of inputs, outputs, timing, and algorithms and mapping them onto the claim’s required limitations. When a claim uses functional language (e.g., “means for doing X” or a functional limitation under applicable statutory provisions), showing that the disclosed component performs the stated function and corresponds to a structure known in the art is critical.

Interpreting acronyms and versioned names

Internal artifacts change names across revisions. Always trace nomenclature across document versions and repositories. An acronym introduced in a design overview should be defined somewhere; if it is not, search related documents and commit logs for the earliest introduction and definitions. Preserve an evidentiary trail that connects the acronym to the technical function and, ultimately, the claim term. If a term is ambiguous, use other artifacts—source code identifiers, test case names, email threads—to demonstrate consistent usage linking the engineer’s term to the claimed concept.

Handling means-plus-function and structural claims

When claims are in means-plus-function format or otherwise specify structure, the mapping must focus on corresponding structure disclosed in the patent or known equivalents. Correlate internal structure names (component identifiers, module numbers, part numbers) to the structure identified or required by claim construction. Where a claim recites a specific algorithm (e.g., a control law), the correspondence must show that the internal component implements that algorithm or its equivalent. Documentation that merely states an intention to implement a function without providing structure is weak proof unless complemented by implementation artifacts.

Burdens and the role of expert witnesses

The patentee bears the burden of proving infringement by a preponderance of the evidence. Expert witnesses play a central role in translating nomenclature and showing technical equivalence. Expert testimony should explain the engineer’s language, map signals and components to claim limitations, and reference other documents and testing that corroborate the mapping in terms that a factfinder can understand. Experts must avoid conveying mere ipse dixit; their opinions should be grounded in demonstrable document trails, reproducible tests, and standard engineering practices.

# Mathematical notation and signal labels as evidentiary proof

Mathematical formulas, parameter tables, and signal labels frequently appear in technical docs and can powerfully show that an accused product meets quantitative claim limitations. They must, however, be tied to measurable quantities and interpreted reliably.

Mathematical notation: what it proves and the foundation required

A formula in a design document that defines a threshold, filter coefficient, or timing relationship can, if authenticated and properly interpreted, provide direct evidence that a system satisfies a numerical claim limitation (e.g., “less than 10 ms,” “attenuation of at least 30 dB,” or a particular mathematical transformation). To be probative, the proponent should show:

- The formula’s provenance and applicability: that the formula governs the accused product’s implementation and is not merely a theoretical note or an offhand calculation.
- The units and constants: mathematical expressions are meaningless without units and defined variables. A symbol such as $T$ must be tied to a measurable time interval, and constants (e.g., sampling rate $f_s$) must be shown by document or measurement.
- Tolerances and implementation approximations: engineering practice often uses approximations. Explain how approximations affect whether the claimed threshold is met—e.g., whether rounding or component tolerances could defeat a literal numerical limitation.

Signal labels and waveform evidence

Signal names and labels on schematics (e.g., “CLK_OUT,” “DATA_IN[7:0],” “reset_n”), together with timing diagrams and annotated waveforms, can demonstrate how signals propagate and whether particular asserted timing or logical relationships are present. To use such evidence:

- Show the mapping from the signal label to the physical pin, net, or variable in firmware/source code.
- Produce timing diagrams or logic analyzer captures from an actual device showing the labeled signals behaving as the documentation predicts.
- Explain how signal naming conventions correspond to functionality rather than merely indicating design intent; corroborate with test logs or source-level identifiers.

Admissibility and hearsay concerns

Mathematical calculations or tabulated values created by engineers are admissible as business records if the foundation is satisfied, but they may also be hearsay if introduced to prove the truth of the matter asserted without a proper exception. Often the safest route is to have an expert adopt the calculation, re-create the computation independently, and explain the methodology and its relevance. Alternatively, the author of the calculations may testify and be cross-examined about assumptions and methodology.

Using simulations and derived calculations

When equations in documentation are used to derive a claim-limiting result (for example, showing that a design achieves a certain signal-to-noise ratio), supporting evidence should include input parameters drawn from authenticated documents and either lab measurements or a reproducible simulation. Document the simulation environment, seed inputs, and the toolchain used so opposing experts can replicate results. Where the formula depends on idealized conditions, be prepared to address how real-world non-idealities affect compliance with the claim limitation.

# Avoiding misreads and clarifying ambiguous legends or labels

Ambiguities in legends, labels, or diagram conventions are common sources of dispute. The proponent must anticipate likely alternate readings and eliminate them through careful corroboration.

Common sources of misreads

Ambiguity often arises from undefined abbreviations, generic boxes in block diagrams, dashed versus solid lines, unlabeled arrows, overlapping layers in multi-sheet designs, and revisions with inconsistent labeling. Draft annotations, redline comments, or placeholders (e.g., “TODO: define interface”) may be mistaken for final specification.

Strategies to disambiguate

1. Seek contemporaneous defining artifacts. Look for documents that define the contested label—design requirement documents, interface control documents, data sheets, codebase comments, or meeting minutes where the term was introduced and explained. A contemporaneous definition is stronger than a later explanation.

2. Use the author or a custodian to clarify. If available, obtain a witness who participated in creating or using the document to explain the intended meaning and whether the document is a schematic of an as-built structure or a conceptual sketch.

3. Cross-check multiple documents. Corroborate the meaning through other artifacts: part numbers in the bill of materials, schematic netlists, firmware variables, test names, and produced hardware. Inconsistencies between documents require explanation; courts are more likely to credit an interpretation that explains all materials coherently.

4. Avoid reliance on a single ambiguous figure. If an argument rests solely on one unclear legend, opposing counsel will exploit the ambiguity. Strengthen the case by combining the diagram with operational evidence (measurements, logs), code that references the label, or physical inspection.

5. Explain conventional meanings. Where a symbol follows standard engineering conventions (for example, a grounded triangle indicating common reference or a dashed arrow denoting optional connection), an expert can explain the conventional reading. This is persuasive, but beware of situations where the designer intentionally repurposed symbols.

Handling revisions and conflicting versions

Design documentation evolves. When confronted with multiple versions that use different labels or legends, map the evolution: identify which version was implemented, which versions were draft or superseded, and whether changes were cosmetic or substantive. Present a timeline supported by metadata and release notes to show which terminology governed the released product. Courts will generally credit the version tied to production and release, not an early conceptual sketch.

Dealing with redactions and redlines

Redactions and redlines complicate interpretation. Preserve copies before redaction where possible; otherwise ensure the unredacted portions suffice to make the mapping. If redactions hide critical definitions, seek discovery or judicial intervention; but be prepared to show that sufficient corroborating evidence exists despite the gaps.

# Linking documents to actual operation, not just design goals

Documents are more persuasive when tied to proof of actual operation. Proof of infringement requires showing how the accused product actually functions in practice, not merely how designers intended it to function.

Design intent versus usage

Design documentation often states goals or nominal operation. Intentional or unintentional deviations can occur in manufacturing, firmware updates, or configurational options. Courts distinguish between what the designers planned and what the shipped product does. Thus, documentation showing an intended feature is helpful but typically insufficient alone. The patentee should tie documentation to as-built evidence.

Connecting documentation to implementation

There are several classes of evidence that bridge from design documents to actual operation:

- Source code and firmware: Comments, variable names, and function modules that implement the design are direct evidence. Demonstrate how code implements the claimed function, and where possible, produce build artifacts showing the code version in the deployed firmware.

- Manufacturing artifacts: Bill of materials, assembly instructions, configuration files, and release notes show that specific components and firmware were assembled and shipped.

- Test reports and quality-control logs: Factory test logs, in-circuit test outputs, and final test reports show that the shipped unit exhibited the behavior described in the documentation.

- Field logs and telemetry: Devices that phone home or store logs can produce real-world evidence of signals, events, or state changes corresponding to claim elements.

- Physical inspection and measurements: Lab testing (component-level and system-level measurements) validates performance claims and timing characteristics. Use calibrated instruments and document the test setup to preclude methodological attacks.

- Demonstrations and reproductions: Controlled reproductions of the accused process or demonstrations of a unit operating under representative conditions can connect the documentation to operation. Preserve and authenticate the demo recordings and test conditions.

Addressing configurability and optional features

Many products have optional features, modes, or configurations that can enable or disable functionality. To prove infringement, show that (1) the feature is enabled in the accused product as shipped, (2) it is enabled by default, (3) it can be easily enabled by the user, or (4) enabling it is inevitable or foreseeable in normal use. Documentation that only shows a feature is possible but turned off by default is weaker; confirm whether firmware updates or user action are required and whether those actions were taken.

Temporal and conditional behavior

Claims may require behavior under particular conditions (e.g., “upon detection of event E, the system performs X”). Documents may state conditional flows; but to prove infringement you need operational proof that those conditions obtain and cause the claimed response. Combine logs capturing the triggering event and the subsequent response with diagrams showing the control flow.

Doctrinal limits: cannot prove infringement by intent alone

A design goal or marketing promise does not, by itself, prove that the product performed as claimed. Consequently, while documents showing intent can inform the reasonable inferences a factfinder may draw, they must be buttressed by operational evidence to carry the preponderance burden. Similarly, for doctrines that depend on equivalence rather than literal structure, corporate intent may be relevant but not dispositive; the expert analysis must show technical equivalence in structure or function.

# Procedural posture and strategic considerations

How documents are used varies with procedural posture—claim construction, summary judgment, or trial.

- Claim construction stage: Documents may be offered as intrinsic or extrinsic evidence to inform claim meaning, but they must be carefully framed so that they do not purport to prove infringement. Experts may explain technical usage of terms in the art but should avoid opining on infringement before construction.

- Summary judgment: At summary judgment, documentary evidence must create genuine disputes of material fact or, conversely, show lack of disputed facts. Documents that demonstrate literal correspondence between claim elements and product components can support or defeat summary judgment, but the foundation for the documents and an expert interpretation are often necessary to show that reasonable jurors could not disagree.

- Trial: At trial, the proponent must produce authenticated documents, supporting witness testimony, demonstratives, and corroborating operational proof to persuade the factfinder. Be prepared for Daubert-style challenges to expert methodology and for cross-examination on document provenance and meaning.

Privileged or protected materials

Engineers’ notes, internal deliberations, and pre-decisional documents may be subject to privilege or protection. When assembling technical documentation, ensure proper waiver analysis and consider whether redaction, privilege logs, or court-ordered in camera review are necessary. Also plan for protective orders governing highly technical trade secrets.

Practical checklist for litigators and experts

- Preserve native files with metadata; obtain version histories and release notes.
- Identify authors and custodians early; seek deposition testimony explaining document meaning.
- Corroborate ambiguous labels with code, tests, BOMs, and logs.
- Recreate mathematical calculations independently; document simulations and instrument calibration.
- Produce demonstrative evidence that traces document elements to physical pins, firmware routines, or test points.
- Address alternative explanations: be ready to show why alternate readings of a diagram are unlikely or inconsistent with other evidence.
- Anticipate and preempt hearsay or authentication objections by having authors testify or by qualifying documents as business records where appropriate.
- Keep in mind claim construction constraints; avoid mapping that conflicts with a court’s construction.

Conclusion

Technical documentation is indispensable in patent infringement litigation, but it is not self-proving. Effective use of diagrams, nomenclature, equations, and signal labels requires careful authentication, rigorous interpretation, corroboration with implementation evidence, and an awareness of procedural and evidentiary pitfalls. By methodically linking documents to actual operation and by preparing experts to explain the technical record in legally cognizable terms, litigators can convert engineering artifacts into the convincing, admissible proof needed to establish infringement.

Introduction

Appellate courts do not merely express legal opinions; they affect the substantive and procedural posture of cases by granting specific forms of relief. Understanding the forms of appellate relief—reversal, vacatur, and remand—and the practical consequences each has for liability and damages is essential for effective appellate advocacy and for anticipating the case’s future course. Appellate remedies determine whether a trial court’s ruling remains enforceable, whether factual questions must be reexamined, and whether a party obtains a judgment as a matter of law or is entitled to a new trial. They also frame who ultimately bears the costs of appeal. This chapter explains the governing concepts, standards, and typical doctrinal applications that shape appellate remedies.

# Reversal vs. vacatur vs. remand

The three principal devices appellate courts use to change the trial-court result are reversal, vacatur, and remand. Though related and often used in combination, each has a distinct legal effect.

Reversal is an appellate court’s substantive correction of an error in the trial court’s judgment. When an appellate court reverses, it concludes that the judgment below was legally incorrect. A reversal may be total—setting aside the entire judgment—or partial—setting aside only portions tied to specific issues. Reversal generally changes the parties’ rights as specified by the appellate court: it may order that judgment be entered for an appellant, or it may require further proceedings consistent with the appellate court’s legal conclusions.

Vacatur is an act that nullifies a lower-court order or judgment, rendering it void for future preclusive effect. Vacatur is often sought where the lower-court decision should not stand as precedent or have collateral-estoppel or claim-preclusive consequences—commonly because the case became moot on appeal or the judgment was procured by settlement or procedural irregularity. Vacatur does not itself resolve the merits; it simply erases the judgment’s legal effect as if it had not been rendered. Vacatur is an equitable remedy, so courts apply principles of equity and sound discretion in deciding whether to vacate a judgment.

Remand is the act of sending a case back to the trial court for further proceedings consistent with the appellate court’s disposition. A remand may be ministerial—directly giving effect to appellate findings and instructing the trial court to enter judgment—or it may require additional proceedings, such as a retrial limited to certain issues (e.g., damages), additional fact-finding, or consideration of issues the trial court did not reach in light of new legal guidance. A remand is constrained by the scope of the appellate mandate; the trial court must act consistent with the appellate court’s legal rulings and any instructions about scope and procedure.

The remedies interact. An appellate court may reverse a trial court’s legal ruling, vacate its judgment, and remand for further proceedings. Alternatively, an appellate court may reverse and order entry of judgment as a matter of law for the prevailing party, obviating remand. Whether reversal alone is adequate or whether vacatur and remand are also necessary depends on the nature of the error, the posture of the case, and equitable considerations.

# Effect of reversing liability on damages awards

Liability and damages are analytically distinct but practically entwined. When an appellate court reverses a trial court’s finding of liability, the natural consequence for a damages award is that it cannot stand: damages rest on a subsisting legal basis—liability—and if liability is extinguished, the damages award typically lacks legal support and must be vacated. Conversely, if an appellate court affirms liability but finds error in the damages computation or admission of evidence affecting damages, the court may affirm liability and remit or vacate the damages award either in whole or in part.

There are several common configurations and procedural implications:

- Complete reversal of liability: If the appellate court reverses the judgment that imposed liability, the damages award is ordinarily vacated because there is no underlying obligation to justify damages. The appellate court may then order entry of judgment for the defendant or direct dismissal of claims. The plaintiff’s damages claim cannot survive absent liability in a way that would produce an inconsistent result.

- Reversal of some liability theories, affirmation of others: Where liability was predicated on multiple theories or claims and the appellate court sustains at least one legal basis for liability, the damages award may survive in whole or in part. The key question becomes whether the surviving liability theory supports the damages awarded and whether the record permits allocation among different theories. If the damages award is indivisible among theories that are partially reversed, the appellate court must determine whether to remand for apportionment or to vacate and order a new determination consistent with the surviving liability. Courts often remand when the record does not permit an accurate apportionment.

- Remand for retrial limited to damages: If liability is upheld but errors taint the damages proceedings (for example, improper admission of expert evidence affecting damages, incorrect jury instructions on damages, or erroneous legal standards governing damages), the appellate court may remand for a new trial limited to damages. The remand will generally specify what is to be reconsidered and whether the trial court should receive supplemental instruction.

- Appellate reduction or remittitur: In some systems, an appellate court may reduce an excessive damages award without ordering a new trial by finding the award unsupported by the evidence. Alternatively, the appellate court may offer remittitur—requesting that the plaintiff accept a reduced award or face a new trial on damages. The availability of remittitur and the standards for reduction versus new trial vary by jurisdiction and are constrained by constitutional limits on judicial interference with jury factfinding (e.g., protections against denying a jury’s role absent clear error).

- Compensatory vs. punitive damages: Reversal of liability typically eliminates reckless or punitive-damage awards tied to that liability. But cases may raise separate appellate considerations for punitive damages—constitutional limits, the need for specific findings, or bifurcation rules—so appellate orders may separately address their validity and proper treatment on remand.

Throughout, appellate courts apply standards of review that govern the disposition. Questions of law (e.g., whether a claim is cognizable) are reviewed de novo; factual findings are reviewed for clear error or substantial evidence depending on the context; discretionary rulings (e.g., admission of evidence) for abuse of discretion. Where an appellate court concludes that errors affected the damages outcome, it must decide whether the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt or whether reversal and remand are necessary to ensure a fair determination.

# Scope of remand instructions

A remand is not a blank slate. The appellate mandate defines the parameters of the trial court’s subsequent proceedings, and the trial court must adhere to the appellate court’s legal conclusions and any mandated procedural steps. The scope of remand instructions can be characterized along several dimensions: the substantive issues left for reconsideration, evidentiary and procedural limits, deference to trial-court factfinding, and preservation of appellate rulings.

An appellate court typically specifies whether the remand is for entry of a specific judgment, further factfinding, additional proceedings on limited issues, or a new trial. The language of the mandate and accompanying opinion shapes the trial court’s authority.

- Mandate-driven review: The trial court is bound by the appellate court’s legal rulings. The law of the case doctrine ordinarily prevents relitigation of issues decided on appeal unless exceptional circumstances exist. Thus, on remand, the trial court applies the appellate court’s holding as the governing law and resolves remaining factual questions consistent with that law.

- Limited remand versus plenary remand: A limited remand confines the trial court to a narrow set of issues (e.g., recalculating damages, applying a particular legal standard, or making additional factual findings about causation). A plenary remand permits broader proceedings, possibly including reconsideration of matters not previously addressed. Courts will describe the permitted scope; absent explicit limitation, courts assume the remand allows all proceedings consistent with the appellate disposition, but trial courts should seek clarification when the scope is unclear.

- Remand with specific instructions: Appellate courts sometimes mandate precise procedural actions—for example, to enter judgment for a particular party, to permit a new evidentiary hearing on a specified issue, or to apply a particular legal test. Such instructions bind the trial court and limit its discretion. If the appellate court has directed specific findings, the trial court may implement them without relitigating settled points.

- Factfinding and new evidence: When remand asks for further factfinding, courts must consider whether new evidence can be received. The appellate court may permit new evidence if it is necessary to decide matters left open, but it may also instruct the trial court to proceed with the existing record if further factfinding is not appropriate. If new trials are ordered, rules governing evidence, discovery, and admissibility apply.

- Preservation of issues and procedural posture: The appellate court’s disposition may preserve or foreclose issues on remand. For example, if the appellate court decides a legal question in a party’s favor, the opposing party cannot relitigate that question on remand. However, parties may raise preserved factual or procedural issues that the appellate court did not finally decide. Trial courts must carefully apply the appellate mandate and avoid expansion beyond the appellate court’s holding.

- Time and enforcement: The mandate often contains procedural directives about deadlines for renewed motions, the entry of judgment, and the timing of further proceedings. The trial court must follow these timelines, subject to reasonable motions for extension or clarification addressed to the appellate court when necessary.

In sum, the remand operates under the appellate court’s control. Clear remand instructions minimize uncertainty and sharpen the focus of further proceedings; lack of clarity can generate additional appeals or motions for clarification of the mandate.

# Entry of judgment as a matter of law vs. ordering a new trial

When errors are found in a trial, an appellate court can either direct that judgment be entered in favor of a party as a matter of law or order a new trial. Choosing between these two remedies depends on the nature of the error, the sufficiency of the evidence, and equitable considerations. The procedural posture—whether the appellant preserved appropriate motions at trial—often governs the available relief.

Entry of judgment as a matter of law is appropriate when, taking the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, no reasonable jury could have found for that party on the contested issue(s). In federal practice, this doctrine often appears as the renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law under procedures that require a timely motion at trial (commonly termed a JMOL or motion for directed verdict) and a renewed motion after the verdict to preserve the right to appeal. On appeal, a court applying this standard conducts de novo review of whether the trial record suffices to support the verdict. If the court concludes that reasonable minds could not reach the verdict, it may reverse and render judgment for the movant, thereby short-circuiting the need for retrial and resolving the dispute as a matter of law.

Ordering a new trial is appropriate when the verdict is against the clear weight of the evidence, but the record nevertheless permits reasonable minds to differ, or when trial errors are of such a nature that retrial is necessary to secure a fair proceeding. The new-trial standard differs from JMOL in important respects. A motion for new trial challenges the correctness of the result in light of the whole trial, often invoking errors of procedure, improper admission of evidence, incorrect jury instructions, or verdicts that shock the conscience. The standard of review for an appellate court determining whether to order a new trial frequently gives the trial court broad discretion because the trial judge is in the best position to judge witness credibility and the fairness of proceedings. Appellate courts apply an abuse-of-discretion standard to the trial court’s ruling on new-trial motions. On direct appellate review, some courts will independently evaluate whether a new trial is warranted, but they typically do so with deference to the trial judge’s superior position.

Several procedural rules and practical considerations affect the choice between entry of judgment and a new trial:

- Preservation requirements: Many jurisdictions require parties to move for directed verdict or judgment as a matter of law at trial and to renew such motions after verdict to preserve the right to appellate judgment. Failure to make or renew these motions can limit the appellate remedy to a new trial rather than entry of judgment.

- Sufficiency versus weight of evidence: Entry of judgment resolves sufficiency questions; a new trial addresses the weight of the evidence and procedural fairness. If the appellate court finds the evidence insufficient as a matter of law, rendering judgment is appropriate. If the evidence is sufficient but tainted by error or unfairness, retrial is the usual remedy.

- Harmless error and prejudice: Even if error exists, appellate courts will not order a new trial if the error was harmless—i.e., it did not affect substantial rights or the outcome. The party seeking reversal bears the burden of showing prejudice unless the error is structural, which may warrant automatic reversal.

- Practical and equitable implications: Appellate courts consider whether rendition (entry of judgment) or retrial will serve justice. For example, if key witnesses are no longer available or evidence has materially changed, a retrial may be unfair; yet if the record conclusively favors one party, rendition promotes finality and efficiency. Courts exercise equitable judgment in choosing the remedy.

- Partial retrial: Courts may order a new trial as to specific issues only, preserving other parts of the judgment. For instance, they may order retrial on damages while allowing liability findings to stand. Whether bifurcated retrial is appropriate depends on whether the errors are separable and whether limiting retrial would preserve the parties’ rights.

In practice, appellate courts strive to avoid making factual determinations that should be the province of a jury unless the record makes those determinations inevitable. They also respect trial-court discretion in managing trials and correcting procedural errors. The interplay between standards of review and preservation rules strongly shapes whether the appellate remedy will be rendition or remand for a new trial.

# Allocation of costs on appeal

Appellate litigation generates costs—court fees, transcripts, and attorney’s fees in certain cases—that must be allocated among the parties. The baseline principle is that appellate costs are taxable to the losing party unless a statute, rule, or equitable considerations allocate them otherwise. Specific rules govern what costs are recoverable, how they are taxed, and when appellate courts may award fees.

Taxable costs typically include items listed by rule—filing and docketing fees, costs of reproducing the record or transcript, and certain copying expenses—subject to statutory ceilings and local rules. Appellate courts disallow costs that are not authorized by statute or rule. The procedure for recovering costs usually requires the prevailing party to file a bill of costs within a short time after the mandate issues, and the opposing party may contest specific items.

Attorney’s fees are distinct from taxable costs. Under the American Rule, each party ordinarily bears its own attorney’s fees unless a statute, contract, or equitable doctrine shifts fees. Many federal and state statutes (civil-rights laws, consumer-protection laws, fee-shifting provisions, and contractual fee provisions) authorize prevailing-party attorney’s fees on appeal; courts applying such statutes determine prevailing-party status, calculate reasonable fees using accepted methods (e.g., lodestar multiplied by adjustment factors), and award fees consistent with the statute’s scope (which may be discretionary or mandatory).

Other considerations affecting allocation include:

- Offers of judgment and sanctions: Offer-of-judgment rules can alter cost and fee allocation by shifting costs or fees against a party who rejects a reasonable settlement and fares worse at judgment. Appellate outcomes influence whether the offer was successful in practical effect and thus whether the offer’s cost-shifting provisions apply. Sanctions for frivolous appeals or misconduct also may require payment of costs, fees, or fines.

- Partial success and apportionment: When the appeal yields mixed results, courts must apportion costs and fees equitably. Prevailing party calculations become more complex if a party wins on some claims but loses others; the court must determine whether the party achieved prevailing-party status overall or only with respect to specific claims and allocate costs proportionately.

- Security for costs and supersedeas bonds: A party seeking to stay enforcement of a judgment pending appeal may be required to post a bond, which covers potential costs and damages. The bond’s amount and the requirements for posting relate to the nature of the judgment and jurisdictional rules. If the appellant fails to post adequate security and loses on appeal, the appellee may recover enforcement costs tied to the delay.

- Taxation timeline and enforcement: Costs on appeal are normally taxed and collected after the mandate issues. Parties should observe rules about filing a bill of costs, objections, and motions for review in the trial court. The trial court normally determines taxable costs; appellate courts seldom award costs themselves except when specifically authorized.

- Equity and discretion: Appellate courts retain discretion to depart from strict cost awards in exceptional circumstances. For instance, if an appeal raises important unsettled law, even an unsuccessful appellant might not be taxed heavy costs as a matter of equitable fairness. Conversely, frivolous appeals or those filed for dilatory purposes may result in punitive cost awards.

Understanding the allocation of costs is strategic: it influences settlement decisions, the calculus of whether to appeal, decisions about requesting a stay, and how to frame fee-shifting claims on appeal.

Conclusion

Appellate remedies determine not only whether trial-court error is corrected but also the practical consequences for the parties’ liabilities, damages, and exposure to future litigation. Reversal, vacatur, and remand are distinct tools: reversal corrects legal error and may change outcomes; vacatur removes a judgment’s legal effect; and remand sends matters back with specific instructions. The effect of reversing liability on damages is usually to vacate the damages award unless liability survives on some basis; remand instructions must be followed closely and limit the trial court’s discretion in important ways. The choice between entering judgment as a matter of law and ordering a new trial hinges on evidentiary sufficiency, preservation of motions, and the balance of fairness and finality. Finally, costs on appeal are allocated according to rules and equitable considerations, with taxable costs and attorney’s fees governed by different regimes. Mastery of these doctrines arms the appellate advocate to request the appropriate remedy and to anticipate the implications of appellate relief for the case’s future.

Introduction

When a patent case is tried to a jury, the appellate path often turns on layered standards of review: the standard a court uses to evaluate a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), the standard for testing whether the jury’s verdict is supported by substantial evidence, and the standard that governs review of claim construction findings that underlie infringement and invalidity rulings. These standards interact: claim construction can be decisive to whether any reasonable jury could have found infringement; the district court’s factual findings that inform claim construction may be reviewed for clear error while legal conclusions are reviewed de novo; and the regional circuit’s procedural law can dictate how JMOL is reviewed on appeal. Understanding these doctrines—how they have evolved, how courts apply them to patent disputes, and how litigators should structure arguments at trial and on appeal—gives a student the tools to read and analyze appellate opinions that resolve close jury verdicts in patent cases.

# De novo review of JMOL under regional circuit precedent

Judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure is the principal vehicle for challenging the legal sufficiency of a jury’s verdict at the close of the opposing party’s case or after verdict. For appeals from district courts within the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction over patent appeals, the Federal Circuit has long recognized that it should apply the standard of review that the regional circuit (the court of appeals for the circuit in which the district court sits) applies when reviewing a district court’s ruling on JMOL. In practice this means that whether the Federal Circuit reviews the denial of JMOL de novo or with some degree of deference may depend on the regional circuit law.

What does this entail in functional terms? A motion for JMOL challenges whether the evidence presented at trial, viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, permits reasonable jurors to reach the verdict they reached. The typical articulation across circuits is that a JMOL should be granted when there is no legally sufficient evidentiary basis for a reasonable jury to find for that party. The critical components of the review are twofold: (1) the appellate court determines the standard the district court applied when deciding JMOL; and (2) the appellate court then applies the same standard to the trial record. Where regional precedent requires that a reviewing court not weigh credibility or resolve conflicts in the evidence, the appellate inquiry focuses on whether substantial evidence supports the verdict (see next section). Where regional law describes a more searching review, the court will conduct that review.

A few practical consequences follow. First, litigants must know and preserve the applicable regional-law standard in district court briefing and at trial. If the region’s controlling precedent directs a particular JMOL test—for example, that a court may not consider witness credibility—parties should frame their motions and trial record in terms that will be recognized on appeal. Second, the appellate court’s application of regional law means that outcomes can vary across circuits: identical trial records might survive JMOL in one regional circuit and not in another depending on the appellate standard the Federal Circuit adopts for that district. Finally, because the standard of review is applied by reference to the record the district court had, preservation of the evidentiary record (including contemporaneous objections and motions) is essential to preserve issues for appellate JMOL review.

# Substantial evidence for jury verdicts in patent cases

“Substantial evidence” is the workhorse evidentiary standard used to test whether a jury’s factual findings can stand on appeal. Substantial evidence means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion. It is not a stringent or weighty standard: it does not require that the evidence be overwhelming or that contrary inferences be impossible. But substantial evidence does impose meaningful limits: the appellate court must be able to point to record evidence that, if credited by the jury, would permit the finding.

How courts apply the substantial-evidence test in patent trials merits close attention because patent disputes often rest on expert testimony, technical demonstrations, and claim interpretation. Appellate courts reviewing for substantial evidence consider the last opportunity a jury had to hear the evidence—taking the record as a whole, viewing evidence in the light most favorable to the prevailing party, and not reweighing credibility. But there are recognized boundaries.

First, jury verdicts cannot be sustained by testimony that is internally inconsistent or self-contradictory in ways that destroy its probative force. Where an expert’s testimony on a key factual element is contradicted by the expert elsewhere in the same trial, or the testimony is so speculative or internally contradictory that no reasonable jury could credit it, appellate courts may conclude that no substantial evidence supports the verdict. Similarly, if the only testimony supporting an element is conclusory and lacks a reliable basis in record facts or demonstrable methodology, appellate review may find the verdict unsupported.

Second, the distinction between credibility determinations and sufficiency review matters. Appellate courts do not reassess witness credibility, but they do evaluate whether the evidence—credibilty assumed—actually provides a sufficient evidentiary basis for the finding. Thus if the only evidence supporting the verdict depends on leaps of inference not supported by the expert’s own data or by the intrinsic record, the court may find insufficient evidence even if the jury believed the witness.

Third, the “substantial evidence” standard is applied differently depending on whether the jury’s finding is primarily factual (e.g., what a device’s structure actually does, whether a given component is present in accused products) or legal (e.g., claim indefiniteness or obviousness often involves legal conclusion based on fact). Appellate courts review legal conclusions de novo while factual findings are tested for substantial evidence. For mixed questions, appellate courts parse the record to identify the factual predicates and review them for substantial evidence before applying legal principles.

Finally, Rule 59’s new-trial standards remain available at the district-court level, and denial of a new trial is generally reviewed for abuse of discretion—this is a separate, often more deferential, vehicle for complaining that the jury’s verdict was against the weight of the evidence. Parties who seek appellate reversal on sufficiency grounds should ensure they pursued both appropriate JMOL and Rule 59 relief as needed to preserve arguments and to put the district court on notice of the grounds for appellate review.

# Claim construction review after Teva

Claim construction historically was treated as a purely legal question reviewed de novo on appeal. The Supreme Court’s decision in Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc. v. Sandoz, Inc. altered that framework by emphasizing that when a district court’s claim construction includes subsidiary factual findings—often arising from extrinsic evidence such as expert testimony, dictionaries, or prosecution history—the appellate court owes those factual findings clear-error review. Purely legal conclusions about the meaning of claim language, informed by intrinsic evidence (the claim language, specification, and prosecution history), remain subject to de novo review.

The Teva framework produces a mixed standard:

- Where claim construction can be resolved solely by intrinsic evidence—the claims themselves, the specification, and the prosecution history—the appellate court may independently construe the claim language (de novo review). Courts often characterize this as reading the patent from the perspective of a person having ordinary skill in the art at the time of the invention.

- Where the district court makes subsidiary factual findings—such as credibility determinations about expert witnesses’ testimony regarding how a skilled artisan would understand a term; findings about the ordinary meaning of a term based on extrinsic sources; or determinations about technical facts necessary to resolve claim scope—the appellate court must uphold those findings unless clearly erroneous. The reviewing court then applies the law to those factual findings de novo.

Understanding the line between intrinsic- and extrinsic-based construction is crucial. Intrinsic evidence generally has primacy: the specification and claims are the best guide to claim meaning. Extrinsic evidence may be consulted to resolve ambiguities or to aid the court’s understanding of the technical context, but cannot be used to override the intrinsic record. Examples of subsidiary factual determinations that survive only under clear-error review include district-court findings about the meaning of specialized technical terms as used in the relevant art, or evaluations of whether a particular dictionary or expert description reflects the understanding of a skilled artisan. Conversely, the ultimate interpretive question—what would a skilled artisan understand the claim to mean—is legal and reviewed de novo, but depends on those factual predicates.

Practically, Teva means appellate courts often affirm parts of a district court’s constructions while reversing others: they accept the district court’s factual determinations unless clearly unsupported and then independently determine the legal effect of those facts on claim scope. Litigators should anticipate that factual disputes introduced at claim construction briefing or at Markman hearings may be reviewed deferentially on appeal, making the district-court fact-finding an important battleground.

# Practical impact on structuring appellate arguments

These intertwined standards should shape how counsel organizes both trial and appellate advocacy. Several practical lessons follow.

First, preserve the record aggressively. To maximize chances on appeal you must develop the evidentiary record in ways that map to the standards of review you will face. For JMOL challenges based on regional-circuit tests, craft the JMOL motion to mirror the standard the appellate court will apply and ensure the trial record contains the necessary admissions, cross-examination, and pointed evidentiary gaps. For claim-construction disputes, when extrinsic evidence will be relied upon, ensure the district court makes express factual findings (and that those findings are supported by admissible evidence) so that deferential review later may be invoked or overcome.

Second, tailor your JMOL and Rule 50(a)/(b) timing and content. File timely Rule 50(a) motions at the close of the opponent’s case (and renew as Rule 50(b) after verdict) to preserve the right to appellate review. Briefs should identify the exact elements the moving party contends the opponent failed to prove and cite the portions of the record that show the evidentiary defect. Avoid generic assertions that “no reasonable jury could have found” without pinpointing specific evidentiary lacunae or contradictions.

Third, structure expert testimony and impeachment to create or dismantle the “substantial evidence” foundation. Because patent jury verdicts frequently hinge on expert witnesses, effective cross-examination that exposes internal inconsistencies or speculative bases is crucial. If you are defending a jury verdict, preserve testimony and demonstratives that a reviewing court can point to as supporting evidence. If you are attacking a verdict, highlight contradictions, lack of foundation, or testimony that attempts to bridge gaps without factual support. Where an expert’s opinion depends on untested assumptions or omitted analyses, emphasize that the opinion is conclusory.

Fourth, anticipate the mixed claim-construction review under Teva. If you rely on extrinsic evidence (dictionaries, standards, tutorials, demonstrative expert testimony) to support a particular construction, present and secure district-court factual findings that tie that extrinsic evidence to how a person of ordinary skill would understand the term. If you seek de novo review, emphasize that the intrinsic record supplies a complete answer, arguing that no subsidiary factual findings are necessary. Conversely, if you litigate to obtain deference to district-court fact-finding, introduce and elicit evidence that permits the district court to make detailed factual findings: call live witnesses, point the court to specific portions of extrinsic sources, and request explicit findings in a claim-construction order.

Fifth, frame appeal briefs to reflect the correct standard of review and to separate factual predicate errors from legal errors. On appeal, structure arguments by first identifying the standard for each contested ruling (de novo, clear error, substantial evidence), then presenting the record bits tied to that standard. For JMOL appeals, show that under the regional circuit’s standard the trial record fails to contain substantial evidence supporting the verdict, or, if regional law uses a more searching review, demonstrate legal error in the district court’s application. For claim construction, identify the district court’s factual findings and show why they are clearly erroneous (if attacking them) or why legal application of those facts is erroneous (if attacking the court’s legal conclusion).

Sixth, consider the tactical interplay between JMOL, new-trial motions, and post-trial claim construction activity. District courts sometimes engage in post-verdict claim reconstruction or resolve claim-construction issues after the jury verdict; appellate courts will scrutinize whether such post-hoc alterations altered the legal landscape in ways that deprived the party of its jury-based protections. Consequently, litigators should object to post-verdict reconstructions that change substantive claim meaning or should move for a new trial where the district court’s post-verdict actions materially prejudiced the party’s rights. Preservation of objections and post-trial motions on these grounds is crucial for appellate review.

Seventh, think about remedies and preservation of alternative relief. When requesting JMOL, also seek (or preserve the right to seek) a new trial under Rule 59, or remittitur/additur where appropriate. Denials of new-trial motions are reviewed for abuse of discretion and thus present a different appellate pathway; sometimes cross-appealing or raising alternative grounds increases the chance that some relief will be obtained.

Conclusion

Jury trials in patent cases sit at the intersection of law and fact. Appellate review applies a mosaic of standards—regional-circuit rules on JMOL, the substantial-evidence test for jury findings, and Teva’s mixed-deference approach to claim construction. Effective litigation requires anticipating how each standard will be applied, building the trial record to supply favorable factual findings (or to expose insufficiencies), framing post-trial motions consistent with the applicable regional standard, and structuring appellate briefs to separate and address legal and factual predicates under their respective standards. Mastery of these doctrines is essential background before reading appellate opinions that resolve disputed patent jury verdicts.

Introduction

In patent litigation, jury findings on infringement and damages are often pivotal to the outcome. When those findings are challenged after trial, courts apply the “substantial evidence” standard to determine whether a jury verdict must stand or be set aside. This chapter explains how courts define and apply substantial evidence in the patent context, how judges evaluate expert mappings and documentary corroboration that underpin jury verdicts, the limits courts place on conclusory testimony, and practical steps litigators must take to preserve sufficiency challenges for appeal. The goal is doctrinal: to equip you with the legal standards and analytical framework that courts use when asked to sustain or overturn jury findings in patent cases.

# Defining substantial evidence in the patent context

“Substantial evidence” is the term courts use to describe the minimal quantum of record evidence necessary to support a jury’s factual finding. Although the phrase is familiar from administrative-law and civil contexts, its application in patent cases follows the same core principle: a finding is supported by substantial evidence if there is relevant evidence that a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support the conclusion reached. Substantial evidence does not require that the evidence be uncontradicted, nor does it require that the evidence be overwhelming; it requires only that the evidence be legally adequate when viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict winner and without reassessing witness credibility.

How courts apply this principle depends on procedural posture and on which court reviews the ruling:

- JMOL (Rule 50) at the district-court level: When a party moves for judgment as a matter of law during trial, or renews such a motion after verdict under Rule 50(b), the district court must determine whether a reasonable jury could have reached the verdict based on the evidence presented. The court does so without weighing credibility or weighing the evidence, and it draws all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party. If the court concludes that the evidentiary basis is insufficient as a matter of law, it may grant JMOL; otherwise it must deny the motion.

- Motion for new trial (Rule 59): A motion for a new trial is a separate procedural vehicle that permits a district court greater discretion (for example, to order a new trial if the verdict is against the clear weight of the evidence). The standard for a new trial is generally more forgiving of the jury’s verdict than JMOL is, and appellate courts review denials of new trials for abuse of discretion.

- Appellate review: On appeal, courts review a district court’s denial of JMOL by applying the governing standard used by the district court (i.e., the applicable regional circuit’s standard) but typically conduct their review without deference on legal questions. In patent appeals the Federal Circuit will review claim construction de novo (subject to the usual exceptions) and will review the jury’s fact findings for substantial evidence. When the appellate court reviews whether substantial evidence supports a jury’s finding, it asks whether reasonable minds could reach the conclusion based on the evidence of record. The appellate court does not reweigh credibility or substitute its own factual determinations for those of the jury.

For patent-specific findings, courts emphasize the need for evidence connecting the asserted claims to the accused product or conduct. A legally sufficient infringement verdict must rest on evidence that shows, element-by-element, that the accused product performs the claimed functions (in the case of apparatus claims) or that the accused acts practice the claimed steps (in method claims). For damages, a verdict must rest on admissible evidence establishing a reasonable royalty or other measure, and that evidence too is subject to the substantial-evidence standard.

# Evaluating expert mappings and documentary support

Expert witnesses play a central role in patent trials. Because patent claims involve technical limitations, parties rely on experts to map claim limitations onto accused products or to explain how the claimed invention differs from the prior art. Courts scrutinize those mappings closely when evaluating whether the record supports infringement or damages findings.

What a legally sufficient expert mapping looks like

A mapping is legally sufficient when it does more than assert a conclusion; it identifies specific features, documents, code, standards, schematics, test data, or testimony and explains reliably how those identified items correspond to each asserted claim element. This requires an expert to perform a reasoned analysis that links the physical or software components (or the steps performed) to the language and scope of the claim. Effective mappings typically have the following characteristics:

- Element-by-element analysis. The expert addresses each claim limitation separately and explains where in the accused product or conduct the limitation is met.

- Specificity. The expert points to tangible sources of evidence: lines of source code, specific functions in firmware or middleware, particular provisions of a standards specification, labeled components in schematics, asserted packets or frames in protocol traces, or demonstrable device behavior in testing.

- Logical reasoning. The expert states a clear logic chain: the accused feature has characteristic X; the claim requires characteristic X; therefore the accused feature satisfies the claim limitation. Where intermediate inferences are necessary (e.g., interpreting an abstract standard term as implemented by a particular module), the expert explains those inferences and why they are reasonable.

- Corroboration from documents, demonstrations, or witnesses. A mapping based solely on an expert’s ipse dixit is weaker than one that links to contemporaneous documents (design notes, requirements, standards) or demonstrative testing that illustrates the asserted behavior.

Admissibility and foundational rigor

Admissibility under the Federal Rules (including Daubert and Rule 702) is a prerequisite to an expert’s evidence being considered. A reliable methodology and proper foundation increase an expert’s mapping’s probative force. If an expert’s methods are admissible, courts then assess whether those methods produced sufficient evidence to support jury conclusions.

Documentary support

Documentary evidence is often decisive in confirming or undermining expert mappings. Relevant documentary material includes source code listings, engineering specifications, design documents, product manuals, standards documents, protocol specifications, system logs, Test and Acceptance Reports, and internal emails reflecting design intent or implementation details. When expert testimony is buttressed by contemporaneous documentary evidence that concretely identifies the accused feature and how it functions, courts are more likely to find that substantial evidence supports a verdict.

When documentary evidence is absent or ambiguous, the mapping must make up for that lack by providing a persuasive technical explanation and, ideally, independent corroboration by testing or demonstratives. Absent these, courts have repeatedly held that the record lacks substantial evidence.

Standards and multifunctionality

Where standards compliance is at issue (for example, when the accused device implements an industry standard and the patentee asserts the standard practice maps to the claim), an effective expert mapping identifies precisely which portions of the standard correspond to each limitation and provides testimony or documents showing that the accused implementation actually follows those portions of the standard. Vague references to the standard as a whole do not suffice.

Evidence of non-infringing design alternatives and competing explanations

The jury does not have to accept an expert’s mapping in the face of competing explanations. But on a sufficiency challenge, a court will not overturn a jury verdict merely because the record contains contrary evidence; the question is whether sufficient evidence supports the verdict. If the record contains reasonable, specific conflicting evidence pointing to noninfringing design choices, the presence of that evidence can make the jury’s verdict more difficult to sustain on appeal only if the patentee’s mapping is otherwise insubstantial. A strong expert mapping that accounts for alternative explanations and explains why they do not negate infringement carries more weight.

# Limits of conclusory testimony

Courts draw a clear line between reasoned explanatory testimony and conclusory ipse dixit. Several recurring principles govern judicial treatment of conclusory testimony:

- “Ipse dixit” is insufficient. An expert’s naked assertion that “the accused product meets limitation X” without factual elaboration or linkage to record evidence is inadequate. Courts demand that the expert explain the basis for the assertion.

- No mere recitation of claim language. Testimony that simply repeats claim language and labels accused features as corresponding to that language, without further explanation, does not satisfy the element-by-element requirement.

- Self-contradiction and inconsistency undermine sufficiency. When an expert gives materially inconsistent statements in the record—e.g., testifying in one venue that a product performs a function and in another that it does not—courts will not treat those inconsistent assertions as collectively establishing substantial evidence. Self-contradictory testimony can be discounted because it lacks the necessary probative force.

- Lay witnesses and corporate designees cannot fill gaps with conclusory assertions. Corporate witnesses may provide facts about design or operation; but when those witnesses provide only conclusory labels rather than factual mapping tied to the claim terms, courts will treat that evidence as weak.

- Legal conclusions are not for experts. Experts may provide technical input, but they must avoid testifying to legal conclusions (for example, “this product infringes claim X”). The expert’s role is to supply the factual predicate—technical features and their correspondence to claim elements—so that the jury can reach a legal conclusion.

- The need for corroboration in close cases. Particularly where the patentee’s own evidence is thin, the court will scrutinize whether the expert’s testimony has independent documentary or demonstrative corroboration. Without it, courts have been unwilling to treat conclusory testimony as substantial evidence.

In practice, appellate courts have set aside infringement verdicts when the only support was conclusory testimony lacking documentary corroboration or when the testimony was internally inconsistent. The lesson is that strong, specific, consistent explanation is required; generalizations and conclusory proclamations are legally fragile.

# Guidance on preserving sufficiency challenges

Procedural rules and case law demand careful preservation of challenges to the sufficiency of the evidence. If a litigant fails to preserve a Rule 50 motion in the district court, appellate review of sufficiency arguments may be foreclosed.

When to move and what to say

- Rule 50(a) motion during trial. A party seeking to preserve a sufficiency challenge must move for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50(a) at the close of the opponent’s case or at the close of all evidence, depending on the grounds being asserted. The motion must state the specific grounds upon which it is made. Generic or cryptic motions risk being deemed insufficient to preserve particular arguments on appeal.

- Renewing the motion under Rule 50(b). After a jury verdict, the moving party must file a renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50(b) to preserve the issue for appeal. A party that fails to renew forfeits the right to challenge sufficiency on appeal in many circuits.

- Alternative grounds. To preserve multiple lines of argument, the Rule 50(a) and Rule 50(b) motions should articulate each specific sufficiency argument; many practitioners include alternative factual or legal grounds in the motion to avoid a forfeiture argument.

Practical drafting tips

- Be specific. Identify which claim elements lack sufficient evidence and why. Point to the absence of corroborating documents or the lack of element-by-element mapping.

- Reference record evidence. Even as you argue insufficiency, cite the record to show why no reasonable jury could have found for the opponent. The court and, later, the appellate court will rely on the record you direct them to.

- Preserve objections to expert testimony. If the sufficiency challenge depends on the admissibility or reliability of an expert’s methods, make appropriate Daubert-style objections and litigate the foundation at trial. A post-verdict sufficiency challenge will have less traction if prior admissibility objections were not made.

- Move (and renew) timely. Make Rule 50(a) motions at the correct phases of trial and renew under Rule 50(b) after the verdict. Failure to comply with Rule 50’s timing requirements often results in waiver of appellate sufficiency review.

Preserving challenges to damages

The same preservation rules apply to sufficiency challenges to damages awards. A party seeking JMOL on damages must move under Rule 50(a) at the close of the party’s damages case (or at the close of all evidence if damages are part of the overall evidence) and must renew under Rule 50(b) after the verdict. The motion should identify the insufficiency: for example, lack of evidence of causation, absence of a reliable damages model, or failure to apportion.

Record development to support future appeals

Because appellate courts review the trial record, counsel should develop the record strategically. Contemporaneous design documents, source code, test reports, deposition testimony from product engineers, standard specifications, and precise expert analyses are the raw materials that appellate courts examine when assessing substantial evidence. If these materials are not in the record, the patentee’s claim is weak on sufficiency review; conversely, a well-developed record makes appellate reversal less likely.

# Applying the doctrine to damages findings

Substantial-evidence principles apply equally to damages. A jury award of a reasonable royalty or lost profits must rest on competent evidence establishing the underlying factual predicates: nexus between the patent and the accused product, the value of the patented feature, apportionment to exclude non-infringing features, and methodology that reliably translates value into an award.

Expert economic testimony supporting damages must explain the valuation methodology, the data used, and the causal link to the asserted patent. Courts scrutinize whether the expert accounted for non-infringing alternatives, properly apportioned value, and relied on accepted economic concepts. Conclusions unsupported by reliable economic analysis or that rest on speculative correlations are vulnerable to sufficiency challenges.

When damages stem from a lump-sum negotiation theory or hypothetical bargaining (the “willing licensor/willing licensee” framework), the expert must identify comparable licenses, explain adjustments, and tie license terms to the facts of the hypothetical negotiation. Mere recitation of numbers without analysis or failure to explain why comparators are comparable will weaken the sufficiency of the evidence for a damages verdict.

# Practical tips for counsel and courts

For counsel for the patentee (plaintiff asserting infringement)

- Build an element-by-element record. Before trial, collect documents and engineer testimony that tie specific product aspects to claim limitations. Identify the best available documentary corroboration and testing.

- Prepare experts to explain their methodology and to point to concrete evidence. Avoid letting experts offer legal conclusions for which the jury is the factfinder.

- Anticipate defensive mappings and develop rebuttal evidence that explains why alternative interpretations do not negate infringement.

For counsel for the accused infringer (defendant)

- Attack the specificity and foundation of the patentee’s mappings at the Daubert stage and through cross-examination. Emphasize gaps, lack of documentary corroboration, or alternative, non-infringing interpretations.

- Prepare strong, coherent noninfringement explanations tied to product documentation and engineer testimony. If the patentee’s evidence is conclusory, ensure the court and jury understand that it lacks a factual foundation.

For district courts

- Gatekeep expert testimony under Rule 702/Daubert and ensure the record is adequately developed. Clarify to the jury when an expert is giving technical facts as opposed to legal conclusions.

- When considering JMOL, apply the standard correctly: view the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, do not weigh credibility, and consider whether reasonable jurors could reach the verdict from the evidence presented.

For appellate courts

- Respect appropriate deference: do not reweigh credibility, but ensure that elemental mappings and documentary support meet the legal threshold of substantial evidence.

# Conclusion

Substantial-evidence review in patent cases is a demand for a reasoned, well-supported evidentiary basis for jury findings of infringement and damages. Expert mappings must do the real work: identify specific accused features, explain how they correspond to claim limitations, and provide documentary or demonstrative corroboration. Courts will not sustain verdicts founded on bare conclusions, inconsistent testimony, or unsupported leaps of logic. At the same time, the standard does not require that the evidence be uncontradicted—only that reasonable minds could accept it as adequate. For litigators, the practical corollary is clear: preserve your challenges with timely, specific Rule 50 motions and build a record that either robustly links products to claims or persuasively exposes the gaps in the opposing party’s proof.

Introduction

Expert testimony often decides complex technical disputes in litigation. Courts and juries rely on expert witnesses to translate specialist facts into legal conclusions—whether a patent claim is practiced, a product meets a regulatory standard, or a causal link exists. But not every expert statement is legally sufficient to sustain a verdict. When an expert’s testimony is internally inconsistent, rests on assumptions contradicted by the record, or fails to map to the legal elements that the plaintiff must prove, courts may conclude that no substantial evidence supports the jury’s finding. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework governing (1) when contradictions in expert testimony render it insufficient as a matter of law, (2) how courts and advocates attempt to reconcile conflicting statements, (3) how this analysis interacts with the “all-elements” rule (the requirement that each element of a claim or cause be proved), and (4) practical strategies for cross-examination and building a record to preserve and win post-trial motions or appeals.

# When contradictions render testimony insufficient as a matter of law

Standards of review and legal sufficiency

When a party moves for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) after a jury verdict, the judge asks whether the evidence, viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, permits a reasonable jury to find for that party. The precise formulation varies by circuit, but federal practice commonly treats JMOL as proper when “substantial evidence” does not support the jury’s verdict and does so without weighing credibility. Substantial evidence generally means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion. On review, appellate courts apply the JMOL standard de novo as to legal questions and ask whether the district court correctly determined sufficiency under the regional circuit’s standard.

Contrast between contradictions that affect weight and contradictions that destroy sufficiency

Not every inconsistency in testimony defeats a verdict. Courts distinguish between contradictions that a jury is entitled to resolve (affecting the weight, credibility, or persuasiveness of testimony) and contradictions that are so fundamental or irreconcilable that the testimony cannot, as a matter of law, constitute substantial evidence for an essential element.

Contradictions that go to credibility are classic jury questions. If two experts disagree or an expert gives inconsistent answers, a jury may credit one version and discredit the other. JMOL is inappropriate where reasonable jurors could accept the testimony as internally coherent or could reconcile apparent tensions.

By contrast, contradictions destroy legal sufficiency when they make the expert’s testimony self-defeating on an essential point, or when the testimony relies on a premise that the record incontrovertibly disproves. Typical examples where courts have found insufficiency include:

- The expert’s testimony is the only evidence of an essential element, and the expert gives directly contradictory answers on that element such that no reasonable jury could find in favor of the proffered conclusion.

- The expert’s ultimate conclusion depends on a factual assumption that is demonstrably false in the record (e.g., the expert assumed a product included a component when the record shows the component was absent), and the expert offers no alternative ground tying the evidence to the element.

- The testimony is internally inconsistent such that the portions that support the verdict are negated by other portions; the positive assertions cannot be isolated from the contradictions without impermissibly reweighing or imputing credibility.

How courts analyze whether a contradiction is fatal

When evaluating whether a contradiction renders testimony insufficient, courts typically:

1. Identify the essential element(s) for which the expert supplies proof.

2. Determine whether the expert’s testimony (or combination of testimony and other record evidence) provides uncontradicted, non-speculative support for that element.

3. Decide whether any contradictions are matters of weight for the jury (resolvable by credibility determinations) or are so fundamental that resolving them would require weighing the same witness’s inconsistent statements—something JMOL forbids.

If the expert’s testimony is the sole evidence of an essential element and is internally contradictory on that element, courts often conclude that substantial evidence is lacking and grant JMOL.

# Reconciling conflicting expert statements

Principles favoring reconciliation

Courts are generally reluctant to throw out jury verdicts and will try to harmonize evidence where a reasonable construction permits it. Reconciling conflicting expert statements can preserve a verdict if the court can construe the testimony as describing different aspects of the same phenomenon, or as providing alternative but cumulative routes to the same legal conclusion.

Methods of reconciliation

Reconcilable conflicts are often treated through one or more of the following approaches:

- Contextual reading: Experts sometimes give abbreviated answers under trial pressure. Reading testimony in context—examining the question, the surrounding exchanges, and the underlying assumed hypotheticals—can show that a seemingly contradictory statement was limited in scope or meant to apply to a different scenario.

- Segmentation: Courts may isolate portions of testimony that are consistent with the record and treat contradictions as arguing only for diminished weight; if the consistent portions alone are sufficient to support the verdict, the contradiction is not fatal.

- Methodological correction: An expert may offer multiple independent bases for an opinion. If one basis is undermined, courts may accept an alternative basis that remains supported by the record, so long as the expert did not conceal the reliance on the alternative basis until appeal.

Limitations on reconciliation

Reconciling testimony has limits. A court cannot salvage a verdict by rewriting testimony or by attributing to the witness an unexpressed or invented view. Reconciliation is acceptable only where the construction is reasonable and does not require the court to credit or discredit competing statements—that is, it cannot involve the very credibility determinations JMOL forbids. If the testimony can only be reconciled by disbelieving parts of the same witness’s testimony (for example, treating one of two directly opposing statements as a misstatement), the court may not do so on JMOL grounds; the proper remedy may be a new trial if the contradictions are profound but juror discretion could have reasonably resolved them.

Reconciling across multiple experts

When the record contains multiple experts, courts perform a similar balancing. One expert’s unequivocal, well-founded testimony may suffice even if another expert contradicts it, because a jury may credit one and not the other. But if all expert testimony on an essential element is internally contradictory, or if all experts’ support depends on a common, unsupported assumption, then the aggregate evidence may still be legally insufficient.

# Interaction with the all-elements rule

The all-elements rule explained

In patent and many other areas of substantive law, the plaintiff must prove every element of a claim or cause of action. Under the “all-elements” (or “all limitations”) rule in patent law, for example, an infringement verdict or finding requires evidence that each claim element is present in the accused product or process. Similarly, for negligence or statutory claims, each statutory element must be proved. The all-elements rule imposes a structural constraint: failing proof on any single required element is fatal to the claim.

How contradictions interact with the all-elements rule

Contradictory expert testimony often raises problems under the all-elements rule in two main ways:

1. Failure to establish an element: If an expert is the sole source of evidence on a particular element, and their testimony is internally contradictory about that element, there is no legally sufficient proof of that element. Even if the expert gives persuasive testimony on other elements, lack of proof on one required element defeats the claim.

2. Gaps filled by speculation or assumption: Experts sometimes seek to bridge evidentiary gaps by assuming facts unsupported by the record. Where such assumptions are essential to an element, contradictions or absence of corroboration make the element unproven as a matter of law. Courts will not infer missing proof simply because other elements appear satisfied.

Mapping testimony to claim elements

To avoid sufficiency problems, expert testimony must do more than supply general support; it must be a reliable bridge between the record facts and each legal element. Effective expert opinions explicitly identify the evidence in the record that corresponds to each element and explain the reasoning that connects the two. The court will scrutinize whether the opinion provides a sufficiently particularized mapping and whether any contradictions undermine that mapping.

Common pitfalls that trigger all-elements failures

- Aggregated or generalized testimony: An expert who testifies in broad generalities without pinning testimony to discrete claim elements invites an all-elements failure. Juries cannot be left to infer that a general opinion covers every element.

- Inconsistent identifications: When an expert identifies alternative ways an element might be met but gives inconsistent accounts about which way actually applies, the uncertainty can make proof of that element legally inadequate.

- Reliance on disputed demonstrations: Opinions that lean heavily on demonstratives, simulations, or hypothesized reconstructions unsupported by admissible evidence are vulnerable if the underlying assumptions are contradicted.

# Strategic cross-examination and record-building

Objectives of strategic cross-examination

Cross-examination aims to test the accuracy, foundation, and consistency of an expert’s testimony, and to create a trial record that can be used in post-trial motions or on appeal. The defense or adverse party seeks to expose inconsistencies, force admissions about foundational assumptions, and establish that an opinion rests on speculation or erroneous facts. The proponent of the expert, in turn, must use direct examination and redirect to shore up foundations, elicit corroborating documentary or scientific support, and reconcile any apparent contradictions in a way that is recordable and repeatable.

Tactics for undermining self-contradictory testimony

1. Pin down the essential element(s). Ask the expert to identify precisely which record facts they rely on to prove each element. Force the witness to state whether a particular document, feature, or measurement is necessary to their conclusion. Short, focused questions make it harder for witnesses to improvise and produce contradictory sound bites.

2. Reduce hypotheticals to specifics. Translate hypothetical variations into concrete, record-substantiated scenarios. If an opinion depends on an assumption, ask whether the conclusion would change if that assumption were false and then introduce admissible evidence demonstrating the assumption’s falsity.

3. Use prior statements and reports. Cross-examine with depositions, prior expert reports, or public statements that conflict with the trial testimony. Having the prior statement in evidence and making the expert explain the discrepancy creates an explicit record of inconsistency.

4. Break complex conclusions into components. Experts often answer in long, compound propositions. Break the overall conclusion into sub-findings and attack each one. Expose any sub-finding that is unsupported or contradicted by other record evidence.

5. Highlight internal contradictions. If the expert makes a statement on direct that conflicts with an earlier answer, read both statements back and ask the witness to reconcile them. Get the witness to concede the inconsistency on the record. A clear admission that the expert said X in one place and Y in another creates a strong basis for JMOL or an appellate challenge.

6. Test methodology and alternatives. Show that the expert used an unreliable methodology or ignored more probative data. If the methodology would yield different answers under different reasonable assumptions, elicit that sensitivity on cross-examination.

Tactics for fortifying expert testimony on direct and redirect

1. Preempt contradictions by building a clear narrative. On direct, require the expert to map evidence to each legal element and to state explicitly which portions of the record support each mapping. Use demonstratives tied to identified exhibits and deposition testimony to make the linkage obvious.

2. Secure corroboration. Where possible, have the expert rely on documents, measurements, or admissions from adverse parties. Corroborative evidence reduces vulnerability to claims that an opinion rests on unsupported assumptions.

3. Limit reliance on broad hypotheticals. Encourage the expert to anchor conclusions in examined artifacts rather than abstract possibilities. If hypotheticals are necessary, have the expert spell out how the hypotheticals are grounded in the actual record.

4. Anticipate cross-examination points. During direct, raise and address anticipated challenges so that the expert can provide a coherent, reconciled explanation before cross-examination seizes the narrative.

Record-building to preserve JMOL or appellate relief

1. Make the essential statements explicit. A judge reviewing a sufficiency challenge will focus on whether the record contains competent evidence on each element. Asking the expert to declare on the record, with citation to exhibits and trial testimony, that each element is met reduces the chance the record will be characterized as ambiguous.

2. Preserve prior inconsistent statements. If you intend to rely on a witness’s inconsistency to argue insufficiency, ensure that the contradictory statements are admitted into the record—through deposition designations, admissions, or exhibit introduction—so the appellate court can review them.

3. Use proper objections and motions. If the opposing expert introduces speculative or legally insufficient opinions, contemporaneous objections (e.g., lack of foundation, improper legal conclusion) preserve the issue. File a timely JMOL or renewed JMOL (Rule 50(a) and (b) in federal practice) and a Rule 59 motion for a new trial if appropriate, explaining how the contradictions, when viewed as a whole, render the verdict unsupported by substantial evidence.

4. Elicit alternative testimony that the jury can rely on. If the expert offers multiple routes to a conclusion, get the witness to identify which route they prefer and to confirm that it is supported by specific exhibits. That creates a discrete basis for the jury’s verdict that may survive scrutiny even if other routes are undermined.

5. Create demonstratives tied to admissible evidence. Visuals can make element-by-element mappings clearer on the record. Ensure every demonstrative is tied to and supported by admitted exhibits to avoid appellate characterization of the demonstrative as speculative.

Preserving disputes about credibility versus sufficiency

Because appellate courts will not reweigh credibility, it is crucial to distinguish factual contradictions that are purely credibility issues from those that eliminate any legally sufficient evidence of an essential element. If the factual record includes admissible, non-contradicted evidence supporting the element independent of the witness’s inconsistency, the appellate argument that a jury could reasonably resolve the conflict is weaker. Conversely, if the contradicted testimony is the only evidence of an element, preserving a clear record of the contradiction and its centrality to the verdict strengthens a JMOL or appellate challenge.

# Conclusion

Self-contradictory expert testimony occupies a critical intersection between evidence, procedure, and substantive law. Courts will not sustain verdicts on testimony that is internally inconsistent in ways that defeat proof of essential elements, yet they will ordinarily leave to juries the resolution of conflicts that go to credibility. Lawyers must therefore build and preserve a record that maps expert reasoning to each legal element, expose and document any foundational falsehoods or contradictions, and know when to move for JMOL or a new trial. Trial judges and appellate courts will probe whether the contradictions are matters of weight for the jury or are so fundamental that permitting the verdict would transgress the requirement that every element be supported by substantial evidence. Understanding these doctrinal lines and employing strategic trial practices are essential to protecting a client’s rights at trial and on appeal.

Introduction

Claim construction is a foundational stage in patent litigation. It determines the metes and bounds of the patentee’s monopoly and therefore shapes every subsequent dispute about infringement and validity. Two related but distinct doctrines play a critical role in claim construction: (1) prosecution disclaimer (also called disavowal), in which statements made during prosecution can restrict claim scope; and (2) the primacy of intrinsic evidence, which requires courts to base claim meaning first on the patent claims, specification, and prosecution history before consulting extrinsic evidence. Courts enforce a high bar for finding a disclaimer and proceed cautiously when considering inventor testimony or amendments. This chapter explains the governing principles, the elements and standards courts apply, and the procedural effects of relying on intrinsic materials such as the prosecution history—while warning against overreading examiner actions or isolated words in the record.

# Clear and unambiguous requirement for disclaimer

Prosecution disclaimer is an estoppel-based doctrine: statements made by a patent applicant during prosecution may be treated as creating a binding limitation on claim scope if those statements are sufficiently clear and unambiguous. The foundational rationale is that a patentee should not be allowed to obtain broad claim scope after having represented to the Patent Office that the invention should be understood more narrowly in order to obtain allowance. But courts do not lightly find disclaimer; the requirement of clarity and unambiguity is strict.

What the patentee must have done. To establish prosecution disclaimer, the patentee’s statements must (1) be made by the applicant (or its agent) during prosecution and (2) constitute an explicit and unmistakable surrender of claim scope. Typical contexts include arguments distinguishing prior art, characterizing the invention to overcome a rejection, or making express commitments to the examiner about what the claims cover. The key question is whether a competitor reading the prosecution record would reasonably conclude that the applicant disclaimed the particular claim scope at issue.

The clarity standard. Courts require language that is clear and unmistakable. Ambiguous statements, advocacy in broad terms, or mere descriptions of preferred embodiments are generally insufficient. The inquiry is objective: would a person of ordinary skill in the art (POSITA), reading the prosecution history, understand that the applicant unequivocally gave up an area of subject matter? If the prosecution statement required background technical knowledge or could reasonably be read in more than one way, courts typically decline to find disclaimer.

Temporal and contextual limits. The disclaimer must have occurred during prosecution and be tied to the claim language that was under examination. Statements in unrelated proceedings, later litigation positions, or general background discussion in the specification are not prosecution disclaimer. Similarly, a patentee’s discussion of an embodiment or an example that merely narrows by illustration (but does not state that other variants are excluded) will not usually qualify as disavowal.

Effect on claim construction. When a court finds a clear and unambiguous disclaimer, it treats the disclaimed meaning as part of the intrinsic record and construes the claim accordingly—effectively subtracting the disclaimed territory from the claim’s reach. Because disclaimer is doctrinally an equitable estoppel rooted in fairness to the public and the PTO, courts are careful to limit its application to the precise subject matter the applicant actually surrendered.

Burden and standard of proof. The party asserting disclaimer bears the burden of proving that the prosecution statements meet the high clarity threshold. On appeal, claim construction is typically reviewed de novo (for legal questions) and factual subsidiary findings—if any—are reviewed for clear error, but the crucial point is that the record must plainly show surrender.

# Caution in using inventor testimony to narrow claims

Inventor testimony and other extrinsic evidence can help courts understand technical terms or background, but courts regard inventor testimony with caution when it is offered to narrow claim scope. The reasons are doctrinal and evidentiary: extrinsic evidence cannot contradict the intrinsic record, and inventors often have incentives or hindsight that may color their statements.

The limits of extrinsic evidence. Federal precedent establishes that intrinsic evidence—claims, specification, and prosecution history—controls claim construction. Extrinsic evidence (inventor declarations, expert reports, dictionaries, treatises, commentary) may be consulted to aid understanding of technical matters but cannot be used to import limitations that are inconsistent with the intrinsic record. Thus, inventor testimony that plainly accords with and clarifies intrinsic evidence can be helpful; testimony that attempts to reshape claim meaning contrary to the specification or prosecution history will be rejected.

Inventor testimony as litigation-driven evidence. Courts scrutinize inventor statements made in litigation because inventors may be motivated to narrow claim scope to avoid invalidity or to take inconsistent positions depending on the litigation posture. An inventor’s contemporaneous statements outside the prosecution record (for example, in affidavits or deposition testimony produced during litigation) do not have the same force as prosecution statements. Courts will typically treat such testimony as secondary, relying on it only to explain the intrinsic record where the intrinsic record is ambiguous.

How courts apply inventor testimony. If intrinsic evidence leaves a dispute over meaning, a court may consider inventor testimony to resolve ambiguities regarding ordinary meaning or technical nuances. But when an inventor’s testimony would narrow claims in a manner that conflicts with the plain claim language or the specification’s broad disclosure, courts will decline to adopt that narrowing. The principle is: do not allow post hoc inventor statements to rewrite claims in conflict with the patent document itself.

Procedural posture matters. The weight given to inventor testimony differs depending on whether it is part of the prosecution history (e.g., inventor remarks in an IDS or amendment) or arises later in litigation. Statements made during prosecution may bear on the prosecution estoppel analysis (see prior section), but the same clarity requirement applies. Later inventor testimony is generally treated as extrinsic and subordinate to the intrinsic record; courts will permit it to resolve ambiguity but will not permit it to create ambiguity where the intrinsic evidence is clear.

# Distinguishing mere amendment from limiting disavowal

Not every amendment or narrowing presented during prosecution amounts to a disavowal of claim scope. Courts distinguish ordinary claim amendments (which may be made to avoid prior art but without express qualification of claim scope) from admissions that the inventor intended limits beyond the language of the resulting claim.

Amendment for patentability versus intentional surrender. During prosecution, applicants commonly amend claims to add limitations to overcome prior art rejections. When an amendment merely narrows claim language to make the claim patentable but does not include arguments implicitly or explicitly promising that broader subject matter will not be claimed, courts are reluctant to read the amendment as a disclaimer. The difference is whether the amendment or accompanying remarks convey that the applicant regarded the added limitation as essential to the invention.

Tests courts apply. Courts look at two related aspects. First, what did the amendment say and how would a POSITA read it in the prosecution context? Second, did the applicant make explicit arguments during prosecution equating the narrowed claim to a particular feature or excluding alternatives? The combination of a narrowing amendment plus an accompanying prosecutor argument that equates the claim to a particular embodiment or excludes alternatives makes disclaimer more likely; a bare amendment without accompanying explicit surrender less so.

Examples of mere amendment. Typical safe amendments include replacing a broad term with a more specific technical term, adding a quantity or parameter to clarify scope, or amending a claim to invoke a standard of a claim limitation (e.g., adding “substantially” modifiers). If the applicant does not say that the added language was chosen to distinguish the claimed invention from a particular prior art reference and does not explicitly disclaim other variants, courts will often treat these edits simply as patentability-driven narrowing rather than a disavowal of all other options.

Doctrine of prosecution history estoppel versus disclaimer. It is important to distinguish prosecution history estoppel in the context of the doctrine of equivalents (a rule that limits the patentee’s ability to capture infringing equivalents after making narrowing amendments) from prosecution disclaimer in claim construction. Both originate in prosecution activity, but estoppel in equivalents can arise from amendments without the high clarity required for disclaimer; yet even here courts look at the reasons for the amendment. For claim construction, however, the lighter weight concept of estoppel is not substituted for the stricter clear-and-unambiguous requirement of disclaimer.

Procedural consequences. The practical impact is that defendants seeking to narrow claim scope should point to specific statements made during prosecution that are unmistakable in conceding territory. Conversely, patentees can often survive efforts to constrict claims if the prosecution amendments were routine and unaccompanied by explicit disavowing argumentation.

# Using prosecution history without overreading examiner citations

The prosecution history (also called the file wrapper) comprises the written exchanges between applicant and the Patent Office: responses to office actions, amendments, examiner’s rejections and reasons, arguments, interview summaries, and sometimes information disclosure statements. The prosecution history is intrinsic evidence and is part of the public record that defines what the patentee obtained. Yet courts must use it with care and avoid overreading isolated examiner comments, examiner citations, or prototypical boilerplate language.

Primacy of intrinsic evidence. The claim language and specification remain the primary sources for meaning; the prosecution history is a supplement when it illuminates how the patent reached allowance. Because the prosecution history is often a negotiation, courts contextualize statements in it rather than treating them as dispositive out-of-context sound bites.

Examiner statements are not dispositive. The examiner’s statements, citations, and reasons for rejection are not binding on claim construction. The examiner acts for the PTO and may have applied legal or factual conclusions that differ from the court’s. While an applicant might have responded to an examiner’s argument in a way that concedes certain territory, the examiner’s original characterization of the prior art or proposed claim scope does not itself create a judicial limit on claim meaning.

Avoiding overreading of examiner citations. Examining art and citations are technical aids for prosecution, but courts should not infer—without clear applicant language—that the patentee relied on or adopted every examiner characterization. For example, an examiner’s citation to a piece of prior art and a comment that the art “teaches X” does not mean the applicant disclaimed X unless the applicant explicitly accepted the examiner’s characterization and made it part of a concession. Applicant agreement or counter-argument is necessary for a clear disavowal.

Context and holistic reading. The prosecution history must be read as a whole. Single sentences or selective quotations, if plucked from a broader context, can misrepresent what was conceded. Courts therefore examine prosecution exchanges in sequence and analyze whether the entire record supports the conclusion that the patentee clearly and unambiguously surrendered particular subject matter. A single ambiguous comment will rarely suffice.

Using prosecution history to interpret technical meaning. Where the specification uses ambiguous terminology or where claim terms have multiple possible meanings in the art, the prosecution history can shed light on which meaning the applicant intended. For example, an applicant responding to an obviousness rejection might explain that the claimed “surface” refers to a particular structure and not to other surfaces in the prior art. If that explanation is clear, the prosecution history can anchor claim meaning. But courts require that the prosecution history actually reflect the applicant’s unequivocal intent to limit the claims in that way.

Burden and appellate review. The party asserting a prosecution-history-based limitation bears the burden of showing the prosecution history supports that limitation. On appeal, claim construction is reviewed de novo; factual underpinnings drawn from the prosecution history are reviewed for clear error. The upshot is that courts often reverse constructions that overread the prosecution history, especially where the intrinsic record supports a broader meaning.

Practical advice for practitioners. When drafting prosecution arguments, applicants should avoid absolute or sweeping disclaimers unless intentionally narrowing claim scope. When responding to examiner rejections, careful phrasing avoiding categorical exclusions or “never” statements helps preserve flexibility. Conversely, challengers seeking to use prosecution history to narrow claims should compile the prosecution record comprehensively and demonstrate how the statements, taken in context, would have given a POSITA an unmistakable understanding that certain subject matter was surrendered.

# Conclusion: doctrinal synthesis and litigation posture

Taken together, these doctrines emphasize fidelity to the intrinsic patent record and caution in importing limitations from external sources. The core principles are straightforward but their application is fact-intensive.

First, prosecution disclaimer requires clear and unambiguous statements by the applicant during prosecution that objectively surrender claim scope. Courts will not infer disclaimer from ambiguous language or routine amendments made for patentability—there must be an unmistakable surrender.

Second, inventor testimony and other extrinsic evidence are subsidiary tools. They can clarify technical terms but cannot be used to contradict or narrow the claims in ways inconsistent with the intrinsic evidence. Statements made during litigation enjoy less weight for claim meaning than contemporaneous statements made in the prosecution record.

Third, a narrowing amendment during prosecution does not automatically equate to a disavowal of all other embodiments; context, accompanying arguments, and the totality of the prosecution history determine whether a surrender occurred.

Finally, prosecution history is an intrinsic source but must be read holistically. Examiner statements or citations do not, by themselves, redefine claim scope. Courts scrutinize the complete file wrapper to determine whether the applicant’s communications created the necessary clarity for disclaimer.

Procedural posture influences outcomes. At claim construction and summary judgment stages, judges engage primarily with intrinsic documents and are alert to the high bar for disclaimer. At trial, a jury’s factual findings (e.g., about infringement or validity) are reviewed under standards like substantial evidence or JMOL principles, but claim construction is typically decided by the court. On appeal, claim-construction errors predicated on overreaching disclaimers or misapplied prosecution history principles can be reversed; conversely, factually supported narrowings grounded in unambiguous prosecution statements will be upheld.

For the law student preparing to read an opinion applying these doctrines, focus attention on how the court frames the prosecution statements, how it weighs the specification against extrinsic materials, and whether it identifies a clear, contextualized surrender. Understanding these analytical steps will illuminate why courts affirm or reject attempts to narrow patent claims based on prosecution conduct.
