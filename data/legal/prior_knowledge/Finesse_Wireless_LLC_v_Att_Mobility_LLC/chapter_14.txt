Introduction

In patent litigation, jury findings on infringement and damages are often pivotal to the outcome. When those findings are challenged after trial, courts apply the “substantial evidence” standard to determine whether a jury verdict must stand or be set aside. This chapter explains how courts define and apply substantial evidence in the patent context, how judges evaluate expert mappings and documentary corroboration that underpin jury verdicts, the limits courts place on conclusory testimony, and practical steps litigators must take to preserve sufficiency challenges for appeal. The goal is doctrinal: to equip you with the legal standards and analytical framework that courts use when asked to sustain or overturn jury findings in patent cases.

# Defining substantial evidence in the patent context

“Substantial evidence” is the term courts use to describe the minimal quantum of record evidence necessary to support a jury’s factual finding. Although the phrase is familiar from administrative-law and civil contexts, its application in patent cases follows the same core principle: a finding is supported by substantial evidence if there is relevant evidence that a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support the conclusion reached. Substantial evidence does not require that the evidence be uncontradicted, nor does it require that the evidence be overwhelming; it requires only that the evidence be legally adequate when viewed in the light most favorable to the verdict winner and without reassessing witness credibility.

How courts apply this principle depends on procedural posture and on which court reviews the ruling:

- JMOL (Rule 50) at the district-court level: When a party moves for judgment as a matter of law during trial, or renews such a motion after verdict under Rule 50(b), the district court must determine whether a reasonable jury could have reached the verdict based on the evidence presented. The court does so without weighing credibility or weighing the evidence, and it draws all reasonable inferences in favor of the nonmoving party. If the court concludes that the evidentiary basis is insufficient as a matter of law, it may grant JMOL; otherwise it must deny the motion.

- Motion for new trial (Rule 59): A motion for a new trial is a separate procedural vehicle that permits a district court greater discretion (for example, to order a new trial if the verdict is against the clear weight of the evidence). The standard for a new trial is generally more forgiving of the jury’s verdict than JMOL is, and appellate courts review denials of new trials for abuse of discretion.

- Appellate review: On appeal, courts review a district court’s denial of JMOL by applying the governing standard used by the district court (i.e., the applicable regional circuit’s standard) but typically conduct their review without deference on legal questions. In patent appeals the Federal Circuit will review claim construction de novo (subject to the usual exceptions) and will review the jury’s fact findings for substantial evidence. When the appellate court reviews whether substantial evidence supports a jury’s finding, it asks whether reasonable minds could reach the conclusion based on the evidence of record. The appellate court does not reweigh credibility or substitute its own factual determinations for those of the jury.

For patent-specific findings, courts emphasize the need for evidence connecting the asserted claims to the accused product or conduct. A legally sufficient infringement verdict must rest on evidence that shows, element-by-element, that the accused product performs the claimed functions (in the case of apparatus claims) or that the accused acts practice the claimed steps (in method claims). For damages, a verdict must rest on admissible evidence establishing a reasonable royalty or other measure, and that evidence too is subject to the substantial-evidence standard.

# Evaluating expert mappings and documentary support

Expert witnesses play a central role in patent trials. Because patent claims involve technical limitations, parties rely on experts to map claim limitations onto accused products or to explain how the claimed invention differs from the prior art. Courts scrutinize those mappings closely when evaluating whether the record supports infringement or damages findings.

What a legally sufficient expert mapping looks like

A mapping is legally sufficient when it does more than assert a conclusion; it identifies specific features, documents, code, standards, schematics, test data, or testimony and explains reliably how those identified items correspond to each asserted claim element. This requires an expert to perform a reasoned analysis that links the physical or software components (or the steps performed) to the language and scope of the claim. Effective mappings typically have the following characteristics:

- Element-by-element analysis. The expert addresses each claim limitation separately and explains where in the accused product or conduct the limitation is met.

- Specificity. The expert points to tangible sources of evidence: lines of source code, specific functions in firmware or middleware, particular provisions of a standards specification, labeled components in schematics, asserted packets or frames in protocol traces, or demonstrable device behavior in testing.

- Logical reasoning. The expert states a clear logic chain: the accused feature has characteristic X; the claim requires characteristic X; therefore the accused feature satisfies the claim limitation. Where intermediate inferences are necessary (e.g., interpreting an abstract standard term as implemented by a particular module), the expert explains those inferences and why they are reasonable.

- Corroboration from documents, demonstrations, or witnesses. A mapping based solely on an expert’s ipse dixit is weaker than one that links to contemporaneous documents (design notes, requirements, standards) or demonstrative testing that illustrates the asserted behavior.

Admissibility and foundational rigor

Admissibility under the Federal Rules (including Daubert and Rule 702) is a prerequisite to an expert’s evidence being considered. A reliable methodology and proper foundation increase an expert’s mapping’s probative force. If an expert’s methods are admissible, courts then assess whether those methods produced sufficient evidence to support jury conclusions.

Documentary support

Documentary evidence is often decisive in confirming or undermining expert mappings. Relevant documentary material includes source code listings, engineering specifications, design documents, product manuals, standards documents, protocol specifications, system logs, Test and Acceptance Reports, and internal emails reflecting design intent or implementation details. When expert testimony is buttressed by contemporaneous documentary evidence that concretely identifies the accused feature and how it functions, courts are more likely to find that substantial evidence supports a verdict.

When documentary evidence is absent or ambiguous, the mapping must make up for that lack by providing a persuasive technical explanation and, ideally, independent corroboration by testing or demonstratives. Absent these, courts have repeatedly held that the record lacks substantial evidence.

Standards and multifunctionality

Where standards compliance is at issue (for example, when the accused device implements an industry standard and the patentee asserts the standard practice maps to the claim), an effective expert mapping identifies precisely which portions of the standard correspond to each limitation and provides testimony or documents showing that the accused implementation actually follows those portions of the standard. Vague references to the standard as a whole do not suffice.

Evidence of non-infringing design alternatives and competing explanations

The jury does not have to accept an expert’s mapping in the face of competing explanations. But on a sufficiency challenge, a court will not overturn a jury verdict merely because the record contains contrary evidence; the question is whether sufficient evidence supports the verdict. If the record contains reasonable, specific conflicting evidence pointing to noninfringing design choices, the presence of that evidence can make the jury’s verdict more difficult to sustain on appeal only if the patentee’s mapping is otherwise insubstantial. A strong expert mapping that accounts for alternative explanations and explains why they do not negate infringement carries more weight.

# Limits of conclusory testimony

Courts draw a clear line between reasoned explanatory testimony and conclusory ipse dixit. Several recurring principles govern judicial treatment of conclusory testimony:

- “Ipse dixit” is insufficient. An expert’s naked assertion that “the accused product meets limitation X” without factual elaboration or linkage to record evidence is inadequate. Courts demand that the expert explain the basis for the assertion.

- No mere recitation of claim language. Testimony that simply repeats claim language and labels accused features as corresponding to that language, without further explanation, does not satisfy the element-by-element requirement.

- Self-contradiction and inconsistency undermine sufficiency. When an expert gives materially inconsistent statements in the record—e.g., testifying in one venue that a product performs a function and in another that it does not—courts will not treat those inconsistent assertions as collectively establishing substantial evidence. Self-contradictory testimony can be discounted because it lacks the necessary probative force.

- Lay witnesses and corporate designees cannot fill gaps with conclusory assertions. Corporate witnesses may provide facts about design or operation; but when those witnesses provide only conclusory labels rather than factual mapping tied to the claim terms, courts will treat that evidence as weak.

- Legal conclusions are not for experts. Experts may provide technical input, but they must avoid testifying to legal conclusions (for example, “this product infringes claim X”). The expert’s role is to supply the factual predicate—technical features and their correspondence to claim elements—so that the jury can reach a legal conclusion.

- The need for corroboration in close cases. Particularly where the patentee’s own evidence is thin, the court will scrutinize whether the expert’s testimony has independent documentary or demonstrative corroboration. Without it, courts have been unwilling to treat conclusory testimony as substantial evidence.

In practice, appellate courts have set aside infringement verdicts when the only support was conclusory testimony lacking documentary corroboration or when the testimony was internally inconsistent. The lesson is that strong, specific, consistent explanation is required; generalizations and conclusory proclamations are legally fragile.

# Guidance on preserving sufficiency challenges

Procedural rules and case law demand careful preservation of challenges to the sufficiency of the evidence. If a litigant fails to preserve a Rule 50 motion in the district court, appellate review of sufficiency arguments may be foreclosed.

When to move and what to say

- Rule 50(a) motion during trial. A party seeking to preserve a sufficiency challenge must move for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50(a) at the close of the opponent’s case or at the close of all evidence, depending on the grounds being asserted. The motion must state the specific grounds upon which it is made. Generic or cryptic motions risk being deemed insufficient to preserve particular arguments on appeal.

- Renewing the motion under Rule 50(b). After a jury verdict, the moving party must file a renewed motion for judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50(b) to preserve the issue for appeal. A party that fails to renew forfeits the right to challenge sufficiency on appeal in many circuits.

- Alternative grounds. To preserve multiple lines of argument, the Rule 50(a) and Rule 50(b) motions should articulate each specific sufficiency argument; many practitioners include alternative factual or legal grounds in the motion to avoid a forfeiture argument.

Practical drafting tips

- Be specific. Identify which claim elements lack sufficient evidence and why. Point to the absence of corroborating documents or the lack of element-by-element mapping.

- Reference record evidence. Even as you argue insufficiency, cite the record to show why no reasonable jury could have found for the opponent. The court and, later, the appellate court will rely on the record you direct them to.

- Preserve objections to expert testimony. If the sufficiency challenge depends on the admissibility or reliability of an expert’s methods, make appropriate Daubert-style objections and litigate the foundation at trial. A post-verdict sufficiency challenge will have less traction if prior admissibility objections were not made.

- Move (and renew) timely. Make Rule 50(a) motions at the correct phases of trial and renew under Rule 50(b) after the verdict. Failure to comply with Rule 50’s timing requirements often results in waiver of appellate sufficiency review.

Preserving challenges to damages

The same preservation rules apply to sufficiency challenges to damages awards. A party seeking JMOL on damages must move under Rule 50(a) at the close of the party’s damages case (or at the close of all evidence if damages are part of the overall evidence) and must renew under Rule 50(b) after the verdict. The motion should identify the insufficiency: for example, lack of evidence of causation, absence of a reliable damages model, or failure to apportion.

Record development to support future appeals

Because appellate courts review the trial record, counsel should develop the record strategically. Contemporaneous design documents, source code, test reports, deposition testimony from product engineers, standard specifications, and precise expert analyses are the raw materials that appellate courts examine when assessing substantial evidence. If these materials are not in the record, the patentee’s claim is weak on sufficiency review; conversely, a well-developed record makes appellate reversal less likely.

# Applying the doctrine to damages findings

Substantial-evidence principles apply equally to damages. A jury award of a reasonable royalty or lost profits must rest on competent evidence establishing the underlying factual predicates: nexus between the patent and the accused product, the value of the patented feature, apportionment to exclude non-infringing features, and methodology that reliably translates value into an award.

Expert economic testimony supporting damages must explain the valuation methodology, the data used, and the causal link to the asserted patent. Courts scrutinize whether the expert accounted for non-infringing alternatives, properly apportioned value, and relied on accepted economic concepts. Conclusions unsupported by reliable economic analysis or that rest on speculative correlations are vulnerable to sufficiency challenges.

When damages stem from a lump-sum negotiation theory or hypothetical bargaining (the “willing licensor/willing licensee” framework), the expert must identify comparable licenses, explain adjustments, and tie license terms to the facts of the hypothetical negotiation. Mere recitation of numbers without analysis or failure to explain why comparators are comparable will weaken the sufficiency of the evidence for a damages verdict.

# Practical tips for counsel and courts

For counsel for the patentee (plaintiff asserting infringement)

- Build an element-by-element record. Before trial, collect documents and engineer testimony that tie specific product aspects to claim limitations. Identify the best available documentary corroboration and testing.

- Prepare experts to explain their methodology and to point to concrete evidence. Avoid letting experts offer legal conclusions for which the jury is the factfinder.

- Anticipate defensive mappings and develop rebuttal evidence that explains why alternative interpretations do not negate infringement.

For counsel for the accused infringer (defendant)

- Attack the specificity and foundation of the patentee’s mappings at the Daubert stage and through cross-examination. Emphasize gaps, lack of documentary corroboration, or alternative, non-infringing interpretations.

- Prepare strong, coherent noninfringement explanations tied to product documentation and engineer testimony. If the patentee’s evidence is conclusory, ensure the court and jury understand that it lacks a factual foundation.

For district courts

- Gatekeep expert testimony under Rule 702/Daubert and ensure the record is adequately developed. Clarify to the jury when an expert is giving technical facts as opposed to legal conclusions.

- When considering JMOL, apply the standard correctly: view the evidence in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, do not weigh credibility, and consider whether reasonable jurors could reach the verdict from the evidence presented.

For appellate courts

- Respect appropriate deference: do not reweigh credibility, but ensure that elemental mappings and documentary support meet the legal threshold of substantial evidence.

# Conclusion

Substantial-evidence review in patent cases is a demand for a reasoned, well-supported evidentiary basis for jury findings of infringement and damages. Expert mappings must do the real work: identify specific accused features, explain how they correspond to claim limitations, and provide documentary or demonstrative corroboration. Courts will not sustain verdicts founded on bare conclusions, inconsistent testimony, or unsupported leaps of logic. At the same time, the standard does not require that the evidence be uncontradicted—only that reasonable minds could accept it as adequate. For litigators, the practical corollary is clear: preserve your challenges with timely, specific Rule 50 motions and build a record that either robustly links products to claims or persuasively exposes the gaps in the opposing party’s proof.
