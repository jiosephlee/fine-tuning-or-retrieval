Introduction

When a patent case is tried to a jury, the appellate path often turns on layered standards of review: the standard a court uses to evaluate a district court’s denial of judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), the standard for testing whether the jury’s verdict is supported by substantial evidence, and the standard that governs review of claim construction findings that underlie infringement and invalidity rulings. These standards interact: claim construction can be decisive to whether any reasonable jury could have found infringement; the district court’s factual findings that inform claim construction may be reviewed for clear error while legal conclusions are reviewed de novo; and the regional circuit’s procedural law can dictate how JMOL is reviewed on appeal. Understanding these doctrines—how they have evolved, how courts apply them to patent disputes, and how litigators should structure arguments at trial and on appeal—gives a student the tools to read and analyze appellate opinions that resolve close jury verdicts in patent cases.

# De novo review of JMOL under regional circuit precedent

Judgment as a matter of law under Rule 50 of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure is the principal vehicle for challenging the legal sufficiency of a jury’s verdict at the close of the opposing party’s case or after verdict. For appeals from district courts within the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction over patent appeals, the Federal Circuit has long recognized that it should apply the standard of review that the regional circuit (the court of appeals for the circuit in which the district court sits) applies when reviewing a district court’s ruling on JMOL. In practice this means that whether the Federal Circuit reviews the denial of JMOL de novo or with some degree of deference may depend on the regional circuit law.

What does this entail in functional terms? A motion for JMOL challenges whether the evidence presented at trial, viewed in the light most favorable to the nonmoving party, permits reasonable jurors to reach the verdict they reached. The typical articulation across circuits is that a JMOL should be granted when there is no legally sufficient evidentiary basis for a reasonable jury to find for that party. The critical components of the review are twofold: (1) the appellate court determines the standard the district court applied when deciding JMOL; and (2) the appellate court then applies the same standard to the trial record. Where regional precedent requires that a reviewing court not weigh credibility or resolve conflicts in the evidence, the appellate inquiry focuses on whether substantial evidence supports the verdict (see next section). Where regional law describes a more searching review, the court will conduct that review.

A few practical consequences follow. First, litigants must know and preserve the applicable regional-law standard in district court briefing and at trial. If the region’s controlling precedent directs a particular JMOL test—for example, that a court may not consider witness credibility—parties should frame their motions and trial record in terms that will be recognized on appeal. Second, the appellate court’s application of regional law means that outcomes can vary across circuits: identical trial records might survive JMOL in one regional circuit and not in another depending on the appellate standard the Federal Circuit adopts for that district. Finally, because the standard of review is applied by reference to the record the district court had, preservation of the evidentiary record (including contemporaneous objections and motions) is essential to preserve issues for appellate JMOL review.

# Substantial evidence for jury verdicts in patent cases

“Substantial evidence” is the workhorse evidentiary standard used to test whether a jury’s factual findings can stand on appeal. Substantial evidence means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion. It is not a stringent or weighty standard: it does not require that the evidence be overwhelming or that contrary inferences be impossible. But substantial evidence does impose meaningful limits: the appellate court must be able to point to record evidence that, if credited by the jury, would permit the finding.

How courts apply the substantial-evidence test in patent trials merits close attention because patent disputes often rest on expert testimony, technical demonstrations, and claim interpretation. Appellate courts reviewing for substantial evidence consider the last opportunity a jury had to hear the evidence—taking the record as a whole, viewing evidence in the light most favorable to the prevailing party, and not reweighing credibility. But there are recognized boundaries.

First, jury verdicts cannot be sustained by testimony that is internally inconsistent or self-contradictory in ways that destroy its probative force. Where an expert’s testimony on a key factual element is contradicted by the expert elsewhere in the same trial, or the testimony is so speculative or internally contradictory that no reasonable jury could credit it, appellate courts may conclude that no substantial evidence supports the verdict. Similarly, if the only testimony supporting an element is conclusory and lacks a reliable basis in record facts or demonstrable methodology, appellate review may find the verdict unsupported.

Second, the distinction between credibility determinations and sufficiency review matters. Appellate courts do not reassess witness credibility, but they do evaluate whether the evidence—credibilty assumed—actually provides a sufficient evidentiary basis for the finding. Thus if the only evidence supporting the verdict depends on leaps of inference not supported by the expert’s own data or by the intrinsic record, the court may find insufficient evidence even if the jury believed the witness.

Third, the “substantial evidence” standard is applied differently depending on whether the jury’s finding is primarily factual (e.g., what a device’s structure actually does, whether a given component is present in accused products) or legal (e.g., claim indefiniteness or obviousness often involves legal conclusion based on fact). Appellate courts review legal conclusions de novo while factual findings are tested for substantial evidence. For mixed questions, appellate courts parse the record to identify the factual predicates and review them for substantial evidence before applying legal principles.

Finally, Rule 59’s new-trial standards remain available at the district-court level, and denial of a new trial is generally reviewed for abuse of discretion—this is a separate, often more deferential, vehicle for complaining that the jury’s verdict was against the weight of the evidence. Parties who seek appellate reversal on sufficiency grounds should ensure they pursued both appropriate JMOL and Rule 59 relief as needed to preserve arguments and to put the district court on notice of the grounds for appellate review.

# Claim construction review after Teva

Claim construction historically was treated as a purely legal question reviewed de novo on appeal. The Supreme Court’s decision in Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc. v. Sandoz, Inc. altered that framework by emphasizing that when a district court’s claim construction includes subsidiary factual findings—often arising from extrinsic evidence such as expert testimony, dictionaries, or prosecution history—the appellate court owes those factual findings clear-error review. Purely legal conclusions about the meaning of claim language, informed by intrinsic evidence (the claim language, specification, and prosecution history), remain subject to de novo review.

The Teva framework produces a mixed standard:

- Where claim construction can be resolved solely by intrinsic evidence—the claims themselves, the specification, and the prosecution history—the appellate court may independently construe the claim language (de novo review). Courts often characterize this as reading the patent from the perspective of a person having ordinary skill in the art at the time of the invention.

- Where the district court makes subsidiary factual findings—such as credibility determinations about expert witnesses’ testimony regarding how a skilled artisan would understand a term; findings about the ordinary meaning of a term based on extrinsic sources; or determinations about technical facts necessary to resolve claim scope—the appellate court must uphold those findings unless clearly erroneous. The reviewing court then applies the law to those factual findings de novo.

Understanding the line between intrinsic- and extrinsic-based construction is crucial. Intrinsic evidence generally has primacy: the specification and claims are the best guide to claim meaning. Extrinsic evidence may be consulted to resolve ambiguities or to aid the court’s understanding of the technical context, but cannot be used to override the intrinsic record. Examples of subsidiary factual determinations that survive only under clear-error review include district-court findings about the meaning of specialized technical terms as used in the relevant art, or evaluations of whether a particular dictionary or expert description reflects the understanding of a skilled artisan. Conversely, the ultimate interpretive question—what would a skilled artisan understand the claim to mean—is legal and reviewed de novo, but depends on those factual predicates.

Practically, Teva means appellate courts often affirm parts of a district court’s constructions while reversing others: they accept the district court’s factual determinations unless clearly unsupported and then independently determine the legal effect of those facts on claim scope. Litigators should anticipate that factual disputes introduced at claim construction briefing or at Markman hearings may be reviewed deferentially on appeal, making the district-court fact-finding an important battleground.

# Practical impact on structuring appellate arguments

These intertwined standards should shape how counsel organizes both trial and appellate advocacy. Several practical lessons follow.

First, preserve the record aggressively. To maximize chances on appeal you must develop the evidentiary record in ways that map to the standards of review you will face. For JMOL challenges based on regional-circuit tests, craft the JMOL motion to mirror the standard the appellate court will apply and ensure the trial record contains the necessary admissions, cross-examination, and pointed evidentiary gaps. For claim-construction disputes, when extrinsic evidence will be relied upon, ensure the district court makes express factual findings (and that those findings are supported by admissible evidence) so that deferential review later may be invoked or overcome.

Second, tailor your JMOL and Rule 50(a)/(b) timing and content. File timely Rule 50(a) motions at the close of the opponent’s case (and renew as Rule 50(b) after verdict) to preserve the right to appellate review. Briefs should identify the exact elements the moving party contends the opponent failed to prove and cite the portions of the record that show the evidentiary defect. Avoid generic assertions that “no reasonable jury could have found” without pinpointing specific evidentiary lacunae or contradictions.

Third, structure expert testimony and impeachment to create or dismantle the “substantial evidence” foundation. Because patent jury verdicts frequently hinge on expert witnesses, effective cross-examination that exposes internal inconsistencies or speculative bases is crucial. If you are defending a jury verdict, preserve testimony and demonstratives that a reviewing court can point to as supporting evidence. If you are attacking a verdict, highlight contradictions, lack of foundation, or testimony that attempts to bridge gaps without factual support. Where an expert’s opinion depends on untested assumptions or omitted analyses, emphasize that the opinion is conclusory.

Fourth, anticipate the mixed claim-construction review under Teva. If you rely on extrinsic evidence (dictionaries, standards, tutorials, demonstrative expert testimony) to support a particular construction, present and secure district-court factual findings that tie that extrinsic evidence to how a person of ordinary skill would understand the term. If you seek de novo review, emphasize that the intrinsic record supplies a complete answer, arguing that no subsidiary factual findings are necessary. Conversely, if you litigate to obtain deference to district-court fact-finding, introduce and elicit evidence that permits the district court to make detailed factual findings: call live witnesses, point the court to specific portions of extrinsic sources, and request explicit findings in a claim-construction order.

Fifth, frame appeal briefs to reflect the correct standard of review and to separate factual predicate errors from legal errors. On appeal, structure arguments by first identifying the standard for each contested ruling (de novo, clear error, substantial evidence), then presenting the record bits tied to that standard. For JMOL appeals, show that under the regional circuit’s standard the trial record fails to contain substantial evidence supporting the verdict, or, if regional law uses a more searching review, demonstrate legal error in the district court’s application. For claim construction, identify the district court’s factual findings and show why they are clearly erroneous (if attacking them) or why legal application of those facts is erroneous (if attacking the court’s legal conclusion).

Sixth, consider the tactical interplay between JMOL, new-trial motions, and post-trial claim construction activity. District courts sometimes engage in post-verdict claim reconstruction or resolve claim-construction issues after the jury verdict; appellate courts will scrutinize whether such post-hoc alterations altered the legal landscape in ways that deprived the party of its jury-based protections. Consequently, litigators should object to post-verdict reconstructions that change substantive claim meaning or should move for a new trial where the district court’s post-verdict actions materially prejudiced the party’s rights. Preservation of objections and post-trial motions on these grounds is crucial for appellate review.

Seventh, think about remedies and preservation of alternative relief. When requesting JMOL, also seek (or preserve the right to seek) a new trial under Rule 59, or remittitur/additur where appropriate. Denials of new-trial motions are reviewed for abuse of discretion and thus present a different appellate pathway; sometimes cross-appealing or raising alternative grounds increases the chance that some relief will be obtained.

Conclusion

Jury trials in patent cases sit at the intersection of law and fact. Appellate review applies a mosaic of standards—regional-circuit rules on JMOL, the substantial-evidence test for jury findings, and Teva’s mixed-deference approach to claim construction. Effective litigation requires anticipating how each standard will be applied, building the trial record to supply favorable factual findings (or to expose insufficiencies), framing post-trial motions consistent with the applicable regional standard, and structuring appellate briefs to separate and address legal and factual predicates under their respective standards. Mastery of these doctrines is essential background before reading appellate opinions that resolve disputed patent jury verdicts.
