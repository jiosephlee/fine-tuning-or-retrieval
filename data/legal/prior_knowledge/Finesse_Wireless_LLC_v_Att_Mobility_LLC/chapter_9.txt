Introduction

Patent damages serve two essential purposes: to compensate the patent holder for infringement and to deter future violations. Determining the correct measure and amount of damages is frequently one of the most fact-intensive and contested portions of a patent trial. Courts apply a mixture of statutory command, settled doctrines, and equitable considerations to translate past infringement into an award in money. This chapter sets out the legal framework that governs damages in U.S. patent law, the familiar methodologies used by courts and experts, common evidentiary pitfalls, and the standards courts apply when a damages verdict is challenged or when liability is later disturbed on appeal. The goal is to equip the reader with the doctrinal tools necessary to understand how courts reason about compensatory patent awards and the practical limits on proof.

# 35 U.S.C. § 284 and the reasonable royalty floor

35 U.S.C. § 284 is the statutory anchor for patent damages. It provides that when a patent is infringed the court "may increase the damages up to three times the amount found or assessed" for willful infringement, and otherwise awards "damages adequate to compensate for the infringement, but in no event less than a reasonable royalty for the use made of the invention by the infringer." The statute imposes three central constraints or signals.

First, damages must be adequate to compensate the patentee for the infringement. That is a compensatory principle: the award should make the patentee whole for the economic harm caused by the unlawful use.

Second, the statutory minimum is a reasonable royalty. Even if a patentee cannot prove lost profits, or proves them only in part, the minimum recovery is a reasonable royalty measured by what the infringer would have paid in a hypothetical voluntary license for the infringement period. The reasonable royalty functions as a floor that ensures some recovery when causation or market proof of lost sales is incomplete.

Third, § 284 contemplates enhanced damages for willfulness, but the analysis of willfulness and enhancement follows separate doctrines (for example, the scope of culpability for enhanced damages and related procedural standards). The key point here is that § 284 requires courts first to measure compensatory damages (including reasonable royalties) before considering discretionary enhancement.

Two additional implications of § 284 are frequently litigated. One is whether a patentee who proves only a technical or minimal economic injury may nevertheless recover a reasonable royalty as a floor; courts uniformly allow it so long as the royalty is supported by competent proof. The second is that the royalty must be causal and tied to the patented invention's value; § 284 does not authorize windfalls that are unrelated to the infringement.

The patentee bears the burden of proving damages by a preponderance of the evidence. Damages must be proved with reasonable certainty, not speculative guesswork. Courts are willing to accept reasonable approximations so long as they are based on sound methodology and admissible evidence; however, awards resting on puro conjecture or untethered to the patented contribution typically fail.

# Georgia-Pacific factors and the hypothetical negotiation

When courts assess a reasonable royalty, they normally employ a hypothetical negotiation construct: what royalty rate would a willing licensee and willing licensor have agreed to just before the defendant began infringing, given the patent's value and the parties' economic positions? This framework focuses on ex ante bargaining and uses the circumstances that would have informed that hypothetical negotiation to fix a royalty for past infringement.

The most widely cited articulation of the factors that can shape that hypothetical negotiation are the Georgia-Pacific factors. These are a non-exhaustive list of considerations that courts may weigh in arriving at a reasonable royalty. They include, among others: established royalties for the patent or comparable patents; rates paid by the licensee for prior patents; the nature and scope of the license; the profitability and competitive relationship of the parties; the utility and advantages of the patent; the patent's contribution to the product selling price; the commercial success and long-felt need associated with the patented invention; the availability and cost of noninfringing alternatives; the portion of profit that would be allocated to the patent as opposed to other components; and the opinion testimony of qualified experts.

It is important to emphasize several doctrinal points about Georgia-Pacific and the hypothetical negotiation.

- The factors are neither a rigid checklist nor a mandatory balancing formula. A court need not address every factor so long as the ultimate royalty is supported by sound reasoning and evidence. Some factors may be inapplicable or overlapping; the admissibility and weight of evidence under each factor remains governed by the usual rules of evidence and relevance.

- The hypothetical negotiation is backward-looking in that it seeks to replicate what would have happened before infringement started. That constrains the parties' knowledge: neither side in the hypothetical possesses the benefit of hindsight about actual litigation outcomes or the infringer’s conduct after infringement began. However, parties could consider known marketplace facts and the perceived risks at the time of the putative license.

- Apportionment is a central subtheme of the Georgia-Pacific analysis. Several of the Georgia-Pacific factors directly address the need to tie any royalty to the value of the patented feature: for example, the patent's contribution to the product, the portion of profits attributable to the patented invention, and commercial success due to the patented feature. Courts require that a reasonable royalty compensate for the "use made of the invention," not for added value stemming entirely from unpatented features, brand, or other reasons.

- Evidence that anchors the hypothetical negotiation can come from prior licenses, comparable transactions, negotiated settlements, or internal documents showing the parties' willingness to pay for the patented technology. While prior licenses are often persuasive, they must be sufficiently comparable in technology, market, and timing, and properly adjusted to reflect differences.

- Experts play a major role in applying Georgia-Pacific. Their models should articulate how the facts translate into a royalty rate. Critically, expert testimony must grapple with apportionment, admissibility of comparables, and appropriate time value adjustments for royalties spanning many years.

# Lump-sum vs. running royalties; convoyed sales and apportionment

Two basic royalty structures commonly arise in patent damages: lump-sum (fixed) royalties and running (per-unit or percentage) royalties. Each has different implications for proof, apportionment, and the mechanics of damages computation.

A running royalty ties payment to units sold or revenues earned from products practicing the invention. Damage calculation is straightforward in principle: determine the infringing unit quantity or revenue for the infringement period, multiply by an agreed per-unit royalty or percentage rate, and adjust for offsets, credits, or licensed periods. Running royalties are particularly appropriate when the relationship between the patented feature and each unit sold is clear, and when the patent accounts for a measurable share of unit value.

Lump-sum royalties entail a single negotiated payment covering a period or scope of use. In a damages context, courts will attempt to disaggregate a lump-sum to the relevant infringement period and to apportion the payment among different uses or products if necessary. The valuation of a lump-sum requires present-value analysis and careful allocation of the sum across time, products, and geographic markets. Because a lump-sum can reflect bargaining over risk allocation, uncertainty about future sales, or coverage of multiple patented and unpatented features, courts scrutinize whether a lump-sum offered as comparable is sufficiently analogous to the hypothetical license in question.

Convoyed sales (sometimes called "related sales") occur where a patented feature is sold together with unpatented goods or services as part of a bundle. Typical examples include sales of an entire product that contains both patented and unpatented components (for example, a smartphone containing a patented chip) or a manufactured item sold together with complementary items. When assessing damages, the patentee must isolate (apportion) the value attributable to the patented component from the value of the convoyed, nonpatented elements. Failure to apportion properly risks awarding damages that compensate for value not created by the patented invention.

Apportionment requires experts and courts to distinguish between value derived from the patented features and value stemming from brand, marketing, design, or ancillary improvements. Legal doctrine requires that a patentee’s royalty base reflect the economic footprint of the invention, not the price of an entire multi-feature product unless the patented feature itself drives demand for the whole product. Several methodologies are used for apportionment: estimating the price premium attributable to the claimed feature, using cost-based measures for the patented component, comparing the accused product to a noninfringing alternative, or allocating profits according to comparable license splits. Whatever method is used, it must be logically tied to the facts and supported by evidence.

Key practical considerations when handling lump-sum vs. running royalties and convoyed sales:

- When experts propose using a lump-sum comparable to support a per-unit royalty, they must explain the conversion: how much of the lump-sum would have been allocated to the infringing uses, over what time horizon, and at what discount rate.

- Running royalties require accurate accounting of units and revenue. Disputes often arise over what constitutes a "unit" (e.g., software downloads, subscriptions, or bundled devices) and over that unit’s relation to the claimed invention.

- Convoyed sale situations commonly require elimination of value attributable to unpatented components. Experts must explain why the chosen apportionment methodology produces a reasonable estimate of the patented invention's contribution.

- The court will reject royalty theories that rely on overall product price where the patented feature is a small component and no evidence shows the patented feature drove consumer demand for the entire product.

# Entire market value rule and smallest saleable unit

Two closely related doctrines impose limits on using the entire value of an accused product as the damages base: the Entire Market Value Rule (EMVR) and the Smallest Saleable Unit (SSU) concept.

The Entire Market Value Rule allows a patentee to recover damages based on the entire market value (i.e., whole product price) only in the narrow circumstance where the patented feature is the basis for customer demand for the entire product. In other words, the EMVR applies if the patent-related feature is what causes consumers to buy the whole product. If other features or the product as a whole drive demand, then using the entire product value as the royalty base overcompensates the patentee. Courts require affirmative proof that the patented feature creates the demand for the complete product before permitting an EMVR-based royalty.

Because patentees often seek to base royalties on the full device price (for example, in complex products with many features), courts have developed the SSU idea as a practical constraint. The smallest saleable unit is the least complex, salable item practicing the claimed invention for which sales are recorded. Using the SSU as the royalty base limits the risk of inflating damages by including revenue from unrelated components. The SSU is not a doctrinal substitute for EMVR; rather, it is an evidentiary baseline that helps ensure apportionment. If the patentee can show the patented feature drives demand for the entire product, the SSU limitation may not foreclose using the entire device price; otherwise the SSU or some apportionment of the product price should govern.

Courts recognize that modern products can be complex and modular (e.g., hardware with software and services). In such cases, practical application of SSU involves:

- Identifying what is the smallest component that is sold and practices the claim. For software, this may be an instantiation or module; for hardware, a sub-assembly.

- Evaluating whether that unit is sold in isolation or only as part of a larger assembly. If the SSU is never sold separately, courts focus on whether the patented feature on its own justifies capturing the entire assembled product's value.

- Considering whether the patented feature is a technical improvement that meaningfully contributes to demand. Courts look to consumer surveys, external statements, or market data showing the feature's influence on purchase decisions.

The overarching principle is that damages must be proportionate to the economic contribution of the patent. EMVR allows whole-product damages only as an exception; SSU and apportionment doctrines operationalize the limitation that damages be tied to the patented invention's value.

# Standards for new trial on damages and vacatur when liability is reversed

Because damages derive from mixed legal and factual determinations, post-trial challenges to damages verdicts are common. Two principal procedural remedies exist at the trial-court level and on appeal: motions for a new trial (or remittitur) and vacatur or remand when liability is disturbed on appeal.

Standards for new trial and remittitur

A defendant dissatisfied with a jury's damages award can move for a new trial under the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure or seek remittitur—that is, an alternative to a new trial where the court orders a reduced award instead. The standard for granting a new trial on damages typically requires a showing that the verdict is against the clear weight of the evidence, that the damages award is excessive or inadequate, or that the trial was marred by legal error affecting the damages determination. Trial courts have broad discretion in deciding motions for new trial, and appellate courts review those decisions under deferential standards: abuse of discretion is the norm for most aspects of damage-related gatekeeping.

Appellate courts will review sufficiency of the evidence challenges to determine whether the damages award is supported by substantial evidence. When damages are challenged on legal grounds—such as failure to apportion, misapplication of EMVR, or use of speculative assumptions—appellate review may apply de novo review to legal errors but will generally leave factual determinations undisturbed absent clear error.

Remittitur is an equitable mechanism whereby the court reduces an excessive jury award to an amount the court deems supported by the evidence and gives the prevailing party a choice between accepting the reduced award or undergoing a new trial on damages. Courts apply remittitur cautiously because it implicates the jury's role and the Seventh Amendment; however, it is appropriate where the court finds the jury's award inconsistent with the evidence or law.

Key points regarding motions for new trial and remittitur:

- The moving party bears a heavy burden to show that the jury’s damages award lacks evidentiary support or is legally defective.

- Courts will vacate or order remittitur where expert testimony is inadmissible or legally insufficient to support the award (for example, when an expert fails to apply apportionment or relies on inadmissible comparables).

- Procedural timing matters: a party must preserve objections at trial and move timely for a new trial or judgment as a matter of law to preserve appellate review.

Vacatur when liability is reversed on appeal

When an appellate court reverses liability wholly or in part, the fate of any damages award hinges on the scope of reversal. If liability is reversed in toto (no infringement), any damages awarded for the reversed infringement period typically cannot stand because there is no underlying liability to support them. The appellate decision will generally vacate the damages award and remand for further proceedings as necessary—this may include retrial on damages if some infringement liability remains, or entry of judgment for the defendant when the reversal is complete and no further proceedings are required.

If liability is reversed only in part (for example, some asserted claims are invalidated or some accused products are found not to infringe), courts must determine whether the remaining liability supports the previously entered damages award. This often requires parsing the award to allocate damages to particular claims and products. If the trial record supports a division of damages by claim or product, a remand for recalculation or a revised judgment can often cure the error. If the damages award is inextricably conflated across claims or products, vacatur and a new trial limited to damages or liability may be required.

Practical rules that guide vacatur and remand:

- When liability is reversed entirely, damages are vacated because the legal predicate for any compensation no longer exists.

- When liability is partially reversed, appellate courts try to preserve well-founded portions of the damages award, but will vacate or remand where damages cannot be reliably apportioned among valid and invalid bases.

- Appellate courts may also remand with instructions to the trial court to consider whether a new trial on damages is necessary or whether the existing record permits recalculation consistent with the appellate holdings.

- The standards for vacating damages are legal in nature when they depend on a legal defect (e.g., erroneous admission of certain evidence that was essential to the award) but may involve further factfinding when apportionment or recalculation is possible.

- If the appellate reversal implicates willfulness or enhanced damages, those remedial aspects may likewise require reconsideration or vacatur since enhancement presupposes underlying culpable infringement.

# Common evidentiary pitfalls and best practices

Proof of damages is as much about admissibility and method as it is about numbers. Several recurrent pitfalls can undermine a damages case.

Unreliable comparables. Prior licenses and settlements can be powerful evidence for reasonable royalty determinations, but they are probative only if sufficiently comparable. Courts will exclude or discount comparables that differ materially in technology, scope, market conditions, or bargaining posture. Experts should explain adjustments for timing, market size, and covered patents.

Failure to apportion. Perhaps the most critical and frequent error is failing to tie the royalty base to the patented invention. Experts must show how their methodology isolates the patent's economic contribution. Just taking the entire product price without proof that the patent drives demand will typically be rejected.

Hindsight bias in the hypothetical negotiation. Experts and fact witnesses sometimes rely on knowledge obtained after infringement began (including litigation outcomes) to justify a particular negotiated rate. The hypothetical negotiation must mimic the ex ante positions of a willing licensor and licensee; reliance on post-infringement knowledge must be carefully justified or avoided.

Improper use of the entire market value. Treat EMVR as an exception: courts will require compelling proof that the patent created demand for the whole product. Absent that, start from the SSU or otherwise apportion.

Discounting and present value errors. When damages span multiple years—particularly with lump-sum conversions—experts must correctly account for time value of money and appropriate discount rates. Mistakes in discounting can materially distort present-value calculations.

Speculative lost profits. Lost profits require proof of but-for sales, causation, and foreseeability, including proof that the patentee would have made the sales absent infringement and that sufficient market demand existed. Courts will reject lost-profit claims that rest on speculative assumptions about demand or customer behaviors.

Unsubstantiated assumptions about market share or profitability. Using overall corporate profit margins to justify a share attributable to the patent is risky absent specific evidence linking profitability to the patented feature.

To avoid these pitfalls:

- Tie every step of the damages model to admissible evidence in the record.

- Explicitly address apportionment early and show how the chosen royalty base reflects only patented value.

- When using comparables, carefully identify similarities and differences and explain any adjustments.

- Preserve objections at trial and be prepared to defend expert methodologies under Daubert or similar standards.

- Use sensitivity analyses to show how conclusions vary with changes in key assumptions; this increases transparency and helps courts assess the reasonableness of estimates.

Conclusion

Calculating patent damages is a hybrid legal-economic exercise that requires aligning statutory commands, doctrinal constraints, and careful factual proof. 35 U.S.C. § 284 mandates compensation at least as large as a reasonable royalty; the hypothetical negotiation and Georgia-Pacific factors provide a flexible but structured way to fix a royalty; and doctrines such as apportionment, the EMVR, and the SSU protect against awards untethered to the patented contribution. The form of royalty—lump-sum or running—affects valuation mechanics and evidentiary needs, and convoyed sales heighten the need for apportionment. Trial courts possess broad discretion in awarding damages and in granting new trials or remittitur, but appellate courts will intervene where legal errors or insufficient evidence undermine an award. Understanding these doctrines and common evidentiary traps is essential for litigants, experts, and judges to produce damages awards that are compensatory, proportional, and legally sustainable.
