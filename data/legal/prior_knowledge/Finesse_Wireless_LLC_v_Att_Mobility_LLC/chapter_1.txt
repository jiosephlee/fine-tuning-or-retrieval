Introduction

Federal appellate review of patent cases is governed by a specialized jurisdictional regime: appeals from district court decisions involving patents generally go to the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit (the Federal Circuit) rather than to the regional circuit where the district court sits. That statutory allocation centralizes patent law development in a single appellate tribunal, but it does not mean the Federal Circuit writes on a clean slate. The Federal Circuit must apply its own distinctive body of patent law on substantive patent issues while often applying regional-circuit law to many procedural and non-patent questions. Mastery of the interplay between statutory jurisdiction, the final judgment rule, allocation of governing law, standards of review, and the rules for awarding costs and fees on appeal is essential for predicting outcomes and planning appellate strategy in patent litigation.

This chapter explains the scope of the Federal Circuit’s statutory jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1), the significance of the final judgment rule for appealability, how the Federal Circuit and regional circuits divide authority over procedural and substantive issues on appeal (with particular attention to motions such as judgment as a matter of law and new trials), the contours of Federal Circuit law for patent-specific questions, and the standards and statutes that govern awards of costs and appellate fees. The treatment emphasizes legal reasoning: how courts frame issues, define applicable law, apply standards of review, and account for procedural posture when resolving appeals.

# 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) and the scope of Federal Circuit jurisdiction

Statutory allocation and scope

28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) creates the Federal Circuit’s appellate jurisdiction over civil actions “arising under” the patent laws and certain other federal statutes by providing that appeals in specified categories from district courts shall be taken to the Federal Circuit. Practically, this means that final decisions by a district court in cases involving patent validity, infringement, and related claims are generally reviewable by the Federal Circuit, rather than by the geographically organized regional courts of appeals.

Determining whether an appeal “arises under” the patent laws turns on the complaint’s original character and the issues actually litigated. When patent claims are essential to the plaintiff’s right to relief, or when the resolution of patent law questions is central to the disposition, the Federal Circuit will have jurisdiction. Jurisdiction under § 1295(a)(1) is thus linked to the federal nature and centrality of patent issues in the litigation. The statutory grant is broad in patent disputes but not unlimited: where the patent law question is incidental, hypothetical, or asserted defensively but not central to the parties’ claims and remedies, jurisdictional questions can arise.

Interplay with other statutes and appeals

The Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction under § 1295(a)(1) operates alongside the usual federal appellate jurisdiction statutes, including the statute that creates appeals from final decisions of the district courts. There are statutory exceptions and special provisions—such as the availability of interlocutory review in certain limited circumstances under other subsections of § 1295 and other statutes—which can affect the timing and scope of appeals. Appellate jurisdiction over patent matters is thus statutory and can be defined both by the content of the issues on appeal and by the specific appellate mechanism invoked.

Jurisdictional posture and preservation

The timing and manner of presenting issues at the district court affect whether an issue is ripe for Federal Circuit review. A party that fails to preserve a claim or defense properly in the district court may face procedural bars on appeal regardless of the jurisdictional reach of § 1295(a)(1). In addition, the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction does not automatically authorize review of interlocutory rulings unless a statutory pathway is available; absent an interlocutory statute, a party generally must await a final judgment and then appeal.

# Final judgment rule and appealability

The final judgment rule: appealable order requirement

The final judgment rule is the basic threshold for appellate jurisdiction in federal courts. Under the final judgment doctrine, an appeal may be taken only from “final decisions” of district courts—orders that dispose of all claims against all parties, leaving nothing further for the trial court to do on the merits. This rule promotes efficient and orderly appellate review and prevents piecemeal appeals.

Finality in multi-claim or multi-party cases

Finality is measured by whether the district court’s order finally resolves the rights and liabilities of the parties with respect to the claims and parties encompassed by the appeal. In multi-claim or multi-party litigation, finality can be achieved through an order disposing of the last claim or by the district court’s entry of final judgment as to fewer than all claims or parties when the requirements of the controlling statute (e.g., a certification under rules that permit partial final judgment) are satisfied. Otherwise, an appeal that seeks to challenge only an interlocutory ruling or the resolution of fewer than all claims or parties will typically be premature.

Interlocutory appeals and statutory exceptions

There are well-defined statutory exceptions to the final judgment rule. Certain interlocutory orders may be immediately appealable if a statute authorizes it—examples include certain injunctions, collateral orders meeting the narrow collateral order doctrine (orders that conclusively determine important rights separate from the merits and are effectively unreviewable on appeal from final judgment), and certification for interlocutory appeal under statutes allowing interlocutory review. In the patent context, statutory provisions may allow interlocutory appeals in some circumstances (for example, appeals from decisions on claim construction in some limited contexts heard under specific statutory paths), but the ordinary rule is that the Federal Circuit hears appeals after final judgment.

Effect of post-judgment motions

Post-judgment motions can affect appealability. A timely post-trial or post-judgment motion that affects the judgment may either toll the running of time to appeal or suspend finality until the motion is resolved. Examples include motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), motions for a new trial, motions to alter or amend a judgment, or motions for relief from judgment. The invocation of such motions can alter the procedural posture in which an appellate court assesses the district court’s rulings, and appellate courts frequently require that these preservation mechanisms be used to challenge factual sufficiency or procedural errors that arise during or immediately after trial.

Practical consequences for litigants

For practitioners, the final judgment rule means careful planning. Parties must identify which issues are susceptible to interlocutory review and which must be preserved and renewed through post-trial motions if immediate appeal is sought. Strategic use of post-trial motions not only preserves issues for appeal but also may change the standard of review an appellate court applies, as the appellate court may review the denial of certain motions like JMOL de novo (or under other well-established standards) while treating other findings as final.

# Applying regional circuit law to procedural issues (e.g., JMOL, new trial)

The general choice-of-law principle

When the Federal Circuit hears an appeal from a district court in a patent case, it applies federal law to substantive patent issues but often looks to the law of the regional circuit that decided the case for many procedural questions. This is a choice-of-law principle recognizing that a district court operates within the regional circuit’s procedural and interlocutory framework, and that a uniform approach to federal procedure is desirable. Therefore, for procedural matters that are not uniquely attached to patent law, the Federal Circuit commonly applies the regional circuit’s law, including its precedents interpreting the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and standards for preservation, waiver, or procedural motions.

Scope of “procedural issues”

Procedural issues to which regional circuit law commonly applies include, but are not limited to, preservation rules, standards governing waiver and forfeiture, evidentiary rulings on matters of admissibility where the regional circuit has specific doctrines, and the particulars of motions such as judgment as a matter of law and new trial motions. The idea is that these legal questions are not patent-specific and are better resolved consistently with the regional circuit that oversees the trial court’s ordinary practice.

Standards for JMOL (judgment as a matter of law)

Judgment as a matter of law (JMOL) (sometimes called judgment notwithstanding the verdict or JNOV in prior terminology) challenges the sufficiency of the evidence supporting the jury’s verdict. The controlling standard for JMOL and the timing for making such a motion are set by federal procedural rules, but courts’ interpretation of those rules—particularly what constitutes sufficient evidence, how to view the evidence on review, and whether an appellate court examines the record anew—can vary across circuits in detail. The Federal Circuit typically adopts the regional circuit’s interpretation of the standard (for example, the test of whether a reasonable jury could have found for the non-moving party) and follows the regional circuit’s guidance on such matters as whether the judge may weigh evidence or assess witness credibility.

Standards for new trial motions

New trial motions, which ask the district court to set aside a jury verdict and order another trial, are equitable and discretionary. The standard for granting a new trial—whether because of the verdict’s against the weight of the evidence, errors in the admission or exclusion of evidence, prejudicial conduct, or other trial irregularities—derives from the district court’s local practice and regional precedent. The Federal Circuit defers to the regional circuit’s approach to these discretionary determinations, respecting the trial court’s fact-finding and judgment unless the regional circuit’s law would permit reversal.

Preservation, waiver, and forfeiture

Whether a litigant preserved an objection for appeal often controls appellate reviewability and standard of review. The regional circuit’s doctrines on preservation, waiver, and forfeiture therefore apply. For example, some regional circuits adhere strictly to contemporaneous-objection requirements for evidentiary errors, while others allow more flexibility; the Federal Circuit follows the regional circuit law that governed the trial court when assessing whether an appellant waived or preserved an issue.

Interaction with standards of review

Because the regional circuits may differ in how they apply preservation doctrines or in the intensity of review for discretionary rulings, the choice to apply regional circuit law can materially affect the outcome of an appeal. For instance, under one regional approach, the failure to make a particular motion at trial might result in plain-error review on appeal, making reversal difficult; under another approach, the issue might be considered preserved for more searching review. The Federal Circuit thus pays attention to the procedural posture and to the regional circuit framework that governed the district court’s proceedings before drawing its appellate conclusions.

# Applying Federal Circuit law to patent-specific issues

Substantive patent law as Federal Circuit law

The Federal Circuit is the authoritative national appellate forum for substantive patent law questions. This includes, but is not limited to, claim construction, infringement analysis (literal infringement and doctrine of equivalents), validity questions such as anticipation and obviousness, enablement and written description, indefiniteness, and patentability determinations tied directly to substantive patent doctrine. The rationale is that uniformity in substantive patent law across the nation is desirable to reduce forum shopping and to promote predictable patent standards for inventors, competitors, and the public.

Claim construction: law versus fact

Claim construction—interpreting what the patent claims mean—has both legal and factual components. The overarching principle is that ultimate claim construction is a legal determination reviewed on appeal as a question of law, but subsidiary factual findings that inform claim construction (for example, factual findings about how a person of ordinary skill in the art would understand a term or objective evidence from prosecution history or extrinsic evidence) are reviewed for clear error. Thus, on appeal, legal aspects of claim interpretation are reviewed de novo, while embedded factual determinations receive deference.

Standards for obviousness and anticipation

Obviousness and anticipation revolve around mixed questions of law and fact. The factual components typically include the scope and content of the prior art, differences between the prior art and the claimed invention, the level of ordinary skill in the art, and objective indicia of nonobviousness (such as commercial success, long-felt need, or failure of others). Those factual findings are reviewed for clear error. The ultimate question—whether a person of ordinary skill in the art would have found the claimed invention obvious in view of the prior art—is treated as a legal conclusion based on those facts and is reviewed de novo. For anticipation, factual determinations about whether a single prior-art reference discloses every claim limitation are reviewed for clear error; the ultimate legal conclusion of anticipation extends from those facts.

Infringement (literal and doctrine of equivalents)

Literal infringement analysis applies the claim construction framework—if properly construed claim terms are literally met by the accused device or method, infringement may be found. The Federal Circuit reviews claim construction de novo (with deference to subsidiary facts), and then applies those constructions to the accused product as a matter of law. The doctrine of equivalents, by contrast, is fact-intensive and typically involves questions about the function-way-result and whether prosecution history estoppel bars equivalents. Appellate courts review underlying factual determinations (e.g., whether an element is insubstantially different) for clear error and legal conclusions de novo.

Patent invalidity defenses and burdens of proof

In a civil action, the party asserting invalidity bears the burden of persuasion by clear and convincing evidence when the patent is presumed valid. That burden is a matter of substantive patent law and thus governed uniformly by the Federal Circuit’s treatment of the standard. When validity issues are tried to a jury, the jury’s factual findings are given the usual appellate deference (clear error for factual findings), and the Federal Circuit applies its own law to the legal aspects. If the district court later grants a JMOL on invalidity, appellate review will carefully distinguish between whether the evidence could support the jury’s view and whether legal conclusions derived from facts were correct.

Administrative decisions and appeals (e.g., patent office proceedings)

Where patent validity is determined in administrative proceedings before the patent office (such as post-grant review or inter partes review), the interplay between administrative adjudication and district-court litigation creates doctrinal nuances. The Federal Circuit reviews administrative decisions under administrative-law standards (de novo review of legal questions, substantial evidence review of factual findings, and Chevron/Skidmore-type deference where statutory interpretation is at issue). While this topic bridges administrative law and patent law, the Federal Circuit’s patent expertise increasingly informs its review of both district court and administrative agency determinations.

# Standards for granting costs on appeal

Distinguishing taxable costs and fee-shifting

Appellate costs fall into two broad categories: taxable costs (statutorily enumerated expenses that the prevailing party can recover under 28 U.S.C. § 1920 and related authorities) and attorneys’ fees or other non-taxable costs recoverable only through specific fee-shifting statutes (e.g., 35 U.S.C. § 285 for patent exceptional cases), contractual fee-shifting clauses, or rule-based sanctions (e.g., appellate rules authorizing sanctions for frivolous appeals).

Taxable costs under § 1920 and rules

Taxable costs include specific categories such as fees of the clerk and marshal, fees for transcripts necessarily obtained for use in the case, printing and witnesses, copying costs (subject to limits), and docket fees. On appeal, a prevailing party may file a bill of costs requesting taxable items, and the appellate rules set timelines and formality for such requests. The appellate court applies statutory and rule-based limits to what may be taxed, and the standard is narrower than for broader fee awards—taxable costs are generally awarded unless the court finds a reason to deny them (e.g., prevailing party is not entitled to costs under relevant rules, the costs were not necessary, or equitable considerations counsel denial).

Appellate sanctions and frivolous appeals

Appellate rules provide mechanisms to penalize frivolous appeals. A court may impose sanctions when an appeal is frivolous, vexatious, or taken for improper purposes. The standard typically requires a showing that the appeal has no reasonable basis in law or fact or is pursued in bad faith. When sanctions are warranted, a court may impose monetary penalties, award fees to the other party, or enter other sanctions tailored to deter similar conduct. Courts often require a fairly high threshold for sanctions because appellate advocacy can involve difficult or novel legal arguments that, while ultimately unsuccessful, are not frivolous.

35 U.S.C. § 285 and “exceptional case” fee shifting

In patent litigation, 35 U.S.C. § 285 authorizes a district court to award reasonable attorneys’ fees to the prevailing party in “exceptional cases.” The standard for an exceptional case traditionally has included situations of bad-faith litigation, willful infringement, frivolous claims, or litigation conduct falling outside the norm. On appeal, the appellate court may review awards under § 285 for abuse of discretion as to the decision to award fees and for correctness of legal interpretations of what constitutes an exceptional case. Appellate review of the amount awarded involves assessment of reasonableness (lodestar, multiplier, and related considerations), which the appellate court reviews for abuse of discretion with deference to the district court’s factual findings about the scope and necessity of the work.

Standards for appellate fee awards

When an appellate court contemplates awarding fees or costs arising from the appeal itself, it considers both the statutory authority invoked and the conduct of the parties on appeal. If the appellant’s arguments are frivolous or the appeal was taken in bad faith, the appellate court may impose sanctions under its rulemaking authority or under federal statutes. When appellate fees are sought pursuant to a substantive statute (such as § 285), the appellate court will evaluate whether the district court properly applied the statute and whether the facts supporting an exceptionality finding were clearly erroneous.

Procedural posture and timing of fee requests

The timely filing and proper preservation of fee requests matter. Fee petitions often must be filed in the district court initially (for § 285 awards) and are then reviewable on appeal. Taxable costs on appeal are typically requested in the appellate court under the appellate rules. Whether the appellate court will entertain a fee request or will remand to the district court for a factual determination depends on the statutory basis and the record; appellate courts often remand if the record lacks findings necessary to resolve disputed factual questions about exceptionality or reasonableness.

Equitable considerations and discretion

Even where statutory authority exists, fee awards are discretionary and equitable. Courts weigh the totality of the circumstances—including the litigation’s substantive merit, the parties’ conduct, the necessity and reasonableness of the incurred fees, and considerations of fairness—before awarding fees or denying them. The appellate court respects the district court’s superior vantage to assess many of these factual and discretionary elements but will correct legal errors or abuses of discretion.

Conclusion

Understanding the Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction and choice-of-law framework is crucial for effective appellate practice in patent litigation. The statutory grant in 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1) centralizes substantive patent law in the Federal Circuit, but the final judgment rule, preservation doctrines, and regional-circuit procedural law shape what is appealable and how appeals are reviewed. Procedural motions such as JMOL and motions for a new trial are typically governed by regional-circuit law for questions of procedure and preservation, while substantive patent questions—claim construction, infringement, validity, and other patent doctrines—are resolved under Federal Circuit law with an established mix of de novo review for legal conclusions and clear-error review for subsidiary factual findings. Finally, awards of costs and attorneys’ fees on appeal require careful attention to whether the costs are taxable or fee-shifting, the statutory standard invoked (e.g., § 1920 versus § 285), and the discretionary and equitable nature of such awards. Mastery of these allocation rules and standards will arm a law student or practitioner with the framework necessary to analyze appellate briefing, preserve issues in the trial court, and predict how appellate courts will approach the parties’ disputes.
