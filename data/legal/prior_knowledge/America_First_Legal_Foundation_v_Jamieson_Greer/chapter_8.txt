Introduction

Litigating against federal officials and agencies raises a web of threshold doctrines that determine whether a court can hear the challenge and what relief it may give. Students must understand sovereign immunity principles that ordinarily bar suits against the United States; the statutory waivers that open judicial review, most importantly provisions of the Administrative Procedure Act (APA); the role and limits of the mandamus writ under 28 U.S.C. § 1361 as an extraordinary tool to compel government action; and the narrower doctrine of ultra vires review that seeks to police agency action that exceeds statutory authority. These doctrines intersect in complex ways with statutory review schemes and equitable remedial principles. This chapter explains the governing concepts, the standards courts apply, and how procedural posture and the availability of alternative remedies shape the outcome of lawsuits against federal officers and agencies.

# Official-capacity suits and sovereign immunity

Sovereign immunity is the foundational barrier to most suits against the federal government: absent a waiver, the United States cannot be sued in its own courts. When a plaintiff names a federal official in his or her official capacity, that suit is effectively a suit against the United States because official-capacity liability runs against the officeholder’s public office and, by extension, the sovereign entity. Consequently, an official-capacity claim seeking relief that would bind the sovereign (for example, injunctive relief compelling agency action or declaratory relief that affects the government's legal obligations) ordinarily requires a waiver of sovereign immunity for the court to have power to act.

Two principal aspects of sovereign immunity are important in litigation strategy. First, subject-matter jurisdiction: sovereign immunity doctrines are not merely defenses on the merits; they are jurisdictional limitations. If immunity bars the suit, the court lacks power to adjudicate even if the plaintiff ultimately would prevail on the merits. Second, the scope of permissible relief: some waivers permit certain relief but preclude others (for example, many waivers allow injunctive or declaratory relief but not monetary damages). Thus understanding the precise contours of a statutory waiver determines both whether the claim may be heard and what remedies a court may order.

Separating official-capacity suits from individual-capacity suits is a critical early step. An official-capacity suit asks the court to bind the government or its policy; an individual-capacity suit seeks personal relief against the official (often money damages) for actions taken under color of federal law. While sovereign immunity principles control official-capacity suits, suits alleging constitutional torts against federal officers in their individual capacity (commonly associated with Bivens-type remedies) implicate different doctrines, including qualified immunity and the availability of implied causes of action for damages. This chapter focuses on official-capacity and agency-directed litigation and the waivers and doctrines that govern hearings and remedies.

# APA waiver of sovereign immunity and limits

The Administrative Procedure Act occupies a central role in judicial review of federal agency action. Two related but distinct features make the APA important in the sovereign-immunity context: (1) it permits non-monetary relief against the United States in many situations and thus functions as a partial waiver of sovereign immunity; and (2) it supplies substantive standards and procedures—such as the “arbitrary and capricious” standard and requirements for notice-and-comment rulemaking—by which courts review agency action.

The APA’s waiver language is contained in 5 U.S.C. § 702. That section states that an action seeking relief other than money damages may be brought against the United States for a violation of law by an agency or officer, and that the United States is subject to the judicial review provisions of the APA. Thus, for many plaintiffs seeking injunctive or declaratory relief, § 702 opens the courthouse doors. But several important limitations qualify this waiver.

First, the APA’s waiver is limited to non-monetary relief. The United States still usually enjoys immunity from suits seeking money damages unless another statute waives that immunity explicitly (for example, the Tucker Act or other specific damages-waiving provisions). Therefore, plaintiffs who need monetary compensation must find a separate statutory grant.

Second, a plaintiff invoking the APA must satisfy the jurisdictional predicates for APA review. Not every complaint about government action is justiciable under the APA. A threshold requirement is that the action challenged be “agency action” within the meaning of the APA and, in many contexts, that the action be “final” agency action subject to judicial review. For rulemaking or adjudicative acts, finality often means the action marks the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process and establishes rights or obligations or produces legal consequences. When the alleged agency conduct is preliminary, advisory, or discretionary in nature and produces no final legal effects, courts will often dismiss for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction under the APA.

Third, the APA incorporates important substantive exceptions to review. Two frequent bases on which courts refuse APA review are: (a) actions “committed to agency discretion by law” under 5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2); and (b) situations where a statute precludes judicial review or provides special, exclusive review procedures. The § 701(a)(2) exception is narrow on its text but has been invoked where the statute or the nature of the decision indicates that no meaningful standard exists against which a court could judge the agency’s action. Courts analyze whether there are legal standards that can be applied; if not, then the dispute may not be judicially manageable.

Fourth, even where § 702 confers jurisdiction, § 706 circumscribes the relief: courts may “compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed” and may set aside agency action found to be arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law. The scope of review under § 706 is both procedural and substantive. Courts will examine whether procedures required by statute or the APA were observed, whether the agency considered relevant factors and articulated a reasoned basis for its decision, and whether the decision falls within the bounds of reasonable policy choices. But courts also defer to agency expertise where statutory text and policy choices allow.

Procedurally, the APA’s waiver interacts with standing, ripeness, and exhaustion doctrines. A plaintiff must have a concrete and particularized injury fairly traceable to the challenged agency action to satisfy Article III standing. Ripeness doctrines assess whether the dispute is fit for judicial decision or rests on contingent future events. Some statutes and agency processes require exhaustion of administrative remedies before a court will entertain an APA suit; failure to exhaust may be a jurisdictional bar or may leave the claim premature.

Finally, the APA’s waiver does not permit judicial review when another statute provides a “special and exclusive” review scheme. Many regulatory statutes include their own judicial review provisions that narrow the scope of review, prescribe specialized procedures, or channel review to particular courts (for example, the Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit or the district courts). When Congress designs an alternative review path, courts respect that legislative choice and will often hold the APA unavailable if its invocation would undermine the statutory scheme.

# Mandamus jurisdiction (28 U.S.C. § 1361) and standards for a clear, nondiscretionary duty

The writ of mandamus is an extraordinary equitable remedy aimed at compelling a public official to perform a ministerial, non-discretionary duty that the law plainly requires. In the federal system, 28 U.S.C. § 1361 provides district courts with jurisdiction “to compel an officer or employee of the United States to perform a duty owed to the plaintiff.” But jurisdiction under § 1361 is only the starting point; to obtain mandamus relief a plaintiff must satisfy rigorous substantive and prudential conditions.

Mandamus historically requires three core showings. First, the plaintiff must demonstrate a clear right to the relief requested; second, the defendant must have a clear, nondiscretionary duty to perform the act; and third, there must be no other adequate remedy available. Courts sometimes express these elements as the requirement of (1) a clear and indisputable right, (2) a clear and legal duty on the part of the official to act, and (3) the absence of any other adequate means to attain the relief.

The “clear duty” element is central. Mandamus cannot be used to require an official to make a discretionary policy judgment; it is limited to duties that are ministerial in the technical sense or where the statute or regulation imposes a specific and mandatory command. If the statute says that the agency “may” do something, or if it vests discretion in an agency to determine scope or timing, courts will usually find mandamus unavailable because there is no nondiscretionary duty. Conversely, if the statute uses mandatory language that leaves no room for agency judgment when certain triggering conditions are met, mandamus may be appropriate.

The “no other adequate remedy” requirement is also an important gatekeeping device. When Congress has provided a statutory review scheme, or when the APA or another avenue can provide relief, courts will frequently deny mandamus as unnecessary. Mandamus is not a substitute for regular review channels; it is the remedy of last resort where other processes are unavailable or inadequate to secure prompt relief.

Courts consider prudential equitable factors in deciding whether to issue the writ. Mandamus is flexible in equitable remedies (it may take the form of an order to act), but courts consider separation-of-powers implications and the burdens of supervision on the judiciary. A court will consider whether ordering the official to act would intrude impermissibly on agency policymaking or require the court to do day-to-day oversight. Because it is an extraordinary remedy, mandamus relief is sparingly granted.

Procedural posture matters. Mandamus is often sought when an agency has failed to act (a claim of unreasonable delay) or when a plaintiff seeks to compel a discrete, ministerial act. Timing is critical: a plaintiff who seeks mandamus before exhausting administrative steps, or who seeks to use mandamus to second-guess discretionary decisions in lieu of appellate review, will generally fail. Conversely, where an agency has inexcusably refused to perform a plainly required duty and all other avenues are closed or inadequate, mandamus is a viable route.

Jurisdiction under § 1361 is sometimes misunderstood. Section 1361 gives the federal district courts competence to entertain mandamus claims but does not, on its own, provide a substantive right against the United States separate from the common-law requirements articulated above. Moreover, mandamus does not permit courts to award damages or other monetary relief on a theory that the United States is liable in tort or contract; it compels action rather than compensates for past wrongs.

# Ultra vires claims and their constraints

Ultra vires review is the judicial doctrine that allows courts to invalidate agency actions that exceed the authority delegated by Congress. Where an agency acts beyond its statutory grant of power, the action is voidable because the agency lacks jurisdiction to effectuate it. Ultra vires review goes to the limits of agency power and is doctrinally distinct from doctrines that challenge procedure or reasoned decisionmaking.

At a broad level, an ultra vires claim asserts that the agency’s action is not simply erroneous but illegal because the agency has acted outside the statutory bounds set by Congress. Common examples include promulgating a regulation without statutory authority, interpreting a statute in a way that collapses core limits on agency action, or taking an enforcement action that the agency’s enabling statute does not authorize.

Despite its apparent power, judicially enforceable ultra vires review faces several constraints. First, sovereign immunity and statutory waivers affect remedies for ultra vires claims. A plaintiff seeking to set aside agency action as ultra vires typically invokes the APA to obtain declaratory or injunctive relief under § 702 and to obtain review under § 706. Where the APA is unavailable because Congress has provided a different review scheme, plaintiffs must rely on the alternative remedies available in that scheme. In some circumstances, where a plaintiff sues a federal official in the official capacity and seeks purely prospective relief to prevent enforcement of an ultra vires rule, courts will treat the claim as subject to APA review; but when monetary relief is sought, sovereign immunity may bar relief absent a specific waiver.

Second, courts subject ultra vires claims to the same limits as other judicial review. If Congress has explicitly or implicitly precluded judicial review, then claims that an agency acted beyond its authority may be nonjusticiable. Moreover, if the alleged ultra vires act is discretionary and calls for policy balancing within the agency’s purview, courts may be reluctant to characterize it as beyond authority.

Third, separation-of-powers concerns can narrow ultra vires review. Courts are cautious about substituting their policy judgments for agency determinations in domains where Congress has delegated interpretive or discretionary authority. Ultra vires review is strongest when statutory text is clear and unambiguous about the limits of the agency’s authority; it is weaker when the statutory language is ambiguous and courts must engage in interpretive balancing.

Fourth, remedial constraints arise. When a court finds an action ultra vires, the typical remedy is to set aside or enjoin the unlawful action prospectively. But retrospective unwinding may be limited, particularly where third-party reliance or complex statutory schemes make wholesale nullification impracticable. When Congress has established a statutory review scheme, that scheme may dictate the forms and breadth of remedies even for ultra vires claims.

Finally, procedurally, plaintiffs asserting ultra vires claims must still satisfy standing, ripeness, and other jurisdictional predicates. The plaintiff must show a particularized injury traceable to the ultra vires action and that a favorable judicial decision is likely to redress the injury. Plaintiffs who ask courts to resolve abstract disputes about the limits of agency power without a concrete legal stake will be turned away.

# Overlap and distinctions among APA, mandamus, and specific statutory review schemes

Litigants confronting agency action must choose among several overlapping—but distinct—paths for judicial relief. Understanding the differences among the APA, mandamus, and statutory review schemes is vital to choosing the correct procedural route and to predicting the available remedies.

The APA provides a general, broad route to review for agency action and operates as an important waiver of sovereign immunity for non-monetary relief. When an action is “agency action” and otherwise reviewable (for example, it is final and not committed to agency discretion by law), the APA supplies standards and remedies, including injunctive and declaratory relief and the ability to set aside unlawful action. Because the APA is a general vehicle, it often serves as the go-to statute when no other special review procedure exists.

Mandamus is narrower and framed as an extraordinary equitable writ. It is not a general substitute for APA review and is rarely appropriate where the APA provides an adequate remedy. Mandamus is best deployed to compel a discrete, non-discretionary duty that an officer owes to a plaintiff and not to direct broad policy choices. In practice, then, when the underlying claim is one of unlawful agency delay or a refusal to perform an undeniable statutory duty, plaintiffs may choose between an APA action to compel agency action under § 706(1) and a mandamus action under § 1361. Courts often require plaintiffs to choose the avenue that is most appropriate to the nature of the duty and to consider whether the APA supplies an “adequate” or speedy avenue. Mandamus is often characterized as a supplemental route when the plaintiff lacks a meaningful APA remedy or where speedy judicial intervention is necessary and the duty is ministerial.

Specific statutory review schemes can displace both the APA and mandamus. Congress sometimes prescribes exclusive review routes, time bars, specialized standards of review, or particular courts with jurisdiction to resolve agency disputes. Courts interpret these statutory schemes to determine whether Congress intended to make the review exclusive; if so, the APA will be unavailable and mandamus may be inappropriate because Congress has provided a different pathway. When a statute provides a special and adequate remedy, courts ordinarily deny mandamus and invoke that special review regime. The presence of an exclusive or comprehensive statutory scheme can also narrow the available remedies: Congress may limit relief to review on the record, may require exhaustion, may preclude injunctive relief, or may vest final review in a specified appellate court.

There are also important doctrine-specific distinctions in practice. APA review focuses on agency process and the reasonableness of agency action; courts ask whether the agency complied with procedural requirements, followed reasoned decisionmaking, and acted within the scope of its legal authority. Mandamus focuses on whether the official has a clear duty to act and whether no other adequate remedy exists; it does not invite courts to substitute policy judgments for discretionary agency choices. Ultra vires claims target the legal question whether action was authorized by statute; while such claims are often pursued through the APA, they represent a distinct mode of constitutional and statutory policing of agency boundaries.

Overlap occurs frequently. A plaintiff may argue that an agency action is ultra vires (beyond authority) and therefore arbitrary and capricious under the APA; the same set of facts might support a mandamus action if the agency has failed to perform a mandatory duty and there is no adequate alternative. Courts will parse the remedies the plaintiff seeks and the statutory landscape to decide which route is appropriate. The availability of an APA remedy usually forecloses mandamus unless the APA does not provide adequate and timely relief. If Congress has provided an exclusive review path, both APA and mandamus claims may be barred or limited.

In choosing among remedies, plaintiffs must attend to procedural posture. For example, when an agency action is final, the APA typically provides a clear vehicle for review. When an agency declines to act or unreasonably delays, an APA claim to compel action or a mandamus petition may be available, but deciding which is proper depends on the nature of the duty and on statutory alternatives. When a statute prescribes a special review channel—say, a petition for reconsideration within the agency followed by review in a particular court—failure to follow that scheme may doom a federal court challenge.

Courts are sensitive to separation-of-powers considerations: they avoid granting relief that would require ongoing supervision of executive branch decisionmaking or that would substitute judicial policy preferences for congressionally delegated agency judgments. Thus, remedies are tailored: injunctions may be prospectively limited; declaratory relief confined to concrete controversies; mandamus issued only for clear ministerial duties; and ultra vires findings accompanied by remedies that respect third-party reliance and statutory remediation structures.

Conclusion

Suing federal officials and agencies requires navigating sovereign immunity, statutory waivers such as the APA, the narrow but potent remedy of mandamus, and the doctrine of ultra vires review. Each pathway has distinct jurisdictional prerequisites, elements of proof, and remedial consequences. Practitioners and litigants must analyze the nature of the challenged conduct (action versus inaction, discretionary versus ministerial), the statutory text (including any exclusive review scheme), and the procedural posture (finality, exhaustion, timeliness, and the availability of alternative remedies). Mastery of these doctrines enables a lawyer to pick the right forum and the right claim, and to forecast the likely scope of judicial relief in disputes with the federal government.
