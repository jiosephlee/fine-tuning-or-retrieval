Introduction

Removal adjudications in the United States proceed through a distinct multi-tiered administrative and judicial structure that shapes the opportunities and limits for judicial review. A noncitizen (and the government) navigate from charging and initial hearings before an Immigration Judge (IJ), to appellate review at the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), and from there (subject to statutory limits and timing rules) to the federal courts of appeals by way of a petition for review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252. Each institutional player has defined roles and constraints, and appellate review follows specific rules about the administrative record, standards of review, and the use of lower-level reasoning when the BIA issues terse rulings. This chapter lays out that institutional architecture and the doctrinal rules governing how courts of appeals approach removal decisions so that you can follow and analyze an appellate opinion with a clear sense of the underlying process.

# Roles of DHS, Immigration Judges (IJs), and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA)

The removal process begins with the Department of Homeland Security (DHS). DHS initiates proceedings by issuing a charging document—the Notice to Appear (NTA)—that alleges removability under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). DHS is the prosecuting government agency in immigration court; its attorneys present evidence of removability, oppose claims for relief, and may move to reopen or appeal adverse administrative decisions. DHS also controls custody decisions, detention, and enforcement steps separate from the adjudicative function.

The Immigration Judge is the primary factfinder within the Executive Office for Immigration Review (EOIR). At a merits hearing, the IJ receives evidence, conducts live testimony, rules on admissibility issues, and applies law to the facts. IJs decide whether the noncitizen is removable and whether the noncitizen meets eligibility and burden-of-proof requirements for relief—examples include asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture (CAT), cancellation of removal, and adjustment of status. In many cases the IJ must make detailed credibility determinations, resolve conflicts in testimony, and weigh documentary evidence. The IJ’s decision is contained in an oral disposition followed by a written decision explaining findings of fact and conclusions of law. The IJ also has authority to administratively close, grant continuances, or certify questions to the BIA in narrow circumstances.

The Board of Immigration Appeals is the principal administrative appellate body for immigration cases. It reviews IJs’ decisions for legal error and clear factual error, issues written decisions that may be published as precedents, and has the authority to dismiss, remand, or affirm in whole or in part. The BIA may act through single-member decisions (a single BIA judge), or through multi-member panels (usually three) when issuing precedential decisions or when the case is designated for panel consideration. The BIA also handles motions to reopen and reconsider, and it establishes the law of the agency through precedential decisions that bind IJs and later panels unless overruled by the Attorney General or a subsequent BIA or judicial decision.

The institutional distinction matters for appellate review: courts of appeals normally review final orders of removal issued by the BIA, not interlocutory decisions by IJs, and the nature of the BIA decision—published, unpublished, single-member, or brief affirmance—affects what reasoning is available to the reviewing court and how it evaluates legal and factual findings.

# Single-member vs. panel BIA decisions

The BIA may issue decisions through different internal processes, and the form of the decision affects its precedential force and the amount of explanation provided.

Single-member decisions are authored by one BIA member and are common. A single-member decision can resolve the case on the merits and will be binding on the parties. However, single-member decisions are not generally published as precedents unless designated by the Board. They may be brief or relatively detailed depending on the complexity of the case and the issues raised.

Panel decisions (typically three-member panels) are used when the Board wants to issue a decision with broader precedential effect or where statutory or procedural rules require panel consideration. Panel decisions are more likely to be published as precedents and have greater precedential weight within EOIR. Precedential decisions—whether from a panel or designated single-member decision—are intended to guide IJs and later BIA members on recurring legal issues.

A particular hybrid form occurs when the BIA issues a terse order: for example, a one-line affirmance without opinion or an order that adopts and affirms the IJ’s decision without additional explanation. Such "brief affirmances" conserve Board resources but raise distinct issues on judicial review because the BIA’s reasoning is minimal or absent. When the BIA issues a short order or one that adopts the IJ’s decision, courts of appeals typically look to the IJ’s written opinion to understand the factual findings and legal reasoning that actually supported the administrative outcome. How far a court can rely on IJ reasoning and which parts of the IJ record can be consulted is governed by doctrines discussed below.

# Petitions for review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252

A party seeking judicial review of a final order of removal from the BIA must file a petition for review in the appropriate United States Court of Appeals pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252. The statute establishes the exclusive avenue for judicial review of orders of removal, and it establishes basic jurisdictional rules and timing constraints. Important features include:

- Final order requirement. Generally, courts of appeals have jurisdiction only over "final orders of removal," i.e., those issued by the BIA (or by an IJ in certain circumstances where the BIA has not assumed jurisdiction). Interim or interlocutory rulings by the IJ ordinarily are not reviewable until the final administrative decision is entered.

- Timing. The statute, along with applicable federal rules, imposes strict time limits for filing a petition for review. Practically, this is often a 30-day window from the date of the BIA’s decision to file in the court of appeals (but watch the governing statute and local rules for exact timing and tolling rules).

- Scope and venue. Section 1252 also contains provisions that affect venue, consolidation, and transfer between circuits when multiple petitions involve a single final order. It further channels most challenges to BIA decisions to the courts of appeals, foreclosing direct review in district courts except in narrow constitutional due process contexts or where the statute expressly allows.

- Mandatory vs. discretionary review. Certain classes of removal orders and certain defenses (for example, some decisions involving discretionary relief or factual determinations) are described by the statute as subject to limited review, and in some areas Congress has restricted judicial interference with discretionary determinations. The statutory scheme also incorporates requirements regarding exhaustion of administrative remedies—failure to raise an issue before the BIA may forfeit that issue on petition for review.

- Interplay with motions to reopen and reconsider. Because reopening and reconsideration are brought before the BIA, many litigants file such motions after an adverse IJ decision; successful motions can moot or alter the content of a petition for review. Courts will typically require that claimants exhaust those administrative processes before obtaining judicial review, and federal courts often lack jurisdiction to review denials of motions to reopen in certain circumstances unless constitutional claims are raised.

Understanding § 1252’s jurisdictional and procedural contours is crucial because courts will dismiss petitions filed in the wrong court, filed after the deadline, or that seek review of nonfinal or nonjudicially reviewable matters.

# Record rule: 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A)

The so-called record rule is statutory: 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A) provides that a reviewing court "shall decide the petition only on the administrative record on which the order of removal is based." This rule has several practical consequences for appellate review.

First, it constrains courts to the evidence presented to the IJ and considered by the BIA; courts generally do not receive new evidence or take new testimony. The administrative record includes transcripts of hearings, documentary evidence submitted to the IJ and the BIA, and written decisions and orders. Parties should therefore ensure that all relevant evidence and arguments they wish to preserve for judicial review are placed in the administrative record.

Second, the record rule limits the development of new factual matters on appeal. Claims that depend upon evidence outside the administrative record—new affidavits, newly discovered documents, or new expert reports—cannot typically be introduced in the petition-for-review proceeding. Instead, litigants must seek to present that evidence to the BIA through a timely motion to reopen (which permits the BIA to consider new evidence under specific standards), and only after the BIA denies reopening will courts review the BIA’s denial (subject to statutory limits on judicial review of reopening decisions).

Third, the rule has narrow exceptions. Although § 1252(b)(4)(A) is broadly phrased, courts have recognized limited instances where consideration of extra-record material is appropriate—most notably for claims that require knowledge outside the administrative record to assess jurisdiction or to decide certain constitutional claims (for example, due process claims that rely on official records or proof that was not available in the proceedings). But these exceptions are applied cautiously because the statute’s clear purpose is to channel factual development to the administrative process and to prevent courts from acting as factfinders in the first instance.

In sum, the record rule places the primary burden on litigants to develop the administrative record thoroughly before the IJ and BIA, and it focuses judicial review on the administrative proceedings that produced the final order.

# Standards of review: substantial evidence for facts; de novo for legal issues

Courts of appeals apply different standards of review depending on whether the question presented is factual, legal, or discretionary. The difference is consequential: it determines whether a court will defer to the agency’s determinations or freely substitute its own judgment.

Factual findings. Findings of fact by the IJ (and by the BIA when the BIA makes its own findings) are reviewed under the "substantial evidence" standard. Under this deferential standard, a court will uphold factual determinations if they are supported by "reasonable, substantial, and probative evidence" on the record considered as a whole. Courts do not reweigh evidence or reassess credibility in place of the agency’s factfinder. For credibility determinations in particular—where the IJ has seen the witness testify and observed demeanor—review is especially deferential; appellate courts will overturn such determinations only if they are unsupported by reasonable, substantial evidence in the record.

Legal questions. Pure questions of law—statutory interpretation, questions of constitutional dimension, and the proper application of law to undisputed facts—are typically reviewed de novo. Under de novo review, the court gives no deference to the BIA’s legal conclusions and analyzes the legal issue anew. De novo review is the norm for claims that the agency interpreted the statute incorrectly or violated constitutional protections. However, the lines can blur when questions require mixed determinations of law and fact.

Mixed questions and standards interplay. When an issue requires applying a legal standard to historical facts (a mixed question), courts sometimes identify the proper standard by asking whether resolution of the question depends on factual findings that invoke the agency’s expertise. In some mixed cases, courts will review the legal component de novo but accept the factual predicate under substantial evidence. In others, the "substantial evidence" standard can control whether the legal conclusion is upheld if it depends intimately on factual determinations.

Discretionary decisions and abuse of discretion. Some matters—particularly discretionary relief determinations (e.g., whether to grant discretionary forms of relief such as certain waivers or favorable discretionary immigration relief)—are reviewed for abuse of discretion. Under this standard, a court asks whether the agency exercised its discretion in a principled way and in accordance with statutory standards, whether it considered relevant factors, and whether the decision is arbitrary or capricious. Courts respect the agency’s policy judgments in the discretionary arena but will set aside decisions that lack a rational basis, fail to consider important aspects of the problem, or rest on impermissible considerations.

Burden of proof. Standards of review interact with the substantive burdens required to obtain relief. For example, the petitioner bears the burden of proof to establish eligibility for asylum, withholding, or CAT protection. If the IJ denies relief based on the petitioner’s failure to meet the burden, a court applying substantial evidence review will sustain that denial so long as the agency’s conclusion is reasonable and supported by the record.

# Consulting IJ reasoning when the BIA issues a brief affirmance

A frequent practical issue on appeal arises when the BIA disposes of an appeal with a short order—sometimes a one-line affirmance without opinion—or when the BIA’s decision merely states that it adopts the IJ’s decision. Because the formal BIA opinion may provide little or no reasoning, courts of appeals must determine how to review the final order of removal and where to find the basis for review.

The typical approach is for the court to consult the IJ’s written decision to determine the factual findings and legal reasoning that the IJ used to reach the outcome. The IJ’s decision is part of the administrative record, and when the BIA affirms without issuing its own opinion, the IJ’s reasoning is often the only articulation of the agency’s basis for the final order. Under the record rule, the reviewing court may therefore consider the IJ’s decision as part of the record and evaluate whether that reasoning supports the BIA’s affirmance.

When doing so, the court must be careful to distinguish between reasoning that the IJ actually relied on and matters that are not adequately developed in the written IJ record. The court should not invent facts or reasoning gaps to substitute its own factfinding. Instead, the court asks whether the IJ’s expressed findings and analysis would support the BIA’s outcome if the BIA is understood to have adopted them. If the IJ’s decision is internally inconsistent, terse, or fails to address an argument that was presented to the BIA, the appellate court may remand for clarification or further explanation. In some cases, the court may treat the BIA’s brief affirmance as an implicit adoption of the IJ’s reasoning but still exercise de novo review for legal questions and substantial evidence review for facts as applied.

Courts also recognize limits on borrowing IJ reasoning. If the BIA explicitly rejects or gives different reasons for the agency’s action, the IJ’s rationale cannot be used to bootstrap the BIA’s decision. And if the IJ did not address a particular legal issue that the BIA resolved, the court cannot manufacture IJ reasoning to justify the BIA’s resolution. When the BIA’s order is devoid of reasoning and the IJ’s decision is insufficient to permit meaningful review, remand to the BIA for a fuller explanation is often required to protect the petitioner's right to reasoned administrative action and to permit effective judicial review.

# Relief-specific consolidation in final order review

Orders of removal often involve multiple claims and multiple forms of relief: eligibility for asylum, withholding of removal, relief under CAT, adjustment of status considerations, waiver applications, and statutory bars such as persecution-based grounds or criminal-ineligibility findings. The structure of review—both administratively at the BIA and judicially at the courts of appeals—requires attention to how these claims are raised, decided, and preserved.

Final order review is petition-specific and relief-specific in several ways. First, a petition for review challenges a final order of removal as to particular rulings; appellate jurisdiction is tied to the finality and specificity of the challenged aspects of the order. If multiple petitions are filed in different circuits challenging parts of the same final order, statutory provisions govern consolidation and transfer to a single circuit to avoid conflicting decisions. Parties and courts should therefore coordinate filings and be mindful of venue rules and the consequences of multiple petitions.

Second, appellate review is relief-specific in the sense that courts consider the specific relief requested and the statutory standards for that relief. For instance, review of an IJ’s denial of asylum focuses on establishing a "well-founded fear" of persecution, whereas withholding of removal involves a higher standard—clear probability or more likely than not—and CAT protection requires a likelihood of torture by or with government acquiescence. Because each form of relief has distinct elements and burdens, a court’s review must address the particular legal standards and the factual predicates relevant to each relief type. A court might affirm denial of one form of relief while remanding or reversing as to another, creating nuanced resolutions within a single final order.

Third, consolidation doctrines appear most centrally when multiple claims, motions, or petitions affect the same final order or when relief depends on one another (e.g., where eligibility for one form of relief would make another unnecessary). Courts are attentive to whether issues were presented to the agency and whether they are ripe for review. If a particular claim was not raised before the BIA or the IJ, appellate courts may consider it forfeited. Similarly, claims that depend on a grant of a discretionary form of relief that the IJ denied may be inchoate and require remand to the BIA to exercise the discretion in light of any legal errors identified.

Finally, when several petitions implicate different petitions for review that challenge discrete portions of a final removal order (for example, separate petitions filed by co-applicants), courts may consolidate those petitions for efficiency and consistency when appropriate. Statutory provisions govern where consolidation is mandatory or discretionary and how conflicting circuit petitions are resolved to prevent inconsistent nationwide application of immigration law.

Practical consequence: litigants need to be strategic and precise in identifying which parts of the final order they challenge, ensure issues are properly exhausted at the agency level, and recognize that appellate courts will parse each form of relief under its governing statutory standard and may resolve different relief claims in different ways within the same final order.

Conclusion

Understanding the institutional roles of DHS, IJs, and the BIA, and the procedural path from administrative adjudication to judicial review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, is essential to analyzing appellate opinions in immigration matters. The record rule compels careful administrative development of the record, the distinction between BIA single-member and panel decisions shapes the precedential landscape, and the divergent standards of review determine how much deference a court gives to the agency’s factual findings and legal conclusions. When the BIA issues a brief affirmance, courts look to the IJ’s reasoning—but only within the constraints of the record and the limits of appellate review. Finally, because removal orders often involve multiple forms of relief, relief-specific analysis and consolidation principles govern how courts address the individual claims within a final order. Mastery of these procedural and doctrinal elements will allow you to read and evaluate appellate decisions with clarity about what the court is doing and why.

Introduction

Asylum and withholding of removal are principal forms of international-protection relief in U.S. immigration law. Both seek to prevent return of individuals to countries where they face harm on account of protected characteristics. But they differ in statutory basis, substantive standards, burden of proof, consequences of adjudication, and the role of agency discretion. Mastery of the statutory definitions, the five protected grounds, the standards that govern risk and credibility assessments, derivative-status rules, and the interplay with Convention Against Torture (CAT) protection is essential before reviewing judicial opinions in this area. This chapter sets out the core doctrinal framework: the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) definitions and provisions that create asylum and withholding relief, the regulatory contours developed by the Department of Homeland Security and the former Immigration and Naturalization Service (INS), the legal tests courts use to analyze claims, and the major distinctions among asylum, withholding, and CAT protection.

# INA definition of refugee: 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A)

The INA defines "refugee" in 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A). Although this statute is short, it encapsulates the central elements that asylum applicants must establish. A "refugee" is, with limited exceptions, any person who is outside any country of such person’s nationality (or, if stateless, outside any country in which the person last habitually resided) and is "unable or unwilling to return" because of "persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution" on account of one of five protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion.

Understanding this provision requires unpacking four components:

1. Geographic/status predicates. The statute speaks to persons outside their country of nationality (or last habitual residence for stateless persons). This reflects the historical focus of refugee law on cross-border flight and excludes, for example, claims by persons who seek protection while remaining in their country.

2. Persecution or well-founded fear. The statute requires either past persecution or a well-founded fear of future persecution. Courts distinguish past persecution (which creates a rebuttable presumption of future persecution) from a well-founded fear standard for future risk. What constitutes "persecution" and how "well-founded fear" is demonstrated are central legal debates.

3. Nexus to a protected ground. The feared persecution must be "on account of" one or more of the enumerated grounds. This element—often called "nexus"—requires either that the persecutor acted because of the applicant's protected characteristic or that the persecutor imputed such a characteristic to the applicant. The causation standard can be formulated variably as "one central reason" or "a reason" depending on statute, regulation, and binding precedent; the precise formulation matters in adjudication.

4. Exceptions and bars. The statutory refugee definition is subject to exclusions, such as involvement in serious nonpolitical crimes, participation in persecutory activities, or national security grounds. These exclusions and later statutory bars can remove otherwise eligible claimants from relief.

The INA definition thus sets the substantive baseline: asylum claims seek relief for refugees as defined by § 1101(a)(42)(A), while the categories and standards described there animate all subsequent analysis.

# Asylum eligibility and discretion: 8 U.S.C. § 1158

Asylum in the United States is governed primarily by 8 U.S.C. § 1158. Section 1158(a) creates a statutory right to apply for asylum, authorizing the Attorney General (now the Secretary of Homeland Security and the Secretary of State, with some functions delegated) to grant asylum to eligible applicants. There are several key features to understand.

Eligibility elements. To be eligible for asylum, an applicant must meet the refugee definition: be outside their country, be unable/unwilling to return, and show past persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution because of one of the protected grounds. Asylum applicants ordinarily must file within one year of arrival to the United States, though statutory exceptions can excuse late filing for changed circumstances materially affecting eligibility or extraordinary circumstances related to the delay.

Discretionary nature. A fundamental characteristic of asylum under § 1158 is its discretionary character. Even where an applicant meets the statutory criteria for refugee status, the agency has discretion to grant or deny asylum. The statute directs consideration of various positive and negative factors; for example, the persecution suffered, family ties, age, health, humanitarian concerns, and evidence of rehabilitation may counsel in favor, while serious criminal history, national security concerns, or persecution of others weigh against a grant. Because asylum is discretionary, an applicant who proves eligibility might nevertheless be denied asylum and instead may seek other forms of relief or protection.

Mandatory referral to removal proceedings and adjudicatory settings. The asylum application process occurs in two principal procedural settings. First, affirmative asylum applicants make their claim to U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) outside removal proceedings; adjudicators have authority to grant asylum. If USCIS does not grant asylum, the applicant may be placed into removal proceedings, where they may renew the claim as a defensive application before an immigration judge. Second, noncitizens placed into removal proceedings may raise defensive asylum claims before the Immigration Court. Immigration judges may grant asylum, but the agency retains discretionary control, and adverse decisions can be appealed to the Board of Immigration Appeals and, ultimately, to federal courts.

Burden and evidence. The applicant bears the burden of proof to demonstrate eligibility for asylum under the INA and relevant regulations. Given discretion, however, an applicant may also argue that a grant of asylum is warranted in the exercise of favorable discretion even if the facts are borderline. Adjudicators evaluate testimonial credibility, documentary corroboration, and objective country conditions evidence.

One-year filing deadline and forfeiture. Section 1158(a)(2) establishes a one-year filing deadline with exceptions. Failure to file within one year generally bars asylum unless the applicant shows changed or extraordinary circumstances. This procedural rule is significant because it is often dispositive without reaching substantive eligibility.

Relationship to withholding and CAT. Section 1158 is distinct from withholding of removal under § 1231(b)(3) and CAT protection, both of which have different standards and relief consequences. Asylum confers more benefits (e.g., eligibility for lawful permanent residence after one year), but it is discretionary and thus less certain.

# Protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, particular social group, political opinion

The asylum statute enumerates five protected grounds: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group (PSG), and political opinion. For an asylum or withholding claim to succeed, the applicant must show that the persecution was "on account of" one or more of these grounds. Courts analyze each ground separately even though many cases involve mixed motives or overlapping characteristics. Several general doctrinal points apply across the grounds.

Nexus and causation. The central requirement is nexus: the persecutor’s motivation must be connected to the protected characteristic. Courts typically require that the protected ground be at least one central reason for the persecution. Courts also recognize that persecution can be motivated by multiple reasons; an applicant need not show that the protected ground was the sole reason. Where persecutors impute a protected characteristic to an applicant, nexus may still be satisfied. The evidentiary showing can be direct (statements, written orders, targeting) or circumstantial (pattern of targeting against members of the group).

Race, religion, and nationality. These three grounds are often the most straightforward to identify. Race and nationality often overlap; nationality includes both citizenship/ethnic origin and, in some circuits, language or national origin. Religion is broadly construed to include not only mainstream faiths but also smaller or nontraditional religious beliefs and practices, and courts assess whether the persecutor’s hostility stems from the applicant’s religious belief or practice. Persecution for apostasy or conversion is a common religious-ground issue where imputed beliefs and credibility of practice are often contested.

Political opinion. Political-opinion claims typically arise where the applicant opposes government policies, supports particular political actors, participates in opposition activities, or is perceived as holding a political view. Political opinion can be actual or imputed; the persecutor might punish the applicant because they believe the applicant holds particular political views. Courts analyze whether the political opinion is coherent, whether the persecutor targets the applicant for those views, and whether the opinion is protected in the sense relevant to international refugee law (i.e., the opinion is not a pretext for other motives).

Particular social group (PSG). PSG is the most litigated and conceptually complex ground. The INA does not define PSG, but jurisprudence has developed tests to identify such groups. The defining features generally include:

- Social distinctiveness or visibility: Some circuits require that the group be socially distinct within the relevant society; others require only that the group be perceived as a group. "Social visibility" formulations historically focused on whether neighbors would recognize group members, but courts have shifted toward "social perception" and "immutability" tests.
- Immutability or fundamental characteristic: Members share an immutable characteristic (sex, past experience) or a fundamental characteristic that is so central to identity or conscience that the person should not be forced to change it.
- Particularity: The group must be discrete as a group; this limits overly broad groups like "all women" or "opposition supporters" unless sufficiently particularized.

Different circuits apply different permutations or emphases of these elements. Adjudicators examine whether the asserted group is defined with sufficient clarity and whether societal attitudes are such that the group is recognized and targeted.

Intersection of grounds and composite grounds. Applicants frequently assert multiple grounds—race plus PSG, religion plus political opinion, etc. Courts evaluate whether at least one ground independently supports asylum. When persecution is motivated by a combination of factors, the "on account of" inquiry looks to whether the protected ground was a central reason.

Persecution definition and private actors. Whether an act constitutes persecution (as opposed to non-protected criminal or private violence) is substantive, and courts examine the severity and cumulative effect of harms. Persecution often entails threats to life, freedom, or liberty, serious physical or mental harm, or severe economic deprivation connected to a protected ground. When private actors perpetrate the harm, asylum claims require that the state is unwilling or unable to control them, or that the state is the persecutor, or that the private actor acts with the acquiescence of the state.

Common issues with protected grounds include imputed beliefs (persecutor attributes a political view or social-group membership to the applicant), internal opposition factions (whether membership in an unorganized or amorphous opposition qualifies), and groups defined by behavior (e.g., sexual orientation and gender identity), where courts have moved toward recognizing such classes as PSGs when they meet the immutability/particularity/social perception tests.

# Withholding of removal: 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3); 8 C.F.R. § 1208.16(b)

Withholding of removal is a form of mandatory non-refoulement protection. The primary statutory provision for withholding is 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3), which bars removal to a country "if the alien's life or freedom would be threatened" on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. The implementing regulation 8 C.F.R. § 1208.16(b) sets forth the agency's regulatory articulation of this standard.

Key distinctions from asylum. The most important features that distinguish withholding from asylum are:

1. Standard of proof. Withholding requires a higher standard: the applicant must show a "clear probability" or more-likely-than-not (i.e., >50% likelihood) that their life or freedom would be threatened upon return. Asylum uses the lower "well-founded fear" standard.

2. Mandatory protection. If an applicant meets the withholding standard, the government cannot remove the applicant to the feared country. The protection is mandatory (subject to statutory bars) rather than discretionary—meaning that a finding of eligibility compels relief.

3. Narrower benefits. Withholding confers a narrower remedy: it prevents removal to the country of risk but does not provide a pathway to permanent residence or the broader benefits that asylum confers. Withholding recipients remain in a more precarious status.

4. Clearance from discretionary factors. Because withholding is mandatory, discretionary adverse factors that might lead to denial of asylum cannot be used as a basis to deny withholding, although statutory bars (e.g., serious nonpolitical crime, terrorist activities) preclude withholding as well.

Elements and application. To obtain withholding, the applicant must show that, if returned, it is more likely than not that the persecutor would threaten the applicant's life or freedom on account of a protected ground. Courts and adjudicators consider the same nexus and persecution elements that apply in asylum claims, but the required likelihood is higher. The evaluation includes credibility assessments, corroboration, and country conditions. Where an applicant has already experienced past persecution, that evidence can be used to infer future risk; courts often treat a well-founded fear as a lower threshold that does not automatically satisfy the withholding standard.

State actor and inability/unwillingness. As with asylum, persecution by private actors can ground withholding only if the state is unwilling or unable to control the private perpetrators or if the state itself is the persecutor. The analysis of government acquiescence or inability to protect is often fact-intensive.

Statutory and regulatory bars. Withholding is precluded by the same statutory disqualifiers that exclude refugees, such as having committed a serious nonpolitical crime or being a danger to the community. Additionally, while discretionary grounds cannot be invoked to deny mandatory withholding, public-safety exclusions are strictly applied.

Procedural posture and deportation consequences. Because withholding is raised defensively in removal proceedings, a successful claim prevents the respondent’s removal to the specific country but does not necessarily bar removal to a safe third country. Moreover, the protective order under withholding is country-specific, so changes in country conditions or travel to the country may affect the protection.

# Standards of proof: well-founded fear vs. more-likely-than-not

A cornerstone of asylum and withholding law is the distinction between the two standards of proof: the well-founded fear standard for asylum and the more-likely-than-not (or clear probability) standard for withholding.

Well-founded fear (asylum). The "well-founded fear of persecution" standard is generally understood as a hybrid standard combining subjectivity and objectivity. The applicant must genuinely fear persecution (subjective component), and that fear must be objectively reasonable. Courts sometimes articulate the standard numerically—some find that a showing of a 10% chance of persecution could suffice for well-founded fear—though courts avoid pinning a precise numeric threshold. Because this is lower than the withholding standard, an applicant can satisfy asylum but not withholding.

The well-founded fear inquiry often proceeds in stages. First, is the applicant credible and sincere in their fear? Second, is the fear objectively reasonable given past incidents and country conditions? Third, do any internal-relocation possibilities or state protection options defeat the reasonableness of the fear? Fourth, do any statutory bars remove eligibility?

More-likely-than-not/clear probability (withholding). Withholding requires a showing that it is more likely than not that the applicant’s life or freedom would be threatened upon return—that is, a probability greater than 50%. This is a significantly higher burden. Courts often refer to it as "clear probability." Proof of past persecution may establish a presumption of future persecution, but the presumption can be rebutted by evidence of changed country conditions or internal relocation possibilities.

Adjudicative consequences of different standards. Because of the different standards, an applicant whose testimony and corroboration barely clear the lower standard may be granted asylum but still be removable if the higher withholding standard is not met. Conversely, a showing that meets withholding necessarily meets the asylum threshold. The procedural consequences are important: asylum is discretionary, withholding is mandatory. An applicant capable of meeting the withholding standard can compel non-refoulement, but only asylum affords broader immigration benefits.

Burden of proof. The applicant bears the burden of proof for both forms of relief, and credibility determinations are crucial. Where an applicant’s testimony is credible and persuasive, corroborative documentary evidence and country conditions reports strengthen the case. Where credibility problems arise, failure to corroborate can be dispositive.

# Derivative status in asylum and non-derivative nature of withholding

Derivative status refers to the possibility that family members of a principal beneficiary may acquire protection as a consequence of the principal’s grant. The rules governing derivative status differ between asylum and withholding.

Asylum—derivative status. Asylum has an established derivative component. Spouses and unmarried children under a statutory age (generally under 21) are eligible to derive asylum status from a principal grant of asylum. If the principal applicant’s asylum application is granted, eligible family members may be included in the grant either in the original application or for a period after the grant, subject to regulatory time limits and procedures. Derivative status for asylees is rooted in the humanitarian and family-reunification purposes of asylum.

Derivative status can also be granted to family members who are in the United States at the time of the principal’s grant, within certain timeframes, and sometimes may be applied at consular processing for refugees abroad (refugee status is the overseas counterpart to asylum in-country). There are procedural requirements for adding derivatively eligible family members, and late claims may be subject to limitations.

Withholding—non-derivative, case-by-case. Withholding of removal is not derivative in the same statutory sense. Withholding decisions focus on the individual applicant’s risk. Family members do not automatically derive withholding from a principal beneficiary. Instead, each person seeking withholding is supposed to establish their own entitlement—i.e., that it is more likely than not they would be persecuted upon return on account of a protected ground. In practice, family members who apply for withholding often present similar country conditions and family-targeting evidence that supports separate grants, but the statutory framework does not create automatic derivation.

Practical effects. The absence of derivative withholding can create hardship where a principal beneficiary qualifies for withholding but family members do not independently meet the higher standard. Moreover, asylum’s derivative provisions facilitate family cohesion and long-term residency options that withholding does not. Therefore, applicants often seek asylum where possible, given these collateral benefits.

# Overview of CAT protection and its distinction from asylum/withholding

The Convention Against Torture and Other Cruel, Inhuman or Degrading Treatment or Punishment (CAT) provides a distinct, complementary protection regime in U.S. immigration law. The implementing regulation is 8 C.F.R. § 1208.16(c), and the governing statute derives from obligations under CAT implemented through domestic administrative procedures and immigration law. CAT protection differs from asylum and withholding in several fundamental ways.

Substantive standard. CAT protection is triggered when the applicant demonstrates that it is more likely than not that, if removed to the proposed country of removal, the applicant would be tortured by or with the acquiescence or consent of a public official. The standard—more-likely-than-not—is the same quantitative standard as for withholding, but the substantive type of harm at issue is "torture" rather than "persecution" or threats to life or freedom.

Definition of torture. The CAT defines torture as severe pain or suffering, whether physical or mental, inflicted intentionally for purposes such as obtaining information, punishment, intimidation, coercion, or discrimination, and inflicted by or with the consent or acquiescence of a public official. The key elements include (1) the severity of the harm, (2) intent, (3) purpose, and (4) the role of state actors or their acquiescence. The focus on intent and purpose, and the element of state involvement or acquiescence, distinguishes CAT claims from many asylum claims.

No protected-ground requirement. Unlike asylum and withholding, CAT protection does not require nexus to a protected ground. The feared torture need not be "on account of" race, religion, nationality, PSG, or political opinion. This absence of a nexus requirement broadens the class of individuals who may obtain protection under CAT. For example, someone who would be tortured because of criminal suspicion or unpaid debt might qualify under CAT even if no asylum-protected motive exists.

Non-refoulement, mandatory protection, and non-derivative nature. CAT protection is a form of non-refoulement: if an applicant meets CAT’s standard, removal to the country of risk is barred. Like withholding, CAT protection is mandatory, not discretionary. Also like withholding, CAT protection is non-derivative: each person must individually establish the likelihood of torture upon return.

State acquiescence and private actors. Because CAT requires that the torture be inflicted by or with the consent or acquiescence of a public official, claims against private actors require showing that public officials were complicit, willfully blind, or knowingly acquiescent in the torture. Thus, proof that the state would not intervene to stop torture by private actors may suffice to show acquiescence in some circumstances.

Interaction with asylum and withholding. Practically, applicants often plead relief under all three regimes—asylum, withholding, and CAT—because the standards and protections differ. An applicant could fail to meet the asylum or withholding standards but still qualify for CAT protection, or vice versa. Adjudicators evaluate each claim independently. However, statutory bars (such as the torture exclusion for perpetrators of severe crimes) can preclude CAT relief, and the discretionary factors relevant to asylum do not affect CAT determinations.

Procedural considerations. CAT claims are raised in removal proceedings or before USCIS (in affirmative settings), and applicants bear the burden of proof. Credibility and corroboration standards apply, and country conditions evidence about the prevalence of torture and state practices is often central.

Conclusion

Asylum, withholding of removal, and CAT protection form a tripartite framework for non-refoulement and humanitarian protection in U.S. immigration law. The INA’s refugee definition in 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42)(A) establishes the core criteria—persecution or a well-founded fear on account of protected grounds—while 8 U.S.C. § 1158 implements asylum as a discretionary grant with filing deadlines and procedural rules. Withholding under 8 U.S.C. § 1231(b)(3) and 8 C.F.R. § 1208.16(b) provides mandatory protection under a higher "more-likely-than-not" standard but without asylum’s benefits or derivative features. CAT protection, governed by implementing regulations, addresses a distinct harm—torture—without requiring a protected-ground nexus. Across all forms of relief, courts and adjudicators grapple with issues of nexus, the definition and proof of persecution or torture, the role of state actors, internal relocation, credibility, corroboration, and statutory bars. Understanding these doctrinal building blocks prepares students to analyze how courts apply the law to contested facts and how procedural posture shapes relief-seekers’ rights.

Introduction

In asylum and related forms of international relief, the concept of "persecution" serves as a legal and practical gatekeeper: it distinguishes mistreatment that triggers refugee protection from harms that, while serious, do not. Understanding how courts and immigration adjudicators identify persecution requires attention to not only the nature of single incidents but also how multiple harms, patterns of conduct, context, and consequences accumulate. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework used to evaluate whether conduct rises to persecution, how adjudicators aggregate multiple incidents, the different forms of harm (physical, custodial, property, coercive, psychological), evidentiary approaches to nonphysical injury, the conditions in which threats-only claims may suffice, and the standard for persecution by private actors. Throughout, emphasis is placed on the analytical steps courts take, the relevant burdens of proof, and how procedural posture (e.g., claims of past persecution vs. forward-looking claims) affects the legal analysis.

# Persecution vs. harassment: severity, repetition, and context

The line between harassment and persecution is one of degree and context. Harassment generally denotes mistreatment that is hostile or discriminatory but is either minor in severity or isolated in occurrence; persecution, by contrast, is mistreatment of sufficient seriousness, scope, or cumulative effect to constitute a fundamental impairment of basic human rights or to impose substantial harm or suffering.

Severity is the first and most obvious metric. Severe physical injury (e.g., serious beatings, torture), prolonged detention under inhumane conditions, or deprivation of fundamental necessities will typically be treated as persecution. At the other end, a single, brief insult, a one-time minor beating, or isolated vandalism without significant consequences is more readily characterized as harassment. But severity is not a purely quantitative inquiry; courts consider the quality of harm: the intentionality, the cruelty, and whether the harm was degrading or humiliating in a manner that impairs identity, dignity, or liberty.

Repetition matters because repetitive conduct can transform otherwise nonpersecutory acts into persecution. A single episode of low-level violence might not be persecution, but repeated episodes with similar motives, each imposing psychological or physical tolls, may, when viewed as a pattern, amount to persecution. Repetition is relevant both to showing sustained targeting and to demonstrating cumulative impact.

Context is the connective tissue. The same act may be harassment in one context and persecution in another. Contextual factors include the political and social climate (e.g., whether the acts occur within a broader campaign against a group), the persecutor’s purpose (whether the conduct seeks to coerce, punish, or destroy a protected attribute), the victim’s vulnerability (e.g., inability to seek protection), and temporal context (e.g., during armed conflict, authoritarian crackdowns). Courts examine whether the conduct is targeted at a protected characteristic and whether it is part of a broader, systematic pattern that aims to suppress membership in a social, political, or religious group.

In practice, adjudicators make a fact-specific judgment. They assess whether the harm is so serious, frequent, or systematic that it rises above ordinary harassment and fits within the legal meaning of persecution used in asylum, withholding, or nonrefoulement frameworks.

# Cumulative evaluation of multiple incidents

Because persecution often results from a sequence of abuses rather than a single dramatic event, adjudicators routinely use a cumulative-harm approach. This approach asks whether the aggregate effect of multiple incidents—considered together in their temporal and factual context—amounts to persecution, even if no single incident would independently suffice.

The cumulative analysis generally follows these steps. First, identify and describe each incident with specificity: date, nature of conduct, physical and emotional consequences, perpetrators, and immediate context. Second, assess the quality and severity of each incident. Third, evaluate patterns and relationships among incidents: are they connected by common perpetrators, shared motives (e.g., targeting on account of a protected ground), similar methods, or increasing intensity? Fourth, consider the temporal dimension—whether incidents are close in time, reprisal-based, or spread across years—and whether they produce lingering effects. Finally, determine whether the combined impact is qualitatively equivalent to persecution—that is, whether the sum of harms results in serious impairment of fundamental rights or well-being.

Courts and adjudicators often emphasize that the cumulative approach does not permit counting trivial events to manufacture persecution. The incidents must be credible, corroborated where feasible, and demonstrably linked such that their aggregate effect reaches the level of severity required. Evidence that supports cumulative impact includes injury records, medical and psychological evaluations, consistent testimony describing progressive harm, contemporaneous reports or complaints, and third-party evidence of a continuing pattern against similarly situated persons.

Procedurally, a finding of past persecution based on cumulative harms has important consequences: it shifts burdens in subsequent analysis. Proof of past persecution creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution, alleviating the need to prove a specific future threat in some contexts. Thus, meticulous presentation of cumulative harms in the record is often decisive in claims that rely on past mistreatment to establish entitlement to protection.

# Physical harm, detention, property harm, coercion

Persecution manifests through various concrete forms of mistreatment. Four recurrent categories—physical harm, detention, property harm, and coercion—often appear individually or together in persecution claims. Adjudicators assess each category for intensity, duration, purpose, and consequences.

Physical harm: Severe physical violence—such as sustained beatings, disfiguring assaults, sexual violence, torture, or attempted killing—commonly meets the persecution threshold. Considerations include the nature of injuries, need for medical treatment, evidence of deliberate infliction, and whether force was used to intimidate or to punish. Lesser physical harms may rise to persecution when repeated or when combined with other abuses that create pervasive danger or disability.

Detention: Deprivation of liberty becomes persecution when it crosses thresholds of unlawfulness, inhumanity, or prolonged duration. Short, lawful, and humane arrests for legitimate criminal conduct are not persecution. Detention that is arbitrary, accompanied by torture or cruel conditions, prolonged without due process, or used as a means of political repression or coercion is likely to be persecutory. Conditions within detention matter: overcrowding, denial of medical care, torture by guards, and lack of legal recourse can increase the severity of detention to the level of persecution.

Property harm: Forced seizure, destruction, or significant deprivation of property can amount to persecution if it undermines the individual's economic survival, is motivated by a protected ground, and is part of a broader scheme to punish, coerce, or expel. Isolated acts of vandalism that cause minimal loss may be harassment, but systematic dispossession, expropriation, or repeated arson against a targeted group can be persecutory, especially when paired with threats or physical violence.

Coercion: Coercive acts that compel behavior—such as forced recruitment into armed groups, coerced conversion, or compelled political support—can constitute persecution when they inflict severe consequences for noncompliance or are implemented through threats, violence, or legal penalties. The coercive purpose and the degree to which the acts invade autonomy and fundamental rights are central. Coercion that results in loss of liberty, bodily injury, or destruction of livelihood is more likely persuasive as persecution.

In each of these categories, courts examine intent and motive: whether the mistreatment was inflicted because of the victim’s political opinion, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or other protected attribute. The motive links the harm to the statutory grounds for asylum or withholding and separates persecution grounded in protected reasons from other forms of criminality or interpersonal animus.

# Psychological and emotional harm: evidentiary considerations

Psychological and emotional harms—such as chronic anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and sustained humiliation—can contribute to a finding of persecution, particularly when they are severe and debilitating. Because such harms are often invisible and difficult to quantify, courts require careful evidence to ensure that claims rest on reliable proof rather than subjective assertions.

Evidentiary approaches begin with credible, detailed testimony describing the nature, frequency, and consequences of the events that produced the psychological injury. Corroborating evidence is important: medical records, psychiatric evaluations, treatment histories, prescriptions, and contemporaneous counseling notes can establish the existence, diagnosis, and severity of mental health disorders. Expert reports from mental health professionals who have evaluated the claimant—explaining diagnosis, causal connection between events and symptoms, treatment needs, and functional limitations—carry significant probative value.

Adjudicators also weigh the plausibility and consistency of the psychological symptoms with the claimed traumatic events. Symptoms that align with recognized clinical syndromes (e.g., PTSD symptoms such as intrusive recollections, hypervigilance, nightmares, and avoidance) strengthen the linkage. Documentation of impaired daily functioning—difficulty maintaining employment, social withdrawal, inability to care for oneself or family, and ongoing need for therapy—helps demonstrate severity.

Where direct medical evidence is unavailable, an applicant may support psychological harm claims through reliable third-party accounts, such as testimony from family members, social workers, human rights organizations, or clergy who have observed changes in behavior. Objective country condition reports showing the unavailability of mental health services or stigmatization of mental illness can be relevant to explain gaps in formal documentation.

Courts exercise caution against overreliance on psychological evidence divorced from corroborated material facts. The causal chain between alleged events and diagnosed conditions must be clear; speculative or conclusory expert opinions that ignore alternative causes or fail to substantiate causal links are less persuasive. Moreover, adjudicators consider whether the psychological harm, combined with other harms, suffices to reach the persecution threshold under the standard applicable to the form of relief sought.

# Threats-only claims and when threats may suffice

Threats, in themselves, can constitute persecution if they meet criteria of specificity, gravity, immediacy, and corroboration. A threats-only claim presents particular challenges because there may be no physical injury to demonstrate; therefore, adjudicators closely scrutinize the content of threats and surrounding circumstances.

Specificity refers to whether the threats identify the victim or a class of victims, spell out concrete acts (e.g., "we will kill you on X date if you do not leave"), or otherwise convey a clear and particularized intent to harm. Vague or general expressions of hostility typically do not meet the threshold.

Gravity captures the seriousness of the threatened harm. Threats of severe violence, torture, sexual assault, or death are more likely to qualify as persecution than threats of minor assault or insults. Threats that would lead a reasonable person to anticipate severe injury are materially different from abusive rhetoric.

Immediacy demands a credible likelihood that the threatened violence will occur in the near term unless the applicant avoids or submits to the demand. Temporally remote or conditional threats (e.g., "someday we will get you") are less persuasive. Evidence that threats have been acted upon in the community—e.g., similar threats followed by attacks on others—supports immediacy.

Corroboration is crucial. Threats documented in police reports, witness statements, contemporaneous written communications (letters, emails, messages), and independent country condition reports showing patterns of intimidation lend weight. Where threats arise from specific incidents (e.g., prior attempts), an adjudicator will consider whether the threats were followed by attempts to carry them out or by escalating conduct.

The aggregate question is whether a reasonable person in the applicant's position would have a well-founded fear or whether the threatened harm, considered with other conduct, constitutes past persecution. Threats may suffice in two ways. First, particularly severe and imminent threats, even if unaccompanied by prior physical violence, can be sufficiently severe to be persecution. Second, threats can augment other harms—transforming a series of nonsevere incidents into persecution when the threats create pervasive fear, constrain movement, and impair fundamental rights.

Finally, in assessing threats-only claims, adjudicators also examine whether the applicant had any realistic ability to obtain state protection. If the state was able and willing to protect and the applicant could have sought and obtained protection, the threats are less likely to give rise to persecution. Conversely, threats by perpetrators who act with impunity or with state acquiescence increase the likelihood that threats amount to persecution.

# Private-actor persecution: threshold showing

Persecution need not be committed by state actors. Conduct by private actors can constitute persecution when there is a connection between the private harm and a protected ground and the state is unable or unwilling to provide protection. The analysis requires two linked showings: (1) that the private actor’s conduct rises to the level of persecution, and (2) that the state failed to control or respond adequately to allow protection.

First, the private actor’s conduct must meet the same substantive standards as state persecution: it must be sufficiently severe, targeted, and motivated (at least in substantial part) by a protected characteristic. Isolated criminality or interpersonal disputes are typically not persecution unless they are targeted at the claimant for reasons that implicate a protected ground. Evidence of pattern, motive, contemporaneous statements by perpetrators demonstrating targeting, or attacks on multiple members of a protected group supports the requisite nexus.

Second, applicants must show that the state was unwilling or unable to protect them. This element can be satisfied by evidence of state acquiescence in the persecution, failure to investigate or prosecute known perpetrators, or structural incapacity of the state to offer protection (e.g., corrupt or minimal policing, absence of legal recourse). The degree of state involvement required varies by context, but courts generally require more than a single instance of police inaction; they look for evidence that the state did not take reasonable steps to control the persecutory behavior once it was aware of the threat or that official actors condoned or participated in the abuse.

Acquiescence can be demonstrated by: official statements endorsing the harassment, systematic failure to enforce law against perpetrators of violence against the claimant’s group, or direct participation by state officials. Unwillingness may be inferred from consistent failure to investigate complaints, from documented patterns of impunity, or from credible reports showing state tolerance of group-targeted violence.

Where private actors are the perpetrators, courts also examine whether the claimant could have relocated internally to avoid harm and whether internal protection was realistically available and effective. If safe internal relocation existed and would have sufficed to avoid persecution, the inability to seek international protection may be weakened; conversely, where state protection is ineffective or relocation is unreasonable, private-actor persecution claims are stronger.

Conclusion

Discerning persecution requires a careful, evidence-based synthesis: parsing the severity, repetition, and context of harms; aggregating multiple incidents to assess cumulative impact; evaluating physical, custodial, property, coercive, and psychological harms; determining when threats alone suffice; and establishing the role of private actors and the state’s capacity or willingness to protect. Procedurally, claims based on past persecution can create presumptions about future risk, shifting evidentiary burdens; threats-only and private-actor claims often demand more robust corroboration and analysis of state response. A methodical, fact-specific approach—grounded in credible testimony, corroborating documentation, and attention to motive and context—is essential to distinguish persecution from harassment and to ensure that protection is extended to those who truly face severe, targeted abuses.

Introduction

A central requirement in asylum, withholding of removal, and Convention Against Torture (CAT) analyses is that the harm feared or suffered be "on account of" a protected ground. Without a sufficient connection—commonly called "nexus"—between the persecution and one of the statutory protected grounds (race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion), relief is not available under the refugee and withholding statutes. This chapter explains the doctrinal structure courts use to evaluate nexus, the governing statutory language, the kinds of evidence courts consider probative of motive, and the special problems that arise when persecutors have mixed motives. The discussion emphasizes legal standards, how courts translate facts into legal conclusions, and how the procedural posture and burdens of proof shape the analysis.

# The ‘one central reason’ standard: 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)

The Immigration and Nationality Act predicates eligibility for asylum (and the denial of asylum) on whether the persecution is "on account of" a protected ground. Congress added a statutory clarification that significantly affects mixed-motive inquiries: an alien is eligible for asylum if "race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion was or will be at least one central reason" for his persecution. The operative phrase—"at least one central reason"—establishes a controlling legal test for when a protected ground is sufficiently causative to support asylum.

This statutory formulation has several implications. First, it requires that a protected ground be "central" to the persecutor's decision to act; mere tangential, trivial, or incidental connections will not suffice. Second, the word "one" concedes that the protected ground need not be the sole reason; it may coexist with other motivations. Third, the statute applies to both past persecution and the likelihood of future persecution.

Courts interpret "central" by looking to whether the protected characteristic or belief was a real, substantial, or significant cause of the harm. The analysis is fact-dependent and often requires parsing competing articulations of motive. Judges will ask whether, viewing all relevant evidence, the protected ground played a meaningful causal role in the persecutor's actions. If the protected ground merely operated in the background or was one of many weakly related factors, it will not meet the statutory threshold.

The "one central reason" inquiry is conceptually a causation inquiry. It is not strict but is more demanding than a mere association. Courts evaluate both subjective intent (what motivated the persecutor) and an objective link between the victim and the protected ground. The statutory formulation displaces any earlier approaches that demanded the protected ground be the "primary" or "dominant" reason, but it retains the idea that the protected ground must be substantially causative.

# Evidence of motive: direct statements, timing, circumstantial indicators

Assessing motive is usually a task of inference; direct proof of what another person thought is rare. Courts therefore examine multiple types of evidence to determine whether persecution was on account of a protected ground. The critical categories are direct statements, temporal and situational timing, and circumstantial indicators.

Direct statements. Explicit threats, admissions, or declarations by persecutors are the most straightforward evidence of motive. A persecutor's explicit statement that the victim is targeted because of race, religion, nationality, membership in a group, or political opinion supplies a clear nexus. When contemporaneous statements tie the persecutor's conduct directly to a protected characteristic or a stated political belief, courts treat those admissions as powerful evidence. However, when the persecutor's statements are equivocal, inconsistent, or made days after the events, their probative value decreases.

Timing. The temporal relationship between protected activity and the onset of harm is often probative. Persecution that follows closely after the victim announced a political opinion, joined a social group, converted religion, or otherwise manifested the protected characteristic suggests causation. Conversely, latency between the protected activity and persecution raises doubt about a causal link. Timing must be considered with care because delays can arise from surveillance, planning, or a gradual identification of the victim, so a gap does not necessarily negate nexus.

Circumstantial indicators. Courts regularly rely on circumstantial evidence: patterns of violence by state or nonstate actors against a group, the identity of the persecutor, targeting practices, and the broader sociopolitical context. For example, systemic attacks on persons of a particular ethnicity or the existence of official policies singling out a group are strong circumstantial proof. The identity and relationships of the persecutor matter: actions by government agents or those acting with government acquiescence are treated differently than purely private disputes; where the persecutor is a member of an organized group known to target persons for a protected characteristic, courts will infer nexus.

Other evidentiary considerations include contemporaneous documentation (police reports, medical records), third-party corroboration (witness statements, NGO reports), and country condition evidence showing patterns of persecution. The credibility of the applicant's testimony is central; if an applicant's account is plausible and consistent with other evidence, courts are more willing to infer motive from circumstantial facts. Conversely, inconsistencies or omissions can undermine nexus findings.

# Imputed political opinion

Political opinion as a protected ground is not limited to the applicant's actual subjective beliefs. Courts recognize "imputed" political opinion: a persecutor's belief about the applicant's political stance can constitute a protected ground even when the applicant holds no such view. The doctrine rests on the persecutor's motive—if the persecutor acts because he believes the applicant holds a political opinion, that mistaken belief provides the link to the protected ground.

To establish nexus via imputed opinion, an applicant must show that the persecutor treated him as having a political opinion and that this imputation was a substantial reason for the harm. The inquiry again blends subjective and objective components. Subjectively, courts ask whether the persecutor's conduct reveals an attribution of a political position to the applicant. Objectively, courts examine whether the circumstances would have led a reasonable persecutor to treat the applicant as holding such an opinion—for example, through membership in an organization, prior statements, associations, or perceived actions.

Imputation is often invoked in cases where the persecutor cannot distinguish among groups or where a persecutor targets a class for assumed opposition to authority, loyalty to a faction, or affiliation with an identifiable political movement. It is important to note that imputation does not create a protected ground out of thin air: the persecutor must have construed the applicant as having a political opinion and have acted because of that construed opinion. An applicant cannot prevail solely because he happens to be viewed unfavorably; the persecutor must have tied the unfavorable view to a political stance that motivated the persecution.

# Distinguishing personal disputes, criminal motives, and extortion from protected-ground persecution

Not every attack or harassment qualifies as persecution on account of a protected ground. Courts distinguish private-person motives—personal, criminal, or pecuniary—from discriminatory motives tied to a protected characteristic. The key question is whether the persecutor's reason for harm is connected to one of the enumerated grounds, not whether the harm is wrongful.

Personal disputes. Many asylum claims arise from what is essentially a personal vendetta, family conflict, or interpersonal quarrel. In such situations, courts look for evidence that the dispute has a discriminatory overlay. For example, a dispute over a business deal, land, or a romantic relationship is ordinarily non-protected. But if the personal dispute is a pretext to target the individual because of ethnicity, religion, or perceived political opinions, it may transform into protected persecution. Courts probe whether the protected characteristic was a central cause or whether it merely coincided with a personal motive.

Criminal motives. Criminal actors often target individuals for robbery, retribution, or silencing witnesses. Purely criminal motives—extortion for money, theft, or gang racketeering—are not protected grounds. The inquiry asks whether the criminal behavior was motivated by discriminatory animus. If a gang extorts multiple victims regardless of political stance or social group, it will not usually be persecution on account of a protected ground. However, if the gang selectively targets victims because of their ethnicity, religion, particular social group membership, or perceived political opinion, then the harm may qualify. Courts therefore examine the pattern of the perpetrators' targets and any statements indicating a discriminatory rationale.

Extortion. Extortion claims often generate mixed motives. Extortion for economic gain is non-protected. Yet extortionists sometimes target individuals precisely because of their social characteristics (e.g., vulnerable minorities, members of a particular social group) or because of perceived political affiliations. When extortion is a cover for persecution (for example, when perpetrators seek to punish or remove a group rather than merely extract money), courts may find nexus. Timing and pattern evidence is especially important—repeated selective extortion of members of a protected group supports an inference that the real motive is the protected status.

Analytical approach. To distinguish non-protected motives from protected ones, courts engage in two main tasks. First, they examine the persecutor's intent as revealed in statements, conduct, and context. Second, they assess whether the protected ground was a central reason for the persecution. If the evidence shows that the persecutor's objective was primarily personal gain, revenge, or ordinary criminal purpose, the claim will likely fail unless the applicant can show that those motives were tainted or driven in large part by a protected ground.

# Mixed motives: when a protected ground is central vs. incidental or tangential

Mixed-motive cases are common. Persecutors may have multiple reasons for targeting an individual; some are protected, others are not. The statutory "at least one central reason" test governs these cases for asylum. The principal question is whether the protected ground was a significant causal factor in the persecution rather than a mere incidental, tangential, or post hoc justification.

Evaluating centrality. To determine centrality, courts look at the relative weight of the protected ground among the motives. They consider the clarity and contemporaneity of any admissions, the sequence of events, alarm-raising conduct directed at a protected characteristic, and circumstantial evidence showing selective targeting. When the protected ground is expressly invoked in threats or public actions, it is easier to find centrality. When it is simply one of several plausible explanations, courts must decide whether it meaningfully contributed to the persecutor’s decision.

Procedural implications. The procedural posture matters. In asylum adjudications, the applicant bears the initial burden of showing that a protected ground was at least one central reason for persecution. If the applicant produces credible evidence supporting this, the burden may shift to the government to rebut by demonstrating alternative motives that negate nexus. The government can attempt to show, through credible evidence, that the persecutor acted for reasons unconnected to a protected ground. If the government succeeds in proving independent motives, the applicant must then establish that the protected ground nonetheless was central. The net effect is often a burdenshifting or comparative causation exercise.

Degrees of causation. The statute sets a middle path between "sole" causation and "any" causation. "Central" is more than incidental but less than exclusive. Courts have described it as requiring a protected characteristic to be a significant, substantial, or meaningful reason among others. This is an inherently factual determination, and appellate review typically affords deference to the adjudicator's reasonable credibility determinations and inferences from the record.

Practical examples. Consider a situation where a victim is assaulted partly to obtain money and partly because the assailant dislikes the victim’s ethnicity. If evidence shows the assailant has a history of targeting that ethnicity and makes ethnic slurs during the attack, the protected ground will often be deemed central. Conversely, if the only evidence of ethnic animus is the victim’s membership in an ethnic group and there is strong proof the attack was a one-off robbery, the ethnic motive may be deemed incidental.

# Burden of proof on nexus

Establishing nexus is an evidentiary burden that rests with the applicant. The nature and degree of this burden vary with the form of relief sought and with statutory standards of harm, but the central expectation is consistent: the applicant must demonstrate that persecution is on account of a protected ground.

Standards of harm and corresponding burdens. For asylum, the applicant ordinarily must establish a "well-founded fear" of persecution based on a protected ground. This is a hybrid standard with both objective and subjective elements: the applicant must show a subjective fear of persecution that is objectively reasonable. Courts commonly treat this as a lower evidentiary threshold than the withholding standard. For withholding of removal under the withholding statute, the standard is higher—establishing that it is "more likely than not" that the applicant would face persecution on account of a protected ground. Convention Against Torture claims generally require proof that it is more likely than not that the applicant would be tortured by or with the consent or acquiescence of public officials, and CAT does not require a nexus to a protected ground.

Initial burden and evidentiary production. The initial burden falls on the applicant to produce evidence linking the harm to a protected ground—through testimony, documents, corroborating witnesses, and country conditions evidence. Credibility determinations are pivotal: an applicant's testimony that is consistent, detailed, and corroborated will carry significant weight. In the absence of corroboration, an applicant must explain why corroboration is unavailable; reasonable explanations are acceptable.

Government rebuttal. If the applicant meets the initial burden, the government may present evidence showing independent motivations for the persecutor—evidence that the harm resulted from personal animus, economic motives, criminality, or other non-protected reasons. When the government shows a credible alternative explanation, the adjudicator must weigh the competing explanations and decide whether the protected ground remains a central reason. The burden of proof does not simply flip to the government; rather, the adjudicator resolves whether the applicant's showing persists in light of the government's evidence.

Appellate review and deference. Adjudicators—immigration judges and administrative bodies—often resolve nexus through credibility findings and factual inferences. Appellate courts commonly apply deferential review to these determinations if they are supported by substantial evidence. Accordingly, an applicant’s failure to meet the nexus burden at the administrative level can be difficult to overcome on review absent clear legal error or lack of substantial evidence.

Strategic evidentiary considerations. Practically, applicants should marshal both direct and circumstantial evidence of nexus: contemporaneous statements by persecutors, records documenting discriminatory practices, patterns of group persecution, expert reports on country conditions, and specific examples showing selective targeting. Demonstrating that other members of the protected class have been targeted can help satisfy the nexus burden by showing a pattern rather than an idiosyncratic attack.

Conclusion

Nexus to a protected ground is a threshold and determinative component of asylum and related relief. The "one central reason" statutory standard requires that a protected characteristic be a substantial causal factor, though not necessarily the only one. Courts assess motive through direct statements, timing, circumstantial indicators, and imputations of political opinion. They carefully differentiate protected persecution from personal, criminal, or economic motives, and face recurring mixed-motive problems that hinge on the relative centrality of the protected ground. Because the applicant bears the burden of proving nexus, assembling a coherent, corroborated record linking harm to a protected ground is essential for success. Understanding these doctrinal contours prepares a reader to follow how courts apply these principles to the specific facts of any given case.

Introduction

When an asylum, withholding of removal, or other protection claim is premised on persecution or serious harm by nonstate actors, a central doctrinal question is whether the applicant can show that the home government either inflicted the harm, was complicit in it, or was unable or unwilling to control the private persecutors. This chapter explains the legal framework for proving government responsibility or failure to protect. It treats the different ways courts conceptualize state involvement, the elements claimants must satisfy, the evidentiary approaches courts use, and common doctrinal exceptions that excuse a failure to seek official protection. It also describes how adjudicators assess the sufficiency and quality of governmental responses to private violence and how country-conditions evidence establishes institutional capacity and systemic patterns of failure.

This material is foundational because many asylum and related claims will succeed or fail depending on the court’s assessment of whether state protection was effectively available. The analysis is fact-intensive: courts evaluate not only the existence of a legal duty but the real-world performance of police, prosecutors, and judges. Procedural posture matters: different standards of proof apply in different forms of relief, credibility findings operate as threshold gates, and requests for corroboration interact with claims about the respondent’s ability to seek help.

# Doctrinal test for state protection

At its core, the doctrinal inquiry asks whether the persecutory harm can be attributed to the state or whether the state is effectively unable or unwilling to prevent or redress that harm. The inquiry has two broad branches.

First is state perpetration or participation. If the government itself — through its agents or organs — commits the harm, or if the state actively directs, orders, subsidizes, or otherwise controls the violent actors, then the harm is attributable to the state. In many systems this is a fairly straightforward factual question: was the actor a state agent acting within apparent authority, or was the state the effective source of the violence? Where the government is a direct perpetrator, the claimant need not show state failure to protect.

Second is inability or unwillingness to control private actors. Here the government is not the primary perpetrator, but the claimant asserts that state authorities either lack the capacity to stop the perpetrators or decline to do so. Courts analyze this by looking at the effectiveness of official response to prior complaints and the overall pattern of official conduct. Key elements that courts commonly examine include:

- Notice: whether the authorities were or should have been aware of the threat or attack;
- Response: whether, upon notice, authorities took reasonable, timely, and impartial steps to investigate, arrest, prosecute, or otherwise protect the victim; and
- Adequacy: whether the measures taken were likely to prevent the threatened harm, which requires assessing both the official acts and the institutional capacity available.

The government is deemed “unwilling” where it refuses or fails to act for reasons such as acquiescence, deliberate indifference, corruption, or political bias. It is deemed “unable” where formal capacity is lacking — for example, where police resources, forensic ability, or judicial independence are insufficient to investigate, prosecute, or punish perpetrators and thereby deter future harm.

These concepts are typically applied in a fact-specific, holistic manner. No single factor is determinative; courts weigh the totality of the evidence to decide whether state protection would have been realistically available to the claimant in the circumstances.

# Notice and reporting to authorities: significance and proof

Notice is central to the inability-or-unwillingness analysis because state obligations to investigate and protect are typically triggered by knowledge of a risk. If authorities had no realistic opportunity to learn of the threat, courts are less likely to find them at fault. Conversely, evidence that the government knew or was put on notice of the danger gives rise to an expectation of protective measures; failure to take adequate steps after notice supports a finding of unwillingness or inability.

For claimants, proving notice usually requires showing one of the following: they themselves reported the incident; a third party (family, neighbors, co-workers, NGOs) notified authorities; the incident was of a kind that would ordinarily be known to police (e.g., public attacks, arrests, or media reports); or the claimant was on a list or registry that should have alerted officials. Notice can also be inferred from the persistence or public nature of the persecution.

Proof of reporting can take many forms. Contemporary adjudicative practice accepts:

- Police or complaint reports bearing official stamps or file numbers;
- Hospital records noting a police referral;
- Documentation from NGOs or human-rights organizations indicating they accompanied the claimant to a police station or communicated with authorities on the claimant’s behalf;
- Third-party statements from witnesses or family members who reported the incident;
- Media articles or broadcasts documenting official knowledge; and
- Photographic or electronic evidence (e.g., screenshots of communications with police, receipts for official filings).

The evidentiary value of a claimed report depends on the source’s credibility and the document’s authenticity. If official records are missing, a claimant should explain why records do not exist or were destroyed, and courts will consider plausible explanations such as systemic recordkeeping failures, corruption, or police reluctance to write reports in politically sensitive cases.

Failure to report can negatively affect credibility and the assessment of state responsibility, because reasonable applicants ordinarily seek state help when available. But the absence of reporting is not dispositive: courts will consider whether reporting was practicable and safe, and whether there were obstacles to reporting (fear of retaliation, lack of access, language barriers, or previous unsuccessful attempts) that would justify not seeking official protection.

# Futility and danger exceptions to reporting

Because reporting to authorities is often the starting point for evaluating state protection, claimants commonly face an apparent “duty” to report. Courts, however, recognize exceptions when reporting would be ineffective or would expose the claimant to additional risk. Two doctrinally important exceptions are the futility exception and the danger or risk-of-retaliation exception.

The futility exception excuses a failure to seek protection if the claimant demonstrates that complaining to authorities would have been futile — that is, authorities would not have acted even if informed. Factors that support futility include a history of uninvestigated complaints, persistent impunity for similar crimes, evidence of corruption or collusion between authorities and the persecutors, or explicit statements by officials that they would not intervene. Evidence supporting futility can be individual (prior complaints ignored; statements to the claimant) or systemic (country reports documenting impunity or nonenforcement).

The danger exception excuses reporting where doing so would have exposed the claimant to a realistic risk of further harm from the persecutors or from the authorities themselves. This covers situations in which reporting would alert perpetrators and provoke retaliation, or where authorities themselves are violent or abusive and pose a direct threat. Proof of danger can include direct threats made by persecutors that they will harm the claimant if they go to police; a demonstrated pattern of reprisals against complainants; or a credible fear that authorities will abuse complainants because of bias, political control, or corruption.

Both exceptions are fact-sensitive and rarely presumed. The claimant bears the burden of alleging and proving circumstances that render reporting futile or dangerous. Courts evaluate whether a competent, reasonable person in the claimant’s position would have refrained from reporting given the risk and expected outcome. Importantly, exceptions are assessed at the time the claimant could have sought protection: it is not enough to assert general fear months later without linking it to contemporaneous conditions.

# Assessing governmental efforts: investigations, arrests, protective measures

When authorities have notice, the inquiry shifts to the quality and effectiveness of their response. Courts look beyond mere formal acts to determine whether governmental measures were sufficient to prevent or redress persecution. Key aspects of an adequate official response include promptness, thoroughness, impartiality, and the likelihood that the measures would deter or halt further harm.

Investigations: An effective investigation is timely, serious, and capable of gathering and preserving evidence. Courts will consider whether the authorities interviewed witnesses, gathered forensic or documentary evidence, visited the scene, made contemporaneous reports, and pursued leads. Superficial, perfunctory or sham investigations—those that fail to follow basic investigative steps or that are designed to produce no action—do not satisfy the state’s duty. Evidence of investigative inactivity or of deliberate delays that undermine evidence preservation points toward inability or unwillingness.

Arrests and prosecutions: Arrests and successful prosecutions are strong indicators that authorities took the threat seriously. Courts evaluate whether arrests were conducted in a manner consistent with due process (e.g., promptness, charging decisions, and formal detention), whether prosecutions were commenced and pursued with reasonable vigor, and whether conviction and sentencing took place where appropriate. A single arrest that leads to prosecution and conviction may demonstrate capacity; conversely, repeated arrests without meaningful prosecution may indicate dysfunction or lack of deterrence, particularly where perpetrators are quickly released or charges dropped for political reasons.

Protective measures: Short of arrest or prosecution, protective measures can mitigate risk. These include restraining orders, emergency shelters, police protection details, relocation assistance, witness protection programs, and judicial injunctions. The adequacy of such measures is assessed by their accessibility, enforceability, and effectiveness. For example, a restraining order that cannot be enforced because police refuse to act is functionally worthless. Courts ask whether the measures, as implemented, were likely to prevent the threatened harm in the claimant’s circumstances.

Credibility and corroboration: Because applicants may lack documentary proof, courts rely heavily on credibility determinations. The adjudicator weighs testimonial detail, consistency over time, plausibility, and corroboration. Documentary or third-party corroboration of official steps (e.g., a signed police report, a prosecutor’s docket, or NGO correspondence) strengthens an applicant’s claim that the government was on notice and did not adequately protect. Documented inconsistencies in the applicant’s account concerning reporting or government response may be dispositive if they bear on credibility.

Timing and causation: Courts also consider temporal relationships: did the official action (or lack thereof) occur before or after the persecution? If authorities initiated meaningful action after an incident, did it meaningfully diminish the future risk of recurrence? Where state measures came too late to prevent the harm and offered no deterrent effect, the state may still be characterized as unable or unwilling regarding future protection.

# Systemic vs. isolated failures of protection

A crucial distinction is between isolated failures — acts or omissions by a few lower-level officials — and systemic or pervasive state failures. This dichotomy matters because isolated, exceptional misconduct by a limited number of officials may not render the state unable or unwilling to protect generally, whereas widespread, persistent patterns of nonenforcement or collusion reflect institutional failure.

Isolated failures: Courts may treat a single instance where police failed to intervene or investigated poorly as atypical, especially if the broader system shows evidence of functioning law enforcement. An isolated lapse might be excused if the government demonstrates that it took corrective steps once the lapse became known, such as disciplining officers or reopening investigations. In such contexts, claimants who rely on one-off deficiencies typically must show that the specific lapse is part of a foreseeable pattern affecting them personally.

Systemic failures: Systemic failure is indicated by pervasive impunity, widespread corruption, lack of basic policing infrastructure, judicial paralysis, or official tolerance of targeted violence. Examples of evidence supporting systemic failure include numerous corroborated complaints of uninvestigated crimes, official statistics reflecting negligible prosecution rates for particular crimes, consistent reports from international or domestic monitors documenting a pattern of nonintervention, and cultural or political incentives that encourage nonintervention (e.g., state-sanctioned discrimination against a group). When systemic failure is established, courts are more likely to find that state protection was not realistically available, and the claimant’s failure to report is more easily excused.

A middle category — institutional weakness — recognizes that a state may have some functional law-enforcement capacity in urban centers but not in rural or conflict-affected regions. Courts therefore examine geographic and situational nuance: what was the government’s capacity in the claimant’s locality at the relevant time? A functioning police force elsewhere does not necessarily foreclose a finding of inability in a specific area characterized by lawlessness.

# Country-conditions evidence and institutional capacity

Because the inability-or-unwillingness inquiry is empirical, country-conditions evidence is essential. Such evidence helps adjudicators evaluate institutional capacity, patterns of impunity, and the ordinary operation of law enforcement and the judiciary. Relevant sources include official government reports, foreign-state department country reports, United Nations and other intergovernmental findings, reports by credible NGOs and human-rights groups, academic studies, public-health or forensic data, news media, and expert witness testimony.

Institutional capacity dimensions: Country-conditions evidence should speak to the following capacities:

- Police capacity: number, training, geographic deployment, response times, forensic capability, recordkeeping, and culture (e.g., respect for human rights, corruption indices).
- Prosecution and judiciary: prosecutorial independence, case backlog statistics, conviction rates for relevant crimes, judicial independence, and the availability of legal protections such as restraining orders.
- Administrative infrastructure: availability of shelters, social services, and mechanisms for victim assistance.
- Security context: presence of effective state control over territory, existence of nonstate armed groups, and the level of civil unrest or conflict.
- Corruption and collusion: evidence of officials’ links to criminal actors, reward structures that incentivize nonenforcement, or legal and informal barriers to accountability.

Types of country-conditions evidence and their persuasive weight: Official state statistics may show low prosecution rates that suggest impunity, but governments sometimes underreport or manipulate statistics; corroborating independent data strengthens the case. Reports from reputable NGOs and international organizations are persuasive when they document patterns and are based on systematic inquiry. Expert testimony can bridge the gap between raw reports and case-specific application by explaining how systemic practices would bear on a claimant’s situation. News reports and social-media materials are useful for contemporaneous documentation but require careful authentication. Courts assess the reliability and recency of all materials; outdated reports may not reflect current capacities.

Translating macro evidence to the individual claim: A key analytical task is connecting national- or regional-level findings to the claimant’s circumstances. Adjudicators ask whether the general dysfunction described in reports plausibly affected the claimant’s locality or specific risk. For example, evidence that prosecutors rarely secure convictions for attacks on a targeted group in a particular region supports the inference that a claimant from that region would not have obtained meaningful protection. The connection must be reasonable and supported by evidence, not merely speculative.

# Procedural posture, burdens, and adjudicative practice

Standards of proof: The applicant’s burden varies by form of relief. For asylum, the applicant typically must show a “well-founded fear” of persecution — a relatively low standard that requires a reasonable possibility of future persecution. For withholding of removal, the standard is higher (commonly more-likely-than-not), which requires stronger proof that future persecution would probably occur. In claims under the Convention Against Torture, the standard requires showing it is more likely than not that the applicant would be tortured if returned. The state-protection analysis is embedded within these standards: whether the government is unable or unwilling to protect bears on the likelihood that harm will recur.

Burden of proof and burden shifting: Claimants bear the initial burden of establishing government inability or unwillingness by submitting credible testimony and supporting evidence. Where the claimant provides reasonable, specific, and credible allegations supported by corroboration or country-conditions material, the adjudicator may find that the government would not or could not protect. There is sometimes limited burden-shifting in practice: compelling evidence of state functioning may undermine a claimant’s prima facie case, but formal shifting of any ultimate burden rarely occurs — the applicant remains responsible for meeting the applicable standard of proof.

Credibility, corroboration, and permissible inferences: The adjudicator’s credibility determination is pivotal. If an applicant is found credible, courts may draw reasonable inferences in the applicant’s favor, especially when corroborative evidence is unavailable due to circumstances beyond the applicant’s control. If the applicant’s testimony is found lacking, the absence of documentary proof or corroboration can be fatal. Therefore, counsel should anticipate the need to explain evidentiary gaps and to present country-conditions material and third-party corroboration where possible.

Remedies and remediation evidence: When the government later takes steps to remedy past failures — by investigating, arresting, or adopting new protective measures — adjudicators consider whether such remedial steps are adequate and timely enough to displace a finding of inability or unwillingness. Evidence that the state has reformed institutions, increased capacity, or otherwise altered the factual predicate for a claim may affect relief at the time of adjudication, but the timing and scope of reforms are scrutinized.

Conclusion

Determining whether a government was unable or unwilling to protect an asylum seeker from private persecution is a layered, fact-intensive inquiry that combines legal tests about state responsibility with careful evaluation of documentary and testimonial evidence. Notice and reporting play a foundational role, but recognized exceptions — futility and danger — may excuse a lack of reporting when credibly established. Courts evaluate the adequacy of investigations, arrests, and protective measures on their substance, not merely on form, and distinguish between isolated lapses and systemic institutional failings. Country-conditions evidence is indispensable to demonstrating state capacity or incapacity and to linking general patterns to an individual claimant’s circumstances. Throughout, the claimant’s burden of proof and the standard of review govern how strongly the evidence must support findings of governmental inability or unwillingness.

Introduction

Establishing a "well-founded fear of future persecution" is central to many forms of international protection, including asylum, and it anchors the forward-looking inquiry that follows once an applicant alleges persecution in the past or risk in the future. This chapter explains the doctrinal structure courts and adjudicators use to evaluate forward-looking claims: the dual subjective-objective formulation of fear; the legal consequences of proven past persecution (including the presumption that it gives rise to future risk and how that presumption may be rebutted); how applicants may frame claims as arising from a "pattern or practice" of persecution; the distinction between an individualized risk based on a protected ground and generalized violence; and the kinds of evidence—firsthand testimony, corroboration, country reports, medical or forensic documents, and expert affidavits—that are admissible and persuasive on these issues. The treatment stresses legal tests, burdens of proof, and how adjudicators apply legal rules to factual records.

# Subjective and objective components

The well-founded fear inquiry has two interlocking limbs: a subjective component and an objective component. Both must be satisfied, and they serve distinct functions.

Subjective component: genuine fear. The subjective element asks whether the applicant genuinely fears persecution if returned. This is a credibility inquiry: does the applicant sincerely believe that she will be persecuted? Adjudicators evaluate this by observing demeanor, assessing internal consistency and specificity of testimony, and comparing statements made to immigration forms and earlier interviews. Consistency over time supports the finding that fear is genuine; material inconsistencies that cannot be reasonably explained may undermine it. However, minor discrepancies or imprecision typical of trauma survivors should not defeat credibility. The subjective element is not satisfied by mere rhetorical claim; the applicant must demonstrate a sincere, reasonable fear, often through testimony, corroborating evidence, or both.

Objective component: reasonable possibility of suffering persecution. The objective element asks whether a reasonable possibility exists that the applicant would be persecuted on return. Different protection regimes apply different objective thresholds (discussed below for asylum versus withholding/other protections), but the general inquiry is whether the objective risk is tied to a protected ground and rises above the level of mere speculation. Adjudicators evaluate objective risk by considering country conditions, patterns of violence, individualized targeting, past incidents, and whether internal relocation is feasible and reasonable.

Interaction between the two components. Courts and adjudicators do not treat these components in isolation. Credible testimony that petitioner genuinely fears persecution may itself satisfy or bolster the objective component; conversely, demonstrating a high objective likelihood of persecution may strengthen the credibility finding on subjective fear. The standard is conjunctive—both components must be present—but a strong showing on one may compensate for gaps in the other to a limited degree, because objective evidence can explain and corroborate subjective claims.

Standards of proof and thresholds. It is important to distinguish the "well-founded fear" standard applicable to asylum (a lower, forward-looking standard) from the higher "clear probability" or "more likely than not" standard that governs withholding of removal and some other relief. The asylum well-founded fear standard typically requires a showing of a reasonable possibility of persecution, often described in practice as a non-negligible or reasonable chance that persecution would occur. The withholding standard requires a higher level of probability—often framed as "more likely than not"—that persecution will occur if the applicant is returned. Because the subjective element is about sincerity, it does not vary by relief category, but the objective component requires different burdens of proof depending on the form of protection sought.

# Presumption from past persecution and rebuttal

Proven past persecution has two doctrinal consequences: it can establish both a negative basis (that the applicant already suffered an activity meeting the definition of persecution) and a positive inference that future persecution is likely. Many adjudicatory regimes therefore treat past persecution as giving rise to a rebuttable presumption that the applicant has a well-founded fear of future persecution on account of the same protected ground.

Operation of the presumption. When an applicant proves past persecution on account of a protected ground, adjudicators often presume that the applicant has a risk of future persecution related to that ground. The presumption operates to shift, in practical terms, the burden to the government to rebut the inference that future persecution is likely. The rationale is that past targeted mistreatment is probative of ongoing or repeated mistreatment because persecutory actors often continue to target the same persons or groups.

Limits on the presumption. The presumption is rebuttable and limited by several legal doctrines:

- Changed country conditions. The government can rebut the presumption by showing materially changed circumstances in the applicant's country such that the applicant no longer faces a reasonable possibility of persecution. Changes must be material and sufficiently reliable to undercut the link between past and future risk; mere general improvements or unverifiable claims of change are insufficient.

- Internal relocation or reasonable avoidance options. If the government shows with sufficient specificity that the applicant could reasonably relocate to another part of the country where she would not face persecution, the presumption may be rebutted. The reasonableness of relocation is a fact-specific question: is the alternative location accessible, safe, and such that the applicant would not be at risk of persecution on account of the same protected ground? Courts assess factors such as the pervasiveness of targeting, availability of housing and employment, cultural or language obstacles, and whether local authorities are likely to protect the applicant.

- Lack of nexus or persecution on a protected ground. If the prior harm does not meet the legal definition of persecution, or it was not on account of a protected ground, the presumption does not arise. Similarly, if the government demonstrates that the earlier harm was motivated by non-protected reasons (personal feud, criminal activity unrelated to a protected characteristic), it can be a basis to rebut the inference that future persecution will be on account of the same protected ground.

Government burden in rebuttal. The government must produce evidence—not mere assertion—that changed conditions or feasible internal relocation remove any reasonable possibility of future persecution. In practice, this means submitting reliable country information, showing the reach of state or non-state actors, or demonstrating that protective mechanisms exist in a secure region. The weight required to rebut depends on the strength of the original showing: stronger proof of past persecution makes rebuttal more difficult. Adjudicators weigh the government's evidence against the record as a whole; successful rebuttal requires persuasive, specific, and verifiable proof.

Renewed applicant response. Even when the government introduces evidence of change or relocation, an applicant may contest its relevance or credibility, point to continuing threats, show that relocation is unsafe because the persecutor's reach is national, or demonstrate that state protection is ineffective. Applicants can also supplement records with updated country reports, witness affidavits, or recent incidents that show ongoing danger.

# Pattern-or-practice claims

Pattern-or-practice claims provide a route to protection where the harm is not individually targeted because of the applicant's particular conduct or identity but arises from widespread, systematic persecution of a group. The doctrine recognizes that persecution can be so widespread or systematic that membership in the affected group creates a substantial risk of future persecution, even if the persecutor does not have any individualized animus toward a particular applicant.

Core idea. A pattern-or-practice claim focuses on group-level conduct by persecutory actors—state or powerful nonstate actors—showing that the government or organized groups engage in a systematic campaign of violent, discriminatory, or oppressive acts against a class defined by a protected ground. The applicant argues that because the persecutory practice is general and pervasive, she faces a risk merely by being a member of the group.

How to prove such a claim. The evidentiary showing must establish two related points: first, that a pattern or practice of persecution exists against persons similarly situated; and second, that the applicant, by virtue of membership in that group, faces a realistic risk of being subjected to that practice. The first element is typically proved through country condition reports, human rights documentation, statistical evidence, affidavits, media accounts, and expert testimony demonstrating the existence, scale, and nature of systematic abuses. The second element requires linking the applicant to the targeted group and explaining why membership makes the applicant vulnerable, including circumstances that make the applicant observable or identifiable targets.

Legal effect. A successful pattern-or-practice claim may obviate the need for highly individualized evidence of targeting because it establishes the existence of group-based persecution that predicts future risk. Nevertheless, adjudicators still require a showing that the applicant would be among those affected by the pattern—mere membership in a very large and diffuse group might not suffice unless the group's members generally are targeted.

Limitations and challenges. Pattern-or-practice claims face several practical hurdles. First, the standard of proof requires credible and often extensive country evidence; ad hoc media reports may be insufficient. Second, courts scrutinize whether the practice is truly targeted at a protected ground or whether the violence is generalized criminality or intercommunal conflict without a persecutory motive. Third, fluctuation in the intensity of abuses or the presence of local protective measures can undermine a claim that the pattern is ongoing or that any particular applicant faces risk.

# Individualized risk vs. generalized violence

A central doctrinal distinction in protection analysis is whether an applicant faces individualized persecution on account of a protected ground, as opposed to generalized violence that affects people irrespective of protected characteristics. The distinction matters because protection requires persecution "on account of" a protected ground rather than mere exposure to country-wide conditions of violence or instability.

Individualized risk: targeted persecution. Individualized risk arises when an applicant is singled out or would be singled out for mistreatment because of a protected characteristic—race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. Individualized targeting can be shown by prior specific attacks, threats, arrest records, threats aimed at family members, or evidence that the applicant's activities made the applicant especially visible to persecutory actors. Courts look for a causal nexus from the protected ground to the persecution: the persecutor must have been motivated, at least in part, by the applicant's protected characteristic.

Generalized violence: indiscriminate or non-discriminatory harm. Generalized violence includes civil unrest, criminal gang violence, interethnic clashes, or widespread lawlessness that affects large segments of the population. While such conditions may create grave humanitarian concerns, they do not automatically qualify an applicant for persecution-based protection. To transform generalized violence into persecution, the applicant must show some connection between the violence and a protected ground or a particularized risk that raises the individual's exposure above that of the general population.

Assessing the line between the two. Determining whether violence is individualized or generalized is factual and context-sensitive. Adjudicators consider whether the persecutory actor selects victims based on identifiable characteristics; whether certain subgroups are disproportionately targeted; and whether the applicant's own profile or activities make her a likely target. For example, if a political party systematically attacks members of an ethnic minority, the violence is both group-based and individualized relative to that minority. Conversely, if a country has high homicide rates but the killings are random or driven by criminal profit motives that do not single out victims based on a protected ground, then an applicant who fears random crime will generally not satisfy the persecution requirement.

Nexus requirement and shared risk. Even when violence is widespread, an applicant can prevail if she shows that the risk of harm she faces is connected to a protected ground—this ties back to the nexus requirement. Evidence that members of a protected group are disproportionately victimized by gangs or that the state condones or participates in targeting of a group may bridge the gap between generalized violence and persecution.

State action, acquiescence, and nonstate actors. Persecution requires action by the state or by nonstate actors the state cannot or will not control. Where generalized violence is inflicted by nonstate actors but the state tolerates or facilitates it, an applicant may show persecution if the state is unwilling or unable to protect persons of the protected group. Demonstrating state acquiescence often requires showing a pattern of failures to investigate, prosecute, or suppress attacks against the at-risk group.

# Evidentiary sources: testimony, corroboration, country reports

Proof of well-founded fear is documentary and testimonial. Courts and adjudicators weigh different types of evidence together to form a totality-of-the-evidence determination. The principal categories of evidence are the applicant's testimony, corroborating materials, and external country information; each has distinct evidentiary roles.

Testimony and credibility assessment. First-person testimony is often the core of a fear claim. Applicants typically must recount past harms (if any), threats, and the basis of their fear. Credibility determinations are fact-finding judgments based on demeanor, consistency, specificity, plausibility, and whether testimony aligns with the documentary record. Adjudicators should consider trauma-informed norms when assessing cognitive gaps, dissociation, or vagueness attributable to fear or trauma. Where testimony is internally coherent, consistent with other record evidence, and explains minor inconsistencies, it may suffice to establish subjective fear and contribute to the objective analysis.

Corroboration: when it is required and its effects. Corroboration becomes important when the applicant's testimony is not sufficiently persuasive on its own, or when significant adverse credibility findings suggest parts of the story are unreliable. Corroborating evidence can include police reports, medical or forensic evidence documenting injuries, witness declarations, affidavits from family or community members, identity documents, arrest or conviction records, and records of threats (letters, social media, recordings).

The requirement to corroborate varies with the nature of the claim. If the testimony is credible and detailed, it may be sufficient without independent corroboration. If an adjudicator finds testimony lacking in credibility but does not explicitly find it implausible, the applicant should be given an opportunity to provide corroboration. When corroboration is required, the threshold is not absolute proof but sufficient objective evidence that, taken with the testimony, creates a reasonable possibility of persecution.

Types of corroborative documents and their probative value. Medical reports documenting healing consistent with reported abuse, police reports that show complaints were filed (even if no prosecutions resulted), and forensic certifications of torture or physical harm are highly probative. Letters or affidavits from other victims, community leaders, or local NGOs can corroborate a pattern of targeting. Documentary evidence is less persuasive if it is stale, unauthenticated, or inconsistent with other parts of the record, but it remains useful to corroborate timing, identity, or specific incidents.

Country reports and external sources. Official or quasi-official country reports (from governmental foreign affairs departments, international organizations, and reputable NGOs) are central to the objective inquiry. They provide context on human rights conditions, patterns of state or nonstate actor conduct, the prevalence of persecution against particular groups, police or judicial capacity, and recent political events.

Adjudicators evaluate these reports for currency, specificity, and corroborative value. A single report noting general human rights abuses may be less persuasive than a body of reports showing consistent, corroborated accounts of systematic targeting. Where country conditions change quickly, recent sources are more probative than older reports.

Expert witnesses and country condition specialists. Expert testimony from country specialists, human rights researchers, or clinicians (when assessing psychological harm) can assist the adjudicator in interpreting complex conditions. Experts can explain the structure and behavior of persecutory actors, the likelihood that an individual in the applicant's position would be targeted, and the reliability or meaning of official statements or statistical data.

The weight of such testimony depends on the expert's qualifications, the factual basis for their opinions, and the consistency of their conclusions with other record evidence. Expert statements that merely repeat generalized conclusions without linking them to the applicant's specific circumstances are weaker than those that explain why the group-level conditions bear directly on the applicant.

Corroboration strategy and burdens. Because the applicant bears the initial burden to demonstrate a well-founded fear, reasonable attempts to collect corroboration are expected, especially where documentary evidence would ordinarily exist. However, practical barriers—lack of access to records, destroyed documentation, or fear of retraumatization—can justify the absence of certain corroborative materials, and adjudicators should consider such obstacles. Applicants who cannot obtain direct documents should explain efforts made and offer alternative corroboration.

Judicial deference and implementation. In reviewing fact findings on fear, courts generally defer to administrative adjudicators' assessments of credibility and country-condition fact findings so long as they are supported by substantial evidence. Accordingly, presenting a coherent, corroborated record at the administrative level is crucial: gaps or implausible claims are unlikely to be cured on judicial review absent clear legal error.

Conclusion

Assessing a well-founded fear of future persecution requires careful, layered analysis: testimony must be evaluated for sincerity; objective evidence must be marshaled to show a realistic risk related to a protected ground; established past persecution shifts the analytic posture via a rebuttable presumption; pattern-or-practice claims allow group-level persecution to ground protection where appropriate; and adjudicators must distinguish between individualized targeting and generalized violence. The legal framework sets different burdens for different forms of protection and prescribes how parties may counter presumptions and contest factual findings. A successful claim typically relies on a coherent narrative, credible testimony, and corroborative country-specific evidence calibrated to the legal standard at stake.

Introduction

When assessing claims for asylum and withholding of removal, an immigration judge or Board of Immigration Appeals must evaluate whether an applicant faces a genuine risk of future persecution. A central question in many cases is whether an applicant can avoid persecution by relocating to another part of his or her country of nationality. If relocation within the country (internal relocation) is reasonable, the applicant may have no "well-founded fear" of persecution for asylum purposes, and may fail to meet the higher "clear probability" standard required for withholding of removal. This chapter explains the regulatory framework governing internal relocation, how burdens of proof shift depending on the existence of past persecution and the type of persecutor, the factors courts and adjudicators use to evaluate the reasonableness of relocation, the significance of a persecutor's reach (including threats to family members), and how courts distinguish between denial of relief based on internal relocation and denial based on a "fundamental change in circumstances."

# Regulatory framework: 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)–(3) (asylum) and § 1208.16(b) (withholding)

The regulations articulate how internal relocation affects eligibility for asylum and withholding of removal. For asylum, 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)–(3) sets out the framework. Section 1208.13(b)(1) instructs that an applicant has the burden of proving that he or she is a refugee as defined in section 101(a)(42) of the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA), which includes establishing a "well-founded fear" of persecution on account of a protected ground. Section 1208.13(b)(2) addresses the effect of past persecution: an applicant who has suffered past persecution is presumed to have a well-founded fear of future persecution, although that presumption may be rebutted if conditions in the country have changed sufficiently or if internal relocation would avoid the risk. Section 1208.13(b)(3) explains that the applicant bears the burden of proving that it would be unreasonable to relocate within the country of nationality to avoid persecution.

For withholding of removal, 8 C.F.R. § 1208.16(b) applies. Withholding requires the applicant to show that it is "more likely than not" (i.e., a clear probability) that he or she would face persecution upon return. The regulation explains that withholding applicants must prove that it is not possible to avoid persecution by relocating within the country. The practical effect is that internal relocation can defeat both asylum and withholding claims, but the legal standards governing that inquiry differ because asylum uses the lower "well-founded fear" standard and withholding uses the higher "clear probability" standard.

The regulations thus establish two related principles. First, an applicant must show a risk of persecution; second, an adjudicator must consider whether the country contains parts where the applicant could live safely and reasonably without being persecuted. The term "reasonable" in the regulations imports an objective, fact-specific inquiry into safety, practicability, and fairness, rather than an inquiry into whether some relocation is theoretically possible.

# Burden allocation depending on past persecution and persecutor type

Understanding which party bears which burden at each stage is essential.

Initial burden on the applicant. At the outset, the applicant must establish refugee status for asylum: a well-founded fear of persecution, or past persecution on account of a protected ground. For withholding, the applicant must show a clear probability of persecution on return.

Effect of past persecution. If an applicant proves past persecution on account of a protected ground, there is a rebuttable presumption that he or she has a well-founded fear of future persecution. Under the regulations, the burden then shifts in a limited way: the government may rebut that presumption by demonstrating either (a) fundamental changed circumstances in the country such that the applicant no longer has a well-founded fear, or (b) that the applicant could reasonably avoid persecution by relocating to another part of the country. Thus, past persecution creates a presumption in favor of a grant of asylum, but that presumption can be overcome by proof that internal relocation is reasonable.

For withholding, past persecution is relevant but does not create the same presumption of future persecution for the "more likely than not" standard. The applicant must still show a clear probability of future persecution; evidence of past persecution is powerful but not dispositive. Because the withholding standard is higher, the government can often defeat withholding by showing that internal relocation would avoid the risk of persecution to the statutory standard.

Persecutor type and state protection. The character of the persecutor affects burden allocation and the relocation inquiry. If the persecutor is a government actor or is acting with government acquiescence, then the adequacy of internal relocation is typically harder for the government to establish, because the applicant may be at risk anywhere the government can reach. Conversely, if the persecutor is a localized nonstate actor and the state can provide protection in some parts of the country, reasonable internal relocation is more likely to be found.

When the persecutor is a nonstate actor, the government must show that internal relocation would be safe in light of the state's willingness and ability to protect the applicant. If the applicant can demonstrate that the state cannot or will not offer protection against the persecutor — because of corruption, lack of reach, or indifference — then internal relocation will be less likely to be reasonable. The legal effect is that an applicant's evidence about insufficiency of state protection can rebut the government's showing that relocation is reasonable, and shift the outcome in favor of the applicant.

# Reasonableness factors: safety, economic practicality, social ties, and access to services

Reasonableness is an inherently context-specific determination. Courts and adjudicators weigh multiple factors to determine whether an applicant must relocate internally to avoid persecution and whether such relocation is reasonable under the circumstances. The principal factors include physical safety; economic practicability; family and social ties; and access to necessary services, including medical care, education, and employment.

Physical safety. Safety is the primary concern. An internal relocation is unreasonable if the applicant would still face a real risk of persecution in the proposed area. Adjudicators examine objective country conditions, incidence and patterns of persecution, the persecutor's capacity to find the applicant, and whether the applicant would be identifiable by the persecutor in the proposed area. Demonstrated patterns of countrywide persecution, evidence that the persecutor retains reach across regions, or an inability of the state to offer protection are facts that weigh against the reasonableness of relocation. Conversely, if reliable evidence indicates that persecution is confined to a particular region or group and that other regions are safe, this supports a finding that relocation is reasonable.

Economic practicability. The relocation analysis recognizes that safety alone is not the only relevant consideration. Economic viability is important. If the applicant would be unable to sustain basic subsistence — housing, food, and means of livelihood — in the proposed area, or would be barred from lawful employment, relocation may be unreasonable. Adjudicators look for whether the applicant could obtain housing and work legally and whether poverty or exclusion would render life intolerable. However, the standard is not perfection: relocation is not unreasonable merely because the applicant would face hardship. Courts distinguish between mere hardship and burdens rising to the level of persecution or an intolerable situation.

Social ties and cultural integration. The applicant's personal ties — family, community, linguistic, ethnic, or tribal connections — affect reasonableness. Relocation to a place where the applicant would face severe social ostracism, discrimination, or isolation that would undermine fundamental aspects of life may be considered unreasonable. For example, if the applicant would be forced to live among hostile members of the persecuting group or where they would be uniquely vulnerable because of their identity, relocation may not be reasonable. Conversely, the existence of a prospective community, relatives, or support networks in the proposed area makes relocation more practicable.

Access to services. The availability of essential services — health care, schooling for children, and legal aid — matters to the reasonableness calculus, especially where the applicant has special needs (chronic illness, disability, minor children). Courts examine whether the applicant would have access to these services without undue risk or hardship. A lack of necessary medical treatment or a realistic means to secure shelter and education can make relocation unreasonable.

Totality of circumstances and threshold. Importantly, adjudicators apply a totality-of-the-circumstances approach: no single factor controls, and the claimant need not prove that relocation is impossible, only that it would be unreasonable. Conversely, the government need not show that relocation would be ideal, only that it would be reasonable. The inquiry is objective: what a reasonable person in the applicant's circumstances would be expected to do, bearing in mind the fundamental rights at stake and the protections the asylum statute aims to afford.

# Persecutor’s reach and the role of family safety

An applicant's fear of persecution must be assessed in light of who the persecutor is, how far that actor's reach extends, and whether threats extend beyond the applicant to family members. The persecutor's reach encompasses both physical ability to locate and harm the applicant in another part of the country and the social or organizational networks that enable targeting across regions.

State actors and acquiescence. If the persecutor is the state or it acquiesces in nonstate persecution, the state’s authority and reach generally render internal relocation less likely to be reasonable. State security forces, law enforcement, intelligence services, or a national militia can operate across a country’s territory. If persecution stems from government policy or practice, the applicant may not have a safe haven anywhere within the country.

Transnational or well-networked nonstate actors. Some nonstate actors have extensive networks — political parties, militias, criminal organizations, or ethnic-based groups — that transcend local boundaries. When the persecuting organization maintains national or regional reach, or loyal affiliates in other parts of the country, the risk can persist after relocation. Evidence of such organizational reach — reports of targeted attacks across regions, communications capabilities, or active informant networks — undermines the reasonableness of internal relocation.

Re-identification and records. The persecutor’s ability to re-identify and locate the applicant is a critical element. If the persecutor can identify the applicant by name, family ties, registration records, or public notoriety, relocation may not diminish the risk. Likewise, if official national records, identity papers, or publicized incidents would lead persecutors to the applicant, relocation will often be inadequate.

Family safety. The safety of family members is both a distinct concern and part of the reasonableness calculus. Threats directed at family members can make relocation unreasonable for two reasons. First, moving to a region where the applicant would be safe but family members would remain exposed may be insufficient to eliminate a well-founded fear, particularly where family ties are close and separation would impose intolerable harm. Second, if the persecutor targets family members across the country, relocation may not reduce the danger.

Situations involving family-based threats require courts to consider the applicant’s family obligations and the realistic prospects for family members to relocate together. In many cultures family unity is central, and forced abandonment of vulnerable relatives (children, elderly, sick) can amount to an unreasonable expectation that the applicant relocate. Adjudicators therefore assess whether safe relocation for the family as a whole is possible, and whether the applicant could reasonably be required to abandon family members to obtain protection.

# Fundamental change in circumstances vs. relocation

Adjudicators can deny asylum based on either a finding that internal relocation is reasonable or that there has been a fundamental change in the applicant's country that eliminates a well-founded fear. Distinguishing between these two bases is important both conceptually and procedurally.

Fundamental change in country conditions. A "fundamental change in circumstances" involves alteration of the conditions that caused the past persecution such that a reasonable person would no longer fear persecution upon return. Examples include a dramatic change of government that has ended systemic persecution, implementation of new effective protections, cessation of hostilities, or durable social or legal reforms eliminating targeted threats. The key inquiry is whether the country situation has changed so fundamentally and pervasively that the presumption of future persecution derived from past persecution is rebutted everywhere in the country, not merely in some regions.

Legal consequences. If the government proves a fundamental change in circumstances, the presumption created by past persecution may be rebutted and the applicant may no longer have a well-founded fear. The focus here is on country-wide transformation that removes the risk of persecution generally, not just in a particular locality.

Internal relocation as a narrower rationale. By contrast, a determination that internal relocation is reasonable is narrower and depends on the availability of safe refuge within the country. Here the government does not assert that the country as a whole has changed; instead, it asserts that regions or localities remain free of risk and that the applicant could move to them. A relocation finding leaves open the possibility that other areas of the country remain dangerous; it determines only that the applicant cannot reasonably be expected to face persecution if he or she relocates to a different part of the country.

Procedural and evidentiary implications. The distinction matters procedurally because different kinds of evidence and burdens are implicated. To establish a fundamental change in circumstances, the government must introduce reliable, country-wide evidence demonstrating systemic change — typically authoritative reports, diplomatic assessments, or clear indications of durable legal and political transformation. To show that internal relocation is reasonable, the government may rely on more localized evidence showing safe areas, availability of protection, and the lack of persecutor reach in those areas. The applicant may respond with testimony and country conditions evidence showing continued danger in the proposed area, targeting of returnees, lack of state protection, or other obstacles to relocation.

Interaction with statutory standards. The difference also matters because asylum's "well-founded fear" standard can be rebutted by either showing of fundamental country change or of reasonable internal relocation, whereas withholding requires a showing that it is not more likely than not the applicant will be persecuted anywhere in the country — relocation can defeat withholding if it makes persecution unlikely by the higher standard. In practice, when adjudicators find relocation reasonable, they typically deny both asylum and withholding; when they find a fundamental change, the asylum presumption is overcome but withholding will still be analyzed under the higher standard.

# Conclusion: applying the law to facts and posture

In practice, adjudicators must apply these principles to the individual facts presented, mindful of the procedural posture. An applicant who has not shown past persecution must carry the initial burden of proving a well-founded fear. An applicant who has established past persecution enjoys a rebuttable presumption of fear, which the government can overcome by proving a fundamental change in country conditions or that internal relocation is reasonable. The type of persecutor — state, state-acquiescent, or nonstate actor — affects whether relocation is a realistic alternative and whether state protection exists. Reasonableness is a fact-driven assessment that balances physical safety, economic feasibility, familial and social ties, and access to services. Courts take care to distinguish between circumstances that make relocation unreasonable and those that show a nationwide transformation eliminating fear; the former protects applicants by ensuring they are not forced to accept intolerable or unsafe internal exile, while the latter reflects genuine, durable changes in the country that remove the justification for asylum.

When preparing to read a judicial opinion on internal relocation, focus on how the adjudicator frames the burden-shifting sequence, what evidence was used to establish either past persecution or the government's showing of safety, which reasonableness factors the court found dispositive, and how the court treated the persecutor’s reach and familial considerations. Understanding these doctrinal building blocks will allow you to follow how courts apply legal standards to the particular facts and why similar factual records can produce different legal outcomes depending on the procedural posture and the quality of country-conditions evidence.

Introduction

The "particular social group" (PSG) doctrine is a central, sometimes vexing, component of refugee and asylum law. The doctrine determines whether an applicant belongs to a group whose members are protected against persecution for reasons that international and domestic refugee regimes recognize as legitimate grounds for protection. Understanding PSG doctrine requires distinguishing conceptual elements that test group characteristics, avoiding analytic pitfalls that undermine protection, separating the class-definition inquiry from the causation (nexus) inquiry, and knowing how courts and adjudicators evaluate evidence in country-specific contexts. This chapter sets out the doctrinal building blocks you need before reading judicial opinions that apply or refine the PSG concept.

The analysis below assumes the typical burdens and remedial structure of asylum-type claims: the applicant bears the initial burden to show membership in a protected ground and that persecution resulted, at least in part, because of that protected ground; adjudicators assess credibility and weigh evidence; appellate review typically limits itself to legal error and substantial evidence review of factual findings. Procedural posture—initial application, credible fear screening, merits hearing, or appellate review—affects how evidence is introduced and how sharply the PSG tests are applied. Throughout, I explain the legal reasoning courts use and the common pitfalls that arise in both trial and appellate stages.

# Immutable characteristics, particularity, and social distinction

The PSG inquiry normally has three analytically distinct strands, sometimes phrased as requirements or factors: immutability, particularity, and social distinction. These terms are used to test whether a proposed group fits the concept of a "social group" that refugee law protects.

Immuteability (or fundamental characteristic). A PSG will ordinarily consist of people who share a characteristic that is either immutable (cannot be changed) or so fundamental to identity or conscience that a person should not be required to change it. The immutability element has two common formulations. The first focuses on characteristics that are (i) innate, such as sex, race, or ethnicity, or (ii) unchangeable because they are core to personhood, such as past experience (e.g., being a former member of a persecuted group). The second recognizes "fundamental to identity" traits such as deeply held beliefs, religious faith, or sexual orientation, where the law treats compelling someone to suppress or change the trait as impermissible.

When applying the immutability test, courts examine whether the group is defined by traits that members can literally change without significant sacrifice of self or whether the trait is a voluntary behavior. For example, a group defined by a criminal act or by a choice to pursue a particular political cause may raise immutability concerns; the analysis then asks whether the behavior is so entwined with identity that requiring its cessation would be unreasonable. The immutability inquiry seeks to prevent applicants from defining groups around traits that are freely changeable in ordinary circumstances and where forcing a change would be a valid government response rather than persecution.

Particularity. Particularity asks whether the proposed group is sufficiently discrete to be a "group" in a meaningful, administrable sense. The concept guards against groups that are amorphous, vague, or defined only by broad, unbounded characteristics. Requirements for particularity typically include that the group is defined by characteristics that delineate a distinct subset of the population, that the group's boundaries are clear enough for adjudicators to determine membership, and that the definition does not collapse into a generic class that would include virtually anyone who shares some tenuous attribute.

Particularity problems arise with definitions that are overly broad (e.g., "all citizens opposed to the government") or circular (defining the group purely by reference to the persecution the applicant suffered). Courts examine both the wording of the group's description and how that description would function in the applicant's country: does the label delineate a recognizably bounded class? Is there objective evidence that people in that class are perceived or treated as distinct? Particularity is a descriptive, not normative, test; it focuses on administrative clarity and realism about who would be included.

Social distinction. Social distinction requires that the proposed group be recognized as a group in the relevant society. This is often called the "social visibility" or "social perception" requirement in some jurisdictions. The inquiry asks whether ordinary people in the claimant's society view members of the proposed group as a discrete class. The focus is on the society's perspective, not whether the applicant personally feels distinct. Evidence that supports social distinction includes public discourse identifying the group, patterns of differential treatment, discriminatory laws or practices, media reports, and testimony about social practices.

Social distinction need not require that the group's members are visibly marked in a physical way; it is satisfied by evidence that society distinguishes them conceptually. Thus, invisible traits (e.g., sexual orientation) can satisfy social distinction when there is evidence that the society is aware of and treats such people as a class. The test prevents abstract or idiosyncratic group definitions and ties refugee protection to social realities where persecution happens.

Interaction of the three elements. Although courts and tribunals sometimes apply immutability, particularity, and social distinction as separate gates, good doctrinal reasoning recognizes they are complementary. Immuteability focuses on the nature of the characteristic; particularity ensures administrability and cognitive coherence; social distinction ties the group to social reality and shows why the group would be a target of persecution. Importantly, precise definitions matter: an applicant can craft a PSG definition designed to fit within the three factors, and adjudicators must scrutinize the substance and practical implications of that definition rather than accept labels at face value.

# Circularity and overbreadth problems

Two common analytic pitfalls in PSG litigation are circularity and overbreadth. Both undermine credibility of the group's definition and can lead to rejection of PSG claims.

Circularity. A circular PSG definition occurs when the group is defined by the persecution or by the perpetrator's opposition. For example, defining the group as "persons who have been targeted for X by Y" makes the group's existence contingent on the very harm for which protection is sought. Circularity is problematic because it conflates cause and effect: it treats persecution as the defining trait of the group, rather than showing that the persecutor targeted the group because of a preexisting characteristic. Courts will generally reject PSGs that are defined solely by an act of persecution or by the persecutor's animus, because this approach makes every victim a member of a PSG and collapses the legal distinction between persecution and group membership. Effective group definitions instead identify characteristics that preexisted the persecution and explain why that characteristic triggered the persecutory response.

Overbreadth. Overbreadth is the reverse problem: the proposed group is so expansive that it becomes functionally meaningless or includes people who would not realistically be targeted for the same reason. Examples include groups defined as "all critics of the government" or "anyone who lives in region X." Overbroad definitions raise particularity concerns and make it difficult to demonstrate that the group's members share a trait that leads to persecution. Overbroad PSGs also present problems of administrability—how would an adjudicator determine membership consistently? The overbreadth analysis looks at both the text of the definition and the social context: a group might be broad in form but specific in practice if only a subset experiences or risks persecution.

Avoiding circularity and overbreadth. A sound PSG definition avoids defining the group by the persecution. It starts with an objective trait or a set of traits—immutable or fundamental—and explains why members of that group are likely to be singled out. Narrowing the group by adding criteria—such as geographical limitation, a demonstrable social status, or a combination of characteristics—can cure overbreadth, but such narrowing must reflect reality rather than being an unmoored exercise in label-crafting. The key is to show through evidence that the group exists as defined and that members are targeted because of the defining trait.

# PSG vs. nexus: separate inquiries

Two analytically separate inquiries often get conflated: (1) whether the applicant belongs to a PSG and (2) whether the persecutory harm was on account of membership in that group. It is crucial to keep these questions distinct in legal reasoning.

PSG inquiry: As above, this asks whether the applicant's asserted group meets the characteristics of a protected social group: immutability, particularity, and social distinction. The PSG inquiry is categorical and evaluative; it does not probe the persecutor's motive except insofar as the alleged motive helps explain why the group exists independently of the persecution.

Nexus inquiry: If an applicant establishes membership in a PSG, the court then asks whether the persecution was "on account of" that membership. This is a causation analysis: did the persecutor target the applicant because of the group's defining characteristic? The inquiry examines motive and reason. Evidence may include direct statements by persecutors, consistent patterns of attacks against people sharing the characteristic, contemporaneous documentation, and the broader country context that explains why those traits provoke hostility.

Why separation matters. Treating the two inquiries separately avoids analytic shortcuts. A tribunal must not dismiss a PSG claim by assuming lack of motive without properly considering whether social or political conditions in the country make group-membership a likely reason for persecution. Conversely, acceptance of a PSG definition does not resolve the nexus question; even a clearly defined PSG may not have been what motivated the persecutor in a particular case.

Standards for causation and mixed motives. Jurisdictions vary on how to treat mixed motives. Generally, asylum law allows a protected ground to be one central reason for persecution; it does not require that the protected ground be the sole reason. Thus, if a persecutor had multiple motives, the applicant may still succeed if membership in the PSG was a central reason. However, courts scrutinize whether the protected trait genuinely contributed to the persecution or whether it is a peripheral or pretextual factor. The nature and extent of evidence needed to prove causation differ across stages (e.g., reasonable possibility at screening, clear probability at merits), and between forms of relief (asylum, withholding, or other protections).

Evidentiary approach. In the nexus inquiry, adjudicators consider both direct and circumstantial evidence. Direct evidence is rare: explicit statements that the persecutor targeted victims because of group membership are often not available. Thus, patterns of systematic targeting, the presence of laws or practices that discriminate against the group, or other contemporaneous indicators can support an inference of motive. Courts often adopt a commonsense approach, asking whether it is plausible that the group's defining characteristic would be a cause of the harm given the country context.

# Common PSG archetypes and evidentiary showings

Over time, adjudicators have developed common categories of PSGs that recur in claims. Recognizing archetypes helps applicants and adjudicators frame persuasive group definitions and assemble appropriate evidence. Below are common archetypes, typical legal reasoning about them, and the types of evidence that support them.

1. Family or kinship groups. Families (nuclear or extended) are often recognized as PSGs because kinship is typically an immutable or fundamental characteristic and family members are socially distinct in many societies. Evidence that helps: documentation of family relationships, testimony showing that authorities or nonstate actors target families as families (e.g., collective punishments, threats), community recognition of family ties, and country materials showing persecution or discriminatory practices against particular families or clans.

2. Ethnic, racial, or tribal groups. Ethnicity and race are quintessential immutable characteristics; these groups often satisfy social distinction and particularity. Evidence includes demographic data, census categories, historical or contemporary incidents of ethnic violence, discriminatory laws or policies, and reports by NGOs documenting targeted abuses.

3. Religious groups. Shared religious belief is both immutable in the sense of core identity and societally salient in many contexts. Supporting evidence includes records of religious affiliation, evidence of religious institutions, documentation of religiously motivated attacks, statements by authorities or groups singling out believers, and country reports on religious freedom.

4. Gender and gender-related groups. Gender is an immutable characteristic. In practice, courts have treated "women" or subsets of women (e.g., women who have resisted forced marriage) as PSGs. The evidentiary showings often include statistics on gender-based violence, testimony about societal attitudes toward women, legal codes imposing discriminatory treatment, and international organization reports on gender-related persecution.

5. Sexual orientation and gender identity. Sexual orientation and gender identity are protected as traits fundamental to identity. Evidence can include personal testimony, corroboration from witnesses or community organizations, records of criminalization of same-sex conduct, documented arrests or violence, and reports of social stigmatization.

6. Former military, police, or security personnel. Past membership in certain institutions can be immutable (a past fact) and socially salient, particularly in contexts where such people are persecuted by new regimes or nonstate actors. Evidence includes service records, targeted threats against former personnel, patterns of post-conflict retribution, and documentation of killings or expropriations of former members.

7. Former members of insurgent or criminal organizations. Membership in an organization may be treated as an immutable past fact. Whether this group meets particularity and social distinction depends on the society and whether a group of former members is recognized as a distinct class. Evidence includes documentation of prior membership, threats based on past membership, and country reports of retribution.

8. Profession, social class, or occupation. Occupations are a more contested PSG category because they can be changeable and not always socially distinct. Work-based groups can succeed when the occupation is difficult to change or closely tied to status (e.g., being a judge, journalist, or human rights activist in a repressive society). Evidence includes employment records, targeted violence against people in the profession, societal recognition of the occupation as a distinct class, and country-specific evidence showing risk targeted at that occupation.

9. Political opinion groups (overlap with PSG). "Political opinion" is a separate protected ground in many systems, but politically defined associative or belief-based groups often overlap with PSG claims. Groups defined by political opposition can be difficult under PSG doctrine because political activity is arguably changeable; however, where political opinion is central to identity or where society distinguishes political adherents as a class, such groups can be persuasive. Evidence should focus on both the content of the opinion and the society's treatment of adherents.

For each archetype, the applicant must show both that the group as defined fits the doctrinal elements and that persecution flowed from membership (see nexus discussion). The strength of evidence varies: some archetypes—race, ethnicity, religion, sex—are often easier to establish on the PSG elements, while profession and political group claims require more careful factual development.

# Country-specific social distinction evidence

Because PSG analysis is context-sensitive, evidence that connects the group's characteristics to the social reality of the applicant's country is critical. Adjudicators will examine sources ranging from expert testimony to documentary reports. Below is guidance on the types of materials that persuasive submissions commonly include and how courts evaluate them.

Types of evidence.

- Documentary reports: Country reports by governments, international organizations, and reputable NGOs provide objective background on social and political conditions, including patterns of discrimination and persecution against particular groups. These reports are often treated as foundational.

- Statistical data: Incidence of attacks, arrest records, or demographic statistics can demonstrate systematic targeting or the societal salience of a trait.

- Media sources: News articles, investigative reports, and local media can show contemporary discourse, incidents, and public attitudes that reflect social distinction.

- Expert testimony: Country experts, sociologists, anthropologists, or region specialists can explain cultural practices, social hierarchies, and why a group would be distinguished or targeted. Adjudicators assess expert credibility, qualifications, and the empirical basis for conclusions.

- Witness testimony: Testimony from community members, family members, or others who can describe social perceptions and experiences of the group is probative. Consistency and corroboration strengthen such evidence.

- Official records and documents: Laws, administrative records, police reports, and government communications demonstrating discriminatory policies or targeted enforcement can powerfully support both PSG and nexus claims.

- Digital and social media: In some contexts, social media records capture societal discourse or incidents of harassment and can show social distinction, though reliability and authentication are important.

Evaluative principles.

Courts evaluate country-specific evidence for relevance, credibility, and sufficiency. Relevance looks to whether the material speaks directly to the group's existence, social visibility, and the reasons for persecution. Credibility concerns source reliability—authoritative international organizations and well-respected NGOs typically carry greater weight than anonymous internet posts. Sufficiency requires the totality of evidence to reasonably support a finding that the group exists as defined and that the society recognizes it as distinct.

Temporal and regional specificity. Country conditions change over time and vary within a country; therefore, evidence must be temporally and geographically relevant. Evidence about a national trend may be less helpful if the applicant's experience was localized; conversely, localized evidence may suffice if it shows that, in the applicant's region, the group is societally distinct and targeted. Adjudicators prefer evidence contemporaneous with the persecution or credible about ongoing risks.

Confronting contrary evidence. State reports or other authorities may contradict an applicant's claims. Courts weigh conflicting evidence; an applicant's burden is to present a reasonable, well-supported case, not to disprove every government assertion. Demonstrating patterns of similar incidents or producing corroborating independent sources strengthens the applicant's position.

# Interaction with mixed-motive analysis

Persecution often has multiple causes. Mixed-motive analysis addresses how adjudicators treat cases where the persecutor had both protected and unprotected reasons. This issue arises in the nexus stage but has implications for group definition and the overall strength of a claim.

Legal standard for mixed motives. Different systems formulate the requirement slightly differently, but common formulations require that membership in a protected ground be at least one central or significant reason for persecution. The protected ground need not be the sole or dominating motive, but it must be more than incidental. Adjudicators will examine the strength of the causal link: whether the evidence shows that the protected trait was a reason that substantially motivated the persecutor's conduct.

Application in practice. In practice, mixed-motive analysis considers direct statements by perpetrators, documentary evidence revealing motive, the sequence of events, and patterns of targeting. If an applicant was attacked for economic reasons but also targeted because of group membership, the question is whether group membership was a central reason. For example, if perpetrators singled out the applicant explicitly because she was a member of a religious minority, even in the context of theft, the religious motive can suffice. Conversely, where evidence shows the primary motive was non-protected (e.g., robbery) and the protected trait was only tangential, a nexus may be lacking.

Effect on remedies and standards. Mixed motives can affect the form of relief and the standard of proof required. For asylum (discretionary protection), courts may ask whether the protected-ground motive was a central reason in the totality of circumstances. For withholding of removal—where the standard is higher (more likely than not) in many jurisdictions—establishing a central motive becomes more demanding. Additionally, if an applicant shows persecution motivated both by a protected ground and by personal reasons (e.g., a land dispute), adjudicators will parse whether the protected reason was a decisive element.

Procedural implications. When mixed motives are alleged, adjudicators should explicitly analyze the motives and state their reasoning as to how the evidence establishes that membership in the PSG was a central reason. Failure to do so is a common basis for appellate reversal. Applicants should prepare evidence that isolates the protected motive—statements by perpetrators, patterns showing that persons sharing the trait were targeted, or circumstantial indicators linking the trait to the harm.

Burden allocation. The applicant bears the burden of showing that the protected ground was at least one central reason. Once the applicant makes a prima facie case, the burden may shift in some contexts to the government to show that the harm was motivated principally by non-protected reasons or that the risk does not arise from the protected trait. The dynamics depend on statutory and procedural rules, but strategic presentation of motive-related evidence helps the applicant meet the initial burden.

Conclusion

PSG doctrine combines doctrinal tests and practical evidentiary work. Immuteability, particularity, and social distinction frame the permissible contours of group definitions. Avoiding circular definitions and overbroad classes strengthens credible claims. Courts must keep PSG definition and nexus distinct while recognizing how evidence overlaps the two inquiries. Familiar PSG archetypes provide templates for claims, but country-specific evidence is essential to show that a defined group exists and is targeted. Finally, mixed-motive analysis requires careful proof that a protected trait was a central reason for persecution; the applicant bears the burden of persuasion, and adjudicators must articulate clear causal reasoning. Mastery of these concepts will enable you to follow judicial reasoning and to assess the strengths and weaknesses of PSG-based asylum claims.

Introduction

Withholding of removal is a form of statutory protection that may be granted to noncitizens who establish that their life or freedom would be threatened in a country to which they would be removed because of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. Although conceptually related to asylum, withholding of removal differs in important ways that affect proof, scope of relief, and practical consequences. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework governing withholding: the higher standard of proof, its mandatory (non‑discretionary) nature, limitations on beneficiaries, the absence of certain asylum bars, how internal relocation and state protection bear on the analysis, and the practical and remedial consequences of a grant.

# ‘Clear probability’/more-likely-than-not standard

The legal standard for withholding of removal is commonly described as a “clear probability” that the applicant would be persecuted if removed. Courts and adjudicators typically equate this “clear probability” formulation with a quantitative burden of proof: the applicant must show that it is more likely than not—i.e., a greater than 50% chance—that persecution would occur on account of a protected ground. This is a higher standard than the one that applies to asylum, which can be satisfied by demonstrating a “well‑founded fear” of persecution (a showing that may be satisfied by a reasonable possibility of persecution, often thought to be significantly lower than a greater‑than‑50% probability).

In practice the difference between a “well‑founded fear” and a “clear probability” standard matters at several doctrinal and evidentiary points. Under the higher standard, an adjudicator must be persuaded that the evidence, when all reasonably probative facts are weighed, supports a finding that the applicant’s removal will more likely than not result in persecution. Because the standard is probabilistic, adjudicators consider both the applicant’s personal facts and credible country conditions evidence; if either line of evidence is insufficient to tip the scale past the 50% mark, withholding must be denied. Credibility determinations, documentary proof of past harm, corroborating witness testimony, and up‑to‑date country reports therefore assume elevated importance.

Courts assess the standard as a global probabilistic conclusion about future risk, not merely a series of individual assertions. For example, evidence of past persecution ordinarily gives rise to a presumption of future persecution absent changed circumstances. But even with past persecution, the applicant still must persuade the adjudicator that it is more likely than not that harm will recur upon return. If country conditions have changed or if the applicant can realistically avoid harm through relocation or state protection, the “clear probability” threshold may not be met despite past abuse.

# No discretionary denial; no derivative beneficiaries

A central doctrinal distinction between withholding of removal and asylum is that withholding is mandatory if the statutory standard is satisfied. If an applicant proves by the required standard that removal would result in persecution on account of a protected ground, the immigration judge (and ultimately the Attorney General) has no discretion to deny withholding on discretionary grounds. This contrasts with asylum, where even if an applicant meets the statutory eligibility elements, the grant of asylum remains discretionary and may be denied for various discretionary reasons including adverse immigration history, credibility issues, or humanitarian considerations.

The mandatory nature of withholding means that the adjudicator's role is limited to a legal determination of entitlement under the statutory standard; if entitlement is established, relief must be granted in the form the statute prescribes. That said, mandatory relief does not transform withholding into an affirmative immigration status like lawful permanent residency. It is a non‑discretionary protection against return to a particular country when the statutory predicate is met.

Closely related to the mandatory nature is the rule that withholding provides no derivative beneficiaries. For asylum, qualifying family members (spouses and unmarried children under a certain age) of an asylee can obtain derivative asylum status through the principal applicant. Withholding of removal, by contrast, is individualized. Each person must independently meet the statutory standard for withholding; an applicant cannot obtain withholding relief for family members simply by proving eligibility for oneself. This limitation means that family unity benefits common in asylum practice are not available under withholding, and each family member must present his or her own evidence and establish the “clear probability” standard.

# No one-year filing bar; different nexus standard equivalency

Asylum claims are subject to a one‑year filing bar: an applicant ordinarily must file an asylum application within one year of arrival in the United States unless an exception applies. Withholding of removal is not subject to that temporal bar. The filing timeliness rules that gate asylum do not prevent an otherwise eligible applicant from seeking withholding relief. Consequently, applicants who missed the asylum window or who are time‑barred on their asylum claim may still pursue withholding; the merits of withholding are assessed independently of the asylum filing deadline.

The nexus requirement in withholding mirrors the asylum statutory grounds—race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion—but its application has some nuances. To qualify for withholding, the persecutory harm must be “on account of” one of those protected grounds. Courts interpret this causation requirement by asking whether the protected ground was a central reason for the persecution; the nexus need not be the sole motive, but it must be a significant factor. Under the higher burden of proof, the applicant must show that it is more likely than not that the motivation for potential persecution will include one of the enumerated bases.

A common issue is how mixed‑motive persecution is treated. If the persecutor has both protected‑ground and non‑protected motivations, withholding may still be available if the protected ground plays a central or motivating role. However, adjudicators must be convinced, to the “more likely than not” level, that the protected ground will be operative in the anticipated persecution. This can be a more demanding factual inquiry than for asylum, where an applicant can succeed by showing that a protected ground is one reason among others and that persecution is reasonably possible.

Equivalency principles sometimes used in asylum—such as treating coerced avoidance of a protected characteristic or imputed beliefs as a basis for protection—are applied in the withholding context but under the more exacting “clear probability” lens. For example, an applicant who fears persecution because authorities will impute political opinion may establish nexus, but the evidence must demonstrate that imputation will occur with a probability greater than 50% and that such imputation will result in persecution.

# Interplay with internal relocation and state protection

Internal relocation and available state protection address whether an applicant’s fear of persecution would be realized upon return. For both asylum and withholding, if an applicant can reasonably and safely relocate to another part of the country of removal or can obtain adequate protection from the state, the risk of persecution is diminished. But because withholding requires proof that persecution is more likely than not, internal relocation and state protection often carry greater dispositive weight in withholding adjudications.

Internal relocation is analyzed in three linked dimensions: severity of harm in the proposed destination, reasonableness of relocation (including whether relocation would impose undue hardship), and the applicant’s ability to access basic life necessities and community ties. An adjudicator will credit internal relocation as a sufficient reason to deny withholding if it finds, by a preponderance favorable to the government’s argument but within the framework of the applicant’s burden, that a reasonable alternative location exists where the applicant would not face a more‑than‑50% chance of persecution. Factors relevant to this determination include the geographic reach of the persecutory actors, whether the persecutory motives are national in scope (e.g., a national regime targeting a particular group), cultural or language barriers, economic prohibitions on relocation, and the feasibility of integrating into the alternative locality.

Available state protection is evaluated by considering whether the government of the country of removal is willing and able to protect the applicant from the hypothesized harm. If effective state protection is available such that the applicant can seek and obtain the state's aid with reasonable assurance of safety, withholding is generally inappropriate. The state protection inquiry examines whether law enforcement or judicial remedies exist and whether they are realistically accessible (for example, without retribution), as well as whether the state’s response to similar claims has been effective. Under the “clear probability” standard, it is not enough to show that state protection is uncertain or imperfect; the applicant must persuade the adjudicator that the state’s inability or unwillingness to protect them rises to the level that makes persecution more likely than not.

Practical adjudicative dynamics shape how relocation and protection are weighed. Adjudicators may find it easier to deny withholding by finding a plausible internal relocation alternative or some degree of state protection, given the higher burden of proof. Conversely, strong evidence of past targeted harm, systemic state complicity, or pervasive risk can overcome relocation and protection arguments and meet the “clear probability” threshold.

# Remedy scope and practical consequences

The relief that withholding of removal confers is narrower and more constrained than asylum. Withholding is a protection against removal to a specific country; it does not create a path to lawful permanent residence, does not generally lead to derivative status for family members, and does not automatically bestow the array of benefits available to asylees.

Specifically, withholding prohibits the government from removing the beneficiary to the country (or countries) where the statutory standard is met. It may also prevent return to any country where the person would face the same danger. An important practical limitation is that withholding does not entitle the beneficiary to lawful permanent resident status or to many public benefits. Work authorization may be available under regulations for individuals granted withholding, but eligibility rules can be complex and differ from asylum‑related employment authorization pathways. Likewise, access to certain social services, resettlement assistance, and derivative family protection is limited or nonexistent.

Duration and review are also key practical consequences. Withholding, being a protection against removal rather than a permanent status, can be reviewed if country conditions change. The government may seek to remove a person to a third country that is deemed safe; withholding does not prohibit transfer to a safe third country. Additionally, withholding does not cure other immigration grounds of inadmissibility or deportability; it functions as a shield against removal to a particular place, not as a comprehensive immigration remedy.

Another practical issue is the intersection with protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT). CAT protection is separate from withholding under the INA and has a distinct legal standard. Where withholding requires a “clear probability” of persecution on account of a protected ground, CAT protection requires that it be more likely than not that the applicant would be tortured with state acquiescence. Success under CAT can bar removal to the torturing country regardless of motive, but its elements and defenses differ; for example, CAT has no protected‑ground nexus requirement but does consider whether the applicant participated in criminal activity and whether there are serious reasons to consider the person a danger to the community.

Finally, because withholding does not confer many benefits, applicants and practitioners must consider strategic litigation and procedural choices. For example, absence of derivative benefits makes individual claims more costly for family units; the lack of discretionary relief means that a single adjudicator’s findings on credibility and evidence control the outcome under the statutory standard; and the inability to adjust status based on withholding alone means that beneficiaries must explore other immigration avenues if they seek a more permanent legal status.

Conclusion

Withholding of removal is a mandatory, narrowly focused protection that requires a higher burden of proof than asylum and lacks many of asylum’s benefits and procedural constraints. Its “clear probability” standard imposes rigorous evidentiary demands, and courts balance individual testimony against country conditions, potential state protection, and internal relocation possibilities when determining whether the risk of persecution exceeds 50%. Understanding these doctrinal features and practical consequences is essential for analyzing withholding claims and anticipating how courts and adjudicators will apply the legal criteria in contested removal proceedings.

Introduction

Asylum adjudication is a credibility-centered process. Because many asylum claims rest on a claimant's account of persecution or fear of persecution — often unaccompanied by contemporaneous documentary proof — decisionmakers must evaluate the trustworthiness of testimony and determine whether corroborative evidence is required. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework governing credibility determinations and corroboration in U.S. asylum law, with a focus on the standards created by the REAL ID Act, the enumerated credibility factors, the circumstances that trigger a corroboration requirement, the types and weight of country-conditions and expert evidence, and the consequences of adverse credibility findings versus findings that are credible but legally insufficient. The discussion emphasizes how courts apply rules to facts, the allocation of burdens, and how procedural posture (immigration court, administrative appeal, federal review) affects analysis.

# REAL ID Act standards: 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(ii)–(iii)

The REAL ID Act, enacted in 2005, materially changed the statutory standard for credibility determinations in asylum adjudications. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(ii)–(iii), in deciding whether an applicant has demonstrated a well-founded fear of persecution or persecution on past events, the adjudicator "may base a credibility determination on the demeanor, candor, or responsiveness of the applicant or witness, the consistency between the applicant's or witness's written and oral statements (whenever made and whether or not under oath), the internal consistency of each such statement, the consistency of such statements with other evidence of record (including reports by the Department of State on country conditions), and any inaccuracies or falsehoods in such statements, without regard to whether an inconsistency, inaccuracy, or falsehood goes to the heart of the applicant's claim."

Two critical points follow from this statutory language. First, the statute lists multiple permissible factors for credibility assessments, making clear that adjudicators may rely on demeanor, consistency, corroboration, and the applicant's own statements. Second, the phrase "without regard to whether an inconsistency...goes to the heart of the applicant's claim" means that adjudicators may treat even peripheral inconsistencies or minor falsehoods as valid bases for adverse credibility findings; they are not confined to treating only discrepancies central to the ultimate asylum claim as material. The REAL ID Act therefore authorizes a broad and multifactorial approach to credibility evaluations.

However, statutory authorization is not unchecked. Administrative and judicial review still require that credibility findings be supported by specific, cogent reasons. Decisionmakers must identify the particular reasons underpinning an adverse credibility determination and tie them to record evidence. Additionally, the REAL ID Act's modifications to credibility assessment interact with due process and general administrative law principles (e.g., reasoned decisionmaking). Thus, an adjudicator's reliance on the listed factors must still be explained and supported by evidence in the record.

# Credibility factors and inconsistencies

Credibility assessment is multi-factored. The statutory list from the REAL ID Act supplies major categories, but courts and adjudicators commonly break credibility analysis into particular factors and types of inconsistencies.

Demeanor, Candor, Responsiveness. Adjudicators may evaluate witness demeanor — appearance, emotional affect, eye contact, nervousness — while recognizing the limits of demeanor assessments. Cultural differences, trauma, language barriers, and the stress of removal proceedings can alter demeanor and make these factors imperfect proxies for truthfulness. Candor and responsiveness address whether the witness answers questions directly and fully, or appears evasive or uncooperative.

Consistency: Internal and External. Consistency is assessed both internally (between different parts of the claimant's narrative) and externally (between the claimant's statements and other record evidence). Real ID authorizes consideration of both written and oral statements, and of statements made at different times (e.g., asylum application form, testimony at hearing, prior interviews). The focus is on whether the narrative is stable or whether it contains material contradictions.

Materiality of Inconsistencies. The REAL ID Act allows consideration of inconsistencies "without regard to whether" they go to the heart of the claim. This broad standard means that adjudicators may draw adverse credibility inferences from inconsistencies that a claimant might deem immaterial. Nonetheless, courts often analyze whether an inconsistency is plausibly explained (e.g., by trauma, translation issues, or misunderstanding of the question) and whether the inconsistency affects the central elements of the asylum claim (e.g., past persecution, nexus, or reasonable fear). A reasoned explanation for why a particular inconsistency undermines credibility strengthens an adverse finding; a conclusory statement that an inconsistency exists, without explanation, is vulnerable on review.

Omissions and Implausibilities. Failure to mention a key fact at an earlier time, or the presence of implausible details in a narrative, may be probative of mendacity. But adjudicators must consider innocent explanations for omissions (e.g., interviewer did not ask, applicant forgot, or traumatic memory impairments) and for apparent implausibilities (e.g., imperfect recall of peripheral details). Courts expect adjudicators to assess whether alternative explanations are reasonable and whether the record corroborates or undermines them.

Falsehoods and Material Fabrications. Deliberate false statements, if shown, are powerful bases for adverse credibility. Some statutes or regulations bar asylum when an applicant has engaged in fraud or misrepresentation to obtain immigration benefits. The nature of the falsehood matters: deliberate fabrication of the core claim or of facts that go to eligibility can be dispositive; small false statements about collateral matters may not.

Evaluative Weighting. Adjudicators must weigh the relative significance of different credibility factors and articulate that weighing. For example, a single, minor discrepancy in dates may be insufficient for an adverse credibility finding when the rest of the account is consistent and supported by corroboration. Conversely, multiple, unexcused inconsistencies affecting key elements (e.g., whether an applicant was harmed, reasons for harm, or the identity of persecutors) can justify denial.

Vulnerabilities and Procedural Posture. Credibility assessments may be shaped by procedural posture. In initial immigration judge proceedings, credibility determinations are factual findings entitled to deference on appeal. In the Board of Immigration Appeals (or its modern equivalent), administrative decisionmaking reviews the immigration judge record. In federal court review (habeas or petition for review), credibility findings are reviewed under the deferential substantial evidence or abuse-of-discretion standard, depending on the claim, so the level of required explanation and record support is high.

# When corroboration is required and how to obtain it

The need for corroboration arises when an applicant's testimony is not sufficiently convincing on its own to meet the relevant statutory standard (e.g., the well-founded fear of persecution standard or the clear probability standard for asylum). The governing principle is that an applicant's uncorroborated testimony may be accepted if credible. Conversely, where the adjudicator finds issues with credibility, the REAL ID Act and regulatory framework permit (and sometimes require) adjudicators to seek corroborating evidence before denying relief.

Trigger for Corroboration. Corroboration becomes necessary where the applicant's testimony is insufficiently persuasive in and of itself, but still plausible. This can occur in two principal scenarios:

1. The adjudicator expresses reservations about credibility based on specified inconsistencies or gaps, raising doubt about key factual assertions; or

2. The applicant's testimony lacks independent documentary support and the facts alleged are of a type that are ordinarily susceptible to independent verification.

When doubts are raised, the adjudicator should (a) explain why the testimony is not sufficient, (b) identify what corroboration would be material and why, and (c) afford the applicant an opportunity to present corroborating evidence, or at least to explain why corroboration is unavailable.

Types of Corroboration. Corroboration can take many forms:

- Contemporaneous documents: hospital records, police reports, arrest records, court documents, identity or residence documents, asylum-seeker's own notes, photographs.

- Third-party affidavits and letters: affidavits from witnesses, members of the community, NGOs, or family corroborating arrests, threats, or political activity.

- Official country records: documents from government entities, where feasible (e.g., arrest warrants, conviction records).

- Secondary evidence of country conditions: human rights reports, press accounts, or country-specific reports that confirm patterns of abuse.

- Expert reports: testimony by experts who can confirm that the individual’s experiences are consistent with practices in the country or with known patterns of persecution.

- Forensic evidence: medical reports or psychological evaluations that corroborate injuries or trauma consistent with alleged abuses.

Adjudicator's Duty to Request Corroboration. The adjudicator has a duty to fairly develop the record. Where an applicant has given a plausible account but lacks documents under circumstances where such documents would likely exist, the adjudicator may ask the applicant to provide corroborating evidence and may allow time to obtain it. If the applicant explains why corroboration is unavailable (e.g., destruction of records, inability to access authorities due to fear of reprisal, or lack of state institutions), the adjudicator should consider whether corroboration is reasonably obtainable.

Unavailable Corroboration: Reasonable Explanation Standard. When an applicant alleges that corroboration is not available due to conditions in the source country or genuine obstacles to obtaining documents (e.g., destroyed records, risk of harm in acquiring corroboration), the adjudicator must assess whether the explanation is reasonable. If the explanation is plausible and consistent with other record evidence, the adjudicator should not penalize the applicant for lack of corroboration.

Corroboration Burdens: Allocation and Effect. The statutory allocation remains that the applicant bears the ultimate burden of proof for asylum. However, an applicant's credible testimony can satisfy that burden without corroboration. If testimony is not wholly credible, the burden shifts to the applicant to produce corroboration in the form of available evidence or a reasonable explanation for its absence. Adjudicators must articulate specific reasons for finding corroborative evidence absent or inadequate.

# Country-conditions reports and expert testimony

Country-conditions evidence performs a dual function: it can corroborate the plausibility of an individual applicant's narrative by showing that the asserted patterns of persecution exist, and it can provide context for whether the claimed fear of future persecution is reasonable. Because asylum claims often rest on generalized patterns (e.g., persecution of a political group, violence against a particular gender or ethnic group), high-quality country-conditions evidence can be decisive.

Types and Sources. Country-conditions evidence commonly includes State Department human rights reports, reports from reputable NGOs (e.g., Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch), international body reports (UN agencies), scholarly articles, media reporting, and local or regional NGO documentation. Decisionmakers consider the reliability, recency, and detail of reports. Country-conditions reports may document general political conditions, patterns of abuse, or specific events relevant to a claimant's account.

Expert Witnesses. Experts provide interpretive assistance: they can explain local social structures, the behavior of government or non-state actors, the plausibility of particular claims, or the availability of protection. Experts may be academic specialists, country analysts, former government officials, or practitioners with first-hand experience. Courts generally allow expert testimony relevant to country conditions, country-specific language or cultural explanations, or trauma-informed assessments (e.g., psychological evaluations).

Limits and Proper Use. Experts should not testify to facts best proven by documentary evidence or first-hand observation they lack; instead they should offer specialized knowledge to interpret or contextualize facts. Adjudicators must evaluate the basis of the expert's knowledge and the methodology used in forming opinions. For example, an expert who relies on publicly available reports can synthesize those materials, but their value depends on the underlying evidence' reliability.

Corroboration vs. Country Reports. Country reports can corroborate the existence of a pattern of persecution and thus strengthen the plausibility of an applicant's claim. However, they usually cannot substitute for evidence that a particular applicant was targeted unless they describe specific incidents or systemic targeting of a group matching the applicant's profile. Judges should carefully link conclusions drawn from country reports to the specific elements of the claim (e.g., nexus to protected ground, likelihood of future harm, availability of state protection).

# Weight of affidavits and secondary evidence

Affidavits and secondary evidence frequently appear in asylum records. The law sets principles for assessing their reliability and weight.

Affidavits and Third-Party Statements. Affidavits from witnesses can corroborate claims of incidents, membership in organizations, or threats. The reliability of such affidavits depends on their source, potential bias, content specificity, and whether the affiant has direct knowledge. Affidavits from family or close associates can be powerful, but they require scrutiny for possible interest or collusion. Adjudicators look for detail, dates, places, and explanations for how the witness knows the facts asserted.

Secondary Evidence. Secondary evidence includes news reports, NGO accounts, and other materials not generated contemporaneously with the claimed events. The weight of secondary evidence depends on the source's reliability, independence, and corroborative value. Multiple independent reports corroborating the same pattern of abuse increase probative force. Be wary of duplicative or derivative materials that merely repeat a single source.

Corroboration Standards for Affidavits and Secondary Evidence. The key inquiry is whether the secondary evidence makes the applicant's testimony more likely true than not. A few principles guide that inquiry:

1. Specificity: A detailed affidavit that corroborates particular facts (names, dates, patterns) is more persuasive than generalized assertions.

2. Independence: Affidavits from neutral third parties or reputable organizations are more credible than statements from interested parties.

3. Contemporaneity: Contemporary documents (medical, police) are more probative than post hoc letters prepared for immigration proceedings, though post hoc evidence may still be valuable if credible and consistent.

4. Corroborative Mosaic: Multiple pieces of complementary secondary evidence that together make the applicant’s account plausible can suffice even if no single document proves the central facts.

Problems with After-the-Fact Affidavits. Adjudicators are properly skeptical of affidavits drafted after asylum proceedings begin because they may be tailored to support the claim. Such materials are not necessarily excluded, but their timing and the circumstances of preparation should factor into weight. Proof of independent creation or contemporaneity increases probative value.

Chain of Custody and Authenticity. For documentary evidence, adjudicators must consider authenticity. If the record contains foreign-language documents, certified translations should be provided to establish meaning. When authenticity or authorship is uncertain, decisionmakers may request clarifying evidence. Courts expect adjudicators to explain how they evaluated the affidavits’ and documents’ plausibility and reliability.

# Adverse credibility vs. credible but insufficient claims

An adverse credibility finding and a determination that an applicant's claim is credible but insufficient for relief are distinct outcomes with different legal consequences.

Adverse Credibility: Meaning and Effects. An adverse credibility determination means the adjudicator finds the applicant's testimony not credible. This finding typically renders the applicant's uncorroborated testimony insufficient to meet the statutory standard for asylum, withholding of removal, or protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT). Because credibility often goes to the core facts (e.g., whether the applicant was persecuted or has a well-founded fear), an adverse finding can be dispositive of all forms of protection that hinge on the underlying facts.

Procedural Requirements for Adverse Credibility. A lawful adverse credibility determination must be supported by specific, cogent reasons linked to evidence in the record. The decisionmaker should identify the inconsistencies, implausibilities, or falsehoods, explain why alternative explanations are not persuasive, and, if appropriate, indicate whether corroboration could cure the deficiency. Silent or conclusory adverse findings are vulnerable on appeal.

Credible but Insufficient: Concept and Consequences. In some cases, an adjudicator accepts an applicant's account as credible but concludes that, even taking those facts as true, the legal elements for relief are not satisfied. This can occur where the facts do not establish persecution, lack of nexus to a protected ground, availability of state protection, or where the likelihood of future persecution does not meet the statutory standard. A finding that testimony is credible but legally insufficient still requires the adjudicator to explain how the law applies to the facts.

Different Standards and Remedies. The distinction matters for review and for alternative forms of relief. If an application is denied because testimony is credible but does not meet asylum criteria, an applicant may seek relief under different statutory provisions (e.g., withholding of removal, present-tense CAT protection) if those standards are met. Conversely, an adverse credibility finding often precludes relief unless independent corroboration of key facts exists or the applicant can overcome the adverse finding on appeal.

How Courts Treat These Findings. Appellate review treats credibility findings as factual determinations subject to deferential standards. Where the administrative record contains specific, supported reasons for an adverse credibility determination, courts usually uphold it unless the reasons are irrational or unsupported. When a decision finds the applicant credible but legally insufficient, courts review the legal application of facts de novo to the extent they are legal conclusions, but will accept the factual findings underlying the legal conclusion if supported by substantial evidence.

Remedial Role of Corroboration. Corroborative evidence can convert an otherwise insufficient or doubtful case into a grant of relief. If the adjudicator finds testimony credible but incomplete on certain points critical to eligibility, targeted corroboration may establish the missing elements. Conversely, if an adverse credibility finding rests on minor inconsistencies reasonably explained or corrected by corroboration, documentary or expert evidence can rehabilitate the claim.

# Conclusion: Applying standards in practice

In practice, adjudicators must apply the REAL ID Act’s broad catalog of credibility considerations while adhering to administrative-law constraints: they must give specific, reasoned explanations for adverse findings, allow applicants a fair opportunity to present corroborating evidence or to explain gaps, and weigh country-conditions and expert evidence carefully. The applicant bears the ultimate burden of proof, but a credible account alone may carry that burden; where credibility is in question, corroboration is often required. Affidavits, secondary evidence, and expert testimony are valuable tools for corroboration, but their weight depends on specificity, independence, authenticity, and timing. Finally, distinguishing between an adverse credibility finding and a finding of credible but legally insufficient evidence is crucial, both substantively and procedurally, because each carries different implications for relief and for appellate review. Understanding these doctrines and their interaction is essential preparation for evaluating asylum opinions and for effective advocacy in asylum proceedings.

Introduction

Appellate review in immigration and administrative law is bounded by procedural prerequisites that protect the agency’s ability to decide cases and preserve an adequate record for judicial review. Courts often dismiss or decline to reach legal claims that were not adequately presented to, or decided by, the administrative body. This chapter explains the principal doctrines that govern when an issue is preserved for appellate review, what it means to exhaust administrative remedies under 8 U.S.C. § 1252(d)(1), how to present claims to the Immigration Judge (IJ) and Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), and how failure to raise claims can result in waiver or abandonment. It also covers limits on judicial review in the face of post hoc rationalizations—often described under the Chenery principle—and the particular consequences when Convention Against Torture (CAT) and constitutional due process claims are not pursued administratively.

# Statutory Exhaustion: 8 U.S.C. § 1252(d)(1)

Statutory exhaustion is a threshold jurisdictional or prudential requirement that often determines whether a federal court may reach the merits of an immigration claim. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1252(d)(1), a court "may review a final order of removal only if the alien has exhausted all administrative remedies available to the alien as of right." The provision reflects Congress’s delegation of initial factfinding and application of immigration law to the Executive Branch, and its desire that agencies have a full opportunity to apply their expertise and correct errors before a court intervenes.

Scope and purpose. Section 1252(d)(1) requires that claims be presented to the administrative adjudicator in a manner that gives the agency an opportunity to consider and rule on them. The exhaustion rule furthers several goals: it ensures institutional respect for agency expertise; it prevents parties from bypassing procedures designed to build an adequate factual and legal record; it conserves judicial resources by permitting the agency to correct its own errors; and it promotes the development of a consistent body of administrative rulings.

What it requires. Exhaustion under § 1252(d)(1) typically means that the specific legal or factual claim must have been raised before the IJ and the BIA (when the BIA has jurisdiction to review that claim) in a way that puts the agency on notice of the claim and allows it to rule. Mere general references to law or facts are often insufficient; courts look to whether the issue was clearly presented and argued so the agency had a fair opportunity to address it. For legal claims, stating the legal theory or citing controlling law is important. For factual claims, the evidence underlying the allegation must ordinarily have been introduced into the record.

Timing and available remedies. The statute speaks of administrative remedies "available … as of right." Thus, a claim that could be raised through appeal to the BIA, through a motion to reopen or reconsider, or through a timely objection in the proceedings must generally be pursued. If an avenue of administrative review was not available by right at the relevant time (for example, because of a jurisdictional bar or untimely filing), courts may consider whether exhaustion is excused.

Exceptions and equitable considerations. Although § 1252(d)(1) is broadly applied, courts recognize several narrow circumstances in which a failure to exhaust may be excused. Typical exceptions include (1) lack of jurisdiction in the agency to grant the relief sought; (2) futility where administrative remedies could not provide effective relief; (3) inadequacy of procedures to develop material issues of fact; and (4) waiver or forfeiture of the exhaustion requirement by the government. Moreover, due process concerns occasionally justify judicial consideration of unexhausted claims where administrative review would be inadequate to protect constitutional rights. The party invoking an exception bears the burden of proving its applicability.

Consequences of nonexhaustion. When an appellate court concludes that § 1252(d)(1) bars review, the court generally will dismiss for lack of jurisdiction or deny the petition for review without reaching the merits. In some circumstances the appropriate remedy is a remand to the agency for consideration of the claim in the first instance, particularly when the record is undeveloped or the agency has not had an opportunity to apply its expertise to the issue.

# Raising Issues Before the IJ and BIA

Preservation of issues for appeal requires affirmative steps during administrative proceedings. The way a claim is presented to the IJ and then to the BIA often determines whether federal courts may later review it.

How to present claims to the IJ. The initial forum is the immigration court, where factual testimony, documentary evidence, and legal arguments should be presented. For legal claims, counsel should make specific arguments on the record and, where appropriate, provide legal memoranda and citations. For factual claims such as eligibility for relief or asylum, witnesses should testify, exhibits should be admitted, and the record should include explicit assertions of the facts underlying the claim. If relief depends on particular legal standards (e.g., hardship, persecution, nexus, etc.), counsel should articulate how the facts meet each element.

Objections and motions. To preserve procedural challenges (e.g., to evidentiary rulings, bias, or denial of continuances), an explicit, timely objection should be made and, if necessary, a motion filed. Failure to make a contemporaneous objection generally forfeits the argument on appeal. Motions to reopen or reconsider before the IJ are available for certain procedural or newly discovered issues; an untimely or unspecific motion may not preserve the issue.

Administrative briefing and BIA appeals. After an IJ decision, appeals to the BIA must be timely and must identify the issues being appealed. The appellate brief to the BIA should present the legal and factual bases for each claim with sufficient detail to place the Board on notice and to permit adjudication. The BIA’s jurisdiction is limited by statute and regulation; certain claims (e.g., some forms of discretionary relief) may be subject to different standards of review or may not be reviewable at all by the BIA.

Writing the record. Because the administrative record is what a court will later review, counsel should ensure that the record contains the essential evidence and arguments. If the agency denies an evidentiary request or excludes evidence, that ruling should be preserved via a clear objection and an offer of proof to show what the excluded evidence would have demonstrated. An offer of proof is especially important for appellate review when oral testimony is excluded or curtailed.

Strategic considerations. Counsel must balance raising multiple claims with the risk of overburdening the administrative record. However, failing to present plausible legal arguments to the agency risks forfeiture. When in doubt, present the issue in the administrative forums and preserve the record for appeal; if the claim is novel, emphasize its significance and the factual basis for relief.

# Waiver and Abandonment on Appeal

Distinguishing waiver, forfeiture, and abandonment clarifies when an appellate tribunal may decline to consider a claim.

Waiver versus forfeiture. Waiver is an intentional relinquishment of a known right. A party who deliberately chooses not to raise an argument or who expressly abandons a claim has waived it; courts generally refuse to consider waived issues. Forfeiture (often used interchangeably with waiver in practice, but analytically distinct) occurs when a party fails to make a timely assertion of a right—typically through neglect, oversight, or inadvertence. Forfeited issues are generally reviewed only for plain error (in civil contexts, courts may sometimes apply less stringent standards), and the appellate court has discretion whether to consider them.

Abandonment. An appellate brief that fails to address an argument that was raised in the petition or earlier filings can be treated as abandoning that issue. Similarly, counsel’s explicit statements at oral argument that certain claims will not be pursued amount to abandonment. Courts routinely dismiss or refuse to consider issues that the appellant abandons by omission.

Standards of review for preserved and unpreserved claims. When an issue is properly preserved (raised and decided in the administrative forums), courts review legal questions de novo and factual findings under the substantial evidence standard (or the deferential standards applicable under the Immigration and Nationality Act). Procedural and constitutional claims may receive heightened scrutiny. For forfeited issues that were not preserved, courts apply plain-error review: the appellant must show an error that was clear or obvious and that affected substantial rights; even where plain error is demonstrated, reversal is discretionary and requires a showing that the error seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of the judicial proceedings.

Consequences of tactical decisions. Strategic decisions to focus on certain claims may lead to waiver of others. Appellants should be cautious: courts penalize gamesmanship such as raising new arguments on appeal that were deliberately withheld from the administrative proceeding. Where counsel consciously chooses an argument in administrative proceedings to the exclusion of alternatives, appellate courts are reluctant to entertain late-shifted theories.

Preservation doctrine’s interplay with judicial economy. The doctrines serve to limit appellate dockets and to require parties to build and test their claims in the agency setting. When a party interposes a claim in an incomplete or ambiguous fashion, appellate courts may remand for clarification rather than decide the matter, particularly where the administrative record is insufficient.

# Chenery and Limits on Post Hoc Rationales

The Chenery principle—rooted in administrative law—constrains courts from upholding agency action on grounds not invoked by the agency itself. The basic rule is that a court reviewing an administrative decision must judge the propriety of that decision based on the reasons the agency provided when it acted; courts should not supply their own reasons or affirm on new rationales offered for the first time on judicial review.

Rationale and application. The Chenery limitation rests on the separation of functions between agencies and courts. Agencies, not courts, bear the responsibility for articulating the reasons for their decisions and for developing the administrative record to support those reasons. Allowing courts to introduce new rationales on review would allow the agency to avoid articulating the real reasons for its decisions and would deprive interested parties of the opportunity to respond in the administrative process.

Practical effect in review. When an agency provides one set of reasons in its order, an appellate court must evaluate whether those reasons are adequate and consistent with the governing law. If the reasons are inadequate, courts must generally remand so the agency can develop an appropriate record and apply its expertise. Courts normally will not affirm based on alternative grounds that the agency did not adopt and did not have an opportunity to consider. Where the agency’s stated reason is factually unsupported or legally deficient, remand is the typical remedy.

Exceptions and nuances. There are limited circumstances in which courts may sustain agency action on grounds not articulated in the agency’s decision. For example, if the agency’s original rationale is validly susceptible to an interpretation that embraces the alternative ground, or if the alternative ground merely supplies additional reasons that inexorably flow from the agency’s stated rationale, courts sometimes will consider them. Also, when the new rationale constitutes a pure legal question subject to de novo review and does not alter the record’s factual underpinning, courts may be more willing to consider it. Finally, courts may treat later-advanced rationales as harmless if the record demonstrates that the agency would inevitably have adopted the alternative ground. These exceptions are narrow; the overarching rule disfavors post hoc rationalization.

Implications for litigants. Practitioners should ensure that all legal and factual bases for agency action are explicitly articulated in administrative decisions and briefing. If an appealing party believes the agency’s reasons are insufficient, the proper course is to ask for remand or further explanation rather than urging the court to supply new rationales. Conversely, government counsel should avoid relying on new theories for the first time on appeal when the agency did not adopt them below.

# Consequences for CAT and Due Process Claims Not Pursued

Convention Against Torture (CAT) claims and constitutional due process claims have particular features in the administrative-exhaustion context. These claims often arise in removal proceedings and have distinct procedural and substantive aspects that affect preservation and review.

CAT claims. CAT relief is distinct from asylum and withholding in that it focuses on the likelihood of torture upon removal, rather than persecution. Administrative exhaustion for CAT claims generally requires that the alien present the claim to the IJ and, if applicable, to the BIA in a manner that permits adjudication. Because CAT claims are sometimes viewed as statutory or treaty-based rather than purely constitutional, agencies and courts carefully enforce exhaustion: a failure to raise a CAT claim administratively may bar judicial review under § 1252(d)(1). Moreover, CAT often requires the development of a factual record—expert testimony, country conditions evidence, and individualized proof—so mere passing references are often insufficient.

Timing and specificity. To preserve a CAT claim, the applicant should specifically request CAT protection at the merits hearing and, if adverse relief is ordered, should raise the CAT claim in the appeal to the BIA. The claim must be framed with adequate specificity so the IJ and BIA can consider whether the factual record supports CAT relief. Generalized statements that an applicant might face harm on return may not suffice.

Due process claims. Procedural and substantive due process claims may arise from alleged defects in the removal proceedings—bias, denial of counsel, inadequate notice, or denial of a full and fair hearing. Preservation of constitutional claims requires raising the alleged defect before the IJ and, typically, to the BIA. Many due process errors are fact-specific and therefore require contemporaneous objection and a clear record of the error and its consequences.

Consequences of failure to pursue CAT or due process claims. When CAT or due process claims are not pursued before the agency, federal courts commonly decline to review them under the exhaustion doctrine. The result can be dismissal of the claim for lack of jurisdiction or denial on procedural grounds without a merits examination. Because CAT relief depends heavily on record evidence, an undeveloped administrative record often leads to remand rather than outright relief.

Equitable excuses: ineffective assistance and changed circumstances. There are narrow equitable paths to excusing failure to raise CAT or constitutional claims. One path is to demonstrate that ineffective assistance of counsel prevented proper presentation of the claim. Typically, the claimant must show deficient performance by counsel and prejudice—i.e., a reasonable likelihood that the outcome would have been different but for the error. Administrative rules often require a motion to reopen that documents the ineffective assistance according to particular procedural safeguards (for example, an affidavit, complaint to a bar association, and a statement of the facts alleged to have been omitted). Another path is to show a material change in country conditions or new evidence that could not have been reasonably discovered and presented earlier; in such cases a motion to reopen is the normal procedural vehicle.

Balancing finality and fairness. Courts balance the need for orderly administrative processes against the serious consequences facing individuals removed without adequate protection. Where exhaustion would be futile or where a fundamental constitutional right is at stake, courts sometimes find that exhaustion should not bar review. Nonetheless, these exceptions are narrowly applied, and counsel should not rely on them as a substitute for diligent presentation of claims in the administrative forums.

Conclusion

Issue preservation, exhaustion, waiver, and abandonment are critical gatekeeping doctrines that shape whether and how federal courts review immigration and administrative decisions. Section 1252(d)(1) embodies the statutory exhaustion requirement, placing the burden on litigants to present issues clearly to the agency. To preserve claims, counsel must develop the record and make timely, specific arguments before the IJ and the BIA. Failure to do so risks waiver or forfeiture, with appellate review limited to plain-error standards or precluded entirely. The Chenery principle restricts courts from supplying new rationales on review, reinforcing the need for the agency to articulate its grounds. Finally, CAT and due process claims are especially vulnerable to forfeiture if not pursued administratively, though narrow equitable exceptions may apply in exceptional circumstances. Mastery of these doctrines is essential for effective advocacy and for understanding the procedural posture of appellate opinions in this area.

Introduction

Immigration removal proceedings present a hybrid administrative-judicial process in which noncitizens face the prospect of loss of liberty and expulsion from the United States without the procedural trappings of ordinary criminal courts. Because removal carries grave consequences, constitutional due process principles and federal statutes establish a baseline of procedural protections. At the same time, Congress has limited the federal judiciary’s role and preserved the government’s authority to deport noncitizens under specified procedures. A law student preparing to read judicial opinions about due process and the right to counsel in removal proceedings should understand the governing doctrines: the limited but meaningful right to counsel at no government expense, the core elements of procedural due process (notice, opportunity to be heard, and an impartial adjudicator), statutory and regulatory protections such as interpreters and admissibility of evidence, the substantive and procedural test for prejudice when a due process violation is alleged, and the standards that apply when a noncitizen seeks a continuance to obtain counsel.

This chapter explains these doctrines in detail, emphasizing legal rules, typical fact-patterns, burdens of proof, and the types of reasoning courts use when they assess whether an immigration adjudicator complied with constitutional and statutory obligations.

# Right to counsel at no government expense

A central, often-misunderstood principle is that noncitizens in removal proceedings do not have a right to appointed counsel at government expense. Unlike criminal defendants enjoying the Sixth Amendment right to appointed counsel when incarceration is possible, respondents in civil immigration removal proceedings have no entitlement to government-paid lawyers as a matter of constitutional or statutory law. The consequence is that removal proceedings are typically adversarial but counsel must be retained privately or provided pro bono.

Statutory and regulatory framework. Federal statutes and regulations expressly provide the right to be represented, at the noncitizen’s own expense, by counsel of the noncitizen’s choosing. Rules require that an appearance of counsel be filed with the immigration court and that counsel comply with registration, admission, and practice requirements. The regulations also describe form and timing requirements for substitutions of counsel and withdrawals, and they empower immigration judges (IJs) and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) to sanction practice misconduct.

Scope and limits of the right. The “right to representation” grants respondents the freedom to retain counsel but does not guarantee appointed counsel, nor does it guarantee that any particular lawyer will be permitted to appear (unlicensed practice may be excluded). Because counsel is not provided by the government, access to counsel depends on a respondent’s resources, availability of pro bono representation, and local legal services. Courts are attentive to the practical consequences of this lack of appointment, particularly where language barriers, mental incapacity, or other factors impede a respondent’s ability to proceed pro se.

Consequences of lack of counsel. In evaluating claims that lack of counsel violated due process, courts consider whether the respondent was effectively denied a meaningful opportunity to present claims or defenses. Where a respondent’s inability to obtain counsel results in demonstrable prejudice—such as failure to apply for relief or waiver of claims—courts may find a due process violation. But lack of counsel, alone, is rarely sufficient to mandate relief unless connected to a constitutional error (for example, an IJ’s refusal to continue proceedings to allow counsel to enter, where prejudice is shown).

Waiver and competency. A respondent may choose to proceed pro se and, if the choice is knowing and voluntary, courts will generally treat the decision as a waiver of counsel. Courts examine the record to determine whether the waiver was informed and voluntary: did the respondent understand the nature of the proceedings, the charges, and the consequences? Where cognitive or linguistic disabilities impair a respondent’s capacity, apparent waivers are scrutinized more carefully.

# Notice, opportunity to be heard, and impartial adjudicator

The Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause protects noncitizens against deprivation of life, liberty, or property without due process of law, and its protections apply in removal proceedings. The core requirements consist of timely and adequate notice of the proceedings, a meaningful opportunity to be heard, and an impartial decisionmaker. The precise content of due process in any given immigration case is context-dependent and is frequently articulated by applying a balancing test.

Notice. Due process requires that a respondent receive adequate notice of the charges (the charging document), the time and place of the hearing, and the legal bases on which removal is sought. Statutorily, the Notice to Appear (NTA) or equivalent charging instrument must inform a respondent of the nature of the proceeding and the allegations. Adequate notice includes sufficient specificity to enable a respondent to prepare a defense: identification of the removability grounds alleged, the immigration status implicated, and the opportunity to assert relief or protection from removal.

Practical problems with notice arise when NTAs are defective (for example, lacking a time or place) or when service is disputed. Courts assess whether any defects deprived the respondent of meaningful notice and whether service was reasonably calculated to inform the respondent. Where notice is constitutionally inadequate, the remedy ranges from reopening or remanding proceedings to dismissal, depending on the circumstances and the degree of prejudice.

Opportunity to be heard. The opportunity to be heard is not an abstract right; it requires the ability to present evidence, witnesses, and legal arguments in a meaningful manner. This encompasses access to counsel (at private expense), time to prepare, the ability to obtain and present documentary and testimonial evidence, and access to interpreters where necessary. Procedural safeguards include the right to a hearing before an IJ, the right to present a claim for relief (such as asylum or cancellation), and the right to appeal adverse decisions. The timing and adequacy of the opportunity to be heard may be measured against the Mathews balancing test, which weighs: (1) the private interest at stake; (2) the risk of erroneous deprivation under the existing procedures and the probable value of additional protections; and (3) the government’s interest, including fiscal and administrative burdens.

Impartial adjudicator. Due process requires decisionmakers who are neutral and independent. The constitutional inquiry focuses on whether an IJ (or other immigration adjudicator) had actual bias or an appearance of impartiality. Types of impermissible bias include: direct financial interest in a specific outcome; prejudgment of facts or issues such that the judge will not consider the evidence; personal relationships or animus that reasonably call into question neutrality; and structural bias arising from institutional arrangements that create an unacceptable potential for partiality.

Courts evaluate claimed bias under both procedural and constitutional standards. For a due process violation, the showing must generally make probable actual bias or a serious risk of it. Mere adverse rulings or harsh questioning do not by themselves establish bias. Remedies for proven lack of impartiality include recusal of the adjudicator, remand for a new hearing before a different adjudicator, or other corrective actions.

Interaction among the elements. These three elements—notice, opportunity to be heard, and impartial adjudicator—operate together. A deficiency in one area may be cured, or mitigated, by strength in another, but a substantial failure in any core element that results in a deprivation of a meaningful hearing will trigger due process scrutiny and possible relief. Courts typically focus on the actual impact of procedural defects on the fairness of the outcome.

# Interpreter and evidentiary fairness

Language access and basic evidentiary fairness are essential to a meaningful opportunity to be heard. Because many respondents are not fluent in English, the availability of competent interpretation and fair rules of evidence plays a central role in ensuring that proceedings are fundamentally fair.

Right to an interpreter. Federal immigration rules and constitutional principles require that respondents who do not understand or speak English have access to interpreters, provided at no cost in most hearing contexts. The obligation to provide an interpreter aims to ensure that the respondent understands the nature of the proceedings, can communicate with counsel, and can testify and respond to evidence. The interpreter must be competent and impartial; poor or biased interpretation may vitiate the fairness of a hearing.

The practical evaluation of interpreter adequacy considers: whether the respondent’s need for an interpreter was brought to the court’s attention, whether an interpreter was made available in the respondent’s language, whether the interpreter accurately translated questions, answers, and documents, and whether any significant mistranslation affected the outcome. Courts may remand where the record shows material misunderstandings caused by inadequate interpretation.

Evidentiary standards and fairness. Immigration proceedings employ an evidentiary regime distinct from criminal or civil courts. Formal rules of evidence do not strictly apply; hearsay is commonly admissible and often necessary because evidence from foreign jurisdictions or documents from remote sources cannot be produced in person. Nevertheless, admissibility does not equate to dispositive weight: hearsay is admissible but must be weighed for reliability and probative value.

Key features include:

- Burdens of proof. The government bears the burden of proving removability by clear and convincing evidence in some contexts (e.g., certain criminal grounds), and by a preponderance in others; for removal generally, the government must establish the factual predicates for removability. Respondents bear the burden of establishing eligibility for discretionary relief or affirmative forms of relief and protection (e.g., asylum, cancellation, voluntary departure). The allocation of burdens affects which facts and documents each party must present.

- Documentary evidence. Documents may be submitted to support claims of identity, nationality, persecution, family relationships, or hardship. Adjudicators examine authenticity and probative value; they may request corroboration when documentary evidence is sparse. Weight is often placed on contemporaneous records and independent country-condition reports.

- Witness testimony. Witnesses may testify in person or, in some circumstances, by proffer or written statements. Credibility determinations are critical when testimonial evidence is the primary source and adjudicators assess demeanor, consistency, detail, and plausibility.

- Corroboration and adverse inferences. When a respondent’s testimony lacks corroboration, an IJ may request or require corroboration and may draw adverse inferences if credible corroborating evidence is available but not produced. Conversely, the government’s failure to produce evidence that it could reasonably be expected to possess may also undermine credibility.

- Reliability and due process. Because hearsay and documents from abroad can be crucial, the due process question is whether the rules and procedures permit a fair opportunity to contest adverse evidence or to present countervailing proof. Respondents must generally be given notice of adverse documentary evidence and an opportunity to examine and rebut it.

Overall, evidentiary fairness means that material evidence controlling the outcome must be subject to notice and some meaningful opportunity for challenge. Where a respondent could not reasonably procure contradicting evidence or was not given a chance to respond to significant adverse evidence, the proceeding may fall short of due process.

# Prejudice requirement for due process violations

Not every procedural error in removal proceedings rises to the level of a constitutional violation or warrants reversal. Courts demand a showing of prejudice: the procedural deficiency must have been material and likely to have affected the outcome. The prejudice requirement functions as a limiting principle, ensuring that relief follows only where the error is not harmless.

The standard for prejudice. In constitutional and statutory contexts, courts often evaluate whether the error affected “substantial rights” or whether it was “prejudicial.” The inquiry asks whether there is a reasonable probability that, absent the error, the outcome would have been different. The contours of prejudice vary with the nature of the claimed violation:

- Structural vs. trial error. Some violations are considered structural (e.g., total deprivation of counsel in a criminal trial where counsel is constitutionally guaranteed) and require automatic reversal. Because there is no constitutional right to appointed counsel in civil immigration proceedings, most alleged errors are treated as trial errors requiring a showing of prejudice rather than per se reversal.

- Harmless error approach. Courts often apply a harmless-error analysis, asking whether the error had a substantial and injurious effect on the decision. If the record independently supports the outcome and the error relates only to noncritical process, the error is deemed harmless.

- Contextual assessment. The requisite showing of prejudice depends on context. If an IJ denied a continuance to obtain counsel or refused an interpreter, courts assess how those denials affected the respondent’s ability to present key evidence, testify, or apply for relief. Where the record contains corroborative evidence submitted late or not at all due to the error, courts are likely to find prejudice.

Proving prejudice. To satisfy the burden, a respondent typically must identify specific ways in which the procedural failing undermined the proceeding: for example, that counsel would have filed a meritorious application for relief, that necessary documentary proof or witness testimony could have been obtained with time or assistance, or that a different credibility finding was likely with proper translation or legal advice. General assertions of disadvantage are usually insufficient.

Remedies when prejudice is shown. If prejudice is established, courts may order remand for further proceedings (often the typical remedy), reopen proceedings, or in rare cases dismiss the removal proceedings. Where prejudice is minimal and the overall record supports the initial decision, courts may affirm despite procedural imperfections.

Interaction with appellate review. On administrative appeal, the BIA and federal courts defer to factual findings but review constitutional questions and legal interpretations de novo. However, when a respondent alleges a due process violation, appellate tribunals consider whether the error was prejudicial to the outcome. Demonstrating prejudice is critical to prevail in appellate review or habeas corpus petitions seeking relief from removal orders.

# Continuances to obtain counsel and related standards

Continuances are routine requests in immigration proceedings, typically made to obtain counsel, gather evidence, or resolve jurisdictional issues. Immigration judges have broad discretion to grant or deny continuances, but that discretion is constrained by due process principles. When a respondent seeks a continuance to obtain counsel, courts consider several factors to determine whether the IJ abused discretion or committed a constitutional violation.

Legal framework governing continuances. Regulations and case law vest IJs with the authority to control the conduct of hearings and to grant continuances for good cause. “Good cause” is evaluated in light of the respondent’s need for time, the reasonableness of the request, the government’s interest in expedition, and case-specific logistics, such as availability of counsel or witnesses and the likelihood that a continuance will produce significant new evidence.

Factors courts examine. In reviewing denials of continuances, courts typically consider:

- The reason for the continuance. A purpose grounded in obtaining counsel is legitimate; courts distinguish between a specific, actionable plan to secure counsel (such as a scheduled meeting with a secured attorney) and vague intentions (e.g., “I hope to get a lawyer”). Requests tied to a specific, imminent opportunity to obtain counsel weigh in favor of granting a continuance.

- Diligence. Courts evaluate whether the respondent acted diligently to obtain counsel or evidence. Delay that appears strategic or dilatory undermines claims for relief. A history of repeated continuance requests without concrete progress is disfavored.

- Likelihood of securing counsel. Evidence that counsel has been contacted, has agreed in principle to represent the respondent, or is otherwise available supports granting a continuance. Mere assertions that counsel might be found are insufficient.

- Length of the requested continuance. Reasonable short continuances are more likely to be granted; prolonged or indefinite delays weigh against continuance. The expected time necessary to obtain counsel is balanced against the government’s interest in prompt adjudication.

- Prejudice to the government and witnesses. The government’s need for finality and efficiency, along with witness availability and potential loss of evidence over time, are weighed against the respondent’s interest.

- Complexity of the case. Complex matters involving multiple claims for relief, foreign records, or legal issues may merit additional time to prepare.

- Prior continuances. A history of prior continuances for the same reason reduces the likelihood of a favorable ruling absent new justification.

When denial becomes a due process issue. A denial of a continuance becomes constitutionally problematic when it deprives the respondent of a meaningful opportunity to be heard. Courts ask whether the denial prevented the respondent from obtaining counsel who would have changed the outcome—i.e., whether prejudice exists. A court is particularly sensitive to denials where the respondent is unrepresented, does not speak English, has mental incapacity, or where critical evidence is unavailable without delay.

How courts assess claims that counsel would have obtained a different result. When a continuance is denied, petitioners must often corroborate that counsel would have been obtained and would have made a material difference. This can be shown by affidavits from the would-be counsel, proof of communication with counsel, or particulars about the evidence counsel would have procured and how it would have affected eligibility or credibility. Courts look for tangible indications of prejudice rather than speculative assertions.

Practical procedural protections. To protect due process while guarding against abuse, IJs often require respondents seeking continuances to provide:

- A written statement describing the efforts made to obtain counsel;
- Contact information for the prospective counsel and any communications demonstrating counsel’s willingness to appear;
- An estimate of the time needed and a plan for timely rescheduling;
- A description of the evidence or witnesses expected to be secured during the continuance and why they are material.

Conditional continuances. Immigration judges sometimes grant limited or conditional continuances—for example, a short postponement to allow counsel to file a notice of appearance—rather than indefinite delays. This serves efficiency while providing the respondent a realistic opportunity to secure representation.

Remedies for erroneous denials. If a court determines that an IJ abused discretion in denying a continuance and that the denial was prejudicial, typical remedies include vacating the removal order and remanding for a new hearing. The precise remedy depends on whether the error can be corrected on remand and whether additional prejudice can be remedied by reopening the case.

Conclusion

A firm grasp of the procedural protections in removal proceedings equips students to understand judicial opinions that assess the fairness of immigration adjudications. The interplay among the right to retain counsel (without entitlement to appointed counsel), the constitutional core of notice, a meaningful opportunity to be heard, and an impartial adjudicator determines whether proceedings pass constitutional muster. Interpreter access and evidentiary practices are critical to ensuring that hearings are fair in practice, not just on paper. Critically, claimants alleging due process violations must typically show prejudice—that the procedural error meaningfully affected the outcome. Finally, continuances to obtain counsel raise discretionary and constitutional questions that hinge on diligence, likelihood of securing counsel, and the practical effect on the fairness of the proceeding. Understanding these doctrines prepares the reader to follow appellate reasoning about errors and remedies in the removal context.

Introduction

Distinguishing political persecution from criminal or personal targeting is critical in asylum and related refugee-law contexts. Many prospective claimants who flee violence or coercion come from environments where criminal actors—gangs, organized crime, cartels, or corrupt officials—are common perpetrators. At the same time, these actors may have complex motives that blend economic gain, personal grievance, and political aims. The legal question is whether persecutory acts are sufficiently connected to a protected ground—such as race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion—to meet the nexus element required for protection. This chapter provides doctrinal tools to separate ordinary criminal targeting from persecution on account of a protected ground, explains how courts infer motive, and describes how certain facts, statements, or patterns of conduct can convert seemingly criminal conduct into politically motivated persecution.

To understand the analysis that courts apply, a student must keep in mind two structural features. First, the claimant must show that a protected characteristic or perceived attribute was at least a central reason for the mistreatment. This is a motive requirement: the persecutor acted because of, or substantially because of, something that is a protected ground. Second, when the perpetrator is not a state actor, courts often require an added layer of factual showing about the state’s role—whether it condoned, acquiesced in, or was unable to control the persecutor—so that the harm can be attributed to the state or deemed actionable. How courts resolve motive inquiries depends heavily on contextual evidence: the words and actions of perpetrators, patterns of targeting, the broader political or criminal environment, and the temporal relationship between protected activity and harm.

# Economic motives vs. political motives

The starting point in many fact patterns is distinguishing an economic motive (extortion, ransom, theft, competition over markets) from a political motive (punishing or silencing opposition, coercing political change, attacking a social group). Economic motives are often the most obvious explanation in extortion and gang contexts: demands for money, control of territory, theft, or profit-seeking violence. Political motives arise when the actor seeks to advance or retaliate against political beliefs, political activities, or membership in a political group.

Legal analysis requires more than labeling the motive. Courts look at whether a protected ground—most often political opinion or membership in a particular social group—played a central role in the persecutor’s decision to harm the victim. Key distinctions include:

- Content of the demand. An actor making demands for money, property, or services—without reference to political views—tends to indicate an economic motive. Conversely, demands that include political concessions (cease political activities, support a movement, stop criticizing a political figure) point toward a political motive.

- Selective targeting. If actors extort many people of similar economic profile regardless of politics, the conduct is more likely economic. If they single out critics, activists, known dissidents, or members of a political group, that supports a political motive.

- Method and message. Economic extortioners may use anonymous threats or the prospect of violence to extract payments. Political persecution often contains symbolic violence or statements designed to silence, intimidate, or punish political expression. Public seizures, targeted public humiliation, or messaging that frames the victim as an enemy of a movement or regime is more readily characterized as political.

- Duration and sustainability. Economic crimes may be episodic: a single kidnapping for ransom, a one-time robbery, or a demand for protection money. Political persecution often involves sustained pressure—repeated harassment, imprisonment, trials on trumped-up charges, or ongoing surveillance—intended to alter political behavior. That said, a single severe act can still be persecution if it is on account of a protected ground.

Mixed motives are common. Organizations and fighters may profit economically even while chasing a political agenda; criminal groups may co-opt political grievances; and political actors may use criminal charges as pretext for suppression. The legal significance of mixed motives is that it will not necessarily defeat an asylum claim if the protected ground was one of the central reasons for the harm. The claimant bears the burden of showing nexus, but if substantial evidence reveals that political characteristics or perceived opinions materially contributed to the attack, the presence of an economic component does not automatically negate persecution.

# Personal vendettas and property disputes

Personal vendettas and property disputes often appear indistinguishable from persecution at first glance: threats, assaults, arson, land grabs, and forced evictions are both personal-criminal and persecutory on their face. The legal task is to determine whether the aggressor’s animus stems from a private, individualized grievance (personal vendetta) or whether that animus is tied to a protected ground.

Courts analyze several factors to differentiate these motives:

- Source and basis of the conflict. If the dispute arises from a private disagreement—an interpersonal quarrel, a family conflict, a business dispute—without reference to political characteristics, it points to a personal vendetta. If the dispute stems from, or is framed in terms of, the victim’s protected status (for example, land seizures targeted against a particular ethnic group to displace them), then the harm may be persecutory.

- Pattern and scope. A dispute involving one family or business that does not fit a broader pattern of group-wide targeting is more likely private. Conversely, systematic dispossession of property from members of an identifiable group signals persecution.

- Agent of persecution. When state actors use or manipulate private disputes to remove unwanted groups, courts are more likely to find political motive. Similarly, if private actors coordinate in a way that targets a group, and that conduct is politically motivated (e.g., ethnic cleansing), it is persecution.

- Substantive intent and outcome. If the ultimate goal is to deprive the victim of fundamental rights tied to identity—residence, livelihood, participation in political life—this weighs toward persecution rather than a simple property dispute. Forced displacement that aims to alter the demographic or political composition of a region suggests persecutory intent.

- Post-harm measures. Use of legal or administrative machinery to legitimize dispossession or to punish dissenters under the guise of civil law can be evidence of persecution. When discriminatory practices are formalized in otherwise neutral-seeming proceedings, the underlying motive may be political.

In assessing personal vendettas, courts also consider whether an alleged personal dispute is plausibly a pretext. Persecutors sometimes cloak political motives as personal quarrels to avoid scrutiny. Patterns of selective application, sudden escalation after political expression, or targeting of individuals who are vocal critics can indicate that a personal feud is in fact politically motivated.

# Evidence that converts criminal conduct into political persecution

Certain categories of evidence, when present, tend to tip the balance from ordinary criminality to persecution on account of a protected ground. No single piece of evidence is dispositive, but a combination can make a compelling nexus showing.

1. Perpetrator statements. Direct statements by the persecutor that reference the victim’s political views, membership in a group, or status as a dissident are powerful. Explicit threats to “punish” someone for their politics, calls for their silence, or admissions that the victim is being targeted “because” of perceived beliefs are strong indicators of motive. Courts give particular weight to contemporaneous statements made during or immediately before the harm.

2. Pattern evidence and selective targeting. Evidence that the persecutor targets a class of people defined by a protected characteristic—dissidents, union organizers, followers of a religion, members of an ethnic group—supports an inference of political persecution. Statistical disparities, lists of targeted names, and multiple similar incidents will help convert otherwise criminal acts (e.g., robberies, killings) into political persecution.

3. Political context and timing. Harm that follows political activity, public criticism, filing of complaints against officials, or involvement in organized political activity suggests a nexus. Temporal proximity between protected activity and persecution strengthens the claim; campaigns of reprisals immediately after public acts or statements are especially probative.

4. Use of political symbols or rhetoric. If perpetrators use political slogans, flags, or invoke ideological rhetoric when committing crimes, this contextualizes the harm as political. Similarly, punishment framed as “defending the state,” “combating traitors,” or “protecting the revolution” indicates political motive.

5. Disproportionate or unusual methods. Criminal actors seeking profit tend to employ methods focused on extraction with minimal risk to avoid law enforcement. By contrast, punishments intended to humiliate, deter, or scapegoat—show trials, public executions, torture used to silence dissent—are more consistent with political persecution.

6. State involvement, acquiescence, or failure to protect. When the state participates in or tolerates crimes against individuals on account of protected grounds—by providing resources, turning a blind eye, or using criminal laws selectively—criminal acts are elevated to persecution. Even where perpetrators are private actors, state failure to investigate or punish targeted attacks may demonstrate acquiescence sufficient to show a persecutory environment.

7. Documentary and corroborative evidence. Membership lists, minutes from meetings, intercepted communications, media reports, or police files that connect perpetrators’ motives to the victim’s protected characteristic help establish nexus. Expert reports on country conditions that document systematic repression of certain political groups also convert individual criminal acts into components of broader persecution.

Courts view the totality of the evidence. A well-founded fear or a finding of past persecution is supported by an accumulation of indicia—statements, patterns, context, and state behavior—that together demonstrate the protected ground’s centrality.

# Imputed political opinion and anti-corruption claims

Imputation occurs when a persecutor attributes a political opinion to the victim, regardless of the victim’s actual beliefs. Imputed political opinion can satisfy the nexus requirement if the persecutor targets the individual because the perpetrator believes the individual holds a disfavored opinion. The key legal considerations are what the persecutor perceived and whether that perception motivates the harm.

Elements of an imputation analysis include:

- Persecutor’s perception. The inquiry centers on how the persecutor viewed the victim. Did the actor believe the victim supported a particular political movement, belonged to a political group, or held certain views? Evidence of such perceptions can come from threats, labels used by perpetrators, or actions that signal the actor’s belief.

- Connection to a protected ground. The perceived attribute must fall within a protected ground category (typically political opinion, but sometimes membership in a social group defined by political activity or association). Merely perceiving someone as a criminal does not suffice unless that perception is tied to their political activity or status.

- Causation. The persecution must be on account of the imputed opinion—i.e., the perception motivated the harm. This is proven by connecting the persecutor’s statements, conduct, and target selection to the imputed belief.

Anti-corruption claims often involve complex interactions between criminality and political persecution. Individuals who expose corruption, report bribery, or otherwise confront state or non-state corruption may be at risk. The legal question is whether their anti-corruption activity constitutes a “political opinion” or falls within a protected social group.

Key doctrinal points for anti-corruption claims:

- Nature of anti-corruption activity. Private, pecuniary complaints (e.g., demanding repayment of a debt) differ from public whistleblowing or political advocacy. Where anti-corruption action is tied to efforts to change public policy, expose governmental wrongdoing, or engage in political debate, it more plausibly constitutes political expression.

- Persecutor’s motive. Did the persecutor target the complainant because the complainant opposed corruption, threatened the corrupt actors’ political ends, or publicly denounced officials? If so, the claimant may be able to show persecution on account of political opinion, even if the persecutor masks the attack as a criminal retaliation.

- Imputation of opinion to the claimant. Corrupt actors may view a whistleblower as an opponent or as aligned with a political faction, and will target them for that reason. In such cases, the persecution rests on the persecutor’s perception and is thus remediable under the imputation doctrine.

- Public vs. private dimension. Anti-corruption activities with public visibility—media revelations, organizing protests, filing public complaints—are stronger bases for political-opinion claims than private grievances pursued solely through personal channels. Public exposure poses a political threat to corrupt networks and better satisfies the “on account of” requirement.

Courts scrutinize anti-corruption claims for signs that the persecutor’s motive is genuinely political rather than purely economic. Demonstrating that the violence was aimed at quashing dissent, preserving political influence, or suppressing public criticism helps establish persecution. Conversely, if the attack was primarily about silencing a single private complaint that could have been economically resolved, the case risks being classified as criminal retaliation.

# Assessing statements by perpetrators and temporal links

Perpetrator statements and the timing of harm relative to protected activity are among the most probative forms of evidence of motive. Courts treat both forms of evidence as central to causation analysis, while recognizing limitations—perpetrators may avoid explicit statements, lie, or issue ambiguous remarks. A nuanced approach assesses reliability, corroboration, and context.

Perpetrator statements

Direct statements—threats, denunciations, or confessions—are highly persuasive because they reveal the actor’s contemporaneous motive. Courts consider several attributes of such statements:

- Timing. Statements made during or immediately before the harm carry more weight than later denials or post-hoc rationalizations. A threat uttered while the victim is being attacked directly links motive and action.

- Content and specificity. Statements that explicitly reference the victim’s protected characteristic, political opinions, or role as a dissident are particularly probative. Vague insults or general criminal threats are less helpful.

- Speaker identity and credibility. The credibility of the speaker and the circumstances under which the statement was made matter: a leader announcing a campaign against “traitors” has greater evidentiary value than an anonymous comment from a peripheral actor.

- Corroboration. Independent corroboration of a statement—photographs, third-party testimony, contemporaneous recordings, or subsequent similar statements—reinforces reliability.

- Consistency with other evidence. A statement that fits the larger pattern of conduct, country conditions, and known motives of the group strengthens a claim. Incongruity between the content of the statement and objective facts raises suspicion.

Temporal links

Temporal proximity between protected activity and harm is a common, though not dispositive, indicator of motive. The legal significance of timing can be understood in three respects:

- Immediate retaliation. Harm occurring directly after protected conduct—such as an arrest or beating following a protest—strongly suggests retaliation for that conduct and thus political motive.

- Short-term escalation. A rapid intensification of harassment or violence after political expression supports a causal link; courts infer retaliatory intent where discriminative treatment follows shortly after the protected act.

- Long-term patterns. Delayed reprisals do not negate political causation. Persecution can be longstanding or occur after a latent period; what matters is whether a temporal sequence, viewed alongside other evidence, indicates that political factors motivated the harm. For example, a campaign of intimidation that ramps up around an election or after a transfer of power can evidence politically timed harassment.

Interpreting timing requires care:

- Avoid overreliance on clock time. A short temporal gap is not necessary for a nexus finding; political motives can manifest after months or years. Conversely, a short gap is not always dispositive of political intent if other evidence indicates an alternative motive.

- Combine timing with other indicia. Temporal links are strongest when coupled with statements, patterns, or contextual information that together point to political motive.

- Consider known patterns of repression. In some environments, states or groups may respond to political acts not immediately but through staged legal processes or indirect measures. Understanding typical modes of reprisal in the claimant’s country may be critical.

Burden and standards of proof

Finally, courts apply these tools within specific burdens of proof. For claims of past persecution or a well-founded fear of future persecution, applicants traditionally must show that a protected ground was at least a central reason for the harm. This is a fact-intensive inquiry where the applicant bears the burden of proof and must produce credible, corroborated evidence when available. The standard of proof varies by relief sought; asylum claims generally require a lower, well-founded fear standard, while withholding claims may require showing that the central reason standard is met to a higher degree.

Conclusion

In mixed-threat environments, separating criminality from political persecution requires careful assessment of motive. Courts look to perpetrator statements, patterns of targeting, the political context, state involvement or acquiescence, temporal links between protected activity and harm, and corroborating documentary or expert evidence. Economic motives such as extortion do not automatically negate political motives; equally, private disputes and property quarrels are not per se persecution. Imputed political opinion and anti-corruption activities present special issues because persecutors’ perceptions matter and whistleblowing can be political if it challenges public authority or policy. The ultimate legal question is whether the evidence as a whole establishes that the victim was targeted on account of a protected ground—whether actual, imputed, or tied to membership in a protected group—taking into account the credibility and weight of statements, timing, and patterns within the sociopolitical setting.

Introduction

Asylum law pivots around the statutory definition of a "refugee" and the doctrinal elements courts use to determine whether an applicant meets that definition. Before reading any judicial opinion that applies asylum law, a student should understand the statutory baseline, how the doctrine distinguishes past persecution from future fear, the role of state protection (the "unable or unwilling" prong) when persecution is inflicted by private actors, and the way findings of past persecution affect the parties' burdens on claims of future persecution. This chapter presents a systematic treatment of those foundational concepts and the common analytical tests and burdens courts apply. It also explains how procedural posture—findings of fact by immigration judges (IJs) and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), standards of review on appeal, and the immigration regulatory context—affects the analysis.

# Refugee definition and core asylum elements

The statutory baseline for asylum flows from the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The INA defines a "refugee" as any person who is outside the country of nationality and is "unable or unwilling to return to, and is unable or unwilling to avail himself or herself of the protection of, that country because of persecution or a well-founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion." This text establishes two interrelated categories of eligibility: (1) those who have suffered past persecution and (2) those who have a well-founded fear of future persecution.

To establish refugee status (and thus asylum eligibility), an applicant must satisfy three core requirements derived from that statutory language. First, the applicant must show that the claimant either suffered past persecution, has a well-founded fear of future persecution, or both. Second, the persecution (past or threatened future) must be "on account of" one of the five protected grounds listed in the statute—race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group (PSG), or political opinion. Third, the claimant must show that the harm is such that the applicant is "unable or unwilling" to return to the country because of that persecution or fear; for private-source persecution, this statutory phrase imports the requirement that state protection be unavailable, ineffective, or dangerous.

When courts parse these requirements, they separate the evidentiary and legal inquiries. The first inquiry—whether persecution occurred or is reasonably likely to occur—is fact-intensive and often turns on credibility, corroboration, and country conditions evidence. The second legal inquiry—whether the persecution is "on account of" a protected ground—is doctrinal and involves tests for nexus: whether the protected ground was at least one central reason for the harm (the "one central reason" or "motivation" standard), and how to treat mixed motives. The third inquiry—whether the applicant is unable or unwilling to obtain protection from the government—requires examination of the availability, adequacy, and accessibility of state protection.

Courts apply these elements to the version of relief the applicant seeks. Asylum is discretionary but requires refugee status; withholding of removal (often called "restriction on removal" post-IIRIRA) is mandatory if an applicant shows that persecution is "more likely than not"; protection under the Convention Against Torture has a distinct standard—more than a 50% probability of torture by or with acquiescence of public officials—and separate legal elements.

# Past persecution as a gateway to future-fear presumption

Past persecution and future fear are related but distinct concepts. If an applicant proves past persecution on account of a protected ground, many doctrinal frameworks treat that showing as creating a presumption of a "well-founded fear" of future persecution. The presumption operates because the fact of past harm on account of a protected ground makes it more plausible that similar harm would recur. That presumption significantly affects the evidentiary burdens and the course of adjudication.

Defining "past persecution." Although the INA does not define "persecution," courts have developed a common understanding: persecution requires harm rising beyond mere harassment, economic disadvantage, or restrictions that are incidental to ordinary crime. Persecution usually entails serious physical injury, severe mistreatment, or cumulative harms that together amount to severe deprivation—examples include torture, severe beatings, sexual violence, prolonged arbitrary detention with mistreatment, forced sterilization, and comparable violations of fundamental human dignity. Lesser harms—isolated insults, brief detentions without mistreatment, or threats unaccompanied by other adverse consequences—often do not meet the threshold. However, courts recognize cumulative-effect analysis: repeated threats, beatings, and extortion over time may aggregate to persecution even if no single episode would suffice.

Criteria for when past persecution gives rise to a presumption. Most courts apply two analytic steps. First, the applicant must prove the fact of persecution by a preponderance of the evidence (or, in practice, by credible testimony corroborated as necessary). Second, the persecution must have been on account of a statutory ground. If both are satisfied, a rebuttable presumption ordinarily arises that the applicant has a well-founded fear of future persecution if returned. The presumption is rebuttable because the government can introduce evidence showing that current circumstances have changed such that future persecution is unlikely.

What the presumption means practically. When the presumption attaches, the burden typically shifts—practically or formally—to the government to rebut the presumption by demonstrating that the likelihood of future persecution is low. Common rebuttal grounds include effective state protection, reasonable internal relocation alternatives within the applicant's home country, and fundamental changes in country conditions that have eliminated the risk of recurrence.

Limits on the presumption. The presumption is not absolute. Courts distinguish between past harm that is sufficiently severe and that which is not, and between harms that are individualized (targeted at the applicant for a protected reason) and harms that were the result of generalized violence or criminal activity unconnected to a protected ground. Additionally, the presumption may be limited when the applicant was the target of private criminality rather than persecution linked to a protected ground. In short, past harm must be both sufficiently severe and causally linked to a protected ground to trigger the presumption.

# Government ‘unable or unwilling’ component within the elements

When persecution is inflicted by private actors (as opposed to direct state agents), the asylum framework requires consideration of whether the country’s government can or will protect the applicant. The statute's "unable or unwilling" language thus functions as a state-protection prong in private-persecution cases. Understanding how courts analyze inability or unwillingness to protect is critical because it is often dispositive: a credible claim of private persecution may fail absent evidence that state protection is ineffective, inaccessible, or dangerous.

Core conceptual elements. The analysis generally asks three interrelated questions: (1) Would reporting the harm to authorities be effective or likely to be effective in preventing further harm? (2) Would seeking protection be reasonable and safe for the applicant (i.e., would it expose the applicant to greater danger or to reprisals)? (3) Are there realistic alternative sources of protection within the country, or would internal relocation be feasible to avoid persecution?

Evidence that supports a finding that the government is "unable or unwilling" to protect. Courts look for specific, credible evidence showing that the particular authorities either did not respond adequately to prior complaints, actively colluded with the persecutors, were unable due to corruption or lack of capacity, or that police or other authorities are indifferent to the applicant’s plight. Examples of favorable evidence include consistent past reports and complaints to police that resulted in no protection or resulted in retaliation; contemporaneous documentation or correspondence showing official indifference or collusion; country condition reports demonstrating systemic lawlessness, corruption, or state incapacity in the relevant region; medical records or witness statements corroborating harm and reporting attempts; and evidence that the applicant personally attempted to seek protection but was rebuffed or punished.

The reporting/notice rule and the futility/danger exceptions. Many decisions require that an applicant show she sought protection from authorities or at least attempted to do so; this makes intuitive sense because the statute asks whether the applicant is "unable or unwilling to avail [herself] of the protection" of her country. However, courts recognize exceptions: where it would be futile or would expose the applicant to greater danger to seek state protection, the requirement to show prior reporting is relaxed. The futility exception applies when reporting would be pointless because the authorities are widely known to be colluding with or protecting the persecutors, or where doing so would predictably trigger reprisals. The danger exception applies when seeking protection would be hazardous in itself (for example, where police are proxies for the persecutors). Whether the exception applies is a fact-specific inquiry that turns on country conditions, the applicant's social position, and any past encounters with officials.

Temporal and geographic considerations. The ability or willingness of the government to protect must be assessed realistically in the relevant time frame and geographic context. Evidence that a central government has disclaimers of protection in remote areas, or that local law enforcement is corrupt or complicit, can be dispositive. Likewise, improvements in national-level protections do not always rebut inability if the applicant’s particular locality remains lawless or if evading the persecutors would require onerous relocation within the country.

Standards of proof for the state-protection prong. The applicant bears the burden of proof for asylum eligibility generally; as with other elements, an applicant must show eligibility by a preponderance of the evidence for withholding-of-removal or by a lower standard for the "well-founded fear" prong of asylum (which involves both subjective fear and an objective reasonable possibility). For the state-protection prong, courts require an applicant to prove inability or unwillingness by credible testimony supported by corroboration as necessary; much depends on whether the applicant can explain why corroborating evidence is not available.

# Effect on burden shifting for future fear

Proving past persecution has two major legal consequences that affect burdens and the subsequent analysis of future fear.

First, as explained above, a finding of past persecution on account of a protected ground commonly generates a rebuttable presumption that the applicant has a well-founded fear of future persecution. In practice, once an immigration judge finds past persecution, an evidentiary and tactical burden often shifts to the government to show by substantial evidence that future persecution is unlikely. The government may seek to rebut the presumption through any of several showings: that the applicant could relocate internally to a safe part of the country, that the government now provides effective protection such that persecutors cannot or will not pursue the applicant, or that the country’s conditions have changed fundamentally (e.g., the persecuting regime fell, or systemic reforms eliminated the prior risk). The government must produce persuasive country-conditions evidence or case-specific proof that undermines the reasonable likelihood of recurrence.

Second, the standard required for withholding of removal (restriction on removal) is higher than that for asylum's "well-founded fear." Withholding requires a showing that it is "more likely than not" (a probability greater than 50%) that the applicant would be persecuted on return. While past persecution creates a presumption of future persecution for asylum purposes, for withholding the factfinder must gauge whether past persecution renders future persecution more likely than not. Courts treat this as a probabilistic assessment: past persecution aids the applicant, but the government can still rebut by showing that the balance of probabilities favors safety on return.

Practical implications for adjudication. Because a past-persecution finding shifts evidentiary burdens and focuses the inquiry on government submitters’ evidence of change, counsel and adjudicators concentrate on narrow factual showings: documentary country-conditions reports, contemporaneous official records, proof of police reports and outcomes, and credible testimony about any attempts to seek protection. Where the government relies on evidence of changed conditions, the agency must show (with an appropriate evidentiary record) that changes are durable, general, and specific enough to apply to the applicant’s situation. If the applicant rebuts the government's evidence—showing, for example, that the reforms are confined to the capital while the applicant's locality remains ungoverned—the presumption survives and may carry the case in the applicant’s favor on asylum; it may also be dispositive for withholding if the probability threshold is met.

Interplay with nexus and protected-ground analysis. The effect of past-persecution findings also depends on the nexus determination. If the past persecution was not on account of a protected ground, there is no statutory presumption. Conversely, if the past persecution was on account of a protected ground but the government successfully rebuts the presumption for the applicant as a whole, the applicant may still attempt to prove a well-founded fear of future persecution through direct evidence of individualized targeted threats or a generalized pattern of persecution affecting similarly situated members of the protected class.

# Procedural posture and standards of review

Understanding how factfinding and legal conclusions are reviewed on appeal is essential. At the trial level an IJ resolves credibility, weighs evidence, and makes factual findings. The BIA typically reviews and either adopts the IJ’s decision or issues its own analysis. On judicial review, courts use different standards for different components. Factual findings—such as whether past persecution occurred, whether state protection is unavailable, and credibility determinations—are reviewed under the substantial-evidence standard: the court will uphold the agency's determination unless no reasonable adjudicator could have reached the same conclusion based on the record. Legal conclusions—such as whether the statutory definition of "refugee" applies to certain facts, or the interpretation of ambiguous regulatory language—are reviewed de novo, although courts often afford Chevron deference to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous provisions. Mixed questions (application of law to facts) can receive either deferential or de novo review depending on how the issue is framed in the agency decision.

Practical consequences. Because credibility and many factual inferences are accorded deference, applicants often seek to preserve detailed, corroborated records and explain inconsistencies. Attorneys must anticipate the government’s ability to present country-conditions evidence and be prepared to argue the inadequacy or temporal irrelevance of such evidence. On appeal, successful challenges often point to a lack of explanation in the IJ's or BIA’s opinion for rejecting central claims, or to record evidence that compellingly undermines the agency’s factual conclusions such that no reasonable adjudicator could have reached them.

# Common tests, burdens, and doctrinal formulations summarized

Throughout asylum jurisprudence, certain recurring tests and burdens appear:

1. Well-founded fear test (for future fear in asylum): a two-part inquiry into subjective fear and objective reasonableness. The applicant must demonstrate both genuine subjective fear and either a 10 percent-ish objective chance of persecution (courts do not fix a numeric threshold but treat "well-founded" as a reasonable possibility) or an objective basis grounded in credible evidence and country conditions.

2. Past-persecution presumption: proof of past persecution on account of a protected ground gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of future persecution, shifting burdens to the government to show safety.

3. Nexus test: the persecutory harm must be "on account of" a protected ground, frequently articulated as requiring that the protected ground be at least one central reason for the harm. Mixed-motive analysis applies: an applicant need not prove exclusivity, only that the protected ground substantially motivated the persecutor.

4. State-protection (unable/unwilling) prong: where non-state actors perpetrate persecution, applicants must show the state cannot or will not protect them—established by evidence of ineffective response, collusion, or danger/futility of seeking assistance.

5. Internal flight/relocation alternative: if the applicant can reasonably relocate to a part of the country where protected-ground persecution is unlikely, that may rebut the presumption of future fear. The government bears the evidentiary burden in rebutting presumption if it relies on relocation; the applicant may counter with evidence that relocation is unreasonable or dangerous in the applicant’s circumstances.

6. Standard for withholding of removal: a higher probability standard—"more likely than not"—rather than the reasonable possibility standard applied to asylum’s well-founded fear.

# Conclusion

A careful understanding of the refugee definition and the three key elements for asylum—(1) past persecution or well-founded fear of future persecution, (2) nexus to a protected ground, and (3) inability or unwillingness to avail oneself of state protection for private persecution—is indispensable for reading and evaluating asylum opinions. The doctrine treats past persecution as a potent evidentiary predicate that triggers a rebuttable presumption of future fear and that reallocates burdens. The "unable or unwilling" state-protection inquiry is fact-intensive and hinges on both reporting and exceptions for futility and danger; it often determines the outcome when violence arises from private actors. Finally, the procedural posture and standards of review shape how findings are sustained or overturned on appeal, which should inform advocacy and appellate strategy.

Introduction

A central question in asylum and withholding-of-removal law is whether harm inflicted on an applicant occurred "on account of" a protected ground: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group (PSG), or political opinion. In mixed-motive situations, where harm may stem from several overlapping reasons, courts must decide how much causal weight to assign to the protected ground. The Tenth Circuit has developed a deliberate doctrinal framework for this inquiry that focuses on whether the protected ground was at least "one central reason" for the harm, and rejects theories that treat a protected ground as sufficient when it is merely incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate. This chapter explains that statutory standard and its application: the structure of mixed-motive analysis; the kinds of evidence and inferences courts find probative; the concept of incidental or subordinate motives and how they are distinguished from central ones; and the particular implications for political-opinion and PSG claims. A grasp of these principles is essential before reading appellate decisions that turn on nexus questions.

# Statutory “one central reason” standard

The statutory phrase that determines refugee status is whether the applicant is "unable or unwilling to return" because of persecution on account of one of the enumerated grounds. Courts interpret "on account of" to require a causal connection between the persecution and a protected ground. The Tenth Circuit's formulation in mixed-motive contexts is that a protected ground need not be the sole or even the primary reason for persecution, but it must be at least "one central reason" motivating the persecutor’s conduct. That standard occupies a middle ground between two extremes: it is more demanding than treating any incidental or opportunistic reference to a protected ground as dispositive, but less stringent than requiring the protected ground to be the but-for cause of the harm.

How this standard operates doctrinally: when an applicant alleges persecution that may have multiple causes, the adjudicator must assess whether a protected characteristic or belief was a central motivating reason for the harm. The inquiry is not a metaphysical attempt to split actors’ souls; rather, courts focus on whether the record supplies a reasonable basis to conclude the persecutor targeted the applicant at least in part because of the protected ground. If a protected ground is established as "one central reason," then the statutory nexus requirement is satisfied for purposes of asylum and withholding analyses. If not, the claim fails on nexus even if the applicant otherwise demonstrates persecution or a well-founded fear.

The standard has two important consequences. First, it allocates causal weight: the applicant need not exclude all other motives, but must show the protected ground reached the level of centrality in the persecutor’s calculus. Second, it preserves an evaluative role for the adjudicator: motive is often inferred from circumstances, not merely alleged, and the "central reason" standard provides a principled threshold for those inferences.

# Mixed-motive analysis and evidentiary showings

Mixed-motive cases arise when persecutory harm appears to flow from a combination of factors—economic exploitation, private criminality, personal disputes, or general criminality overlapped with political or social characteristics. Courts approach these cases through a mixed-motive lens that involves two basic tasks: first, the applicant must present evidence that a protected ground plausibly figured in the perpetrator’s motivation; second, the adjudicator must determine whether that evidence suffices to conclude the protected ground was at least one central reason for the harm.

Burden and order of proof. Asylum applicants bear the initial burden of proving refugee status—that is, nexus to a protected ground in addition to persecution or fear of persecution. In practice, this initial burden requires the applicant to produce evidence that links the persecutor’s actions to a protected ground. Evidence can be direct (threats, statements, documents) or circumstantial (pattern of targeting, contemporaneous targeting of persons sharing the trait, timing, comparative treatment). Where the applicant shows past persecution on account of a protected ground, a presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution often follows; but nexus must still be established for the past act itself to qualify as persecution "on account of" a protected ground.

Types of evidence and inferences. Adjudicators commonly rely on several types of evidence in mixed-motive analysis. An explicit statement by a persecutor that references a protected ground is powerful proof of nexus (for example, "we are targeting you because you oppose X"). When direct statements are lacking, courts infer motive from context: targeted selection of persons with the same characteristic (a pattern), contemporaneous threats or demands that make the protected ground salient, the persecutor's treatment of similarly situated non-protected persons, and the temporal relationship between protected conduct and adverse acts. Documentary corroboration (police reports, hospital records, affidavits from witnesses) and country-conditions evidence demonstrating that particular groups are singled out also strengthen the nexus showing.

Evaluating mixed motives. Not every plausible connection suffices. The adjudicator must weigh the totality of the record and decide whether the protected ground attains centrality in the persecutor’s motivation. Courts look for objective indicators that the protected ground was more than incidental: repeated references to the characteristic, systematic targeting of similarly situated members, and a consistent pattern linking the persecutory actor to anti-protected-ground animus. Conversely, if the evidence suggests the persecutor acted primarily for reasons such as theft, extortion, personal vendetta, or common criminality with only a peripheral reference to the protected ground, then the protected ground will not be found central.

Standard of review and procedural posture. When an immigration judge or the Board makes factual findings about motive, appellate review is deferential—typically the substantial-evidence standard. Legal conclusions about the interpretation of the statutory nexus, including the scope of "on account of," are reviewed de novo. Because the assessment of motive often blends fact and law, appellate courts will respect agency findings if reasonable but will correct legal errors in applying the nexus standard.

# “Incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate” motives

The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly warned against treating incidental or subordinate references to protected characteristics as sufficient to establish nexus. The line between a central motive and an incidental one can be subtle, but doctrinally important.

Defining incidental motives. An incidental motive is one that is present but not determinative of the persecutor’s action. Examples include opportunistic criminals who extort wealth from victims irrespective of their protected status, but who might also use a protected characteristic to intimidate; private actors who commit violence primarily for personal reasons but make passing references to a protected ground; or situations where the primary driver is economic gain (robbery, seizure of property) and any mention of the victim’s political opinion or group membership is peripheral.

Legal significance. The law treats incidental motives as insufficient for asylum nexus because the statute protects persons persecuted for who they are or what they believe, not persons who suffer harm that merely coincides with a protected characteristic. If the protected ground is only a pretext or an afterthought that does not inform the persecutor’s central objective, then the statutory scheme is not implicated. This prevents stretching the refugee concept to cover ordinary crime and personal disputes that happen to involve persons with protected traits.

Identifying subordinate motives in practice. Courts examine whether the persecutor's behavior would have occurred absent the protected characteristic, whether the persecutor consistently targeted people for that characteristic, and whether the protected trait was made the operational reason for the harm (for example, by demands, propaganda, or selection criteria tied to the trait). Evidence that the persecutor made other demands—financial or otherwise—and applied the same conduct to persons without the trait suggests a subordinate role for the protected ground. Conversely, evidence that the persecutor singled out the applicant or others because of the protected trait—particularly when the harm follows a refusal to change a political stance, to join a group, or to cease a protected activity—supports a central-motive finding.

Burden implications. The applicant must produce sufficient evidence showing the protected trait was not merely incidental. If the record is ambiguous, the adjudicator may find the nexus lacking under the "central reason" standard. Appellate courts will reverse only if no reasonable factfinder could conclude nexus is absent or present, depending on which side benefits from the agency determination.

# Application to political-opinion claims

Political opinion presents characteristic complexities for nexus analysis because persecutors may ascribe opinions to victims (true or imputed), retaliate against people perceived to oppose them, or target persons for reasons that are intertwined with political conflict.

Protected political opinion and direct opposition. Where an applicant has openly opposed a persecutory actor's political aims—through speech, voting, membership in an opposition party, or refusal to join an insurgent group—courts look for a causal link showing the persecutor punished that opposition. Explicit threats tied to an opposition stance or persecution that immediately follows political expression often satisfy the "one central reason" test. Temporal proximity and direct threats that reference the applicant's stated opinion are strong evidence that the political opinion was a central reason.

Imputed political opinion. A critical nuance is imputed political opinion—where a persecutor attributes a political stance to the applicant based on family, employment, associations, or perceived sympathies. The law recognizes that persecutors may respond to such ascriptions. If an applicant is harmed because the persecutor believes the applicant holds a disfavored political opinion, that belief suffices as a nexus so long as the persecutor’s belief was a central reason for the harm. Evidence useful to establish imputed opinion includes documented statements by persecutors attributing the opinion, patterns of targeting people linked to the same organization or family, or a context in which the persecutor routinely punishes those perceived as dissenters.

The boundary with incidental motive. Political-opinion claims must overcome the risk that political labels are sometimes used as pretexts. Adjudicators will scrutinize whether the persecutor’s motives were genuinely political or whether the "political" label masks material motives such as extortion. The protected ground loses force when the political label is employed selectively to coerce funds or property and is not the persecutor's operative objective. In contrast, if the political characterization shapes the persecutor’s demands or the selection of victims, that supports centrality.

Contextual and corroborating evidence. Contextual country conditions—such as evidence that particular political actors routinely persecute opponents—can be highly probative. Corroboration from witnesses, contemporaneous reports, or consistent statements by multiple victims increases the credibility of a political-opinion nexus. Where the record contains direct threats or systematic targeting connected to political activity, courts have a firmer basis to conclude that political opinion was one central reason for the persecution.

# Application to particular social group (PSG) claims

PSG claims turn on two linked inquiries: (1) whether the group is cognizable under immigration law, and (2) whether membership in that group was at least one central reason for persecution. Nexus doctrines developed for political-opinion claims inform PSG analysis, but PSG claims present unique evidentiary and doctrinal issues.

Cognizability of a PSG. Before considering nexus, an adjudicator must determine whether the claimed group meets the legal criteria for a PSG. Courts typically require that a PSG be defined by an immutable or fundamental characteristic, a shared past experience, or a characteristic so central to individual identity that it cannot be changed. The "social visibility" or "particularity" formulations used by different circuits probe whether the group is sufficiently discrete and recognizably distinct in the relevant society. These threshold inquiries shape nexus analysis, because a nebulous or overly broad group that fails cognizability will typically also have difficulty establishing motive-based targeting.

Membership and motive. If the group is cognizable, the applicant must show that the persecutor targeted him or her because of group membership. Evidence that the persecutor selected victims according to membership—through statements about the group, systematic attacks on group members, or treatment that singles out the group compared to others—supports a finding that group membership was a central reason. Demonstrating that the persecutor demanded conformity or punished refusal to conform to norms associated with the group can also be relevant.

Imputation and association. The concept of imputed membership applies as it does for political opinion: persecutors sometimes target persons they perceive to belong to a group even if the individual denies membership. A persecutor's belief about membership can establish nexus if that belief was a central factor in the harm. Similarly, membership-by-association claims—where membership is imputed because of family or social ties—require evidence that the persecutor treated the individual as a member and acted because of that perceived membership.

Distinguishing central versus incidental targeting in PSG cases. PSG claims face the same risk of being marginalized when the persecutor's principal motives are economic, criminal, or personal. If attackers loot, extort, or commit violence primarily for material gain but occasionally single out people who happen to belong to a particular group, the PSG nexus may be insufficient. Courts therefore look for patterns of targeting, repeated or systematic attacks on group members, or statements linking the group's identity to the reason for harm.

Examples of probative and unhelpful evidence. Probative evidence includes contemporaneous threats referencing the group, evidence that members of the group are systematically targeted where country conditions demonstrate such targeting, and witness accounts corroborating selective attacks. Less helpful evidence includes general assertions of being targeted without details tying the harm to the group's defining characteristic, or isolated criminal incidents where group membership appears only tangentially related.

# Integrating the doctrines: reasoning, burdens, and the factfinder’s role

When presented with a mixed-motive asylum claim, the adjudicator must proceed methodically. First, determine whether the alleged harm rises to the level of persecution under applicable standards (physical violence, severe economic or social deprivation, or cumulative mistreatment). Second, assess whether the applicant has alleged a protected ground and whether any threshold requirements (e.g., PSG cognizability) are satisfied. Third, evaluate the nexus: did the protected ground constitute at least one central reason for the harm?

Throughout this process, the factfinder’s role is central. Motive is rarely proved by direct evidence; it is often inferred from circumstances. Reasoned analysis requires the adjudicator to explain how the record supports an inference that the protected ground was a central reason, or conversely, how the evidence points to other motivations. Procedural posture matters because appellate courts under the substantial-evidence standard will not lightly overturn an agency’s factual nexus finding if it rests on reasonable inferences, but they will correct misapplications of the legal test (for example, by tolerating only incidental protected motives).

Practical tips for evaluating nexus claims. In assessing an applicant’s claim, attention should focus on contemporaneity (did the harm follow the protected activity or the persecutor’s awareness of a trait?), consistency (do multiple incidents point to the same motive?), specificity (are there concrete statements or acts linking the harm to the ground?), comparative treatment (are non-members treated differently?), and country conditions (do external reports corroborate the pattern alleged?). Absence of direct evidence is not dispositive, but the inference of centrality must be grounded in at least some objective indicia.

Conclusion

The Tenth Circuit’s "one central reason" formulation mediates between an overly permissive and an overly restrictive approach to mixed-motive asylum claims. It requires that a protected ground play a central, not merely incidental, role in motivating persecution. This standard shapes how applicants present evidence and how adjudicators analyze motive: direct statements, patterns of targeting, contextual country conditions, and corroboration are the most persuasive means to show that the persecutor’s action was at least partly driven by the protected ground. For political-opinion and PSG claims, the emphasis falls on demonstrating that the persecutor punished the applicant because of the opinion or group membership—whether actual or imputed—and not primarily for independent motives such as profit or personal vendetta. Understanding these doctrinal contours will equip the reader to evaluate appellate opinions that turn on the fine distinctions between central and subordinate motives.

Introduction

Asylum and withholding of removal claims turn on whether an applicant has suffered, or reasonably fears suffering, persecution on account of a protected ground (race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group). Before reading a judicial opinion that applies precedent, a law student needs a clear doctrinal map of (1) what counts as persecution (and what does not), (2) how courts treat multiple lower‑severity incidents when aggregated, (3) the treatment of threats unaccompanied by physical injury, and (4) the role of psychological harm absent physical injury. This chapter explains those topics as developed in the Tenth Circuit’s asylum jurisprudence and related federal immigration principles. Emphasis is on legal standards, how adjudicators apply rules to facts, and how procedural posture and burden‑allocations shape analysis.

# Persecution defined beyond ‘restrictions or threats’

Persecution is a legal threshold distinct from routine civil or criminal mistreatment. The Immigration and Nationality Act does not define “persecution,” so courts supply a working definition by combining statutory text and precedent. In the Tenth Circuit, persecution requires more than de minimis harm, mere restrictions on liberty, or ordinary harassment and threats; it generally entails the infliction of suffering or harm regarded as offensive because of a protected characteristic.

Elements and general formulation. The asylum framework requires an applicant to show she is a refugee—meaning she is “unable or unwilling” to return to her country because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of a protected ground. In practice, adjudicators parse two components: (A) whether the conduct at issue rises to persecution, and (B) whether there is a nexus between the persecution and a protected ground. The persecution inquiry itself looks to the nature, severity, frequency, and consequences of the harm.

What distinguishes persecution from lesser mistreatment. Adjudicators apply several lenses to decide whether mistreatment crosses the persecution threshold:

- Severity and type of harm. Persecution commonly involves physical violence (beating, torture, rape, mutilation), severe economic deprivation imposed selectively, detention under harsh conditions, or other harms that cause significant bodily injury, bodily intrusion, or serious impairment of personal liberty. Non‑physical coercion can constitute persecution if it inflicts severe consequences—e.g., forced sterilization, compelled exile, or prolonged arbitrary detention.

- Impact on the victim’s life. Courts examine whether the harm produced lasting effects: significant injury, long‑term disability, social ostracism that destroys livelihood, or conditions that effectively strip the applicant of meaningful life choices. The cumulative disruption to family, employment, or safety matters.

- Purpose and context. Persecution is more likely found when the harm is inflicted to punish, coerce, or eliminate members of a protected group, or when it is systematic, targeted, or condoned in a social or political context. Isolated common‑crime victimization, random harassment, or non‑targeted violence ordinarily will not qualify.

- State involvement or acquiescence. Persecution may be easier to establish where state actors inflict harm, or where the state is unwilling or unable to protect against serious private violence. But persecution can arise from private actors as well—provided there is proof that the private violence reaches the necessary severity and is linked to a protected ground and that the government cannot or will not provide protection.

Standards of proof and effect of a finding. For past persecution, an applicant need only convince the factfinder that persecution occurred by the ordinary civil standard used in immigration hearings (the applicant bears the burden of proof). If past persecution is established, it generally creates a presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution unless the government rebuts that presumption. For future fear claims, the applicant must show a reasonable possibility or, depending on the form of relief, a higher probability of future persecution. Restriction on removal (the statutory withholding remedy) requires a showing of a “clear probability” (or likelihood) that persecution would occur if returned, which is a higher standard than the well‑founded fear standard for asylum.

# Cumulative‑harm lens for multiple incidents

Single severe incidents readily satisfy the persecution threshold. The more challenging factual scenario is multiple incidents that, taken individually, might fall short of persecution but together may amount to persecution. Courts apply a cumulative‑harm analysis to determine whether the aggregate effect meets the persecution standard.

Why aggregation matters. Many forms of political, ethnic, or gender‑based mistreatment unfold as a pattern of harassment, beatings, thefts, and threats over time. A doctrinal rule that treats each incident in isolation would ignore the real‑world cumulative damage and could undermine the protective purpose of asylum law. Thus, adjudicators examine the totality of circumstances: frequency, escalation, duration, and cumulative impact.

How courts aggregate. In practice, adjudicators consider the following features when aggregating incidents:

- Frequency and duration. Repeated incidents over an extended period more readily establish persecution than a single brief episode. A sustained campaign of harassment and minor assaults can cumulatively produce the severity necessary to constitute persecution.

- Escalation and pattern. An upward trend in seriousness or a pattern of discrete harms (e.g., repeated home invasions, beatings, threats, property destruction) showing an intent to coerce or eliminate the individual supports a finding of persecution.

- Severity of the individual incidents. Even if most incidents are low‑grade, a few somewhat more serious episodes (e.g., one beating that leaves injuries, repeated sexual assaults) change the aggregation calculus.

- Consequences and impairment. The cumulative impact on employment, family life, mental health, social standing, or physical health—loss of job, inability to support dependents, serious trauma—may evidence persecution when added together.

- Contextual factors and nexus. Aggregation is persuasive only if the cumulative pattern is connected to a protected ground. If the harms target the applicant because of her race or political opinion, courts will view the aggregate harm as more likely persecution.

Application to adjudication. When analyzing cumulative claims, adjudicators are expected to explain why the aggregate conduct does or does not meet the persecution threshold. Credibility findings factoring into the weight of the evidence are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard: appellate courts will uphold factfindings unless a reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude otherwise. Procedurally, failure to consider cumulative evidence or to explain how the aggregation was weighed can be reversible error.

# Threats‑only doctrine and exceptions

General rule: threats alone generally insufficient. The Tenth Circuit and other circuits have articulated a cautionary rule: mere threats, absent accompanying harm or a demonstrated ability and intent to carry them out, typically do not rise to persecution. Threats are common in many unstable settings, and the asylum system requires a level of harm beyond general intimidation to qualify as persecution. The analytical rationale is twofold: (1) the statutory focus on “persecution” contemplates actual infliction (or at least a clear likelihood) of severe harm, and (2) treating threats per se as persecution would risk conflation with ordinary criminal intimidation.

When threats may suffice. There are important, recognized exceptions where threats—especially when credible, specific, and tied to a protected ground—can amount to persecution or at least, when considered with other facts, support a finding of past persecution or well‑founded fear:

- Credible and imminent threats that cause severe psychological or functional impairment. A threat that is credible, specific, imminent, and likely to be executed can produce a well‑founded fear of persecution equivalent to a likelihood of harm. For example, death threats accompanied by surveillance, delivery of the weapon, or a near‑execution of the threat can convert mere words into reproachable persecution.

- Threats accompanied by a pattern of coercive conduct. When threats occur as part of an ongoing campaign that includes harassment, property destruction, attacks on family members, or extortion, courts aggregate the conduct; the threats add to the totality and may push the cumulative harm over the persecution threshold.

- Threats coupled with a real inability or unwillingness of the state to protect. Threats from private actors can be effectively persecutions if the state is unable or unwilling to prevent or redress the threats—especially where reporting is futile or dangerous. The government‑protection analysis thus intersects with the threats‑only inquiry.

- Threats causing severe non‑physical harm. In some instances, threats alone can inflict severe psychological trauma or social ostracism that produces disabling effects (e.g., forced isolation, loss of means of survival). When the consequences of threats are legally cognizable as severe harm—particularly where medical or expert evidence corroborates serious mental injury—threats can support a persecution finding.

- Targeted threats tied to a protected ground. Credible threats that explicitly reference a victim’s protected characteristic (e.g., “We will kill you because you are [X]”) and are corroborated by context are stronger evidence of persecution than generic menaces.

Burden and nexus. An applicant alleging threats must satisfy the usual burdens: show that the threats are credible and tied at least in part to a protected ground (the “one central reason” or mixed‑motive approach discussed below). Moreover, for withholding of removal, the threat must create a clear probability of persecution; for asylum, it must create a well‑founded fear.

Adjudicator practice. Immigration judges and the Board must articulate why threats alone do or do not satisfy persecution in each case—addressing imminence, credibility, state protection, and nexus. Appellate review focuses on whether the factfinder reasonably assessed these features in view of the record.

# Role of psychological harm without physical injury

Legal recognition of non‑physical persecution. Increasingly, courts recognize that severe psychological harm—when it meets objective severity standards—can qualify as persecution even in the absence of physical injury. The key is that the psychological harm must be serious, chronic, and disabling to the extent that it is the legal equivalent of persecution.

Standards for psychological injury to count. Adjudicators typically evaluate mental‑harm claims along dimensions similar to physical harm claims:

- Objective severity. The applicant must show, through credible testimony and preferably corroborating evidence (medical records, psychiatric evaluations, expert reports), that the mental suffering is severe. Symptoms like clinical depression, post‑traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), panic attacks, suicidal ideation, or other incapacitating conditions weigh heavily.

- Causation and nexus. Evidence must link the psychological harm to past mistreatment or credible threats caused by persecutory conduct tied to a protected ground. The presence of other stressors that could explain the mental condition (illness, unrelated trauma) is analyzed to ensure the connection is genuine.

- Duration and functional impact. Temporary fear or transient anxiety is insufficient; the harm should interfere materially with daily functioning—work, education, social participation, or self‑care. Longstanding trauma that eliminates meaningful life prospects strengthens a persecution claim.

- Corroboration and medical evidence. While medical proof is not always required, psychiatric or medical records and expert testimony bolster the claim and may be essential where adjudicators find applicant testimony alone ambiguous. Treating clinicians’ reports, physician affidavits, or psychological testing are common forms of corroboration.

Past persecution presumption and psychological harm. A successful showing of past persecution via severe psychological harm can trigger the presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution. The presumption’s availability depends on the intensity and permanence of the psychological injury and the broader context (e.g., whether the persecutory conduct is likely to recur).

Interplay with threats‑only doctrine. Psychological harm often arises from threats. Courts assessing threats that did not culminate in physical injury often ask whether the threats produced clinically significant mental injury. If the threats led to such substantial psychological damage, the threats may, in effect, have produced persecution.

Practical adjudication tips. Because claims of psychological harm can be subjective, adjudicators must carefully evaluate credibility and corroboration. Applicants should provide contemporaneous medical records, mental‑health evaluations, and specific factual descriptions of how the trauma affects daily life. The Board and courts will uphold a denial only if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s findings that psychological effects were not severe, not credibly tied to persecutory conduct, or easily treatable and unrelated.

# Nexus and the “one central reason” standard

Although the focal subject of other chapters, nexus (whether the harm was “on account of” a protected characteristic) is inseparable from persecution analysis. The Tenth Circuit follows the mixed‑motive or “one central reason” test: an applicant need not show that the protected ground was the sole reason for the harm; rather, it must be at least one central reason. That is especially significant in mixed‑motive contexts—e.g., violence motivated by economics and ethnicity, or gangs acting partly for profit and partly to punish dissent.

Key features of nexus analysis:

- Mixed motives permitted. The presence of non‑protected motives does not defeat asylum if the protected ground is a central or substantial reason for the mistreatment. Courts examine statements by persecutors, pattern and selectivity of attacks, and contextual evidence to infer motives.

- Tangential or incidental motives insufficient. If a protected trait played only an incidental or tangential role—e.g., the applicant was attacked principally for a personal dispute without any connection to the protected ground—then nexus is lacking.

- Corroborative evidence and inference. Admissions by attackers, targeting patterns (e.g., attacks aimed specifically at members of a protected group), and contemporaneous documents or testimony can support the required connection.

# Government unable‑or‑unwilling prong and its role with private actors

When the source of persecution is a private actor (not a government official), the applicant must show that the government of the home country is unable or unwilling to control the persecutors or to protect the applicant. The inability/unwillingness question is fact‑intensive and interacts with the nature of threats and cumulative harm.

Conventional tests and proofs. Courts examine whether the applicant reported the mistreatment, whether the authorities took reasonable steps to investigate and punish perpetrators, and whether reporting is feasible or would increase risk. A showing that the police ignored reports, made collusive statements, detained the victim improperly, or are themselves supportive of the persecutors favors a finding of inability or unwillingness.

Futility or danger exception. The applicant may be excused from reporting if doing so would be futile or would create additional risk (for example, where the police are co‑conspirators or where reporting leads to immediate retaliation). Evidence that reporting has historically resulted in reprisals or that police systematically turn a blind eye supports the futility or danger argument.

State protection adequacy. Not every government response suffices; the question is whether the state will provide effective protection. Token gestures, inadequate investigations, or mere promises without enforcement do not negate an applicant’s inability/unwillingness claim.

Burden and appellate review. The applicant bears the burden of proving inability/unwillingness as part of establishing refugee status. The Board and IJ assess the country‑conditions record, police reports, and applicant testimony. Appellate courts defer to factual determinations absent compulsion to the contrary but review legal interpretations (e.g., the standard to excuse reporting) de novo.

# Procedural posture and standards of review

It is important to keep in mind the different legal standards and how procedural posture shapes review:

- Burden of proof. For asylum, the applicant must show refugee status by a preponderance of the evidence (subject to credibility determinations). For withholding of removal, the applicant must show a “clear probability” (a likelihood) of persecution. For relief under the Convention Against Torture, the standard is that it is more likely than not that the applicant would be tortured.

- Credibility. Many persecution claims rest on applicant testimony about private incidents. Credibility determinations are central; they affect whether multiple incidents can be aggregated and whether threats were credible. Immigration judges must make specific findings on credibility; appellate courts defer to those findings unless the record compels a contrary conclusion.

- Substantial‑evidence review. The Board’s factual findings are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard. Legal conclusions (e.g., whether a set of facts qualifies as persecution) are reviewed de novo, but where legal interpretations involve ambiguous statutory provisions, Chevron deference principles may apply to agency interpretations.

- Remand for inadequate analysis. If the IJ or Board fails to address cumulative evidence, psychological harm, or the inability/unwillingness of the state, appellate courts may remand for clarification and further proceedings rather than resolve the merits themselves.

# Practical implications for applicants and advocates

- Provide detailed factual narratives. To prove cumulative harm or psychological injury, present a chronological account of incidents with specifics on dates, participants, locations, and consequences.

- Corroboration is critical. Medical/psychological reports, police reports, contemporaneous communications, affidavits from witnesses, and country‑conditions materials strengthen allegations of severe harm, threats’ credibility, and state inability/unwillingness.

- Tie harms to a protected ground. Demonstrate why persecutors targeted the applicant for a protected reason—use statements by attackers, patterns of selective targeting, or evidence of group‑based animus.

- Address state protection proactively. Show attempts to seek police help (or explain why reporting was impossible or dangerous) and supply documentary evidence about police responsiveness or complicity.

- Anticipate mixed‑motive attacks. Frame the protected ground as at least a central reason; courts accept mixed motives so long as the protected ground is central, not merely incidental.

Conclusion

Understanding what rises to persecution requires fine‑grained analysis: severity, frequency, context, nexus, and state protection all matter. Courts in the Tenth Circuit analyze whether multiple incidents, even if individually modest, aggregate to persecution; they generally treat threats alone with skepticism unless threats are credible, imminent, tied to a protected ground, or produce severe psychological harm or occur in a context of government non‑protection. Psychological injury can satisfy the persecution requirement when it is objectively severe and linked to harm on account of a protected ground. Procedural rigor—careful findings on credibility, cumulative evidence, nexus, and state protection—governs appeals, and applicants who understand these elements can structure evidence and testimony to meet the relevant standards.

Introduction

A central question that arises repeatedly in asylum law is when harm inflicted by private actors amounts to persecution “on account of” a protected ground and, crucially, when the state’s failure to prevent or redress that harm renders the applicant eligible for protection. Courts have developed doctrines for assessing whether a government is “unable or unwilling” to protect an asylum seeker from private-actor persecution. These doctrines operationalize several interrelated inquiries: Did the applicant report the harm to authorities? If so, what was the government’s response? If not, was reporting reasonably required, or was it excused by futility or danger? How does the state’s reaction—or lack of notice—affect the nexus and persecution analyses? This chapter synthesizes the governing concepts, tests, evidentiary considerations, and burdens that a student must understand before reading judicial opinions addressing state protection, reporting, and the futility-or-danger exception.

# Importance of reporting past harm to authorities

Reporting to authorities is often a key factual predicate in evaluating whether a government had the opportunity to protect an asylum applicant from private persecution. When the applicant alleges past persecution by non-state actors, courts commonly ask: Did the victim give local law enforcement notice of the threat or injury, and did the government take reasonable steps to investigate, detain, or otherwise protect the complainant?

The doctrinal reasons for focusing on reporting are both practical and normative. Practically, a report creates an administrative or criminal record that courts and adjudicators can review to assess government awareness and response. Normatively, asylum law presumes that sovereigns have primary responsibility for prevention of and redress for crime and violence within their borders; an effective state response undermines the claim that the state “cannot or will not” protect the victim and therefore weakens the inference that the country is a source of persecution.

What courts look for in reports and official records
- Timing and specificity. A prompt report (close in time to the incident or to a material escalation) that describes the agents or motives of the attackers is stronger evidence that the government had an opportunity to act. Vague or belated complaints are weaker.
- Formality and record. Police reports, hospital records documenting injuries and statements, court filings, bail papers, or detention logs provide documentary corroboration of an allegation and of government awareness.
- Action taken. Merely filing a complaint is not decisive. Courts evaluate whether authorities investigated, detained suspects, provided protection (e.g., relocated the victim, provided police patrols), or declined to act—and why.
- Continuity. Repeated reporting or follow-up attempts can show persistence of the threat and continued government inaction.

Limits of the reporting focus
- Absence of a report is not dispositive of lack of persecution. A failure to report can be explained by fear, cultural constraints, lack of access to authorities, corruption, language barriers, or belief that the authorities are allied with the persecutors. Asylum adjudicators must assess these explanations.
- Reporting is context-dependent. In some countries police are effective; in others, submitting a complaint is plainly useless or dangerous. The relevance of a report therefore depends on the background country conditions and the nature of the persecutors.

# Assessing government response and protective efforts

When an applicant did report to authorities, adjudicators evaluate the sufficiency of the government’s response. The central question is whether the state’s actions were adequate to protect the applicant from the danger that produced the persecution claim. Two related standards guide the assessment: an objective reasonableness standard for state action, and an inability-or-unwillingness construct as it applies to remedial measures.

Objective reasonableness of governmental protection
- Reasonable steps test. Courts usually ask whether the government took reasonable steps under the circumstances to investigate, punish, or restrain the private actors. Reasonableness is judged in light of available resources, prevailing law-enforcement norms, and the specific risk to the applicant.
- Examples of reasonable steps: launching a bona fide investigation; arresting or charging suspects; imposing restraining orders or custody; providing or arranging relocation or protection services; offering to accept and follow up on complaints. A formal apology without follow-through, perfunctory “paper” investigations, or statements of concern with no enforcement typically fall short.
- Examples of insufficient steps: ignoring complaints, dismissing evidence without inquiry, returning complainants to dangerous locales, or colluding with the persecutors. Corruption or explicit state facilitation of the persecutors typically demonstrates unwillingness.

Governmental inability
- Resource limits can show inability. In settings of widespread violence or where institutions are weak, the state may lack the institutional capacity to prevent certain harms. Evidence of overwhelmed police forces, systemic breakdowns, or de facto anarchy supports an inability finding.
- Structural or systemic failures. Chronic understaffing, lack of legal frameworks to protect certain classes of victims, or geographic impossibility of providing protection (e.g., remote areas with no police presence) justify a conclusion that the state is unable to protect.

Governmental unwillingness
- Active refusal. Official statements that the state will not intervene, threats by officials, or deliberate misuse of state power to aid persecutors indicate unwillingness.
- Passive indifference combined with foreseeable consequences. Repeated failure to act in the face of clear danger—especially when the state has the means to act—can indicate unwillingness.

Burden of proof and allocation
- The asylum applicant bears the burden of proving refugee status by demonstrating past persecution, a well-founded fear of future persecution, or entitlement to withholding on the statutory standard. When alleging private-actor persecution and asserting state failure, the applicant must show either that he reported and the government failed to protect, or that reporting was excused and the government was aware or likely to become aware but unable or unwilling to act.
- The standard of proof varies by relief: asylum requires that the applicant be a “refugee” as defined in 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42); withholding of removal requires a higher standard—more likely than not (clear probability) that persecution would occur if returned. Adjudicators apply factual findings under substantial-evidence review on appeal.

How courts synthesize evidence
- Totality of circumstances. Courts do not mechanically count documents; they evaluate the interplay of an applicant’s testimony, corroborating records, country conditions, and government action (or inaction).
- Credibility matters. Adjudicators assess whether the applicant’s testimony about reporting and subsequent interactions with authorities is credible and consistent with the documentary record and country conditions.

# When failure to report is excused

Adjudicators often confront cases in which applicants did not report harassment or attack to authorities. Recognizing that reporting is not always a realistic option, courts have developed doctrines that excuse failure to report under certain circumstances. The most important is the futility-or-danger exception; related concepts include constructive notice and realistic notice alternatives.

Futility or danger exception (when reporting would be futile or dangerous)
- Core principle. An applicant need not report to authorities when doing so would be futile—because the authorities are known to be colluding with or sympathetic to persecutors—or when reporting would expose the applicant to further danger.
- Showings required. To invoke the futility-or-danger exception, applicants typically must provide specific, credible evidence that attempting to seek protection would have been useless or would have escalated the threat. Evidence may include prior adverse experiences with police, public statements by officials supporting the persecutors, widely known impunity for similar attacks, or direct threats that reporting would bring further harm.
- Objective and subjective components. Courts consider both the applicant’s reasonable subjective fear of reporting and objective evidence of the state’s record. An applicant’s fear must be plausible in the context of country conditions and corroborated where feasible.

Constructive notice and alternatives to formal reporting
- Constructive notice. When the persecutors’ acts are public or pervasive, courts may find that authorities had constructive notice even if the applicant did not file a formal complaint. Examples include attacks reported in the media, public raids, or repeated incidents in which authorities were plainly aware.
- Alternative channels. When formal reporting would be dangerous or ineffective, evidence that the applicant sought help through other channels—tribal leaders, political actors, human rights organizations, or foreign embassies—can substitute for a police report. Courts will examine whether those alternatives were reasonably available and whether they effectively registered the risk with authorities.

When the court will require reporting despite claimed futility
- Specificity of proof. Where an applicant asserts futility without contemporaneous corroboration, an adjudicator may require some form of corroborating evidence (medical records, statements from witnesses who fled with the applicant, country-condition reports) before excusing non-reporting.
- Temporal considerations. If the applicant waited years to present the claim without a plausible explanation, adjudicators may be more skeptical of the futility claim. Conversely, immediate flight from the country or a credible history of threats can explain delay.

Examples of admissible corroboration
- Country condition reports describing institutional corruption or impunity.
- Affidavits from witnesses or community leaders attesting to known collusion between police and persecutors.
- Medical or hospital records showing injury consistent with the alleged attack alongside evidence that the victim avoided reporting to police for fear of reprisal.
- Evidence of prior reprisals against other complainants.

# Implications for private-actor persecution claims

Understanding state protection and reporting doctrines is crucial because they intersect with several core elements of asylum law: whether the harm constitutes persecution, whether it was “on account of” a protected ground, and whether the applicant qualifies as a refugee.

Linking state protection to persecution and nexus
- Persecution threshold. Private-actor harms must rise to the level of persecution—typically requiring substantial mistreatment such as physical violence, severe psychological harm, or loss of basic freedoms. The absence of state protection can amplify otherwise borderline harms into persecution when the state’s failure is systematic or deliberate.
- Nexus to a protected ground. To qualify for asylum or withholding, an applicant must show that the persecution was “on account of” race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. When private actors are the perpetrators, state failure can be relevant in two ways:
  - It can help show that the harm was targeted because of a protected ground (e.g., authorities ignored attacks against a particular ethnic group), establishing a pattern or motive.
  - It can demonstrate that the state is a proximate cause of persecution by tolerating or facilitating attacks against the protected class.

Mixed motives and “one central reason”
- Mixed-motive analysis. Courts recognize that persecutors may have multiple motives. The “one central reason” or “centrality” standard asks whether a protected ground was at least one central reason for the harm. If it was, a private-actor persecution claim can succeed even if the persecutor had other non-protected motives.
- Role of state in mixed-motive cases. A state’s failure to act against private actors who consistently target a protected class strengthens an inference that protected status was a central reason.

How failure to report affects credibility and the record
- Potential for adverse credibility findings. Failure to report, without credible explanation, can undermine an applicant’s trustworthiness. Adjudicators may discount testimony lacking corroboration or expecting evidence that was plausibly available but unproduced.
- Tactical importance of evidence collection. Applicants should, when safe and feasible, collect and preserve corroborating materials (medical records, witness statements, country reports) that corroborate both the persecution and the reason reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

Procedural posture and appellate review
- Immigration judge factfinding. IJs resolve credibility and weigh evidence at the first instance. They must explain adverse credibility findings adequately and address relevant legal theories the applicant raises.
- Board of Immigration Appeals and courts of appeals. The BIA reviews legal interpretations de novo and factual findings for substantial evidence. Federal courts defer to BIA and IJ factual findings unless no reasonable adjudicator could reach the same conclusion. Thus, how the administrative record documents reporting, government response, and futility claims is critical—sparse records are difficult to overturn on appeal.
- Remand issues. If the IJ or BIA fails to consider key evidence or legal arguments (for example, the futility exception or the state’s constructive notice), appellate courts often remand for further factfinding rather than resolving disputed facts themselves.

Practical considerations for counsel and adjudicators
- Build a documentary record. Where reporting occurred, obtain police reports, hospital records, court docket entries, and sanctions or lack thereof. Where reporting did not occur, collect affidavits explaining why, country reports demonstrating systemic problems, and statements from NGOs or local leaders.
- Address nexus explicitly. Link the persecutors’ motives and the state’s response (or lack of response) to the protected ground clearly in briefs and testimony.
- Anticipate credibility attacks. Prepare to explain timing of reporting and flight, and to justify any delay with concrete, corroborated facts.

Conclusion

The doctrines governing reporting, state protection, and the futility-or-danger exception are interdependent facets of the broader “unable or unwilling” inquiry. Courts examine whether an applicant notified authorities, whether the government had knowledge and took reasonable steps to protect, and whether reporting was excused by credible fear of futility or danger. Properly presenting and adjudicating these issues requires careful factual development, contextualization within country conditions, and clear legal framing. Because adjudicative determinations of credibility and government response are subject to substantial-evidence review on appeal, claimants and their counsel should prioritize obtaining corroborating documentation and making explicit legal arguments about why reporting was or was not required in the circumstances.
