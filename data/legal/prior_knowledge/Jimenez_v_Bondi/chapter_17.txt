Introduction

A central question that arises repeatedly in asylum law is when harm inflicted by private actors amounts to persecution “on account of” a protected ground and, crucially, when the state’s failure to prevent or redress that harm renders the applicant eligible for protection. Courts have developed doctrines for assessing whether a government is “unable or unwilling” to protect an asylum seeker from private-actor persecution. These doctrines operationalize several interrelated inquiries: Did the applicant report the harm to authorities? If so, what was the government’s response? If not, was reporting reasonably required, or was it excused by futility or danger? How does the state’s reaction—or lack of notice—affect the nexus and persecution analyses? This chapter synthesizes the governing concepts, tests, evidentiary considerations, and burdens that a student must understand before reading judicial opinions addressing state protection, reporting, and the futility-or-danger exception.

# Importance of reporting past harm to authorities

Reporting to authorities is often a key factual predicate in evaluating whether a government had the opportunity to protect an asylum applicant from private persecution. When the applicant alleges past persecution by non-state actors, courts commonly ask: Did the victim give local law enforcement notice of the threat or injury, and did the government take reasonable steps to investigate, detain, or otherwise protect the complainant?

The doctrinal reasons for focusing on reporting are both practical and normative. Practically, a report creates an administrative or criminal record that courts and adjudicators can review to assess government awareness and response. Normatively, asylum law presumes that sovereigns have primary responsibility for prevention of and redress for crime and violence within their borders; an effective state response undermines the claim that the state “cannot or will not” protect the victim and therefore weakens the inference that the country is a source of persecution.

What courts look for in reports and official records
- Timing and specificity. A prompt report (close in time to the incident or to a material escalation) that describes the agents or motives of the attackers is stronger evidence that the government had an opportunity to act. Vague or belated complaints are weaker.
- Formality and record. Police reports, hospital records documenting injuries and statements, court filings, bail papers, or detention logs provide documentary corroboration of an allegation and of government awareness.
- Action taken. Merely filing a complaint is not decisive. Courts evaluate whether authorities investigated, detained suspects, provided protection (e.g., relocated the victim, provided police patrols), or declined to act—and why.
- Continuity. Repeated reporting or follow-up attempts can show persistence of the threat and continued government inaction.

Limits of the reporting focus
- Absence of a report is not dispositive of lack of persecution. A failure to report can be explained by fear, cultural constraints, lack of access to authorities, corruption, language barriers, or belief that the authorities are allied with the persecutors. Asylum adjudicators must assess these explanations.
- Reporting is context-dependent. In some countries police are effective; in others, submitting a complaint is plainly useless or dangerous. The relevance of a report therefore depends on the background country conditions and the nature of the persecutors.

# Assessing government response and protective efforts

When an applicant did report to authorities, adjudicators evaluate the sufficiency of the government’s response. The central question is whether the state’s actions were adequate to protect the applicant from the danger that produced the persecution claim. Two related standards guide the assessment: an objective reasonableness standard for state action, and an inability-or-unwillingness construct as it applies to remedial measures.

Objective reasonableness of governmental protection
- Reasonable steps test. Courts usually ask whether the government took reasonable steps under the circumstances to investigate, punish, or restrain the private actors. Reasonableness is judged in light of available resources, prevailing law-enforcement norms, and the specific risk to the applicant.
- Examples of reasonable steps: launching a bona fide investigation; arresting or charging suspects; imposing restraining orders or custody; providing or arranging relocation or protection services; offering to accept and follow up on complaints. A formal apology without follow-through, perfunctory “paper” investigations, or statements of concern with no enforcement typically fall short.
- Examples of insufficient steps: ignoring complaints, dismissing evidence without inquiry, returning complainants to dangerous locales, or colluding with the persecutors. Corruption or explicit state facilitation of the persecutors typically demonstrates unwillingness.

Governmental inability
- Resource limits can show inability. In settings of widespread violence or where institutions are weak, the state may lack the institutional capacity to prevent certain harms. Evidence of overwhelmed police forces, systemic breakdowns, or de facto anarchy supports an inability finding.
- Structural or systemic failures. Chronic understaffing, lack of legal frameworks to protect certain classes of victims, or geographic impossibility of providing protection (e.g., remote areas with no police presence) justify a conclusion that the state is unable to protect.

Governmental unwillingness
- Active refusal. Official statements that the state will not intervene, threats by officials, or deliberate misuse of state power to aid persecutors indicate unwillingness.
- Passive indifference combined with foreseeable consequences. Repeated failure to act in the face of clear danger—especially when the state has the means to act—can indicate unwillingness.

Burden of proof and allocation
- The asylum applicant bears the burden of proving refugee status by demonstrating past persecution, a well-founded fear of future persecution, or entitlement to withholding on the statutory standard. When alleging private-actor persecution and asserting state failure, the applicant must show either that he reported and the government failed to protect, or that reporting was excused and the government was aware or likely to become aware but unable or unwilling to act.
- The standard of proof varies by relief: asylum requires that the applicant be a “refugee” as defined in 8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(42); withholding of removal requires a higher standard—more likely than not (clear probability) that persecution would occur if returned. Adjudicators apply factual findings under substantial-evidence review on appeal.

How courts synthesize evidence
- Totality of circumstances. Courts do not mechanically count documents; they evaluate the interplay of an applicant’s testimony, corroborating records, country conditions, and government action (or inaction).
- Credibility matters. Adjudicators assess whether the applicant’s testimony about reporting and subsequent interactions with authorities is credible and consistent with the documentary record and country conditions.

# When failure to report is excused

Adjudicators often confront cases in which applicants did not report harassment or attack to authorities. Recognizing that reporting is not always a realistic option, courts have developed doctrines that excuse failure to report under certain circumstances. The most important is the futility-or-danger exception; related concepts include constructive notice and realistic notice alternatives.

Futility or danger exception (when reporting would be futile or dangerous)
- Core principle. An applicant need not report to authorities when doing so would be futile—because the authorities are known to be colluding with or sympathetic to persecutors—or when reporting would expose the applicant to further danger.
- Showings required. To invoke the futility-or-danger exception, applicants typically must provide specific, credible evidence that attempting to seek protection would have been useless or would have escalated the threat. Evidence may include prior adverse experiences with police, public statements by officials supporting the persecutors, widely known impunity for similar attacks, or direct threats that reporting would bring further harm.
- Objective and subjective components. Courts consider both the applicant’s reasonable subjective fear of reporting and objective evidence of the state’s record. An applicant’s fear must be plausible in the context of country conditions and corroborated where feasible.

Constructive notice and alternatives to formal reporting
- Constructive notice. When the persecutors’ acts are public or pervasive, courts may find that authorities had constructive notice even if the applicant did not file a formal complaint. Examples include attacks reported in the media, public raids, or repeated incidents in which authorities were plainly aware.
- Alternative channels. When formal reporting would be dangerous or ineffective, evidence that the applicant sought help through other channels—tribal leaders, political actors, human rights organizations, or foreign embassies—can substitute for a police report. Courts will examine whether those alternatives were reasonably available and whether they effectively registered the risk with authorities.

When the court will require reporting despite claimed futility
- Specificity of proof. Where an applicant asserts futility without contemporaneous corroboration, an adjudicator may require some form of corroborating evidence (medical records, statements from witnesses who fled with the applicant, country-condition reports) before excusing non-reporting.
- Temporal considerations. If the applicant waited years to present the claim without a plausible explanation, adjudicators may be more skeptical of the futility claim. Conversely, immediate flight from the country or a credible history of threats can explain delay.

Examples of admissible corroboration
- Country condition reports describing institutional corruption or impunity.
- Affidavits from witnesses or community leaders attesting to known collusion between police and persecutors.
- Medical or hospital records showing injury consistent with the alleged attack alongside evidence that the victim avoided reporting to police for fear of reprisal.
- Evidence of prior reprisals against other complainants.

# Implications for private-actor persecution claims

Understanding state protection and reporting doctrines is crucial because they intersect with several core elements of asylum law: whether the harm constitutes persecution, whether it was “on account of” a protected ground, and whether the applicant qualifies as a refugee.

Linking state protection to persecution and nexus
- Persecution threshold. Private-actor harms must rise to the level of persecution—typically requiring substantial mistreatment such as physical violence, severe psychological harm, or loss of basic freedoms. The absence of state protection can amplify otherwise borderline harms into persecution when the state’s failure is systematic or deliberate.
- Nexus to a protected ground. To qualify for asylum or withholding, an applicant must show that the persecution was “on account of” race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. When private actors are the perpetrators, state failure can be relevant in two ways:
  - It can help show that the harm was targeted because of a protected ground (e.g., authorities ignored attacks against a particular ethnic group), establishing a pattern or motive.
  - It can demonstrate that the state is a proximate cause of persecution by tolerating or facilitating attacks against the protected class.

Mixed motives and “one central reason”
- Mixed-motive analysis. Courts recognize that persecutors may have multiple motives. The “one central reason” or “centrality” standard asks whether a protected ground was at least one central reason for the harm. If it was, a private-actor persecution claim can succeed even if the persecutor had other non-protected motives.
- Role of state in mixed-motive cases. A state’s failure to act against private actors who consistently target a protected class strengthens an inference that protected status was a central reason.

How failure to report affects credibility and the record
- Potential for adverse credibility findings. Failure to report, without credible explanation, can undermine an applicant’s trustworthiness. Adjudicators may discount testimony lacking corroboration or expecting evidence that was plausibly available but unproduced.
- Tactical importance of evidence collection. Applicants should, when safe and feasible, collect and preserve corroborating materials (medical records, witness statements, country reports) that corroborate both the persecution and the reason reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

Procedural posture and appellate review
- Immigration judge factfinding. IJs resolve credibility and weigh evidence at the first instance. They must explain adverse credibility findings adequately and address relevant legal theories the applicant raises.
- Board of Immigration Appeals and courts of appeals. The BIA reviews legal interpretations de novo and factual findings for substantial evidence. Federal courts defer to BIA and IJ factual findings unless no reasonable adjudicator could reach the same conclusion. Thus, how the administrative record documents reporting, government response, and futility claims is critical—sparse records are difficult to overturn on appeal.
- Remand issues. If the IJ or BIA fails to consider key evidence or legal arguments (for example, the futility exception or the state’s constructive notice), appellate courts often remand for further factfinding rather than resolving disputed facts themselves.

Practical considerations for counsel and adjudicators
- Build a documentary record. Where reporting occurred, obtain police reports, hospital records, court docket entries, and sanctions or lack thereof. Where reporting did not occur, collect affidavits explaining why, country reports demonstrating systemic problems, and statements from NGOs or local leaders.
- Address nexus explicitly. Link the persecutors’ motives and the state’s response (or lack of response) to the protected ground clearly in briefs and testimony.
- Anticipate credibility attacks. Prepare to explain timing of reporting and flight, and to justify any delay with concrete, corroborated facts.

Conclusion

The doctrines governing reporting, state protection, and the futility-or-danger exception are interdependent facets of the broader “unable or unwilling” inquiry. Courts examine whether an applicant notified authorities, whether the government had knowledge and took reasonable steps to protect, and whether reporting was excused by credible fear of futility or danger. Properly presenting and adjudicating these issues requires careful factual development, contextualization within country conditions, and clear legal framing. Because adjudicative determinations of credibility and government response are subject to substantial-evidence review on appeal, claimants and their counsel should prioritize obtaining corroborating documentation and making explicit legal arguments about why reporting was or was not required in the circumstances.
