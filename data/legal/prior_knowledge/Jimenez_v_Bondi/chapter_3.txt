Introduction

In asylum and related forms of international relief, the concept of "persecution" serves as a legal and practical gatekeeper: it distinguishes mistreatment that triggers refugee protection from harms that, while serious, do not. Understanding how courts and immigration adjudicators identify persecution requires attention to not only the nature of single incidents but also how multiple harms, patterns of conduct, context, and consequences accumulate. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework used to evaluate whether conduct rises to persecution, how adjudicators aggregate multiple incidents, the different forms of harm (physical, custodial, property, coercive, psychological), evidentiary approaches to nonphysical injury, the conditions in which threats-only claims may suffice, and the standard for persecution by private actors. Throughout, emphasis is placed on the analytical steps courts take, the relevant burdens of proof, and how procedural posture (e.g., claims of past persecution vs. forward-looking claims) affects the legal analysis.

# Persecution vs. harassment: severity, repetition, and context

The line between harassment and persecution is one of degree and context. Harassment generally denotes mistreatment that is hostile or discriminatory but is either minor in severity or isolated in occurrence; persecution, by contrast, is mistreatment of sufficient seriousness, scope, or cumulative effect to constitute a fundamental impairment of basic human rights or to impose substantial harm or suffering.

Severity is the first and most obvious metric. Severe physical injury (e.g., serious beatings, torture), prolonged detention under inhumane conditions, or deprivation of fundamental necessities will typically be treated as persecution. At the other end, a single, brief insult, a one-time minor beating, or isolated vandalism without significant consequences is more readily characterized as harassment. But severity is not a purely quantitative inquiry; courts consider the quality of harm: the intentionality, the cruelty, and whether the harm was degrading or humiliating in a manner that impairs identity, dignity, or liberty.

Repetition matters because repetitive conduct can transform otherwise nonpersecutory acts into persecution. A single episode of low-level violence might not be persecution, but repeated episodes with similar motives, each imposing psychological or physical tolls, may, when viewed as a pattern, amount to persecution. Repetition is relevant both to showing sustained targeting and to demonstrating cumulative impact.

Context is the connective tissue. The same act may be harassment in one context and persecution in another. Contextual factors include the political and social climate (e.g., whether the acts occur within a broader campaign against a group), the persecutor’s purpose (whether the conduct seeks to coerce, punish, or destroy a protected attribute), the victim’s vulnerability (e.g., inability to seek protection), and temporal context (e.g., during armed conflict, authoritarian crackdowns). Courts examine whether the conduct is targeted at a protected characteristic and whether it is part of a broader, systematic pattern that aims to suppress membership in a social, political, or religious group.

In practice, adjudicators make a fact-specific judgment. They assess whether the harm is so serious, frequent, or systematic that it rises above ordinary harassment and fits within the legal meaning of persecution used in asylum, withholding, or nonrefoulement frameworks.

# Cumulative evaluation of multiple incidents

Because persecution often results from a sequence of abuses rather than a single dramatic event, adjudicators routinely use a cumulative-harm approach. This approach asks whether the aggregate effect of multiple incidents—considered together in their temporal and factual context—amounts to persecution, even if no single incident would independently suffice.

The cumulative analysis generally follows these steps. First, identify and describe each incident with specificity: date, nature of conduct, physical and emotional consequences, perpetrators, and immediate context. Second, assess the quality and severity of each incident. Third, evaluate patterns and relationships among incidents: are they connected by common perpetrators, shared motives (e.g., targeting on account of a protected ground), similar methods, or increasing intensity? Fourth, consider the temporal dimension—whether incidents are close in time, reprisal-based, or spread across years—and whether they produce lingering effects. Finally, determine whether the combined impact is qualitatively equivalent to persecution—that is, whether the sum of harms results in serious impairment of fundamental rights or well-being.

Courts and adjudicators often emphasize that the cumulative approach does not permit counting trivial events to manufacture persecution. The incidents must be credible, corroborated where feasible, and demonstrably linked such that their aggregate effect reaches the level of severity required. Evidence that supports cumulative impact includes injury records, medical and psychological evaluations, consistent testimony describing progressive harm, contemporaneous reports or complaints, and third-party evidence of a continuing pattern against similarly situated persons.

Procedurally, a finding of past persecution based on cumulative harms has important consequences: it shifts burdens in subsequent analysis. Proof of past persecution creates a rebuttable presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution, alleviating the need to prove a specific future threat in some contexts. Thus, meticulous presentation of cumulative harms in the record is often decisive in claims that rely on past mistreatment to establish entitlement to protection.

# Physical harm, detention, property harm, coercion

Persecution manifests through various concrete forms of mistreatment. Four recurrent categories—physical harm, detention, property harm, and coercion—often appear individually or together in persecution claims. Adjudicators assess each category for intensity, duration, purpose, and consequences.

Physical harm: Severe physical violence—such as sustained beatings, disfiguring assaults, sexual violence, torture, or attempted killing—commonly meets the persecution threshold. Considerations include the nature of injuries, need for medical treatment, evidence of deliberate infliction, and whether force was used to intimidate or to punish. Lesser physical harms may rise to persecution when repeated or when combined with other abuses that create pervasive danger or disability.

Detention: Deprivation of liberty becomes persecution when it crosses thresholds of unlawfulness, inhumanity, or prolonged duration. Short, lawful, and humane arrests for legitimate criminal conduct are not persecution. Detention that is arbitrary, accompanied by torture or cruel conditions, prolonged without due process, or used as a means of political repression or coercion is likely to be persecutory. Conditions within detention matter: overcrowding, denial of medical care, torture by guards, and lack of legal recourse can increase the severity of detention to the level of persecution.

Property harm: Forced seizure, destruction, or significant deprivation of property can amount to persecution if it undermines the individual's economic survival, is motivated by a protected ground, and is part of a broader scheme to punish, coerce, or expel. Isolated acts of vandalism that cause minimal loss may be harassment, but systematic dispossession, expropriation, or repeated arson against a targeted group can be persecutory, especially when paired with threats or physical violence.

Coercion: Coercive acts that compel behavior—such as forced recruitment into armed groups, coerced conversion, or compelled political support—can constitute persecution when they inflict severe consequences for noncompliance or are implemented through threats, violence, or legal penalties. The coercive purpose and the degree to which the acts invade autonomy and fundamental rights are central. Coercion that results in loss of liberty, bodily injury, or destruction of livelihood is more likely persuasive as persecution.

In each of these categories, courts examine intent and motive: whether the mistreatment was inflicted because of the victim’s political opinion, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or other protected attribute. The motive links the harm to the statutory grounds for asylum or withholding and separates persecution grounded in protected reasons from other forms of criminality or interpersonal animus.

# Psychological and emotional harm: evidentiary considerations

Psychological and emotional harms—such as chronic anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and sustained humiliation—can contribute to a finding of persecution, particularly when they are severe and debilitating. Because such harms are often invisible and difficult to quantify, courts require careful evidence to ensure that claims rest on reliable proof rather than subjective assertions.

Evidentiary approaches begin with credible, detailed testimony describing the nature, frequency, and consequences of the events that produced the psychological injury. Corroborating evidence is important: medical records, psychiatric evaluations, treatment histories, prescriptions, and contemporaneous counseling notes can establish the existence, diagnosis, and severity of mental health disorders. Expert reports from mental health professionals who have evaluated the claimant—explaining diagnosis, causal connection between events and symptoms, treatment needs, and functional limitations—carry significant probative value.

Adjudicators also weigh the plausibility and consistency of the psychological symptoms with the claimed traumatic events. Symptoms that align with recognized clinical syndromes (e.g., PTSD symptoms such as intrusive recollections, hypervigilance, nightmares, and avoidance) strengthen the linkage. Documentation of impaired daily functioning—difficulty maintaining employment, social withdrawal, inability to care for oneself or family, and ongoing need for therapy—helps demonstrate severity.

Where direct medical evidence is unavailable, an applicant may support psychological harm claims through reliable third-party accounts, such as testimony from family members, social workers, human rights organizations, or clergy who have observed changes in behavior. Objective country condition reports showing the unavailability of mental health services or stigmatization of mental illness can be relevant to explain gaps in formal documentation.

Courts exercise caution against overreliance on psychological evidence divorced from corroborated material facts. The causal chain between alleged events and diagnosed conditions must be clear; speculative or conclusory expert opinions that ignore alternative causes or fail to substantiate causal links are less persuasive. Moreover, adjudicators consider whether the psychological harm, combined with other harms, suffices to reach the persecution threshold under the standard applicable to the form of relief sought.

# Threats-only claims and when threats may suffice

Threats, in themselves, can constitute persecution if they meet criteria of specificity, gravity, immediacy, and corroboration. A threats-only claim presents particular challenges because there may be no physical injury to demonstrate; therefore, adjudicators closely scrutinize the content of threats and surrounding circumstances.

Specificity refers to whether the threats identify the victim or a class of victims, spell out concrete acts (e.g., "we will kill you on X date if you do not leave"), or otherwise convey a clear and particularized intent to harm. Vague or general expressions of hostility typically do not meet the threshold.

Gravity captures the seriousness of the threatened harm. Threats of severe violence, torture, sexual assault, or death are more likely to qualify as persecution than threats of minor assault or insults. Threats that would lead a reasonable person to anticipate severe injury are materially different from abusive rhetoric.

Immediacy demands a credible likelihood that the threatened violence will occur in the near term unless the applicant avoids or submits to the demand. Temporally remote or conditional threats (e.g., "someday we will get you") are less persuasive. Evidence that threats have been acted upon in the community—e.g., similar threats followed by attacks on others—supports immediacy.

Corroboration is crucial. Threats documented in police reports, witness statements, contemporaneous written communications (letters, emails, messages), and independent country condition reports showing patterns of intimidation lend weight. Where threats arise from specific incidents (e.g., prior attempts), an adjudicator will consider whether the threats were followed by attempts to carry them out or by escalating conduct.

The aggregate question is whether a reasonable person in the applicant's position would have a well-founded fear or whether the threatened harm, considered with other conduct, constitutes past persecution. Threats may suffice in two ways. First, particularly severe and imminent threats, even if unaccompanied by prior physical violence, can be sufficiently severe to be persecution. Second, threats can augment other harms—transforming a series of nonsevere incidents into persecution when the threats create pervasive fear, constrain movement, and impair fundamental rights.

Finally, in assessing threats-only claims, adjudicators also examine whether the applicant had any realistic ability to obtain state protection. If the state was able and willing to protect and the applicant could have sought and obtained protection, the threats are less likely to give rise to persecution. Conversely, threats by perpetrators who act with impunity or with state acquiescence increase the likelihood that threats amount to persecution.

# Private-actor persecution: threshold showing

Persecution need not be committed by state actors. Conduct by private actors can constitute persecution when there is a connection between the private harm and a protected ground and the state is unable or unwilling to provide protection. The analysis requires two linked showings: (1) that the private actor’s conduct rises to the level of persecution, and (2) that the state failed to control or respond adequately to allow protection.

First, the private actor’s conduct must meet the same substantive standards as state persecution: it must be sufficiently severe, targeted, and motivated (at least in substantial part) by a protected characteristic. Isolated criminality or interpersonal disputes are typically not persecution unless they are targeted at the claimant for reasons that implicate a protected ground. Evidence of pattern, motive, contemporaneous statements by perpetrators demonstrating targeting, or attacks on multiple members of a protected group supports the requisite nexus.

Second, applicants must show that the state was unwilling or unable to protect them. This element can be satisfied by evidence of state acquiescence in the persecution, failure to investigate or prosecute known perpetrators, or structural incapacity of the state to offer protection (e.g., corrupt or minimal policing, absence of legal recourse). The degree of state involvement required varies by context, but courts generally require more than a single instance of police inaction; they look for evidence that the state did not take reasonable steps to control the persecutory behavior once it was aware of the threat or that official actors condoned or participated in the abuse.

Acquiescence can be demonstrated by: official statements endorsing the harassment, systematic failure to enforce law against perpetrators of violence against the claimant’s group, or direct participation by state officials. Unwillingness may be inferred from consistent failure to investigate complaints, from documented patterns of impunity, or from credible reports showing state tolerance of group-targeted violence.

Where private actors are the perpetrators, courts also examine whether the claimant could have relocated internally to avoid harm and whether internal protection was realistically available and effective. If safe internal relocation existed and would have sufficed to avoid persecution, the inability to seek international protection may be weakened; conversely, where state protection is ineffective or relocation is unreasonable, private-actor persecution claims are stronger.

Conclusion

Discerning persecution requires a careful, evidence-based synthesis: parsing the severity, repetition, and context of harms; aggregating multiple incidents to assess cumulative impact; evaluating physical, custodial, property, coercive, and psychological harms; determining when threats alone suffice; and establishing the role of private actors and the state’s capacity or willingness to protect. Procedurally, claims based on past persecution can create presumptions about future risk, shifting evidentiary burdens; threats-only and private-actor claims often demand more robust corroboration and analysis of state response. A methodical, fact-specific approach—grounded in credible testimony, corroborating documentation, and attention to motive and context—is essential to distinguish persecution from harassment and to ensure that protection is extended to those who truly face severe, targeted abuses.
