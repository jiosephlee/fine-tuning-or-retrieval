Introduction

Removal adjudications in the United States proceed through a distinct multi-tiered administrative and judicial structure that shapes the opportunities and limits for judicial review. A noncitizen (and the government) navigate from charging and initial hearings before an Immigration Judge (IJ), to appellate review at the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), and from there (subject to statutory limits and timing rules) to the federal courts of appeals by way of a petition for review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252. Each institutional player has defined roles and constraints, and appellate review follows specific rules about the administrative record, standards of review, and the use of lower-level reasoning when the BIA issues terse rulings. This chapter lays out that institutional architecture and the doctrinal rules governing how courts of appeals approach removal decisions so that you can follow and analyze an appellate opinion with a clear sense of the underlying process.

# Roles of DHS, Immigration Judges (IJs), and the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA)

The removal process begins with the Department of Homeland Security (DHS). DHS initiates proceedings by issuing a charging document—the Notice to Appear (NTA)—that alleges removability under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). DHS is the prosecuting government agency in immigration court; its attorneys present evidence of removability, oppose claims for relief, and may move to reopen or appeal adverse administrative decisions. DHS also controls custody decisions, detention, and enforcement steps separate from the adjudicative function.

The Immigration Judge is the primary factfinder within the Executive Office for Immigration Review (EOIR). At a merits hearing, the IJ receives evidence, conducts live testimony, rules on admissibility issues, and applies law to the facts. IJs decide whether the noncitizen is removable and whether the noncitizen meets eligibility and burden-of-proof requirements for relief—examples include asylum, withholding of removal, relief under the Convention Against Torture (CAT), cancellation of removal, and adjustment of status. In many cases the IJ must make detailed credibility determinations, resolve conflicts in testimony, and weigh documentary evidence. The IJ’s decision is contained in an oral disposition followed by a written decision explaining findings of fact and conclusions of law. The IJ also has authority to administratively close, grant continuances, or certify questions to the BIA in narrow circumstances.

The Board of Immigration Appeals is the principal administrative appellate body for immigration cases. It reviews IJs’ decisions for legal error and clear factual error, issues written decisions that may be published as precedents, and has the authority to dismiss, remand, or affirm in whole or in part. The BIA may act through single-member decisions (a single BIA judge), or through multi-member panels (usually three) when issuing precedential decisions or when the case is designated for panel consideration. The BIA also handles motions to reopen and reconsider, and it establishes the law of the agency through precedential decisions that bind IJs and later panels unless overruled by the Attorney General or a subsequent BIA or judicial decision.

The institutional distinction matters for appellate review: courts of appeals normally review final orders of removal issued by the BIA, not interlocutory decisions by IJs, and the nature of the BIA decision—published, unpublished, single-member, or brief affirmance—affects what reasoning is available to the reviewing court and how it evaluates legal and factual findings.

# Single-member vs. panel BIA decisions

The BIA may issue decisions through different internal processes, and the form of the decision affects its precedential force and the amount of explanation provided.

Single-member decisions are authored by one BIA member and are common. A single-member decision can resolve the case on the merits and will be binding on the parties. However, single-member decisions are not generally published as precedents unless designated by the Board. They may be brief or relatively detailed depending on the complexity of the case and the issues raised.

Panel decisions (typically three-member panels) are used when the Board wants to issue a decision with broader precedential effect or where statutory or procedural rules require panel consideration. Panel decisions are more likely to be published as precedents and have greater precedential weight within EOIR. Precedential decisions—whether from a panel or designated single-member decision—are intended to guide IJs and later BIA members on recurring legal issues.

A particular hybrid form occurs when the BIA issues a terse order: for example, a one-line affirmance without opinion or an order that adopts and affirms the IJ’s decision without additional explanation. Such "brief affirmances" conserve Board resources but raise distinct issues on judicial review because the BIA’s reasoning is minimal or absent. When the BIA issues a short order or one that adopts the IJ’s decision, courts of appeals typically look to the IJ’s written opinion to understand the factual findings and legal reasoning that actually supported the administrative outcome. How far a court can rely on IJ reasoning and which parts of the IJ record can be consulted is governed by doctrines discussed below.

# Petitions for review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252

A party seeking judicial review of a final order of removal from the BIA must file a petition for review in the appropriate United States Court of Appeals pursuant to 8 U.S.C. § 1252. The statute establishes the exclusive avenue for judicial review of orders of removal, and it establishes basic jurisdictional rules and timing constraints. Important features include:

- Final order requirement. Generally, courts of appeals have jurisdiction only over "final orders of removal," i.e., those issued by the BIA (or by an IJ in certain circumstances where the BIA has not assumed jurisdiction). Interim or interlocutory rulings by the IJ ordinarily are not reviewable until the final administrative decision is entered.

- Timing. The statute, along with applicable federal rules, imposes strict time limits for filing a petition for review. Practically, this is often a 30-day window from the date of the BIA’s decision to file in the court of appeals (but watch the governing statute and local rules for exact timing and tolling rules).

- Scope and venue. Section 1252 also contains provisions that affect venue, consolidation, and transfer between circuits when multiple petitions involve a single final order. It further channels most challenges to BIA decisions to the courts of appeals, foreclosing direct review in district courts except in narrow constitutional due process contexts or where the statute expressly allows.

- Mandatory vs. discretionary review. Certain classes of removal orders and certain defenses (for example, some decisions involving discretionary relief or factual determinations) are described by the statute as subject to limited review, and in some areas Congress has restricted judicial interference with discretionary determinations. The statutory scheme also incorporates requirements regarding exhaustion of administrative remedies—failure to raise an issue before the BIA may forfeit that issue on petition for review.

- Interplay with motions to reopen and reconsider. Because reopening and reconsideration are brought before the BIA, many litigants file such motions after an adverse IJ decision; successful motions can moot or alter the content of a petition for review. Courts will typically require that claimants exhaust those administrative processes before obtaining judicial review, and federal courts often lack jurisdiction to review denials of motions to reopen in certain circumstances unless constitutional claims are raised.

Understanding § 1252’s jurisdictional and procedural contours is crucial because courts will dismiss petitions filed in the wrong court, filed after the deadline, or that seek review of nonfinal or nonjudicially reviewable matters.

# Record rule: 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A)

The so-called record rule is statutory: 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A) provides that a reviewing court "shall decide the petition only on the administrative record on which the order of removal is based." This rule has several practical consequences for appellate review.

First, it constrains courts to the evidence presented to the IJ and considered by the BIA; courts generally do not receive new evidence or take new testimony. The administrative record includes transcripts of hearings, documentary evidence submitted to the IJ and the BIA, and written decisions and orders. Parties should therefore ensure that all relevant evidence and arguments they wish to preserve for judicial review are placed in the administrative record.

Second, the record rule limits the development of new factual matters on appeal. Claims that depend upon evidence outside the administrative record—new affidavits, newly discovered documents, or new expert reports—cannot typically be introduced in the petition-for-review proceeding. Instead, litigants must seek to present that evidence to the BIA through a timely motion to reopen (which permits the BIA to consider new evidence under specific standards), and only after the BIA denies reopening will courts review the BIA’s denial (subject to statutory limits on judicial review of reopening decisions).

Third, the rule has narrow exceptions. Although § 1252(b)(4)(A) is broadly phrased, courts have recognized limited instances where consideration of extra-record material is appropriate—most notably for claims that require knowledge outside the administrative record to assess jurisdiction or to decide certain constitutional claims (for example, due process claims that rely on official records or proof that was not available in the proceedings). But these exceptions are applied cautiously because the statute’s clear purpose is to channel factual development to the administrative process and to prevent courts from acting as factfinders in the first instance.

In sum, the record rule places the primary burden on litigants to develop the administrative record thoroughly before the IJ and BIA, and it focuses judicial review on the administrative proceedings that produced the final order.

# Standards of review: substantial evidence for facts; de novo for legal issues

Courts of appeals apply different standards of review depending on whether the question presented is factual, legal, or discretionary. The difference is consequential: it determines whether a court will defer to the agency’s determinations or freely substitute its own judgment.

Factual findings. Findings of fact by the IJ (and by the BIA when the BIA makes its own findings) are reviewed under the "substantial evidence" standard. Under this deferential standard, a court will uphold factual determinations if they are supported by "reasonable, substantial, and probative evidence" on the record considered as a whole. Courts do not reweigh evidence or reassess credibility in place of the agency’s factfinder. For credibility determinations in particular—where the IJ has seen the witness testify and observed demeanor—review is especially deferential; appellate courts will overturn such determinations only if they are unsupported by reasonable, substantial evidence in the record.

Legal questions. Pure questions of law—statutory interpretation, questions of constitutional dimension, and the proper application of law to undisputed facts—are typically reviewed de novo. Under de novo review, the court gives no deference to the BIA’s legal conclusions and analyzes the legal issue anew. De novo review is the norm for claims that the agency interpreted the statute incorrectly or violated constitutional protections. However, the lines can blur when questions require mixed determinations of law and fact.

Mixed questions and standards interplay. When an issue requires applying a legal standard to historical facts (a mixed question), courts sometimes identify the proper standard by asking whether resolution of the question depends on factual findings that invoke the agency’s expertise. In some mixed cases, courts will review the legal component de novo but accept the factual predicate under substantial evidence. In others, the "substantial evidence" standard can control whether the legal conclusion is upheld if it depends intimately on factual determinations.

Discretionary decisions and abuse of discretion. Some matters—particularly discretionary relief determinations (e.g., whether to grant discretionary forms of relief such as certain waivers or favorable discretionary immigration relief)—are reviewed for abuse of discretion. Under this standard, a court asks whether the agency exercised its discretion in a principled way and in accordance with statutory standards, whether it considered relevant factors, and whether the decision is arbitrary or capricious. Courts respect the agency’s policy judgments in the discretionary arena but will set aside decisions that lack a rational basis, fail to consider important aspects of the problem, or rest on impermissible considerations.

Burden of proof. Standards of review interact with the substantive burdens required to obtain relief. For example, the petitioner bears the burden of proof to establish eligibility for asylum, withholding, or CAT protection. If the IJ denies relief based on the petitioner’s failure to meet the burden, a court applying substantial evidence review will sustain that denial so long as the agency’s conclusion is reasonable and supported by the record.

# Consulting IJ reasoning when the BIA issues a brief affirmance

A frequent practical issue on appeal arises when the BIA disposes of an appeal with a short order—sometimes a one-line affirmance without opinion—or when the BIA’s decision merely states that it adopts the IJ’s decision. Because the formal BIA opinion may provide little or no reasoning, courts of appeals must determine how to review the final order of removal and where to find the basis for review.

The typical approach is for the court to consult the IJ’s written decision to determine the factual findings and legal reasoning that the IJ used to reach the outcome. The IJ’s decision is part of the administrative record, and when the BIA affirms without issuing its own opinion, the IJ’s reasoning is often the only articulation of the agency’s basis for the final order. Under the record rule, the reviewing court may therefore consider the IJ’s decision as part of the record and evaluate whether that reasoning supports the BIA’s affirmance.

When doing so, the court must be careful to distinguish between reasoning that the IJ actually relied on and matters that are not adequately developed in the written IJ record. The court should not invent facts or reasoning gaps to substitute its own factfinding. Instead, the court asks whether the IJ’s expressed findings and analysis would support the BIA’s outcome if the BIA is understood to have adopted them. If the IJ’s decision is internally inconsistent, terse, or fails to address an argument that was presented to the BIA, the appellate court may remand for clarification or further explanation. In some cases, the court may treat the BIA’s brief affirmance as an implicit adoption of the IJ’s reasoning but still exercise de novo review for legal questions and substantial evidence review for facts as applied.

Courts also recognize limits on borrowing IJ reasoning. If the BIA explicitly rejects or gives different reasons for the agency’s action, the IJ’s rationale cannot be used to bootstrap the BIA’s decision. And if the IJ did not address a particular legal issue that the BIA resolved, the court cannot manufacture IJ reasoning to justify the BIA’s resolution. When the BIA’s order is devoid of reasoning and the IJ’s decision is insufficient to permit meaningful review, remand to the BIA for a fuller explanation is often required to protect the petitioner's right to reasoned administrative action and to permit effective judicial review.

# Relief-specific consolidation in final order review

Orders of removal often involve multiple claims and multiple forms of relief: eligibility for asylum, withholding of removal, relief under CAT, adjustment of status considerations, waiver applications, and statutory bars such as persecution-based grounds or criminal-ineligibility findings. The structure of review—both administratively at the BIA and judicially at the courts of appeals—requires attention to how these claims are raised, decided, and preserved.

Final order review is petition-specific and relief-specific in several ways. First, a petition for review challenges a final order of removal as to particular rulings; appellate jurisdiction is tied to the finality and specificity of the challenged aspects of the order. If multiple petitions are filed in different circuits challenging parts of the same final order, statutory provisions govern consolidation and transfer to a single circuit to avoid conflicting decisions. Parties and courts should therefore coordinate filings and be mindful of venue rules and the consequences of multiple petitions.

Second, appellate review is relief-specific in the sense that courts consider the specific relief requested and the statutory standards for that relief. For instance, review of an IJ’s denial of asylum focuses on establishing a "well-founded fear" of persecution, whereas withholding of removal involves a higher standard—clear probability or more likely than not—and CAT protection requires a likelihood of torture by or with government acquiescence. Because each form of relief has distinct elements and burdens, a court’s review must address the particular legal standards and the factual predicates relevant to each relief type. A court might affirm denial of one form of relief while remanding or reversing as to another, creating nuanced resolutions within a single final order.

Third, consolidation doctrines appear most centrally when multiple claims, motions, or petitions affect the same final order or when relief depends on one another (e.g., where eligibility for one form of relief would make another unnecessary). Courts are attentive to whether issues were presented to the agency and whether they are ripe for review. If a particular claim was not raised before the BIA or the IJ, appellate courts may consider it forfeited. Similarly, claims that depend on a grant of a discretionary form of relief that the IJ denied may be inchoate and require remand to the BIA to exercise the discretion in light of any legal errors identified.

Finally, when several petitions implicate different petitions for review that challenge discrete portions of a final removal order (for example, separate petitions filed by co-applicants), courts may consolidate those petitions for efficiency and consistency when appropriate. Statutory provisions govern where consolidation is mandatory or discretionary and how conflicting circuit petitions are resolved to prevent inconsistent nationwide application of immigration law.

Practical consequence: litigants need to be strategic and precise in identifying which parts of the final order they challenge, ensure issues are properly exhausted at the agency level, and recognize that appellate courts will parse each form of relief under its governing statutory standard and may resolve different relief claims in different ways within the same final order.

Conclusion

Understanding the institutional roles of DHS, IJs, and the BIA, and the procedural path from administrative adjudication to judicial review under 8 U.S.C. § 1252, is essential to analyzing appellate opinions in immigration matters. The record rule compels careful administrative development of the record, the distinction between BIA single-member and panel decisions shapes the precedential landscape, and the divergent standards of review determine how much deference a court gives to the agency’s factual findings and legal conclusions. When the BIA issues a brief affirmance, courts look to the IJ’s reasoning—but only within the constraints of the record and the limits of appellate review. Finally, because removal orders often involve multiple forms of relief, relief-specific analysis and consolidation principles govern how courts address the individual claims within a final order. Mastery of these procedural and doctrinal elements will allow you to read and evaluate appellate decisions with clarity about what the court is doing and why.
