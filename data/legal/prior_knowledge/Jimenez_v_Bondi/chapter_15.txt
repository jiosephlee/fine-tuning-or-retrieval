Introduction

A central question in asylum and withholding-of-removal law is whether harm inflicted on an applicant occurred "on account of" a protected ground: race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group (PSG), or political opinion. In mixed-motive situations, where harm may stem from several overlapping reasons, courts must decide how much causal weight to assign to the protected ground. The Tenth Circuit has developed a deliberate doctrinal framework for this inquiry that focuses on whether the protected ground was at least "one central reason" for the harm, and rejects theories that treat a protected ground as sufficient when it is merely incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate. This chapter explains that statutory standard and its application: the structure of mixed-motive analysis; the kinds of evidence and inferences courts find probative; the concept of incidental or subordinate motives and how they are distinguished from central ones; and the particular implications for political-opinion and PSG claims. A grasp of these principles is essential before reading appellate decisions that turn on nexus questions.

# Statutory “one central reason” standard

The statutory phrase that determines refugee status is whether the applicant is "unable or unwilling to return" because of persecution on account of one of the enumerated grounds. Courts interpret "on account of" to require a causal connection between the persecution and a protected ground. The Tenth Circuit's formulation in mixed-motive contexts is that a protected ground need not be the sole or even the primary reason for persecution, but it must be at least "one central reason" motivating the persecutor’s conduct. That standard occupies a middle ground between two extremes: it is more demanding than treating any incidental or opportunistic reference to a protected ground as dispositive, but less stringent than requiring the protected ground to be the but-for cause of the harm.

How this standard operates doctrinally: when an applicant alleges persecution that may have multiple causes, the adjudicator must assess whether a protected characteristic or belief was a central motivating reason for the harm. The inquiry is not a metaphysical attempt to split actors’ souls; rather, courts focus on whether the record supplies a reasonable basis to conclude the persecutor targeted the applicant at least in part because of the protected ground. If a protected ground is established as "one central reason," then the statutory nexus requirement is satisfied for purposes of asylum and withholding analyses. If not, the claim fails on nexus even if the applicant otherwise demonstrates persecution or a well-founded fear.

The standard has two important consequences. First, it allocates causal weight: the applicant need not exclude all other motives, but must show the protected ground reached the level of centrality in the persecutor’s calculus. Second, it preserves an evaluative role for the adjudicator: motive is often inferred from circumstances, not merely alleged, and the "central reason" standard provides a principled threshold for those inferences.

# Mixed-motive analysis and evidentiary showings

Mixed-motive cases arise when persecutory harm appears to flow from a combination of factors—economic exploitation, private criminality, personal disputes, or general criminality overlapped with political or social characteristics. Courts approach these cases through a mixed-motive lens that involves two basic tasks: first, the applicant must present evidence that a protected ground plausibly figured in the perpetrator’s motivation; second, the adjudicator must determine whether that evidence suffices to conclude the protected ground was at least one central reason for the harm.

Burden and order of proof. Asylum applicants bear the initial burden of proving refugee status—that is, nexus to a protected ground in addition to persecution or fear of persecution. In practice, this initial burden requires the applicant to produce evidence that links the persecutor’s actions to a protected ground. Evidence can be direct (threats, statements, documents) or circumstantial (pattern of targeting, contemporaneous targeting of persons sharing the trait, timing, comparative treatment). Where the applicant shows past persecution on account of a protected ground, a presumption of a well-founded fear of future persecution often follows; but nexus must still be established for the past act itself to qualify as persecution "on account of" a protected ground.

Types of evidence and inferences. Adjudicators commonly rely on several types of evidence in mixed-motive analysis. An explicit statement by a persecutor that references a protected ground is powerful proof of nexus (for example, "we are targeting you because you oppose X"). When direct statements are lacking, courts infer motive from context: targeted selection of persons with the same characteristic (a pattern), contemporaneous threats or demands that make the protected ground salient, the persecutor's treatment of similarly situated non-protected persons, and the temporal relationship between protected conduct and adverse acts. Documentary corroboration (police reports, hospital records, affidavits from witnesses) and country-conditions evidence demonstrating that particular groups are singled out also strengthen the nexus showing.

Evaluating mixed motives. Not every plausible connection suffices. The adjudicator must weigh the totality of the record and decide whether the protected ground attains centrality in the persecutor’s motivation. Courts look for objective indicators that the protected ground was more than incidental: repeated references to the characteristic, systematic targeting of similarly situated members, and a consistent pattern linking the persecutory actor to anti-protected-ground animus. Conversely, if the evidence suggests the persecutor acted primarily for reasons such as theft, extortion, personal vendetta, or common criminality with only a peripheral reference to the protected ground, then the protected ground will not be found central.

Standard of review and procedural posture. When an immigration judge or the Board makes factual findings about motive, appellate review is deferential—typically the substantial-evidence standard. Legal conclusions about the interpretation of the statutory nexus, including the scope of "on account of," are reviewed de novo. Because the assessment of motive often blends fact and law, appellate courts will respect agency findings if reasonable but will correct legal errors in applying the nexus standard.

# “Incidental, tangential, superficial, or subordinate” motives

The Tenth Circuit has repeatedly warned against treating incidental or subordinate references to protected characteristics as sufficient to establish nexus. The line between a central motive and an incidental one can be subtle, but doctrinally important.

Defining incidental motives. An incidental motive is one that is present but not determinative of the persecutor’s action. Examples include opportunistic criminals who extort wealth from victims irrespective of their protected status, but who might also use a protected characteristic to intimidate; private actors who commit violence primarily for personal reasons but make passing references to a protected ground; or situations where the primary driver is economic gain (robbery, seizure of property) and any mention of the victim’s political opinion or group membership is peripheral.

Legal significance. The law treats incidental motives as insufficient for asylum nexus because the statute protects persons persecuted for who they are or what they believe, not persons who suffer harm that merely coincides with a protected characteristic. If the protected ground is only a pretext or an afterthought that does not inform the persecutor’s central objective, then the statutory scheme is not implicated. This prevents stretching the refugee concept to cover ordinary crime and personal disputes that happen to involve persons with protected traits.

Identifying subordinate motives in practice. Courts examine whether the persecutor's behavior would have occurred absent the protected characteristic, whether the persecutor consistently targeted people for that characteristic, and whether the protected trait was made the operational reason for the harm (for example, by demands, propaganda, or selection criteria tied to the trait). Evidence that the persecutor made other demands—financial or otherwise—and applied the same conduct to persons without the trait suggests a subordinate role for the protected ground. Conversely, evidence that the persecutor singled out the applicant or others because of the protected trait—particularly when the harm follows a refusal to change a political stance, to join a group, or to cease a protected activity—supports a central-motive finding.

Burden implications. The applicant must produce sufficient evidence showing the protected trait was not merely incidental. If the record is ambiguous, the adjudicator may find the nexus lacking under the "central reason" standard. Appellate courts will reverse only if no reasonable factfinder could conclude nexus is absent or present, depending on which side benefits from the agency determination.

# Application to political-opinion claims

Political opinion presents characteristic complexities for nexus analysis because persecutors may ascribe opinions to victims (true or imputed), retaliate against people perceived to oppose them, or target persons for reasons that are intertwined with political conflict.

Protected political opinion and direct opposition. Where an applicant has openly opposed a persecutory actor's political aims—through speech, voting, membership in an opposition party, or refusal to join an insurgent group—courts look for a causal link showing the persecutor punished that opposition. Explicit threats tied to an opposition stance or persecution that immediately follows political expression often satisfy the "one central reason" test. Temporal proximity and direct threats that reference the applicant's stated opinion are strong evidence that the political opinion was a central reason.

Imputed political opinion. A critical nuance is imputed political opinion—where a persecutor attributes a political stance to the applicant based on family, employment, associations, or perceived sympathies. The law recognizes that persecutors may respond to such ascriptions. If an applicant is harmed because the persecutor believes the applicant holds a disfavored political opinion, that belief suffices as a nexus so long as the persecutor’s belief was a central reason for the harm. Evidence useful to establish imputed opinion includes documented statements by persecutors attributing the opinion, patterns of targeting people linked to the same organization or family, or a context in which the persecutor routinely punishes those perceived as dissenters.

The boundary with incidental motive. Political-opinion claims must overcome the risk that political labels are sometimes used as pretexts. Adjudicators will scrutinize whether the persecutor’s motives were genuinely political or whether the "political" label masks material motives such as extortion. The protected ground loses force when the political label is employed selectively to coerce funds or property and is not the persecutor's operative objective. In contrast, if the political characterization shapes the persecutor’s demands or the selection of victims, that supports centrality.

Contextual and corroborating evidence. Contextual country conditions—such as evidence that particular political actors routinely persecute opponents—can be highly probative. Corroboration from witnesses, contemporaneous reports, or consistent statements by multiple victims increases the credibility of a political-opinion nexus. Where the record contains direct threats or systematic targeting connected to political activity, courts have a firmer basis to conclude that political opinion was one central reason for the persecution.

# Application to particular social group (PSG) claims

PSG claims turn on two linked inquiries: (1) whether the group is cognizable under immigration law, and (2) whether membership in that group was at least one central reason for persecution. Nexus doctrines developed for political-opinion claims inform PSG analysis, but PSG claims present unique evidentiary and doctrinal issues.

Cognizability of a PSG. Before considering nexus, an adjudicator must determine whether the claimed group meets the legal criteria for a PSG. Courts typically require that a PSG be defined by an immutable or fundamental characteristic, a shared past experience, or a characteristic so central to individual identity that it cannot be changed. The "social visibility" or "particularity" formulations used by different circuits probe whether the group is sufficiently discrete and recognizably distinct in the relevant society. These threshold inquiries shape nexus analysis, because a nebulous or overly broad group that fails cognizability will typically also have difficulty establishing motive-based targeting.

Membership and motive. If the group is cognizable, the applicant must show that the persecutor targeted him or her because of group membership. Evidence that the persecutor selected victims according to membership—through statements about the group, systematic attacks on group members, or treatment that singles out the group compared to others—supports a finding that group membership was a central reason. Demonstrating that the persecutor demanded conformity or punished refusal to conform to norms associated with the group can also be relevant.

Imputation and association. The concept of imputed membership applies as it does for political opinion: persecutors sometimes target persons they perceive to belong to a group even if the individual denies membership. A persecutor's belief about membership can establish nexus if that belief was a central factor in the harm. Similarly, membership-by-association claims—where membership is imputed because of family or social ties—require evidence that the persecutor treated the individual as a member and acted because of that perceived membership.

Distinguishing central versus incidental targeting in PSG cases. PSG claims face the same risk of being marginalized when the persecutor's principal motives are economic, criminal, or personal. If attackers loot, extort, or commit violence primarily for material gain but occasionally single out people who happen to belong to a particular group, the PSG nexus may be insufficient. Courts therefore look for patterns of targeting, repeated or systematic attacks on group members, or statements linking the group's identity to the reason for harm.

Examples of probative and unhelpful evidence. Probative evidence includes contemporaneous threats referencing the group, evidence that members of the group are systematically targeted where country conditions demonstrate such targeting, and witness accounts corroborating selective attacks. Less helpful evidence includes general assertions of being targeted without details tying the harm to the group's defining characteristic, or isolated criminal incidents where group membership appears only tangentially related.

# Integrating the doctrines: reasoning, burdens, and the factfinder’s role

When presented with a mixed-motive asylum claim, the adjudicator must proceed methodically. First, determine whether the alleged harm rises to the level of persecution under applicable standards (physical violence, severe economic or social deprivation, or cumulative mistreatment). Second, assess whether the applicant has alleged a protected ground and whether any threshold requirements (e.g., PSG cognizability) are satisfied. Third, evaluate the nexus: did the protected ground constitute at least one central reason for the harm?

Throughout this process, the factfinder’s role is central. Motive is rarely proved by direct evidence; it is often inferred from circumstances. Reasoned analysis requires the adjudicator to explain how the record supports an inference that the protected ground was a central reason, or conversely, how the evidence points to other motivations. Procedural posture matters because appellate courts under the substantial-evidence standard will not lightly overturn an agency’s factual nexus finding if it rests on reasonable inferences, but they will correct misapplications of the legal test (for example, by tolerating only incidental protected motives).

Practical tips for evaluating nexus claims. In assessing an applicant’s claim, attention should focus on contemporaneity (did the harm follow the protected activity or the persecutor’s awareness of a trait?), consistency (do multiple incidents point to the same motive?), specificity (are there concrete statements or acts linking the harm to the ground?), comparative treatment (are non-members treated differently?), and country conditions (do external reports corroborate the pattern alleged?). Absence of direct evidence is not dispositive, but the inference of centrality must be grounded in at least some objective indicia.

Conclusion

The Tenth Circuit’s "one central reason" formulation mediates between an overly permissive and an overly restrictive approach to mixed-motive asylum claims. It requires that a protected ground play a central, not merely incidental, role in motivating persecution. This standard shapes how applicants present evidence and how adjudicators analyze motive: direct statements, patterns of targeting, contextual country conditions, and corroboration are the most persuasive means to show that the persecutor’s action was at least partly driven by the protected ground. For political-opinion and PSG claims, the emphasis falls on demonstrating that the persecutor punished the applicant because of the opinion or group membership—whether actual or imputed—and not primarily for independent motives such as profit or personal vendetta. Understanding these doctrinal contours will equip the reader to evaluate appellate opinions that turn on the fine distinctions between central and subordinate motives.
