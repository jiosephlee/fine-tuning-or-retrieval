Introduction

Asylum and withholding of removal claims turn on whether an applicant has suffered, or reasonably fears suffering, persecution on account of a protected ground (race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group). Before reading a judicial opinion that applies precedent, a law student needs a clear doctrinal map of (1) what counts as persecution (and what does not), (2) how courts treat multiple lower‑severity incidents when aggregated, (3) the treatment of threats unaccompanied by physical injury, and (4) the role of psychological harm absent physical injury. This chapter explains those topics as developed in the Tenth Circuit’s asylum jurisprudence and related federal immigration principles. Emphasis is on legal standards, how adjudicators apply rules to facts, and how procedural posture and burden‑allocations shape analysis.

# Persecution defined beyond ‘restrictions or threats’

Persecution is a legal threshold distinct from routine civil or criminal mistreatment. The Immigration and Nationality Act does not define “persecution,” so courts supply a working definition by combining statutory text and precedent. In the Tenth Circuit, persecution requires more than de minimis harm, mere restrictions on liberty, or ordinary harassment and threats; it generally entails the infliction of suffering or harm regarded as offensive because of a protected characteristic.

Elements and general formulation. The asylum framework requires an applicant to show she is a refugee—meaning she is “unable or unwilling” to return to her country because of persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of a protected ground. In practice, adjudicators parse two components: (A) whether the conduct at issue rises to persecution, and (B) whether there is a nexus between the persecution and a protected ground. The persecution inquiry itself looks to the nature, severity, frequency, and consequences of the harm.

What distinguishes persecution from lesser mistreatment. Adjudicators apply several lenses to decide whether mistreatment crosses the persecution threshold:

- Severity and type of harm. Persecution commonly involves physical violence (beating, torture, rape, mutilation), severe economic deprivation imposed selectively, detention under harsh conditions, or other harms that cause significant bodily injury, bodily intrusion, or serious impairment of personal liberty. Non‑physical coercion can constitute persecution if it inflicts severe consequences—e.g., forced sterilization, compelled exile, or prolonged arbitrary detention.

- Impact on the victim’s life. Courts examine whether the harm produced lasting effects: significant injury, long‑term disability, social ostracism that destroys livelihood, or conditions that effectively strip the applicant of meaningful life choices. The cumulative disruption to family, employment, or safety matters.

- Purpose and context. Persecution is more likely found when the harm is inflicted to punish, coerce, or eliminate members of a protected group, or when it is systematic, targeted, or condoned in a social or political context. Isolated common‑crime victimization, random harassment, or non‑targeted violence ordinarily will not qualify.

- State involvement or acquiescence. Persecution may be easier to establish where state actors inflict harm, or where the state is unwilling or unable to protect against serious private violence. But persecution can arise from private actors as well—provided there is proof that the private violence reaches the necessary severity and is linked to a protected ground and that the government cannot or will not provide protection.

Standards of proof and effect of a finding. For past persecution, an applicant need only convince the factfinder that persecution occurred by the ordinary civil standard used in immigration hearings (the applicant bears the burden of proof). If past persecution is established, it generally creates a presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution unless the government rebuts that presumption. For future fear claims, the applicant must show a reasonable possibility or, depending on the form of relief, a higher probability of future persecution. Restriction on removal (the statutory withholding remedy) requires a showing of a “clear probability” (or likelihood) that persecution would occur if returned, which is a higher standard than the well‑founded fear standard for asylum.

# Cumulative‑harm lens for multiple incidents

Single severe incidents readily satisfy the persecution threshold. The more challenging factual scenario is multiple incidents that, taken individually, might fall short of persecution but together may amount to persecution. Courts apply a cumulative‑harm analysis to determine whether the aggregate effect meets the persecution standard.

Why aggregation matters. Many forms of political, ethnic, or gender‑based mistreatment unfold as a pattern of harassment, beatings, thefts, and threats over time. A doctrinal rule that treats each incident in isolation would ignore the real‑world cumulative damage and could undermine the protective purpose of asylum law. Thus, adjudicators examine the totality of circumstances: frequency, escalation, duration, and cumulative impact.

How courts aggregate. In practice, adjudicators consider the following features when aggregating incidents:

- Frequency and duration. Repeated incidents over an extended period more readily establish persecution than a single brief episode. A sustained campaign of harassment and minor assaults can cumulatively produce the severity necessary to constitute persecution.

- Escalation and pattern. An upward trend in seriousness or a pattern of discrete harms (e.g., repeated home invasions, beatings, threats, property destruction) showing an intent to coerce or eliminate the individual supports a finding of persecution.

- Severity of the individual incidents. Even if most incidents are low‑grade, a few somewhat more serious episodes (e.g., one beating that leaves injuries, repeated sexual assaults) change the aggregation calculus.

- Consequences and impairment. The cumulative impact on employment, family life, mental health, social standing, or physical health—loss of job, inability to support dependents, serious trauma—may evidence persecution when added together.

- Contextual factors and nexus. Aggregation is persuasive only if the cumulative pattern is connected to a protected ground. If the harms target the applicant because of her race or political opinion, courts will view the aggregate harm as more likely persecution.

Application to adjudication. When analyzing cumulative claims, adjudicators are expected to explain why the aggregate conduct does or does not meet the persecution threshold. Credibility findings factoring into the weight of the evidence are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard: appellate courts will uphold factfindings unless a reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude otherwise. Procedurally, failure to consider cumulative evidence or to explain how the aggregation was weighed can be reversible error.

# Threats‑only doctrine and exceptions

General rule: threats alone generally insufficient. The Tenth Circuit and other circuits have articulated a cautionary rule: mere threats, absent accompanying harm or a demonstrated ability and intent to carry them out, typically do not rise to persecution. Threats are common in many unstable settings, and the asylum system requires a level of harm beyond general intimidation to qualify as persecution. The analytical rationale is twofold: (1) the statutory focus on “persecution” contemplates actual infliction (or at least a clear likelihood) of severe harm, and (2) treating threats per se as persecution would risk conflation with ordinary criminal intimidation.

When threats may suffice. There are important, recognized exceptions where threats—especially when credible, specific, and tied to a protected ground—can amount to persecution or at least, when considered with other facts, support a finding of past persecution or well‑founded fear:

- Credible and imminent threats that cause severe psychological or functional impairment. A threat that is credible, specific, imminent, and likely to be executed can produce a well‑founded fear of persecution equivalent to a likelihood of harm. For example, death threats accompanied by surveillance, delivery of the weapon, or a near‑execution of the threat can convert mere words into reproachable persecution.

- Threats accompanied by a pattern of coercive conduct. When threats occur as part of an ongoing campaign that includes harassment, property destruction, attacks on family members, or extortion, courts aggregate the conduct; the threats add to the totality and may push the cumulative harm over the persecution threshold.

- Threats coupled with a real inability or unwillingness of the state to protect. Threats from private actors can be effectively persecutions if the state is unable or unwilling to prevent or redress the threats—especially where reporting is futile or dangerous. The government‑protection analysis thus intersects with the threats‑only inquiry.

- Threats causing severe non‑physical harm. In some instances, threats alone can inflict severe psychological trauma or social ostracism that produces disabling effects (e.g., forced isolation, loss of means of survival). When the consequences of threats are legally cognizable as severe harm—particularly where medical or expert evidence corroborates serious mental injury—threats can support a persecution finding.

- Targeted threats tied to a protected ground. Credible threats that explicitly reference a victim’s protected characteristic (e.g., “We will kill you because you are [X]”) and are corroborated by context are stronger evidence of persecution than generic menaces.

Burden and nexus. An applicant alleging threats must satisfy the usual burdens: show that the threats are credible and tied at least in part to a protected ground (the “one central reason” or mixed‑motive approach discussed below). Moreover, for withholding of removal, the threat must create a clear probability of persecution; for asylum, it must create a well‑founded fear.

Adjudicator practice. Immigration judges and the Board must articulate why threats alone do or do not satisfy persecution in each case—addressing imminence, credibility, state protection, and nexus. Appellate review focuses on whether the factfinder reasonably assessed these features in view of the record.

# Role of psychological harm without physical injury

Legal recognition of non‑physical persecution. Increasingly, courts recognize that severe psychological harm—when it meets objective severity standards—can qualify as persecution even in the absence of physical injury. The key is that the psychological harm must be serious, chronic, and disabling to the extent that it is the legal equivalent of persecution.

Standards for psychological injury to count. Adjudicators typically evaluate mental‑harm claims along dimensions similar to physical harm claims:

- Objective severity. The applicant must show, through credible testimony and preferably corroborating evidence (medical records, psychiatric evaluations, expert reports), that the mental suffering is severe. Symptoms like clinical depression, post‑traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), panic attacks, suicidal ideation, or other incapacitating conditions weigh heavily.

- Causation and nexus. Evidence must link the psychological harm to past mistreatment or credible threats caused by persecutory conduct tied to a protected ground. The presence of other stressors that could explain the mental condition (illness, unrelated trauma) is analyzed to ensure the connection is genuine.

- Duration and functional impact. Temporary fear or transient anxiety is insufficient; the harm should interfere materially with daily functioning—work, education, social participation, or self‑care. Longstanding trauma that eliminates meaningful life prospects strengthens a persecution claim.

- Corroboration and medical evidence. While medical proof is not always required, psychiatric or medical records and expert testimony bolster the claim and may be essential where adjudicators find applicant testimony alone ambiguous. Treating clinicians’ reports, physician affidavits, or psychological testing are common forms of corroboration.

Past persecution presumption and psychological harm. A successful showing of past persecution via severe psychological harm can trigger the presumption of a well‑founded fear of future persecution. The presumption’s availability depends on the intensity and permanence of the psychological injury and the broader context (e.g., whether the persecutory conduct is likely to recur).

Interplay with threats‑only doctrine. Psychological harm often arises from threats. Courts assessing threats that did not culminate in physical injury often ask whether the threats produced clinically significant mental injury. If the threats led to such substantial psychological damage, the threats may, in effect, have produced persecution.

Practical adjudication tips. Because claims of psychological harm can be subjective, adjudicators must carefully evaluate credibility and corroboration. Applicants should provide contemporaneous medical records, mental‑health evaluations, and specific factual descriptions of how the trauma affects daily life. The Board and courts will uphold a denial only if substantial evidence supports the IJ’s findings that psychological effects were not severe, not credibly tied to persecutory conduct, or easily treatable and unrelated.

# Nexus and the “one central reason” standard

Although the focal subject of other chapters, nexus (whether the harm was “on account of” a protected characteristic) is inseparable from persecution analysis. The Tenth Circuit follows the mixed‑motive or “one central reason” test: an applicant need not show that the protected ground was the sole reason for the harm; rather, it must be at least one central reason. That is especially significant in mixed‑motive contexts—e.g., violence motivated by economics and ethnicity, or gangs acting partly for profit and partly to punish dissent.

Key features of nexus analysis:

- Mixed motives permitted. The presence of non‑protected motives does not defeat asylum if the protected ground is a central or substantial reason for the mistreatment. Courts examine statements by persecutors, pattern and selectivity of attacks, and contextual evidence to infer motives.

- Tangential or incidental motives insufficient. If a protected trait played only an incidental or tangential role—e.g., the applicant was attacked principally for a personal dispute without any connection to the protected ground—then nexus is lacking.

- Corroborative evidence and inference. Admissions by attackers, targeting patterns (e.g., attacks aimed specifically at members of a protected group), and contemporaneous documents or testimony can support the required connection.

# Government unable‑or‑unwilling prong and its role with private actors

When the source of persecution is a private actor (not a government official), the applicant must show that the government of the home country is unable or unwilling to control the persecutors or to protect the applicant. The inability/unwillingness question is fact‑intensive and interacts with the nature of threats and cumulative harm.

Conventional tests and proofs. Courts examine whether the applicant reported the mistreatment, whether the authorities took reasonable steps to investigate and punish perpetrators, and whether reporting is feasible or would increase risk. A showing that the police ignored reports, made collusive statements, detained the victim improperly, or are themselves supportive of the persecutors favors a finding of inability or unwillingness.

Futility or danger exception. The applicant may be excused from reporting if doing so would be futile or would create additional risk (for example, where the police are co‑conspirators or where reporting leads to immediate retaliation). Evidence that reporting has historically resulted in reprisals or that police systematically turn a blind eye supports the futility or danger argument.

State protection adequacy. Not every government response suffices; the question is whether the state will provide effective protection. Token gestures, inadequate investigations, or mere promises without enforcement do not negate an applicant’s inability/unwillingness claim.

Burden and appellate review. The applicant bears the burden of proving inability/unwillingness as part of establishing refugee status. The Board and IJ assess the country‑conditions record, police reports, and applicant testimony. Appellate courts defer to factual determinations absent compulsion to the contrary but review legal interpretations (e.g., the standard to excuse reporting) de novo.

# Procedural posture and standards of review

It is important to keep in mind the different legal standards and how procedural posture shapes review:

- Burden of proof. For asylum, the applicant must show refugee status by a preponderance of the evidence (subject to credibility determinations). For withholding of removal, the applicant must show a “clear probability” (a likelihood) of persecution. For relief under the Convention Against Torture, the standard is that it is more likely than not that the applicant would be tortured.

- Credibility. Many persecution claims rest on applicant testimony about private incidents. Credibility determinations are central; they affect whether multiple incidents can be aggregated and whether threats were credible. Immigration judges must make specific findings on credibility; appellate courts defer to those findings unless the record compels a contrary conclusion.

- Substantial‑evidence review. The Board’s factual findings are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard. Legal conclusions (e.g., whether a set of facts qualifies as persecution) are reviewed de novo, but where legal interpretations involve ambiguous statutory provisions, Chevron deference principles may apply to agency interpretations.

- Remand for inadequate analysis. If the IJ or Board fails to address cumulative evidence, psychological harm, or the inability/unwillingness of the state, appellate courts may remand for clarification and further proceedings rather than resolve the merits themselves.

# Practical implications for applicants and advocates

- Provide detailed factual narratives. To prove cumulative harm or psychological injury, present a chronological account of incidents with specifics on dates, participants, locations, and consequences.

- Corroboration is critical. Medical/psychological reports, police reports, contemporaneous communications, affidavits from witnesses, and country‑conditions materials strengthen allegations of severe harm, threats’ credibility, and state inability/unwillingness.

- Tie harms to a protected ground. Demonstrate why persecutors targeted the applicant for a protected reason—use statements by attackers, patterns of selective targeting, or evidence of group‑based animus.

- Address state protection proactively. Show attempts to seek police help (or explain why reporting was impossible or dangerous) and supply documentary evidence about police responsiveness or complicity.

- Anticipate mixed‑motive attacks. Frame the protected ground as at least a central reason; courts accept mixed motives so long as the protected ground is central, not merely incidental.

Conclusion

Understanding what rises to persecution requires fine‑grained analysis: severity, frequency, context, nexus, and state protection all matter. Courts in the Tenth Circuit analyze whether multiple incidents, even if individually modest, aggregate to persecution; they generally treat threats alone with skepticism unless threats are credible, imminent, tied to a protected ground, or produce severe psychological harm or occur in a context of government non‑protection. Psychological injury can satisfy the persecution requirement when it is objectively severe and linked to harm on account of a protected ground. Procedural rigor—careful findings on credibility, cumulative evidence, nexus, and state protection—governs appeals, and applicants who understand these elements can structure evidence and testimony to meet the relevant standards.
