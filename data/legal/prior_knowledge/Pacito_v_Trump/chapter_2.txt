Introduction

A stay pending appeal is an equitable remedy that temporarily suspends the operation of a lower court order while an appellate court considers the merits of an appeal. The remedy is common when litigants seek to prevent irreparable consequences that would render appellate review meaningless, or when enforcement of a judgment would upend the status quo in ways that cannot be fully undone. Because a stay intrudes into the ordinary course of litigation and may affect third parties and public interests, courts treat stays as extraordinary relief and apply a multi-factor balancing test. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework courts use to decide stay motions, the relative weight of the factors, how status-quo considerations shape the analysis, requirements for bonds or other security, how stays pending appeal relate to injunctions pending appeal, and how courts tailor stays to address specific equities.

# Four-factor test for stays pending appeal

Most federal and state courts analyze motions for a stay pending appeal under a four-factor test. The moving party must generally establish each of the following elements:

1. A likelihood of success on the merits of the appeal (or, in some circuits, a substantial case on the merits);
2. A likelihood of irreparable harm to the moving party if the stay is denied;
3. That the balance of equities (or balance of harms) favors granting the stay; and
4. That the public interest favors a stay.

These elements derive from the principles underlying preliminary injunction doctrine and equitable relief more broadly. A stay, like a preliminary injunction, is prophylactic: it preserves the court’s ability to grant effective relief after appeal by maintaining the pre-deprivation conditions or preventing actions that would render reversal illusory.

Although the four factors are routinely listed as discrete elements, they operate together in a single equitable inquiry. Courts do not mechanically tally points; instead, they weigh the factors against one another. Some courts describe the analysis as conjunctive—requiring a showing on all four—while others permit one factor to compensate for a weaker showing on another under a sliding-scale approach. For example, a very strong showing of likelihood of success can sometimes justify a stay despite a less compelling showing on irreparable harm, and vice versa, depending on the jurisdiction and the circumstances. But courts uniformly stress that a stay is extraordinary relief, so the moving party bears a significant burden to justify it.

A few procedural qualifications inform the four-factor inquiry. First, the movant bears the burden of proof. Second, the moving papers should identify the precise order to be stayed, explain the issues likely to be presented on appeal, and provide evidence supporting allegations of irreparable harm and effects on third parties and the public. Third, timing matters: an application made before a final judgment, during expedited litigation, or in the context of emergency relief raises different considerations about the appropriate scope and duration of the stay.

# Primacy of likelihood of success and irreparable harm

In practice, the two most important elements in most stay cases are the movant’s prospects on the merits and the risk of irreparable harm absent a stay. Courts often treat these as threshold requirements, although formulations vary.

Likelihood (or substantial case) on the merits. Courts require a showing that the appeal raises serious legal questions likely to result in reversal or at least that there is a substantial case on the merits. The exact phrasing differs by circuit. Some courts demand a “probability” or “likelihood” of success; others accept a less exacting “substantial case” standard where the legal question is close and the balance of hardships tips strongly in favor of the movant. In evaluating likelihood, appellate courts focus on the strength of the legal arguments and the record facts as developed in the district court. The movant should identify the standard of review the appellate court will use—de novo review favors a stronger likelihood showing, while clear-error review may make success less likely—and explain why the district court erred under that standard.

Irreparable harm. Irreparable harm means harm that cannot be adequately remedied by money damages or by relief after final judgment. It is harm that is certain, great, actual, and imminent. The moving party must present concrete evidence showing that denial of a stay will cause harm that cannot be undone by a favorable appellate decision. Examples include loss of constitutional rights, dismantling of an ongoing business enterprise, irreversible destruction of property, or enforcement of a statute that would change legal relationships permanently. The harm must be tied to the effect of the order being stayed; speculative or merely economic injuries usually do not suffice unless they are so severe that later monetary relief would be inadequate.

Relationship between the two factors. Courts often treat a strong showing on one of these two elements as compensating for a weaker showing on the other. This is the so‑called sliding-scale approach: if the movant makes a very strong showing of likelihood of success, less evidence of irreparable harm may be necessary, and if irreparable harm is manifest and imminent, a weaker showing on the merits might suffice. However, the weight courts give to this trade-off varies, and some circuits insist that the two elements remain essential. Practically, the most persuasive stay applications carefully establish both prospects of reversal and imminent, non-compensable harm.

# Status-quo considerations

A central purpose of a stay pending appeal is preservation of the status quo that existed before the challenged action would take full effect. Courts often define the “status quo” as the last uncontested state of affairs between the parties that can be maintained while appellate review proceeds. This concept serves both as a guiding principle in tailoring stays and as an independent factor in the equitable balance.

Preservation of the status quo. A stay typically seeks to freeze positions, prevent enforcement of an order, or maintain existing operations until the appellate court can rule. Preserving the status quo aims to ensure that a reversal on appeal will not be rendered moot by irreversible changes. For example, if a lower court orders the immediate transfer, destruction, or disposition of property, or the immediate startup or shutdown of operations, a stay will be particularly important to preserve effective appellate relief.

Identification of the appropriate baseline. Courts must determine which baseline to preserve. The relevant status quo might be the last uncontested state (for instance, the arrangement before a preliminary injunction was issued), or it might be the position that existed immediately before implementation of the order sought to be stayed. Parties can contest what the natural status quo is—was the challenged action already in effect, or was it prospective?—and that dispute affects the stay request. The choice of baseline can be outcome-determinative because courts are less inclined to permit dramatic changes in settled conditions.

Avoiding disruption without undue delay. While preserving the status quo is important, courts also weigh the disruptive effects of an excessively broad or indefinite stay. A stay should aim to preserve relief that matters for appellate review without freezing every contested aspect of the case indefinitely. Courts tailor stays to limit collateral harm and to respect third-party reliance interests and public needs while keeping the appellate issues intact.

# Bonds and security

Requiring security in connection with a stay is a traditional mechanism to protect the nonmoving party against losses caused by a stay. Both federal and state courts retain discretion to require a bond or other form of security, and appellate rules often direct appellate courts to consider whether a security requirement is appropriate.

Purpose of bonds. The primary purpose of a bond is to provide a source of recovery for the party that would be harmed by the stay if the appeal is unsuccessful. For example, if a stay suspends payment of a money judgment or allows the release of property, a bond can assure the judgment creditor of funds to satisfy the judgment or compensate for loss. In non-monetary contexts, courts may require security calibrated to the potential economic harm that a stay would cause.

Standards and alternatives. The amount of security is within the court’s sound discretion and should be reasonable under the circumstances. Courts consider the potential damages the nonmoving party might suffer, the movant’s ability to post a bond, and whether alternative forms of security (such as an escrow, letter of credit, or undertaking) are acceptable. In some circumstances—particularly where the stay serves a strong public interest or where the movant is a government entity—the court may reduce or waive bond requirements. Conversely, where the movant is a private party and the stay would impose measurable economic costs on others, courts routinely impose bonds.

Timing and enforcement. Security should be posted promptly and in a form acceptable to the court. If the movant cannot obtain a bond quickly, courts may tailor relief—granting a short stay to allow time to secure a bond, conditioning the stay on later posting, or requiring an alternative security arrangement. If the stay is granted without adequate security, courts may later revisit the issue and require compliance as a condition of continuing the stay.

# Comparing stays pending appeal with injunctions pending appeal

Stays pending appeal and injunctions pending appeal are related but distinct remedies. Both are equitable devices that preserve rights or positions during appellate review, but they arise in different procedural contexts and sometimes under different standards.

Nature and purpose. A stay pending appeal suspends enforcement or operation of a lower-court order while appeal proceeds. An injunction pending appeal (sometimes called an injunction pending resolution of an appeal) is a specific form of injunctive relief that may be entered by an appellate court to preserve the status quo or prevent irreparable harm during appellate review. In practice, the terms overlap: a court may grant a stay that functions like an injunction, and an appellate court may issue an injunction as part of its stay power.

Procedural vehicles. A district court can stay its own order pending resolution of post-judgment motions or appellate filing, and an appellate court can stay enforcement of lower-court orders under its supervisory authority and applicable appellate rules. Parties may seek relief in either forum—first asking the district court to stay enforcement and then, if denied or if expedited relief is needed, seeking emergency relief from an appellate court. Procedural rules (for example, the Federal Rules of Appellate Procedure) supply mechanisms for emergency motions for stays or injunctions pending appeal, and they sometimes require a showing that the movant sought relief in the district court before approaching the appellate court.

Standards of review and burden. The substantive equitable standard for an injunction pending appeal is essentially the same four-factor inquiry used for stays; appellate courts often apply similar balancing analyses. However, in some instances, appellate courts apply a stricter standard—particularly when the appeal is unlikely to succeed or when the requested injunction would effect a major change in public policy. Appellate practice also introduces different considerations: appellate courts are mindful of the finality of district-court judgments and of comity with district courts, and they may be reluctant to substitute their view on questions of fact or to grant broad relief in a summary, emergency proceeding.

Effect and scope. An injunction pending appeal may be broader or narrower than a stay, depending on what the appellate court finds necessary to preserve the parties’ rights. Because injunctions often require specific acts or restraints, the drafting must be precise to avoid overbroad disruption. A stay tends to be narrowly focused on suspending the operative effect of an order rather than imposing affirmative duties. The choice between the two remedies is often tactical: a party seeking to prevent an action’s enforcement will typically request a stay; a party seeking more affirmative protection (for example, continued operation of a program while appeal is pending) may seek an injunction.

# Tailoring and partial stays

Equitable relief is most effective when tailored to the precise injury and the appellate questions presented. Courts exercise wide discretion to fashion stays that are limited in duration, scope, geography, and subject matter to reduce collateral harm while preserving meaningful appellate relief.

Narrow scope. A court can grant a stay that suspends only certain provisions of an order rather than the entire judgment. Partial stays may pause enforcement as to particular parties, narrow categories of conduct, or specific temporal segments. The goal is to address the cognizable harms identified by the movant without unnecessarily interfering with other lawful activities. For instance, a stay might preserve the operation of a statute only with respect to a class of stakeholders whose rights are at issue on appeal.

Temporal limits. Courts commonly set explicit deadlines for stays, either by specifying an expiration date or by conditioning the stay on the filing of an appeal or the posting of security. Time-limited stays ensure that the matter receives prompt appellate attention and that the pause does not become indefinite. Courts often require the movant to show good cause for any extension.

Geographic or party-based tailoring. When an order’s effects vary by location or implicate different parties differently, courts may limit a stay to particular jurisdictions or to certain litigants. Tailoring in this manner reduces the impact on third parties and on the public while preserving appellate review for those directly affected.

Interim and phased relief. In complex cases, courts may adopt phased stays that permit some activities to continue under conditions while halting others. For example, a court may allow entities to continue in operation subject to reporting requirements, monitoring, or restrictions designed to protect third parties. Such conditioning is a hallmark of equitable tailoring: courts weigh competing interests and fashion a remedy that minimizes harms while maintaining the integrity of appellate review.

Procedural conditions. Courts frequently attach conditions to a stay—requiring the posting of a bond, submission of periodic status reports, or assurances that parties will maintain certain activities. Conditions maintain oversight and provide avenues for modifying or dissolving the stay if circumstances change.

# Procedural posture and special considerations

The procedural posture of the case affects the stay analysis. Different contexts—emergency stays, stays after final judgment, stays pending interlocutory appeal, stays of contempt orders, stays in criminal cases, or stays of administrative enforcement—raise distinct concerns.

Urgency and emergency relief. When a stay is sought on an emergency basis, appellate courts give weight to the speed with which the movant sought relief in the lower court, the immediacy of the harm alleged, and whether delay would make relief ineffective. Emergency motions often arise in truncated timeframes and require concise evidentiary showings.

Final judgment and ordinary appeals. Stays after final judgment pending ordinary appeal implicate the efficient administration of justice and the risk of mooting appellate review. Appellate courts weigh finality and the need to avoid upending settled results if stay applicants fail to persuade. Statistical trends or routine practices in the relevant area of law (e.g., enforcement of fiscal judgments) may influence the court’s equity analysis.

Interlocutory appeals and collateral consequences. Stays pending interlocutory appeals (including appeals authorized by statute or certification) may be more likely if the interlocutory issue is dispositive of the case or if enforcement of the order would render the appeal moot. Courts consider whether the interlocutory appeal materially affects the litigation’s outcome.

Public entities and public-interest considerations. When the movant is a government entity or when public programs are at issue, courts give special attention to public-interest factors. Public entities may have broader discretion to act in the public interest, and courts may be reluctant to enjoin governmental programs unless the movant establishes a strong likelihood of error and compelling irreparable harm. Conversely, when a stay would harm important public interests—such as public health, safety, or fiscal stability—the public-interest factor may weigh heavily against a stay.

# Practical implications for litigants and courts

Litigants seeking stays should do the following: identify and develop the strongest legal arguments on appeal, marshal concrete evidence of irreparable harm, articulate the precise status quo to be preserved, propose reasonable security arrangements, and suggest narrowly tailored relief that minimizes collateral harm. Appellate courts and district courts should require specificity in stay motions, consider whether less intrusive alternatives would suffice, and craft conditions that protect nonmoving parties.

Because the law varies across jurisdictions as to the relative weight of the four factors and the appropriateness of sliding-scale balancing, counsel should research controlling circuit authority on whether a movant must make a strong showing on both likelihood of success and irreparable harm or whether a strong showing on one element can offset a weaker showing on the other. Finally, because stays are discretionary and equitable, outcomes often turn on the particular facts, the nature of the appealed order, and competing public-policy considerations.

Conclusion

A stay pending appeal is an equitable, provisional remedy intended to preserve meaningful appellate review. The governing doctrine rests on a four-factor balance—likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm, balance of equities, and public interest—with particular emphasis on the first two elements. Courts focus on status-quo preservation, require reasonable security when appropriate, and have wide leeway to tailor relief to the specific equities of a case. Understanding these principles enables litigants to frame effective stay motions and prepares readers to analyze appellate orders and emergency relief in the context of ongoing litigation.
