Introduction

Federal assistance instruments—grants and cooperative agreements—provide the principal means by which the federal government advances public policy through funding non-federal entities, including nonprofit organizations that deliver social services. For lawyers who will read judicial opinions about disputes arising from federally funded programs, it is essential to understand the statutory framework that authorizes assistance instruments, the line between assistance and procurement, the legal effect of terms and conditions (and how they may be changed, suspended, or terminated), the distinct remedial tracks available when program recipients object to agency action, the rules that govern exclusion of parties through debarment and suspension, and how courts treat reliance interests when agencies alter or end funding. This chapter lays out those doctrinal principles, the common tests courts employ, the typical burdens of proof and procedural posture issues, and the landscape of administrative and contract law that frames litigation over federal grants and cooperative agreements.

# Federal Grant and Cooperative Agreement Act (31 U.S.C. §§ 6301–6308)

Statutory purpose and basic scheme

The Federal Grant and Cooperative Agreement Act (FGCAA), now found at 31 U.S.C. §§ 6301–6308, establishes a statutory preference framework distinguishing procurement contracts from federal assistance instruments (grants and cooperative agreements) and delegates to agencies the authority to use grants and cooperative agreements where the principal purpose is to transfer money to accomplish a public purpose. The FGCAA reflects the principle that when the federal government’s primary objective is to subsidize or stimulate activity in the public interest (rather than to obtain goods or services for the government’s direct use), the appropriate vehicle is an assistance award rather than a procurement contract.

Key provisions and agency responsibilities

The statute requires agencies to use procurement contracts when the primary objective is to acquire property or services for the direct benefit or use of the federal government. Conversely, when the primary objective is to carry out a public purpose authorized by statute—such as providing refugee services, health care, or educational programs—agencies are expected to use grants or cooperative agreements. The FGCAA thus places substantive and administrative constraints on agencies: they must identify the instrument type that matches the principal purpose and must justify use of assistance instruments consistent with their statutory authorities. The Act also directs agencies to issue guidance and procedures for the administration of grants and cooperative agreements.

Cooperative agreements as a hybrid instrument

The FGCAA recognizes cooperative agreements as a distinct category: they are assistance instruments like grants, but they permit "substantial involvement" by the federal agency in program operations. In practice, cooperative agreements are used when the government intends to participate actively in the funded program’s design, implementation, or evaluation but is not acquiring property or services for its own direct use. Courts and administrative authorities will examine the substance of that "substantial involvement" to determine whether the instrument remains an assistance award (subject to grants rules and judicial review doctrines applicable to agency action) or effectively functions as a procurement contract (subject to procurement law and contract remedies).

# Distinguishing procurement contracts from assistance instruments

The principal-purpose test

A central doctrinal tool for distinguishing procurement contracts from assistance instruments is the “principal purpose” test. The test asks whether the primary objective of the federal instrument is (1) to obtain goods or services for the direct benefit or use of the federal government (a procurement contract) or (2) to transfer money or property to accomplish a public purpose authorized by statute (a grant or cooperative agreement). The test is functional: courts examine the substance and effect of the instrument, not simply the label the agency uses.

Factors courts consider

In applying the principal-purpose test, courts and administrative tribunals typically consider multiple factors:

- The statutory authorization and program context: Is the program’s statutory purpose to provide subsidies or benefits to the public, or to procure services for federal operations?
- The nature of the work performed and who benefits: Does the recipient perform services that primarily benefit private parties or the public at large, or does the government receive a product or service that it will use directly?
- Payment and procurement terms: Are the payment arrangements and administrative terms consistent with federal procurement practice (cost-reimbursement clauses, FAR flowdown terms, contract claims procedures) or with grant administrative regimes (uniform administrative requirements, performance reporting rather than deliverable acceptance)?
- Degree of government control and involvement: Does the agreement give the government day-to-day control, require detailed technical direction, or retain a unilateral right to direct the recipient’s operations? Or does the government exercise oversight and monitoring consistent with a grant relationship?
- Remedies and dispute resolution: Does the instrument set forth contractor-style remedies, termination for convenience provisions, appeals to procurement fora, and other contract-law features? Or does it limit remedies to administrative adjustment, audit recovery, or grant closeout?

“Substantial involvement” and cooperative agreements

Where an agency intends to exercise significant oversight or participate actively in performance, the cooperative agreement vehicle is appropriate. But the presence of government involvement does not automatically make an instrument a contract. Courts will probe whether the government’s involvement crosses the line from collaborative oversight into operational control such that the instrument functions as a procurement. If so, contract law (including procurement statutes, the Administrative Disputes Resolution Act when applicable, and jurisdictional routes like the Court of Federal Claims) may govern disputes; if not, administrative law doctrines concerning agency action and grants administration will control.

Practical consequences of the classification

The classification matters because it determines applicable legal regimes: procurement contracts bring the FAR and procurement protest mechanisms (GAO and COFC), contract remedies, contracting officer decision and appeal paths, and the Tucker Act’s jurisdictional provisions. Assistance instruments fall under the grants regulatory framework—uniform administrative requirements (e.g., OMB’s Uniform Guidance), agency grant regulations, and a mix of reviewability constraints under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) and other statutes. In litigation, that classification often determines whether a plaintiff’s claims sound in contract (breach and contract remedies) or in administrative law (APA review seeking injunctive or declaratory relief).

# Terms, conditions, and termination/suspension of cooperative agreements

Standard terms and enforceable conditions

Grants and cooperative agreements are governed by a mix of statutory authority, agency regulations, and the specific award document. The award typically incorporates uniform administrative requirements (e.g., those promulgated in 2 C.F.R. Part 200), program-specific conditions, reporting obligations, allowable cost principles, audit and record-keeping rules, and compliance obligations (e.g., nondiscrimination, procurement standards). These terms are binding on recipients and create expectations about performance, monitoring, and allowable expenditures.

Agencies also condition awards on statutory requirements and may include special award conditions for particular recipients (e.g., higher-risk grantees). Courts will enforce conditions set forth in the award and those required by statute or regulation; where questions arise about whether a change in conditions is permissible, the issue often turns on the agency’s statutory authority, notice and comment obligations (if rulemaking is involved), and the award language itself.

Authority to suspend or terminate and the applicable standards

Agencies generally possess express statutory and regulatory authority to suspend or terminate assistance awards for cause (e.g., noncompliance, failure to perform, or criminal or civil wrongdoing) or for convenience in certain circumstances. The uniform administrative rules specify grounds for suspension and termination, notice and opportunity-to-remedy procedures, and the consequences of termination. A few key legal principles govern these actions:

- Procedural requirements. Regulations usually require notice of suspension or termination, an explanation of the reasons, and often an opportunity to cure where appropriate. The exact procedural protections depend on whether the agency is taking immediate action to protect funds (e.g., mandatory suspension pending investigation) or is pursuing a discretionary termination for cause.
- Standard of review. When recipients challenge suspension or termination, courts generally give agencies deference on factual determinations but will require that the action be not arbitrary and capricious, supported by the record, and consistent with applicable statutes and regulations. If the instrument is deemed a contract, contractual standards and procurement-specific procedures (including contracting officer decisions and administrative appeals) will govern.
- Effects of termination. Termination affects closeout, allowable costs, disposition of property acquired with federal funds, and the recipient’s obligations to refund unexpended funds or to submit final reports. The remedial and financial consequences are specific to the award terms and the applicable grant regulations.

Emergency suspensions and temporary measures

In circumstances involving alleged fraud, criminal activity, or imminent misuse of funds, agencies may invoke emergency suspension procedures to protect federal funds and program integrity. Such emergency actions are often time-limited and must be justified by evidence of immediate risk. Judicial review of emergency suspensions will balance the government’s interest in safeguarding funds against the recipient’s due process rights; courts typically examine whether the agency had a reasonable basis to act and whether the agency complied with required procedural safeguards.

Reformation, retroactive modification, and unilateral changes

Agencies sometimes seek to modify award terms retroactively or to impose additional conditions mid-performance. Whether such changes are lawful depends on the award’s modification clause, the scope of the agency’s statutory authority, and whether the changes are arbitrary or retroactively impair legitimate expectations. If the instrument is a contract, unilateral changes must generally be authorized by clear contract clauses and consistent with procurement law; if an assistance award, agencies may have broader regulatory authority but still must respect statutory constraints and avoid arbitrary deprivation of funded activities.

# Programmatic vs. contractual challenges (APA vs. contract remedies)

Two distinct remedial tracks

Challenges to agency action that affects grants and cooperative agreements typically fall into two remedial tracks: (1) administrative-law challenges under the Administrative Procedure Act or other statutes that govern agency action (programmatic challenges), and (2) contract-law remedies available when the instrument operates as a procurement contract (contractual challenges). Identifying the correct track is crucial because it dictates standing, available relief, applicable procedural prerequisites, and the standard of judicial review.

Programmatic challenges: scope and limits

When a recipient attacks an agency’s funding decision, policy change, or program-level action and the instrument is an assistance award (or is treated as such), plaintiffs typically proceed under the APA to challenge final agency action. Key features of APA review in this context include:

- Final agency action requirement. Plaintiffs must identify a final agency action that directly affects their legal rights or obligations.
- Reviewability. Courts will review agency action under the APA unless statutes commit the decision to agency discretion by law or the action involves matters traditionally unreviewable (e.g., certain policy judgments or allocation of broad statutory discretion). Many discretionary programmatic choices—such as funding priorities, allocation decisions, or program design—are often insulated from judicial review.
- Standard of review. For reviewable agency actions, courts apply the familiar arbitrary and capricious standard (5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A)) and require reasoned decisionmaking. The court examines whether the agency considered relevant factors, avoided clear error of judgment, and provided reasoned explanations for departures from prior policy.
- Remedies. Remedies under the APA typically include declaratory relief, injunctive relief, or vacatur/remand of agency action. Monetary relief is generally not available under the APA against the United States except pursuant to specific statutory authorization.

Contractual challenges: breach, remedies, and tribunals

When an instrument is a procurement contract—or is functionally equivalent—recipients may have contract claims. Contractual disputes differ in several important ways:

- Contract law principles. Breach-of-contract claims are governed by common-law contract principles and procurement statutes/regulations. The terms of the contract govern duties, performance standards, and remedies for breach.
- Relief available. Contract remedies can include money damages for breach, restitution, set-off, and in some procurement contexts, specific disputes procedures leading to claims before administrative tribunals (e.g., contracting officer decisions and appeals to boards or the Court of Federal Claims).
- Jurisdictional routes. Contract claims against the United States require a waiver of sovereign immunity. Under the Tucker Act, the United States Court of Federal Claims has jurisdiction over many federal contract claims seeking monetary relief. For procurement bid protests, the Government Accountability Office (GAO) and the Court of Federal Claims have specific roles.

Interaction and choice-of-remedies issues

A central litigation issue arises when an award contains elements of both assistance and procurement or when the agency’s actions implicate both programmatic policy and contractual promises. Several consequences follow:

- If a court deems the instrument a contract or finds the government exercised operational control amounting to a contract, it will allow contract remedies that frequently include money damages and procedural protections not present in APA review.
- If the instrument is an assistance award and the action challenged is a discretionary, policy-based funding decision, courts may refuse to review the action under the APA for lack of finality or because the decision is committed to agency discretion.
- Plaintiffs sometimes plead alternative theories (contract and APA); courts will sort them out in light of the instrument’s substance and applicable jurisdictional statutes.
- Procedural posture is critical: plaintiffs may need to exhaust administrative remedies (e.g., agency administrative appeals, audit resolution processes) before seeking judicial relief, particularly when procedures are specified in the award or by statute.

# Debarment and suspension principles

Overview of the debarment and suspension regime

Debarment and suspension exclude persons or entities from receiving federal funds when they have engaged in misconduct that calls into question their integrity or reliability. The purpose is to protect federal programs from involvement with unreliable or untrustworthy parties and to maintain program integrity. The federal regulatory framework establishes grounds for action, procedural protections, and the practical effects of exclusion.

Grounds for suspension and debarment

Common grounds for suspension and debarment include conviction of or civil judgments for fraud, violation of antitrust laws, embezzlement, false statements, tax evasion, or other offenses indicating lack of responsibility. Administrative determinations of irresponsible behavior—such as significant failure to perform on previous awards—also support exclusion. Agencies must base debarment and suspension on evidence that the recipient’s conduct undermines its propriety to participate in federal programs.

Procedural protections and burden of proof

Suspension is often an interim measure taken when there is an urgent need to protect government funds, typically based on a lower showing of immediate risk; debarment is a final administrative exclusion imposed after procedural process. Procedural protections generally include written notice of the reasons for action, an opportunity to respond or contest, and an administrative record supporting the agency’s determination. High-stakes debarments are subject to fuller procedures and review; suspensions may be used where immediate action is warranted, with subsequent proceedings to determine whether debarment is appropriate.

Regulatory framework and administrative review

The federal government’s debarment and suspension regulations set out standard procedures for referrals, notices, and hearings. Affected parties may seek administrative reconsideration or judicial review of debarment decisions. Judicial review will examine whether the agency observed required procedures, had a factual basis for the action, and followed applicable legal standards; courts will generally not substitute their judgment for the agency’s inferences of risk unless the record lacks a rational basis.

Consequences and collateral effects

Debarment and suspension have immediate consequences: excluded entities cannot receive new federal grants, cooperative agreements, or contracts for the period of exclusion, and existing awards may be terminated or suspended. Collateral reputational effects can be severe; third-party subcontractors or funders may sever relationships. Agencies must weigh program interests against due-process protections and the need for fairness in selecting exclusion as a remedy.

# Reliance interests when altering or ending funded programs

Legitimate expectations and detrimental reliance

When agencies alter or terminate funding for ongoing programs, courts often analyze whether the affected parties had legitimate expectations or made investments in reliance on prior agency commitments. Reliance interests arise where recipients have structured their operations, hired staff, entered into leases, or incurred obligations because of reasonably foreseeable continuation of funding. Courts will examine whether those reliance expectations were reasonable and whether the agency’s conduct created a binding expectation.

Legal doctrines that protect reliance

Several legal doctrines may protect reliance interests, though their applicability against the government is limited:

- Equitable estoppel. Equitable estoppel may bar the government from asserting a change in position where a government official made a clear, unambiguous representation that a party reasonably relied upon to its detriment. However, courts apply equitable estoppel against the government narrowly, often requiring proof of affirmative misconduct, a clear and unequivocal government promise, and substantial reliance that would result in injustice without estoppel. Moreover, equitable estoppel cannot be used to create monetary liability beyond what the law permits.
- Legitimate expectation doctrine. In administrative law, a recipient may argue that an agency’s established practice or specific assurances create a legitimate expectation that program rules or funding levels will continue. Where a legitimate expectation exists, an abrupt change without reasoned explanation or without providing adequate transition may be arbitrary and capricious. This doctrine is most successful when the agency has engaged in consistent, specific representations that induced reliance.
- Contractual promises. Where a funding instrument contains binding terms guaranteeing funding or specifying conditions for termination, recipients may have contractual claims, which protect reliance through contract remedies such as damages or enforcement of contract terms.

Transition, notice, and mitigation

Even when agencies have discretion to alter or end programs, the reasonableness of their process matters. Courts will consider whether the agency provided adequate notice, allowed for a transition period, or tailored the change to mitigate reliance harms. Agencies often take account of practical reliance by offering wind-down funding, transitional assistance, or phased reductions to avoid precipitous disruptions to beneficiaries and recipients.

Remedies and equitable balancing

When courts find that reliance interests have been unduly disregarded, remedies are typically equitable: injunctions to maintain funding for a transition period, remands with instructions to consider reliance, or declarations that the agency’s decision was arbitrary and capricious. Courts are often cautious about ordering long-term funding obligations because of separation-of-powers concerns and congressional control over appropriations. Monetary relief for reliance is unusual against the government absent a contract or statutory waiver of sovereign immunity.

Practical litigation posture considerations

Claims premised on reliance frequently raise preliminary issues that affect justiciability—standing (must show concrete injury), ripeness (action must be final and cause imminent injury), and exhaustion of administrative remedies (if the award prescribes administrative appeals). Courts will examine the record for evidence of reliance, the degree of government involvement in creating expectations, and whether the agency provided reasoned explanations when changing course. In many cases, plaintiffs obtain temporary injunctive relief to preserve the status quo during litigation; courts then assess whether longer-term relief is appropriate given the equities and legal rules.

Conclusion

Lawyers preparing to read judicial opinions about disputes over refugee services or other federally funded nonprofit activities must be conversant with the FGCAA’s framework, the principal-purpose test that distinguishes procurement from assistance, the legal consequences of terms and conditions in grants and cooperative agreements, the different remedial pathways (APA programmatic review versus contract remedies), the protective and punitive roles of debarment and suspension, and the doctrines that govern reliance when agencies alter or end funding. Litigation outcomes hinge on careful analysis of the instrument’s substance, the statutory and regulatory authorities invoked, the procedural posture of the challenge, and the factual record documenting reliance and agency decisionmaking. Understanding these doctrinal building blocks will make judicial opinions in this area intelligible and permit reasoned predictions about litigation strategies and likely remedies.
