Introduction

Stays are among the most important provisional remedies in appellate practice. A stay postpones the effect or enforcement of a lower-court order while a higher court considers the underlying legal challenge. Because stays temporarily alter the status quo and can have profound practical consequences for litigants and third parties, courts have developed careful, flexible standards for deciding when to grant them. This chapter explains the modern governing framework for stays pending appeal, the relative weight courts give to the different factors, how the stay inquiry differs from the preliminary-injunction inquiry, how federal courts have applied and adapted the framework, and practical guidance for litigating stay motions and making the necessary evidentiary showings. The discussion is doctrinally oriented and emphasizes legal reasoning, how courts apply rules to facts, and how procedural posture shapes the analysis.

# General nature of a stay; stay vs injunction

A stay is an order postponing or suspending the effect or enforcement of a judgment, order, or other judicial proceeding. Conceptually, a stay operates on the litigation process or on the enforceability of an order; it does not usually require the court to command ongoing conduct by the opposing party in the way a coercive injunction does. Courts label remedies differently for legal and statutory reasons, and the distinction matters: many statutes limit issuance of injunctions, while stays that operate on appellate proceedings proceed under the court’s inherent or ancillary authority and under appellate rules. The procedural vehicle for a stay depends on context: it may be a stay pending appeal, a stay of execution of judgment, a temporary restraining order, or a stay of a judgment’s enforcement.

Because a stay alters practical entitlements pending appellate review, courts view stays as extraordinary relief. The movant therefore must meet a demanding multi-factor standard. That standard is grounded in courts’ responsibility both to prevent irreversible harm that would render appellate review meaningless and to respect the finality and prompt enforcement of judgments that the law permits to take effect.

# The four-factor stay framework and its weighting

The contemporary governing framework evaluates four factors:

1. Likelihood of success on the merits of the appeal (or alternatively whether the appeal raises substantial questions likely to result in reversal).
2. Likelihood of irreparable harm to the movant absent a stay.
3. Whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure other parties.
4. Where the public interest lies.

These factors are not mechanical; courts balance them to determine whether the movant has carried the burden to justify extraordinary relief. Two general principles about weighting and relationship among the factors govern most opinions.

Primacy of likelihood of success and irreparable harm. Courts typically treat the first two factors—likelihood of success on the merits and irreparable harm—as the most important. A strong showing on both often suffices to justify a stay. If the movant demonstrates both a high likelihood of success and a high risk of irreparable injury absent a stay, the other factors rarely defeat the motion. Conversely, if the movant makes little or no showing of irreparable harm or of legal merit, a stay is typically denied regardless of the public interest.

Sliding-scale formulations and alternatives. The four-factor framework is flexible. Courts often employ a “sliding scale” or “balance” approach: a stronger showing on one of the principal factors can compensate for a weaker showing on the other. That formulation is commonly expressed as: where the likelihood of success is “less than probable” the movant can still obtain a stay by showing that the other factors, among them a substantial risk of irreparable injury, tip strongly in its favor. Some courts permit a “serious questions” alternative: if the appeal raises serious legal questions and the balance of hardships strongly favors the movant, a stay may be appropriate even if the movant cannot show a clear likelihood of success. But courts are careful to require a correspondingly stronger showing on the other elements when the likelihood-of-success prong is reduced.

Allocation of burdens. The movant bears the ultimate burden of persuasion to demonstrate that a stay is justified under the four factors. Where the party seeking stay is the government or another party whose ability to enforce a judgment is supported by statute, courts may treat the burden and balancing with heightened caution, given the public-authority implications. If the movant seeks to maintain the status quo (i.e., to keep things as they were before the lower-court order was implemented), courts sometimes call for a less dramatic showing than would be necessary for a stay that upends the status quo.

Precise formulations. Different courts adopt nuanced language about the showings required. Some require a “strong showing” of likelihood of success; others require the movant to show a “reasonable probability” of success; yet others adopt the “serious questions” plus balance-of-hardships approach. The precise words matter for litigation strategy and proof: more demanding formulations require more extensive legal argument and a fuller record.

# Differences from preliminary-injunction standards

Superficially, the stay test resembles the test for preliminary injunctions: both ask about likelihood of success, irreparable harm, balance of harms, and the public interest. But there are important doctrinal and practical differences.

Purpose and timing. Preliminary injunctions govern prospective conduct in an ongoing case before a final judgment; they are forward-looking and often designed to preserve the status quo to avoid irreparable harm pending trial. Stays generally operate after a final judgment or interlocutory order and function to suspend enforcement while an appellate tribunal reviews that judgment. Because a stay is an appellate procedural device, appellate rules and principles shape its contours.

Degrees of burden and the “sliding scale.” Preliminary-injunction doctrine has evolved to recognize a sliding scale of proof (in some circuit authorities): a strong showing of irreparable harm may offset a lesser showing of likelihood of success, and vice versa. Appellate stays use a similar sliding approach, but appellate courts emphasize the need to protect final judgments and to avoid unwarranted delay in enforcement. Many courts, therefore, require a stronger showing of likelihood of success for stays than for preliminary injunctions that preserve the status quo pre-judgment. Conversely, where the interest at stake implicates liberty or other weighty non-monetary harms, an emergency stay may require a lesser showing of likelihood of success.

Nature of the harms. The irreparable-harm inquiry at the preliminary-injunction stage often centers on harms that will make the eventual remedy inadequate. In appeals, however, the irreparable-harm analysis may focus on whether the lower-court order’s execution will produce consequences that cannot be undone if appellate relief is ultimately granted. For example, the removal of a person from the jurisdiction, irreversible destruction of property, or the consummation of mergers or transactions may present irreparable injury in the appellate context.

Procedural posture and record quality. Preliminary injunctions are typically litigated in a trial-court record with discovery and testimonial evidence. Stay motions are often decided on a truncated record; appellate courts rely primarily on the lower-court record and on affidavits or declarations accompanying the stay motion. Because the appellate record may be limited, courts sometimes frame the merits-inquiry at the stay stage as whether the appeal raises “substantial” or “serious” questions, but they may still require some demonstration that the legal challenge is not frivolous and has a viable path to reversal.

Statutory constraints. Some statutes impose special requirements for injunctions or stay-like relief. Where a statute explicitly constrains equitable relief (for example, by conditioning injunctions on a heightened showing), courts must reconcile those constraints with the conventional stay framework. Courts distinguish stays that “operate on the proceeding” from injunctions that command government action; that distinction can be dispositive in statutory contexts where injunctions are forbidden or limited.

# Procedural posture effects: emergency stays, stays pending appeal, stays pending certiorari, and stays of execution

The procedural posture of the stay request influences the substantive inquiry and the practical relief available.

Emergency stays pending execution or removal. When a stay is sought to prevent imminent execution of an order (for example, imminent removal or demolition), courts often apply an expedited, stringent analysis because of the imminent and potentially irreversible nature of the threatened conduct. The movant must show a high probability of success or strong alternative proof that serious legal questions exist and that irreparable harm is imminent. Courts are also mindful of statutory constraints and of the need to tailor relief narrowly to avoid needless disruption.

Stays pending appeal in the ordinary course. Where an appeal is forthcoming and the stay would preserve the status quo while the appellate tribunal considers legal error, courts take a measured approach. They consider whether the appeal presents a reasonable probability of reversal or serious questions and whether irreparable injury is likely absent a stay. The stay order commonly lasts through the appeal or until the appellate court decides otherwise; it may be conditioned on a bond or other security and narrowly tailored to the issues raised on appeal.

Stays pending filing of a writ of certiorari. When the stay is sought in the Supreme Court’s context or in anticipation of certiorari, the relevant question often becomes whether the petitioner shows “a reasonable probability” that the Court will grant review and, if so, a fair prospect of reversal; coupled with irreparable harm and the public-interest balance, the Court then decides whether to grant a stay pending certiorari. The standard applied here may be in tension with ordinary appellate standards and is often informed by the Court’s practice, which emphasizes the need to avoid unnecessary interference with decisions of lower courts absent a plausible path to reversal.

Stays of execution of judgment. Courts also entertain motions to stay execution of a monetary judgment or other forms of relief. The considerations are similar but take account of the availability of alternatives (bond, escrow, supersedeas bonds) and the risk that monetary relief can be remedied later. Courts commonly require a supersedeas bond to protect the judgment creditor against injury during the stay, unless there are compelling reasons for alternative arrangements.

Scope and duration of stays. Courts tailor stays narrowly: geographic limits, temporal limits, and subject-matter limits are common. Practically, a stay should preserve the issues on appeal and avoid prejudicing third parties unnecessarily. If circumstances change—new evidence, changed regulations, or altered facts—courts may modify or dissolve a stay.

# Subsequent applications in federal courts and doctrinal variations

Federal appellate and district courts have applied the four-factor framework with variations across circuits and contexts. Several themes characterize subsequent applications.

Adoption with flexible formulations. Most federal courts have adopted the core four-factor structure but differ in emphasis. Some circuits articulate the requirement as a “strong showing” of likelihood of success; others accept “serious questions” plus a strong balance of harms where the movant cannot meet a high probability standard. The “sliding scale” rhetoric predominates: stronger showing on one prong may offset weaker showings on another.

Context-sensitive adaptations. Courts adapt the framework to the particular legal and factual context. In First Amendment cases or where fundamental rights or liberty interests are at stake, courts sometimes require less certainty on the likelihood-of-success prong because of the gravity of the harm. Conversely, when the stay would enjoin enforcement of government policy or delay government programs, courts often require a stronger showing of likelihood of success or of public-interest considerations favoring the stay.

Statute-specific modifications. When statutes constrain injunctive relief or channel the remedy to specialized procedures, courts analyze whether a stay is conceptually a substitute for an injunction. If a statute forbids injunctive relief but not stays that affect the enforceability of orders pending appeal, courts may grant stays more readily. But where Congress provides express remedies or limits, courts are cautious about using stay authority to circumvent statutory limits.

Injunction-by-stay concerns. Some courts scrutinize stay requests to ensure the movant is not obtaining via a stay the same relief that would be barred as an injunction under statute. That scrutiny factors into both the merits and the public-interest inquiry, especially where the stay would provide broad nationwide relief or operate beyond the narrow preservation of appellate rights.

Differing standards for stays pending different types of review. Courts draw distinctions among stays pending direct appeal, stays pending collateral review (habeas), and stays pending administrative or enforcement proceedings. In habeas contexts, irreparable-harm and public-interest analyses can differ because the background constitutional harms and the state's interest in finality are both weighty.

Emergency procedural tools. When an appellate court is asked for an emergency administrative stay, courts frequently issue abbreviated rulings accompanied by more detailed orders later. The urgency of the application sometimes leads courts to grant temporary stays to maintain the status quo long enough to consider full briefing.

# Practical guidance for stay motions and evidentiary showings

Drafting and litigating a stay motion requires attention to both legal argument and the evidentiary record. The following guidance addresses strategy, typical evidentiary requirements, timing, and drafting of orders.

Begin early and craft a tight record. Time is often short in stay practice. Assemble declarations, pertinent excerpts of the lower-court record, documentary evidence showing the imminence and irreparability of harm, and any statutory or regulatory materials needed for the merits argument. Because appellate judges will often decide stays on the existing record plus affidavits, present admissible evidence and avoid hearsay problems by using declarations made under penalty of perjury when necessary.

Emphasize the two primary factors. Because likelihood of success and irreparable injury are pivotal, allocate the brief’s strongest material to these prongs. For likelihood of success, identify controlling precedent that supports reversal, legal errors in the lower court, and why the law, properly applied, favors reversal. For irreparable harm, provide concrete, specific evidence of harm that cannot be remedied by money or later relief. Examples include imminent removal from the country, loss of a unique constitutional right, irreversible alteration of property, or irretrievable environmental damage.

Leverage the sliding-scale argument carefully. If a movant cannot show a high probability of success, present “serious questions” with citation to precedent and explain why the balance of hardships and public interest weigh strongly in favor of a stay. But courts scrutinize “serious questions” claims closely; the argument is more likely to persuade when the movant marshals legal authority showing disagreement among circuits or unsettled legal issues.

Address the adversary’s harms and the public interest. Anticipate the opposing party’s arguments about prejudice and public interest. Present evidence showing how a stay will avoid irreparable injury without unduly prejudicing others. When the public interest is implicated (for example, health, safety, immigration, or national security), explain how preserving review aligns with public values, or, if public-interest concerns cut against a stay, propose narrow, time-limited relief that mitigates public harms.

Be precise about scope and duration. A narrowly tailored stay is easier for a court to accept. Specify geographic limits, particular programs or transactions to be paused, and a clear temporal limit (for example, until the appellate court decides the appeal or for a defined period). Offer alternative formulations (e.g., temporary administrative pause followed by expedited briefing) to give the court flexibility.

Propose appropriate security or conditions. If the stay concerns a monetary judgment or an action that could cause quantifiable loss, propose a bond or escrow arrangement. Where a bond is infeasible, propose alternative protections (e.g., an undertaking or periodic reporting). Courts consider such conditions when evaluating the balance-of-harms prong.

Anticipate statutory constraints and address them directly. If the lower-court order derives from a statutory regime that restricts injunctions or requires certain procedures to enjoin enforcement, explain why a stay is legally permissible and how it differs from an injunction. Provide authorities addressing the distinction between stays and injunctions and demonstrate that the relief sought is an ordinary stay of appellate proceedings, not an in-personam order precluded by statute.

Use focused declarations/affidavits. Stay motions are affidavits-intensive. Declarations should be concise, factual, and tied directly to the irreparable-harm and prejudice inquiries. Avoid legal argument in declarations; focus on facts that the judge will need to evaluate imminence, magnitude, and feasibility of alternatives.

Prepare for oral argument and emergency calendars. Be ready to explain, in a few minutes, why the stay is necessary and why the relief sought is the least-burdensome means to preserve appellate review. Have a concrete timeline: when will the opening brief be filed, what expedited scheduling is proposed, and how quickly can the record be prepared?

Draft an enforceable and clear proposed order. Provide the court with a proposed order that is narrowly drawn, sets precise terms, includes any conditions or bonds, and identifies the duration and scope. Precision helps courts avoid open-ended stays and limits the risk of friction or appeal from the stay itself.

When to seek relief in the court of appeals versus the district court. Stays pending appeal are typically sought in the court that will review the underlying judgment. For emergency interim relief (such as a TRO) a district court may be the appropriate forum. Understand the chain of possible filings: a movant may seek emergency relief from the district court and, if denied, proceed to the court of appeals or directly to the appellate court for an emergency administrative stay.

Consider alternatives to a stay. Sometimes alternative or partial relief (e.g., a narrow temporary pause, escrow arrangements, partial enforcement) will achieve the movant’s aims while minimizing hardship to others. Courts are receptive to tailored relief that preserves appellate review but avoids broad disruption.

# Conclusion

Stays pending appeal are extraordinary remedies, governed by a flexible four-factor framework that emphasizes likelihood of success and irreparable harm. Although the stay standard resembles the preliminary-injunction test, differences in purpose, posture, and statutory context cause courts to apply the factors in context-sensitive ways. Practitioners should present a focused factual record, prioritize the most persuasive legal arguments on the merits, tailor the scope and duration of requested relief, and propose appropriate protective conditions. Understanding how courts weigh the competing interests—finality and prompt enforcement against the need to prevent irrevocable injury—will allow counsel to craft persuasive stay motions and to predict how courts are likely to resolve them.
