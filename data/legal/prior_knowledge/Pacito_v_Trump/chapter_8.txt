Introduction

The Administrative Procedure Act (APA) provides the principal framework for judicial review of federal administrative action. For a law student preparing to read judicial opinions that review agency action, it is essential to understand (1) when an APA suit may be brought and against whom, (2) what constitutes "final agency action" that courts can review, (3) who may sue (the zone-of-interests inquiry), (4) the limits on review where decisions are committed to agency discretion, (5) the substantive standards courts apply in reviewing agency decisionmaking—most notably the arbitrary-and-capricious standard and the requirement of reasoned decisionmaking—and (6) the remedies courts may order when they find unlawful agency action. This chapter walks through each of those topics, explaining the legal rules, the typical tests and elements, allocation of burdens, and how procedural posture influences the court’s analysis and relief.

# APA § 702 cause of action and waiver of sovereign immunity

The APA itself is a complex statutory scheme, and one of the most consequential provisions for private litigants is 5 U.S.C. § 702. Section 702 accomplishes two related but distinct functions: it furnishes a waiver of sovereign immunity in certain suits against the United States, and it clarifies that individuals aggrieved by agency action are entitled to judicial review under the APA’s provisions subject to the APA’s terms.

Substantively, § 702 provides that a person who is "adversely affected or aggrieved" by agency action has a right to judicial review. Practically, however, § 702 does not by itself create a cause of action or expand the substantive scope of relief beyond what other APA provisions supply. Rather, courts treat § 702 as the vehicle by which sovereign immunity is waived so that a plaintiff may seek non-monetary relief (such as declaratory relief, injunctive relief, or vacatur) against an agency or an agency officer acting in an official capacity. The APA is therefore the usual statutory source for a plaintiff seeking to challenge agency action and obtain equitable relief.

At the same time, there are important limits. § 702’s waiver is not unconditional. First, Congress may expressly preclude judicial review in the statute that delegates decisionmaking authority to the agency; if Congress does so, § 702’s waiver does not override that congressional command. Second, § 702 does not supply a cause of action for claims that must be pursued under other statutory mechanisms or for which Congress has specified different remedies (including, in some circumstances, claims that seek money damages). In practice, this means that plaintiffs routinely plead APA claims seeking relief that is traditionally equitable—vacatur, declaratory judgments, and injunctions—rather than suits for damages against the United States.

When a plaintiff invokes § 702, courts proceed to determine whether the complaint states a cognizable APA claim, whether the action is reviewable under the APA (for example, whether it is "final"), and whether any other statutory bars to review apply (for example, doctrines that limit review for decisions "committed to agency discretion by law").

# Final agency action (Bennett v. Spear framework)

Federal courts rely on the APA to review only "final agency action." The foundational analytical framework used by courts asks whether the challenged act meets two related criteria: (1) the action marks the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process, and (2) the action is one by which rights or obligations have been determined, or from which legal consequences flow.

The first prong asks whether the agency has completed its decisionmaking on the question at issue. The focus here is on form and effect: courts look for evidence that the agency has arrived at a definitive position on the matter, not that the action is merely interlocutory, tentative, or preparatory. If the agency has expressly indicated that further steps, deliberation, or approvals are expected before the decision will be final, then the action is unlikely to satisfy this prong. Conversely, an agency document that communicates a conclusive determination that the agency intends to rely upon going forward is far more likely to be deemed final.

The second prong asks whether the action produces legal consequences or alters the parties’ legal rights and obligations. This prong is not limited to formal adjudicatory orders or regulations: agencies may issue policy statements, guidance documents, licensing decisions, or other forms of action that produce legally binding effects or practical consequences sufficiently immediate and concrete to satisfy the test. When an agency action affects private parties’ legal rights, imposes new duties, modifies eligibility for benefits, or is likely to subject individuals or entities to enforcement action, that indicates legal consequences sufficient for review. By contrast, expressions of intent, general statements of policy, or advisory opinions that do not bind anyone typically fail the second prong.

Burdens and procedural posture affect application of these prongs. The plaintiff bears the threshold burden of demonstrating that the action is final and reviewable; procedural devices such as motions to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction sometimes hinge on this showing. On the other hand, agency efforts to argue nonfinality may be persuasive when the record shows ongoing internal deliberations, planned follow-up action, or a statutory process that contemplates several discrete decision points.

Courts treat the finality inquiry pragmatically. They assess the document’s content, its context in the agency’s process, and its immediate effects. The “final agency action” question often resolves whether a suit is justiciable at all; a conclusion that the action is not final is typically dispositive and results in dismissal for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction, unless the plaintiff can show that the decision is reviewable on some other ground or becomes final at a later stage.

# Zone-of-interests test

Even when an agency action is final and a plaintiff invokes the APA’s waiver of sovereign immunity, the plaintiff must satisfy the zone-of-interests test, a judicially administered limitation on who may bring suit under a statute. The zone-of-interests inquiry asks whether the plaintiff’s asserted interests fall within the "zone" that a statute—either the statute the agency is implementing or the general interests protected by the APA—was designed to protect. It is not a separate standing doctrine based on Article III, but rather an aspect of statutory reviewability that limits which parties Congress intended to allow to sue.

The test is intentionally modest: plaintiffs need not demonstrate that they are the primary beneficiaries of a statute, nor must their injuries be precisely those the statute targeted. Courts apply a relatively forgiving standard, asking in essence whether the plaintiff’s interests are arguably within the purposes of the relevant statutory provision. If the plaintiff asserts harms unlike any that the statute was meant to redress, the claim will be dismissed on zone-of-interests grounds.

Determining the relevant statute is important. In some challenges plaintiffs point to the statute that authorizes the agency’s action (for example, a statute that authorizes a regulatory program), while in others plaintiffs rely on a more general statutory provision that governs agency behavior or the APA itself. Courts examine congressional intent and statutory text to define the protected zone. The analysis is largely textual and purposive: the court asks whether Congress, by enacting the statute, sought to protect the complainant’s type of interest from the agency behavior at issue.

The allocation of burdens is straightforward: the defendant agency or government argues that the plaintiff’s interests fall outside the statutory zone, and the plaintiff seeks to show that the statutory purpose encompasses the asserted interest. If the court concludes the plaintiff’s interest does not fall within the zone, it dismisses the claim for lack of statutory reviewability. The zone-of-interests inquiry often operates as an early dispositive test, resolved on threshold motions to dismiss or for summary judgment.

# Reviewability and § 701(a)(2) (committed to agency discretion)

Not all agency actions are subject to judicial review. Section 701(a) of the APA establishes two principal categories of nonreviewability. Section 701(a)(1) excepts matters committed to agency discretion by law (for example, matters where a statute precludes judicial review), and § 701(a)(2) provides that the APA’s judicial-review provisions do not apply to actions "committed to agency discretion by law." Courts have interpreted § 701(a)(2) to be a narrow exception: it applies only in those rare situations where the statute provides no law at all for the court to apply—where there is so little statutory guidance that a court would have no meaningful standard to judge the agency’s exercise of discretion.

The doctrinal test for "committed to agency discretion" examines whether Congress has provided an intelligible principle or law that can guide judicial review. If the enabling statute supplies standards, criteria, or procedural constraints that can be judicially applied, then the action is not committed to agency discretion and remains reviewable. Conversely, if the statute speaks in the most generalized terms and leaves core choices entirely to the agency’s unreviewable prerogative, then § 701(a)(2) may bar judicial scrutiny.

Because § 701(a)(2) is construed narrowly, courts often find that meaningful judicial review is possible even where statutes confer broad discretion. The presence of general language does not automatically deprive courts of review; instead, the court asks whether any manageable standard exists. If so, the court will evaluate the agency action against that standard under the appropriate APA review framework.

The practical consequence of § 701(a)(2) is to limit review in specific contexts—often those involving foreign policy, national security, or highly discretionary enforcement priorities—where judicial oversight could intrude on executive or legislative prerogatives. Parties raising § 701(a)(2) defenses typically do so in jurisdictional motions early in the case; once the court decides review is available, it proceeds to the substantive APA standards.

# Arbitrary-and-capricious review and reasoned decisionmaking

When a court determines that an agency action is final and reviewable, the primary substantive standard of review for many agency actions is whether the action is arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law. Section 706 of the APA directs courts to set aside agency action they find unlawful; subsection (2)(A) employs the "arbitrary and capricious" standard. Interpreting and applying that standard involves assessing whether an agency has engaged in reasoned decisionmaking.

At its core, arbitrary-and-capricious review asks whether the agency examined the relevant data, articulated a satisfactory explanation for its action, and considered the important aspects of the issue before it. Courts focus on the adequacy of the agency’s decisionmaking process and its stated basis for the decision rather than substituting the court’s own policy judgment for that of the agency. The inquiry is deferential, but it is not toothless.

Key elements of the arbitrary-and-capricious analysis include the following:

- Factual basis: The agency must have relied on a body of evidence or reasoned analysis that reasonably supports its conclusions. A decision based on erroneous facts, or on ignoring substantial evidence in the record, may be set aside.

- Consideration of relevant factors: The agency must identify and address the relevant statutory factors and policy considerations. Failure to account for an important aspect of the problem—such as neglecting a material risk, effect, or statutory criterion—can render the decision arbitrary.

- Explanation of reasoning: The agency must provide a reasoned explanation that connects the facts before it to the outcome it adopts. This requires more than conclusory statements; the agency should show the path of reasoning by which it reached the decision.

- Reliance on permissible factors: The agency may not base its decision on factors that Congress did not intend it to consider. Courts ensure that the agency’s chosen rationale is within the scope of authority conferred by the statute.

- Response to significant reliance interests and departures from precedent: When an agency changes a longstanding policy, it must acknowledge the change, explain the reasons for it, and address reliance interests that may have developed. A failure to grapple with such issues is a common ground for reversal.

The arbitrariness inquiry accommodates agency expertise: courts often defer to reasonable agency inferences or predictions, particularly in technical or scientific domains, provided those inferences are explained and supported. Conversely, courts will scrutinize an agency’s risk assessments and cost-benefit analyses when they appear to be based on faulty assumptions, implausible extrapolations, or unsupported reasoning.

Two procedural dimensions interplay with arbitrary-and-capricious review. First, the administrative record—the contemporaneous materials that the agency relied upon when it made its decision—serves as the principal source of review. Courts typically confine review to the administrative record and will not admit extra-record evidence unless narrow exceptions apply (for example, when the record is incomplete due to bad faith, or when the issue is whether the agency considered certain information). Second, the administrative procedures followed by the agency matter: in some contexts, agencies must provide notice-and-comment or other procedures, and courts look to whether the agency complied with those requirements and adequately considered public comments.

Where a court finds the agency’s action arbitrary and capricious, the remedy can be setting aside the action in whole or in part (vacatur), and remanding to the agency for further proceedings consistent with the court’s opinion. The court’s choice among remedies turns on the severity of the procedural or substantive defect and considerations of equity and administrative practicality.

# Remedies under the APA (vacatur, remand, injunctions)

When a court determines that an agency action violates the APA, the statute and equitable principles inform the remedy. Section 706 provides that a reviewing court shall "hold unlawful and set aside" agency action found to be unlawful, which authorizes vacatur. But courts exercise discretion in shaping relief, balancing the need to vindicate statutory requirements and protect the public interest against the disruptive effects of invalidating agency action.

Vacatur and remand are the two principal remedies. Vacatur nullifies the challenged agency action; remand sends the matter back to the agency for further proceedings consistent with the court’s opinion. Courts have several remedial options, including vacating the action and remanding with instructions, remanding without vacatur (leaving the agency action temporarily in place while the agency remedies the defect), or—in limited circumstances—declining to remand when a court finds that remand would serve no useful purpose.

The standard factors that guide remedial decisions include:

- The seriousness of the agency’s legal error: If the agency committed a fundamental legal error that undermines the entire decision, courts are more likely to vacate. If the error is narrow or peripheral, a court may choose remand without vacatur so that agency action remains effective during correction.

- Disruptive consequences: Courts weigh the practical effects of vacatur on regulated parties, the public, and governmental operations. If vacatur would create chaos, substantial hardship, or regulatory gaps that significantly damage the public interest, courts may tailor relief (for example, by issuing a stay or remanding without vacating) to avoid such consequences.

- Likelihood of future reconsideration: If the court believes the agency will be able to cure the defect upon remand, judicially imposed remedies might be more limited; but if the court concludes that the agency is unlikely to remedy the deficiency, vacatur may be appropriate to protect regulated parties.

Injunctions—temporary, preliminary, or permanent—are another major form of relief in APA suits. When plaintiffs seek injunctive relief, courts apply traditional equitable principles. For preliminary injunctions, courts typically require plaintiffs to show a likelihood of success on the merits (or at least serious questions), that they will suffer irreparable harm absent relief, that the balance of equities favors relief, and that an injunction is in the public interest. For permanent injunctions following a merits decision in the plaintiff’s favor, the court must find that the plaintiff prevailed on the merits and that equitable relief is warranted based on the same balance-of-harms/public-interest considerations.

In the APA context, courts sometimes modify the usual four-factor preliminary injunction test to incorporate the statutory mandate to "hold unlawful and set aside" agency actions that are unlawful, but equitable considerations remain central. When considering a preliminary injunction against an agency rule, for example, courts especially weigh the degree of potential harm to the public from halting the rule and the harm to the plaintiff from allowing an unlawful rule to stand.

A related remedial tool is an injunction with limited scope or temporal terms—for example, enjoining only a particular enforcement mechanism, staying implementation until a date certain, or enjoining application of a rule to certain parties while leaving it in place for others. Courts craft such tailored remedies where appropriate to balance legal correctness with administrative and practical realities.

Finally, courts have limited equitable authority to award declaratory judgments under the APA; such relief frequently accompanies injunctive relief and clarifies the parties’ rights and obligations. Courts, however, may decline declaratory relief where it would be advisory or otherwise inappropriate.

Procedural posture affects remedy choices. A court resolving a facial challenge on the merits may provide a broader remedy than a court deciding only threshold jurisdictional issues. Similarly, if a plaintiff obtains summary judgment based on an administrative-record review, the court’s remedial choices will reflect findings about the record and the narrowness or breadth of the agency’s failure.

Conclusion

The APA’s framework for judicial review requires courts to resolve sequential questions before reaching a merits determination: whether Congress has waived sovereign immunity and authorized review (§ 702 and related provisions), whether the action is final, whether the plaintiff’s interests fall within the statutory zone of interests, whether any statutory bar to review applies (including § 701(a)(2) commitments), and then whether the agency’s action survives the applicable substantive standard—most commonly arbitrary-and-capricious review under § 706. Each stage involves doctrinal tests, allocation of burdens, and practical considerations that shape litigation strategy and judicial disposition. When courts find agency action unlawful, they exercise equitable discretion to fashion remedies—vacatur, remand, injunctive relief—tailored to the nature of the legal error and the practical consequences of relief. A firm grasp of these foundational concepts prepares the reader to understand and critically analyze judicial opinions that apply the APA to concrete disputes.
