Introduction

A recurring problem in statutory interpretation is determining whether a provision authorizes discretionary action, imposes a mandatory duty, or does both in different circumstances. This question is especially acute in spending and program statutes where funding, eligibility, and agency responsibilities hinge on whether Congress has said that an agency or another actor "may" take an action, "shall" take an action, is "authorized" to act, or is "required" to act "as needed" or under other qualifiers. The distinction matters for private rights, sovereign immunity, enforcement, timing, and judicial review. This chapter provides doctrinal tools to parse authorization versus mandate language, showing how courts approach textual canons, titles and headings, contextual reading of multi-part statutory schemes, surplusage and harmonization concerns, the interpretation of qualifiers like "as needed," and the contemporary administrative law landscape for reviewing agency interpretations.

# Textual canons for "shall" vs. "may/authorized"

The words "shall," "may," and "authorized" are the starting points of statutory interpretation about duties and permissions, but canons concerning them are not absolute. Courts begin with the ordinary meaning of these words but balance that against statutory context and purpose.

"Shall." Historically, "shall" is treated as imposing a mandatory duty. In many judicial and interpretive contexts, "shall" signals command: a party "shall" do X means it must do X. This canon is strongest when the duty is directed to an identified actor, the language is unqualified, and compliance can be enforced by a private right or agency oversight. Several interpretive rules flow from reading "shall" as mandatory: obligations are owed to someone, time deadlines are binding unless qualified, and failure to act may have legal consequences (e.g., forfeiture of rights, potential liability, or exposure to judicial remedy).

However, courts recognize exceptions. "Shall" can carry permissive or directory meanings when context indicates Congress intended to grant discretion. If "shall" appears in a provision where practical impossibility, impossibility under other legal constraints, or a clear statutory structure indicates flexibility, courts may construe "shall" as directory rather than mandatory. For example, a "shall" inserted into a provision that delineates conditions for discretionary grant-making may be best read as describing a baseline standard rather than a command for unconditional action.

"May" and "authorized." The verb "may" and the noun "authorization" are generally permissive: they suggest discretion. A statute saying an agency "may" do X ordinarily means the agency has power to do X but is not required to. "Is authorized to" likewise connotes permission or enabling authority. These permissive words support judicial deference to agency policy choices and non-action, subject to limits imposed elsewhere in the statute or by constitutional constraints.

Still, "may" is not invariably discretionary. The statutory context can convert "may" into a quasi-mandate when other parts of the statute create an expectation of action, prescribe objective criteria that, when satisfied, leave no room for discretion, or attach consequences to non-performance. Courts sometimes treat "may" as mandatory when the statute creates a right in a private party (the "right-creating statute" canon): if Congress uses "may" but structures the provision to benefit identifiable private parties, a court might imply a duty to allow the right holder to obtain relief, and a denial becomes reviewable.

Interaction between words. Frequently, statutes use both words across different provisions. When Congress uses "shall" in one subsection and "may" in another, courts infer Congress intended different levels of compulsion. But when a statute alternates terms without clear structural cues, courts resort to contextual and purposive analysis to discern whether permissions were intended to be broad or limited. The interpretive task is to identify textually whether Congress contemplated mandatory conduct for the public interest or merely permissive authority to implement a policy.

Practical rules and burdens. When construing these words, courts often apply default presumptions—"shall" mandatory, "may" permissive—but the party asserting a mandatory reading typically bears the burden of showing that context overrides the default. This is especially the case where an agency claims that "may" denotes discretionary power; courts will look for statutory indicators of congressional intent to restrict discretion before imposing a command.

# Weight of statutory titles and headings

Statutory titles and headings often provide helpful interpretive context, particularly for ambiguous text. Titles, subchapter headings, and section captions can reflect the structure and purpose of an enactment and sometimes clarify the intended nature of a provision—whether it creates a duty, a power, or a condition.

Canonical force. Courts treat headings as part of the statute's text but generally accord them less weight than operative provisions. A familiar approach is that headings are useful interpretive aids but cannot override clear statutory language. When text is ambiguous, a heading that frames a provision as "Authorization," "Duties," "Requirements," or "Powers" can tip the interpretation toward authorization or mandate. For example, a section within a chapter titled "Duties of the Secretary" will be read more likely to impose obligations than a similar text placed in a chapter titled "Authority to Provide Grants."

Limitations. Headings are statutory navigation tools, sometimes inserted by codifiers or editors rather than the enacting legislature. Because of this, courts are cautious about relying on them to create substantive obligations. If the operative text is unambiguous, heading cannot change the clear meaning. Moreover, headings that conflict with text are subordinated to the statutory language, and if use of headings would produce absurd results or contradict the overall statutory scheme, courts decline to use them as dispositive.

Contextual corroboration. The most persuasive role for headings arises when they corroborate other contextual clues: structure, cross-references, purpose statements, and the legislative history. When multiple elements point in the same direction—textual ambiguity, a heading that uses directive language, and structural placement in a "Duties" subchapter—the combined weight may persuade a court that Congress intended a mandatory reading.

Procedural posture effects. In litigation, reliance on headings is more useful in preliminary stages where the court evaluates plausibility or on summary judgment when textual ambiguities must be resolved as a matter of law. In close cases with contested factual development, the court may be reluctant to resolve statutory meaning with reference to headings alone and may permit further fact-finding or defer to agency interpretation.

# Contextual reading within multi-part statutory schemes

Individual provisions rarely stand alone. A correct interpretation of whether a clause authorizes or mandates often depends on how it fits within the statutory architecture. Multi-part schemes—those that combine authorizing language, conditions, funding provisions, enforcement mechanisms, and eligibility criteria—require reading provisions in relation to each other to ensure coherent operation.

Look at the statutory whole. Courts apply the canon that statutes must be read as a whole. A permissive phrase in one subsection may be qualified by mandatory language in another subsection. For example, a grant program providing that an agency "may award grants" could be constrained by another provision that prescribes mandatory eligibility criteria or directs proportional distribution across recipients. Conversely, a mandatory duty in one provision may be limited by a later subsection that conditions action upon available appropriations—thus turning a nominal command into a contingent mandate.

Categorical and functional divisions. Multi-part statutes often divide functions across actors and stages—eligibility determinations, application processing, certification, funding disbursement, and oversight. Determining whether a provision creates a duty requires identifying the actor to whom the clause speaks and at what stage in the process. A clause that "shall" require applicants to submit documentation imposes a duty on applicants, not the agency. Conversely, if the statute says the agency "shall grant" aid to entities that meet criteria, then the agency's duty is implicated—subject to any procedural or funding conditions elsewhere.

Cross-references and definitions. Definitions and cross-references elsewhere in the statute can change the meaning of authorization language. A defined term that limits scope (e.g., "eligible entity") or a cross-reference that makes compliance dependent on another requirement can alter a provision's mandatory quality. Courts therefore trace the web of statutory cross-references and see whether contemporaneous provisions create prerequisites or contingencies that qualify the verbal command.

Temporal sequencing. Multi-part statutes often establish processes that include discretion at some stages and duties at others. A statutory scheme may give an agency discretion to issue regulations (an authorization) but require certain actions—such as reporting, certification, or rulemaking within specified times—once a triggering event occurs. Reading temporally helps clarify whether a phrase is an initial authorization or an ongoing obligation.

Harmonizing local meanings. When similar words are used across the statutory parts, courts presume consistency unless context signals otherwise. If Congress uses "may" in several provisions to indicate discretion, it probably intended similar liberties elsewhere. But repetition does not render the identical legal effect; variation in recipient, temporal scope, or linkage to enforcement will still bear on whether the provision is authorizing or mandatory.

# Surplusage and harmonization canons

Interpretive canons regarding surplusage and harmonization are particularly salient when deciding whether language imposes obligations. Courts aim to give every clause effect; they avoid readings that render words superfluous. Simultaneously, they seek to harmonize provisions to create a coherent statutory scheme.

Surplusage canon. The surplusage canon posits that Congress is presumed not to have used extraneous words. If construing a provision as permissive would make a neighboring mandatory provision redundant, courts may favor an interpretation that gives distinct work to each provision. For example, if a statute provides both that an agency "is authorized to" and "shall" take actions, reading both as the same in effect would make one phrase superfluous. Therefore, courts often interpret the two as creating different legal consequences: "is authorized to" as conferring discretion, and "shall" as creating a binding duty or different procedural obligation.

Harmonization. Harmonization requires reconciling different provisions to avoid conflict and to make the statute operative in a sensible manner. When one clause appears to impose a duty and another to grant discretion, courts try to read them together so both provisions are effectual. For instance, a general authorization to award grants ("may award") can be harmonized with a specific duty to fund certain classes ("shall provide funds for X") by reading the general provision as providing a framework for discretionary grants outside the specific mandate; conversely, the specific provision imposes a priority.

Principled exceptions. Sometimes the surplusage canon yields to clear textual indicators or evident congressional purposes. If Congress deliberately used redundant language for emphasis or to adopt different legal meanings across contexts (for instance, creating both a funding authorization and a programmatic duty), courts will not eliminate redundancy. Also, where statutory structure or constitutional requirements necessitate overlapping language, the surplusage canon is not dispositive.

Practical approach to conflicts. In practice, courts perform a two-step analysis. First, they identify whether an interpretation would jettison any clause's operative effect. If so, they consider alternative readings that give distinct meaning to each clause. Second, they evaluate whether such an interpretation fits the statute's purpose and structure. If neither reading is persuasive, courts may rely on other tools—such as legislative history, canons of construction, or precedent—to resolve the ambiguity.

# "As needed" and similar qualifiers

Qualifiers such as "as needed," "as appropriate," "as necessary to carry out," and "to the extent practicable" introduce degrees of contingent obligation. Interpreting these qualifiers requires attention to temporal scope, standards for discretion, and interactions with statutory purpose and appropriations.

Nature of qualifiers. These phrases typically indicate that an actor has discretion to act when certain conditions obtain. "As needed" suggests action is permissible when the actor deems it necessary to achieve statutory ends. Nevertheless, the qualifier by itself does not wholly remove reviewability. Courts may construe "as needed" to impose a reasonableness constraint: agencies must show that they considered the necessity question and reached a rational conclusion.

Standards for review. When statutory text grants action "as needed" combined with an overall statutory duty, courts may review agency decisions under an arbitrary-and-capricious standard, asking whether the agency adequately explained why action was or was not necessary. If the qualifier attaches to an individual right—e.g., benefits provided "as needed" to eligible recipients—courts will examine whether administrative decisions denied a person-claimant a legally cognizable benefit and whether there were objective criteria to govern the agency's discretion.

Temporal and funding contingencies. Qualifiers often signal contingent duties related to funding. "As needed" in a spending provision can indicate priority rather than an absolute obligation: the agency shall fund programs as needed, subject to available appropriations. Courts interpret these phrases in light of the appropriation context; if an independent appropriation establishes mandatory funding levels, "as needed" may become less discretionary. Conversely, absence of appropriation or specific funding instructions renders the qualifier a limiting condition.

Objective versus subjective criteria. A key interpretive distinction is whether the qualifier invites subjective judgment or imposes an objective test. "As needed in the judgment of the Secretary" evinces a subjective standard with broad deference, whereas "as needed to meet population X's demonstrated needs" creates an objective standard susceptible to empirical assessment. Courts scrutinize which standard Congress chose, and where objective standards exist, agencies bear a heavier burden to justify their determinations.

Remedies and enforcement. When a statute conditions action "as needed," the availability of remedies for non-action is often limited. Plaintiffs must show that the "need" threshold was satisfied and that the agency's refusal was unreasonable. Where Congress included a private right of action or other enforcement mechanism tied to the qualifier, courts will interpret "as needed" against the backdrop of remedial purpose and the text of the enforcement provision.

# Post-Chevron landscape: de novo review with Skidmore weight; major questions doctrine

Administrative deference doctrine affects how courts treat statutory ambiguities about authorization versus mandate. Historically, the Chevron framework required courts to defer to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes within the agency's expertise. Recent doctrinal developments have modified the degree of deference and the analytical framework, producing a more nuanced field—one in which courts may decide statutory questions de novo, give varying weight to agency interpretations, and apply heightened scrutiny in cases implicating major questions of political or economic significance.

De novo review and Skidmore weight. Under the modern approach followed in many courts, when a statute's text is ambiguous, courts conduct an independent textual analysis (de novo). Instead of automatic deference, courts may give an agency's interpretation respect according to its persuasiveness, consistency, and thoroughness—principles associated with Skidmore deference. Skidmore weight means an agency interpretation may be persuasive because of the agency's expertise, coherence of statutory construction, and demonstrated consistency over time, but it is not binding. Courts weigh the agency's interpretation alongside textual analysis, legislative purpose, and other interpretive tools.

Factors for persuasiveness. When employing Skidmore-style weight, courts consider several factors: the thoroughness of the agency's consideration, the validity of its reasoning, the consistency of the interpretation over time, the formality of the procedure used to adopt it (e.g., rulemaking vs. guidance), and the fit between the agency's expertise and the interpretive question. If an agency adopts a reasonable, well-explained interpretation after notice-and-comment rulemaking, courts are likelier to find it persuasive. Conversely, an ad hoc enforcement position articulated in litigation submissions carries less weight.

Major questions doctrine. A parallel and increasingly influential limit on deference is the major questions doctrine. Under this principle, courts are reluctant to allow agencies to decide matters of vast political or economic significance based on ambiguous statutory grants. When an agency claims broad authority on a question of major national importance—particularly where substantial policy choices or novel regulatory domains are at stake—courts require clear congressional authorization to sustain the agency's position.

Application and tests. In practice, the major questions doctrine functions as a heightened clear-statement rule: agencies must point to unmistakable statutory text or compelling evidence of congressional intent to regulate a major question. Courts look for explicit statutory language, detailed regulatory frameworks, or other indicators that Congress anticipated and authorized agency action on the issue. Absent such clear authorization, courts will construe the statute not to vest that power in the agency.

Interaction with authorization/mandate distinctions. The post-Chevron landscape shapes how courts treat "may," "shall," and qualifiers. For ordinary programmatic choices, courts may accept agency interpretations (granting Skidmore weight) that treat "may" as discretionary or "shall" as nonmandatory when reasonable. But for high-stakes questions—e.g., sweeping policy shifts, large-scale budgetary commitments, or regulatory expansions with major economic effects—courts will be less inclined to accept agency readings that attribute sweeping authority to ambiguous statutory text.

Procedural posture and implications. The degree of deference a court affords can vary with procedural posture. In declaratory-judgment or mandamus contexts, courts may be more willing to resolve statutory meaning de novo. In administrative law challenges to agency action under the Administrative Procedure Act, where agencies have issued formal rules, courts may give their interpretations more persuasive weight. On summary judgment, a court's evaluation of an agency's statutory interpretation as persuasive or unpersuasive can be dispositive. When plaintiffs seek preliminary relief, courts must evaluate the likely statutory meaning of "may" or "shall" to determine irreparable harm and likelihood of success—sometimes resolving close textual issues early in the case.

Practical advice for litigation. Parties should tailor their arguments to the post-Chevron landscape. Those defending agency discretion should develop a reasoned, consistent administrative record, pursue formal rulemaking where possible, and demonstrate statutory grounding and expertise. Those seeking to bind agencies should emphasize clear statutory text, legislative history, and statutory structure, or else frame their claims as involving major questions that require unmistakable congressional intention.

Conclusion

Interpreting whether a statutory provision authorizes action or mandates it requires a textured analysis: start with the plain meaning of "shall," "may," and "authorized," but do not stop there. Consider headings and titles as supportive context, read the provision within the multi-part statutory scheme, apply surplusage and harmonization canons to ensure each clause has work to do, and scrutinize qualifiers like "as needed" for their standards of review and temporal/funding contingencies. Finally, in the contemporary administrative law environment, anticipate that courts will often engage in de novo analysis while giving persuasive weight to reasoned agency interpretations and applying the major questions doctrine to resist agency claims to sweeping authority absent clear congressional authorization. Understanding these tools will prepare you to evaluate how a court may parse ambiguous authorization and mandate provisions before confronting a judicial opinion that applies them.
