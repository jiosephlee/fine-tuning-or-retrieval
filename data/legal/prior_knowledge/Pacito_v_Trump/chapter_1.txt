Introduction

Federal appellate jurisdiction ordinarily is confined to review of final decisions by the district courts. That general rule serves the dual objectives of minimizing piecemeal appeals and promoting efficient litigation. Nevertheless, federal law creates important exceptions allowing appellate courts to review certain interlocutory orders before final judgment. One of the most consequential of these exceptions involves interim orders about injunctions—orders that grant, deny, modify, or dissolve equitable relief. Understanding when a federal court of appeals may hear such interlocutory questions, the standards governing such review, how stays operate, what the appellate record must contain, and what alternative emergency remedies exist is essential background for reading and evaluating judicial opinions that address preliminary or other nonfinal injunctive relief.

This chapter explains the statutory and doctrinal framework that governs appellate review of injunction-related interlocutory orders. It begins with the specific statutory authorization found in 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1), then situates that provision within the broader “final judgment” doctrine and its exceptions. It describes the procedural mechanics and differing functions of motions panels and merits panels in the courts of appeals, explains the nature and effect of administrative stays, describes the requirements for the record on appeal in interlocutory appeals of injunctions, and concludes by contrasting emergency motions (including motions for stays pending appeal) with the extraordinary writ of mandamus.

# 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1) (appeals of injunctions)

28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1) is the primary statutory vehicle by which parties may obtain immediate appellate review of interlocutory orders concerning injunctions. The statute authorizes courts of appeals to hear appeals from interlocutory orders of district courts "granting, continuing, modifying, refusing or dissolving injunctions, or refusing to dissolve or modify injunctions." The scope of the statute is broad and intentionally so: it reaches orders affecting equitable relief in many forms, including preliminary and temporary restraining orders (to the extent they act as injunctions), preliminary injunctions, and certain other orders that alter the parties’ rights before final disposition.

Several points about the operation of § 1292(a)(1) are important for doctrinal analysis and practice.

- What kinds of orders qualify. The statute focuses on orders concerning injunctions, broadly defined. An order qualifies if it has the practical effect of ordering a party either to act, to refrain from acting, or otherwise to change conduct pending final adjudication. Whether a particular order is “an injunction” for purposes of § 1292(a)(1) is a legal question subject to appellate review; application of the legal standard often requires an analysis of the order’s substance and operative effect rather than its label.

- Timing and interlocutory posture. Section 1292(a)(1) applies to nonfinal orders, so parties may invoke it before final judgment. The statute therefore creates an exception to the final judgment rule, allowing immediate review of injunction-related disputes that otherwise would require an appeal only at the end of the case.

- Scope of appellate review. An appellate court’s jurisdiction under § 1292(a)(1) reaches the injunctive order itself and typically ancillary rulings that are inseparably intertwined with the injunction’s issuance or denial. However, the statute does not convert every interlocutory ruling into an appealable order; appellate courts retain the power to dismiss appeals when they lack jurisdiction or when the appeal does not actually concern an injunctive order within the statutory language.

- Practical consequences. Because injunctions can alter the status quo and carry urgent practical consequences, Congress gave litigants the ability to seek immediate appellate correction. The immediate review may be protective of public rights, prevent irreparable harm, or clarify the law where preliminary relief raises novel legal questions.

In short, § 1292(a)(1) is a statutory doorway to early appellate review of orders affecting equitable relief. Whether a litigant can use that doorway in a particular case depends on whether the challenged order operates as an injunction and complies with the statutory language.

# Final judgment rule and exceptions

The final judgment rule is the starting point for understanding appellate jurisdiction. Codified in 28 U.S.C. § 1291, the rule provides that, subject to statutory exceptions, appeals lie only from final decisions of the district courts. A final decision is one that ends the litigation on the merits and leaves nothing for the district court to do but execute the judgment. The rule promotes efficiency by avoiding fragmented review and encouraging comprehensive consideration of factual and legal disputes in a single appeal.

Because appellate review of injunctions frequently must occur before final judgment to prevent irreparable harm or preserve the subject matter, Congress and the courts have recognized several exceptions to the final judgment rule. Key exceptions relevant to injunctive relief include the following.

- Statutory interlocutory appeals. 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1), discussed above, permits immediate appellate review of injunction orders. Another statutory route, 28 U.S.C. § 1292(b), allows a district court to certify for interlocutory appeal a controlling question of law if the district judge believes that an immediate appeal may materially advance the litigation; the court of appeals may accept such an appeal in its discretion. Section 1292(b) appeals involve a distinct certification process and are discretionary for the appellate court.

- Collateral order doctrine. The Supreme Court developed a narrow, judge-made exception—the collateral order doctrine—that permits appeals of orders that, while not final, are effectively unreviewable on appeal from a final judgment and concern rights that would be irretrievably lost absent immediate review. The doctrine applies only to orders that (1) conclusively determine the disputed question, (2) resolve an important issue separate from the merits of the action, and (3) are effectively unreviewable later. The doctrine is applied sparingly and typically is not a routine route for injunctive controversies, but it remains a potential exception in particular circumstances.

- Mandamus and extraordinary writs. In circumstances where no adequate appellate remedy exists or where district court action falls far outside the scope of judicial authority, litigants may seek extraordinary relief in the court of appeals or the Supreme Court by filing a petition for mandamus or prohibition. These are not appeals but equitable recourses that have stringent requirements (discussed below). The availability of mandamus affects the final judgment calculus because it provides a possible avenue for immediate relief when appellate jurisdiction is otherwise lacking.

The interaction between the final judgment rule and these exceptions requires careful procedural analysis. A litigant who appeals an interlocutory injunctive order must ensure the order fits within an available statutory or doctrinal exception; otherwise the appellate court will dismiss for lack of jurisdiction. Conversely, appellate courts are attentive to the interests that underlie the final judgment rule and will construe exceptions narrowly to prevent undue proliferation of piecemeal review.

# Motions panels vs. merits panels

In the typical lifecycle of an interlocutory appeal concerning an injunction, the court of appeals employs two different decision-making bodies at different stages: the motions panel and the merits panel. These panels have distinct roles, procedures, and standards of review.

- Motions panels. A motions panel is a small group (often three judges) appointed to decide urgent or procedural applications arising before the court has resolved the appeal on the merits. Common duties include ruling on motions for stays pending appeal, motions to expedite briefing, motions for administrative stays, motions to expedite oral argument, and sometimes motions to dismiss appeals for jurisdictional defects. Motions panels operate under time pressure and typically employ abbreviated briefing and streamlined procedures; they may issue orders without full briefing and without oral argument. The decisions of a motions panel often are interlocutory in nature and, depending on the circuit’s local rules, may be referred to the merits panel for final disposition. Notably, the panel that decides a stay motion is not necessarily the panel that will decide the appeal on the merits.

Motions panels decide matters often by applying the standards applicable to the relief sought (for example, the standard for a stay pending appeal). Their determinations on procedural and equitable interim relief are reviewed deferentially by the merits panel, but if the merits panel disagrees, it may vacate or modify the motions-panel order in the course of resolving the appeal.

- Merits panels. The merits panel, which may be a three-judge panel or en banc court in some instances, adjudicates the appeal’s substantive issues after briefing and argument. For interlocutory appeals under § 1292(a)(1), the merits panel will conduct the statutory appellate review of the injunctive order, considering the district court’s legal conclusions de novo, factual findings for clear error, and discretionary determinations (such as whether to grant equitable relief) for abuse of discretion. The merits panel also has the authority to reconsider and vacate interlocutory administrative or temporary orders previously entered by a motions panel.

Procedurally, litigants should recognize that the motions panel’s orders are often temporary and subject to revision. For example, a motions panel may enter a stay pending appeal or an administrative stay the court can extend or dissolve later. The merits panel’s engagement allows fuller consideration on the basis of the complete appellate record. As a practical matter, litigants should prepare for both types of proceedings: expedited applications to the motions panel to preserve status quo, and full appellate briefing for the merits panel with attention to applicable standards of review.

# Administrative stays and their effect

Administrative stays are interim measures entered by a court of appeals (or sometimes by the clerk acting under delegated authority) to preserve the status quo while the parties seek a more formal stay or while the court deliberates on an application. Administrative stays are typically short-term and serve an emergency function: they temporarily suspend the district court’s order to prevent immediate irreparable consequences.

Key points about administrative stays include the following.

- Purpose and scope. The primary function of an administrative stay is maintenance of the status quo to forestall potentially irreparable consequences pending a more complete consideration of a stay application. An administrative stay is often narrow in duration and scope, and may simply preserve the relief ordered by the district court until the court of appeals can receive briefing and determine whether to grant a longer-term stay pending appeal.

- Who may issue. Administrative stays can be entered by a motions panel or, in some circuits, by a single judge acting pursuant to delegated authority (often the circuit’s chief judge or a duty judge). Clerks sometimes issue short administrative stays in response to emergency filings, subject to later review. The authority to issue such stays derives from the court’s need to manage its docket and to prevent irreparable harm during the interim.

- Interaction with the merits of a stay. The administrative stay does not decide the ultimate entitlement to relief on the merits. When the court later considers a formal stay application, it will apply the substantive stay standard (commonly a four-factor test discussed below). The administrative stay simply buys time and temporarily alters the parties’ obligations.

- Effect on injunctions. When an administrative stay is entered, it suspends the operative effect of the injunction (or the district court order) in whole or in part, depending on the terms. A stay of an injunction prevents enforcement of the injunctive command while the appeal is pending. Because stays can affect public rights and private conduct, courts typically craft administrative stays narrowly to address immediate risks while preserving the appellate process.

- Procedural consequences and limitations. An administrative stay may be contested and reviewed by a merits panel. Also, administrative stays are temporary and generally leave open the need for a full stay application that addresses the standard for relief, including likelihood of success on the merits (or serious legal questions), the potential for irreparable injury, the balance of equities, and the public interest. Parties seeking to rely on an administrative stay should immediately pursue the full stay application and prepare the appellate record for rapid evaluation.

Understanding administrative stays is crucial because they often are the first form of appellate relief in an emergency. They do not substitute for careful presentation of the legal and factual grounds required for a stay pending appeal, but they can be dispositive in preserving or altering the effect of injunctive relief in the short term.

# Record on appeal in interlocutory review

The appellate court’s ability to evaluate an interlocutory injunction depends heavily on the completeness and clarity of the record transmitted from the district court. The record on appeal in interlocutory review encompasses the trial court docket entries, pleadings, evidentiary submissions (affidavits, declarations, exhibits), transcripts of hearings and evidentiary proceedings, and the district court’s opinions and orders. Because interlocutory appeals often proceed on an expedited timetable, careful attention to the record is essential.

Several principles govern the record on appeal.

- Completeness and preservation. Parties seeking interlocutory review should ensure that the district court’s record contains all materials necessary to resolve the legal issues and to allow the appellate court to assess factual findings. That includes filing complete trial exhibits, affidavits and declarations supporting emergency motions, and ensuring that the district judge makes explicit findings of fact and legal conclusions when issuing or denying an injunction. Omissions or inadequate factual findings may hamper appellate review and can result in remand for additional factfinding.

- Transcripts and proffers. In many interlocutory contexts, hearings are conducted ex parte or on expedited notice; however, appellate courts require transcripts or adequate proffers of oral proceedings if factual determinations were made based on testimony or argument. When a transcript is unavailable in the time required, parties often submit a settled statement or agreed summary to enable appellate consideration. Courts of appeals will sometimes proceed on a partial record, but doing so increases the risk that the appellate court will remand for supplementation.

- Standards of review and record implications. The standard of review affects which parts of the record are most critical. Legal conclusions receive de novo review and therefore hinge on the district court’s reasoning as reflected in its written orders and findings. Factual findings that underlie equitable determinations are reviewed for clear error; accordingly, a robust factual record—documentary evidence, witness statements, and a transcript of any evidentiary hearing—reduces the chance that the appellate court will find a clear error and remand. Discretionary determinations, such as deciding whether equitable relief is warranted, are reviewed for abuse of discretion, but the abuse-of-discretion inquiry still depends on whether the district court correctly applied legal principles and whether its factual base was adequate.

- Scope of review. Appellate review of an interlocutory injunction proceeds on the record as made before the district court. The appeals process is not a venue for receiving new evidence; appellants must generally show they preserved errors below and that the record supports the legal claims. If critical factual matters were not developed in the district court, the appellate court may be constrained in its review or remand for further proceedings.

- Supplemental materials. Where appropriate, parties can and should seek to supplement the record with relevant materials created after the injunction, if the appellate rules and court allow such supplementation and if the materials bear on whether injunctive relief should continue. But supplementation is discretionary and often limited; litigants must know their circuit’s practices and seek leave when necessary.

In summary, the appellate record in an interlocutory appeal must be thoughtfully constructed and preserved. Because appellate review of injunctions often occurs under compressed timeframes, diligent preservation of evidence, clear district court findings, and timely preparation of transcripts and exhibits are indispensable.

# Mandamus and emergency motions (contrasted)

When immediate relief is required but direct appeal is unavailable, or when district-court action exceeds lawful bounds or denies fundamental rights, litigants may pursue two distinct types of extraordinary relief in the courts of appeals: emergency motions (for stays, temporary relief, or injunctions pending appeal) and petitions for mandamus. These remedies differ in nature, standards, and practical use.

- Emergency motions. Emergency motions are procedural devices asking the appellate court to take immediate interim action. The most common emergency motions in the injunctive context are motions for a stay pending appeal and motions for an administrative stay. The relief sought is provisional and designed to preserve the status quo while the appellate process proceeds. The governing standard for a stay pending appeal is equitable: courts balance several factors commonly stated as (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits (or, in some circuits, whether the applicant has raised serious legal questions), (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay, (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure other parties, and (4) where the public interest lies. Some courts frame these factors slightly differently (for example, requiring a showing of probable success or a substantial case on the merits), but the general inquiry balances the merits against urgency and equities.

Emergency motions are typically processed by motions panels or duty judges, can be granted on an abbreviated record, and are designed to be faster and less formal than full appeals. Because the relief is temporary, applicants should expect that the merits panel will reexamine the underlying legal and factual issues on a more complete record.

- Mandamus. Mandamus is an extraordinary writ issued by an appellate court to compel a lower court or a public official to perform a duty that the law plainly requires. In the context of district-court interlocutory action, mandamus may be appropriate when a district judge has acted in excess of jurisdiction, has refused to exercise a clear duty, or has committed a gross abuse of discretion resulting in a usurpation of power. Mandamus is not a substitute for appeal and is reserved for exceptional circumstances.

The traditional elements required for mandamus relief are: (1) the petitioner has no other adequate means to attain the relief sought (i.e., mandamus must be the only available remedy), (2) the petitioner’s right to the writ is clear and indisputable, and (3) the issuing court, in the exercise of its discretion, determines that the writ is appropriate given the circumstances. Courts apply these requirements strictly because mandamus interferes with the ordinary course of litigation and the separation of powers among trial and appellate courts.

- Differences in standard and remedy. The standards for mandamus and emergency stays differ in kind. A stay pending appeal is an equitable, provisional remedy focused on balancing the applicant’s prospect of success against harm and the public interest. Mandamus asks whether a legal duty clearly exists and whether an extraordinary intervention is warranted. Mandamus relief often results in an order compelling a specific act or vacating a lower-court ruling; a stay temporarily pauses an order’s effects.

- Procedural and strategic considerations. Litigants choosing between an emergency motion and mandamus must consider the availability and adequacy of appellate remedies. If an appeal from a nonfinal order is available under § 1292(a)(1) or § 1292(b), mandamus is typically inappropriate because an appeal is an adequate remedy. Conversely, if appellate jurisdiction is lacking and the need is urgent, mandamus may be the only remaining path, but success requires meeting the stringent elements. Moreover, courts are generally reluctant to use mandamus to control discretionary matters—mere error in the trial court is typically insufficient.

- Interaction with appellate jurisdiction. Emergency motions often are filed incident to an appeal or an attempted appeal (for example, motions for stays pending appeal). Mandamus is independent of appeal and can be filed as an original action in the court of appeals. Both routes can produce immediate relief, but they operate under different remedial logics and burden thresholds.

Understanding the distinctions between emergency motions and mandamus is crucial for practitioners and scholars. Emergency motions are the routine mechanism for seeking short-term relief while appellate review proceeds; mandamus is a narrow, extraordinary remedy that corrects only the most egregious or jurisdictionally defective lower-court actions.

Conclusion

Appellate review of injunctions sits at the intersection of equitable pragmatism and procedural structure. 28 U.S.C. § 1292(a)(1) provides a specific statutory pathway for immediate review of orders concerning injunctions, but this pathway operates against the backdrop of the final judgment rule and several narrow exceptions. Motions panels and merits panels play distinct roles in the appellate process: the former manage urgent interim relief and procedural matters, while the latter render substantive appellate adjudication. Administrative stays preserve the status quo in emergencies but await fuller appellate consideration. The appellate record must be carefully prepared to support review, and litigants should understand when to pursue emergency motions for stays and when the extraordinary remedy of mandamus is the only realistic option. Mastery of these doctrines allows a reader to appreciate the reasoning and procedural postures that shape judicial opinions addressing preliminary and interlocutory injunctive relief.
