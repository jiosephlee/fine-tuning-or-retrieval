Introduction

Federal sentencing after United States v. Booker is governed by an advisory Guidelines framework coupled with statutory factors in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). On appeal, the Supreme Court’s decisions in Rita v. United States and Gall v. United States framed how courts of appeals review sentencing decisions and established that an appellate court may apply a presumption that a within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable. The Eighth Circuit has adopted and applied that presumption in its sentencing jurisprudence. Understanding the nature and scope of that presumption, how a defendant can rebut it through § 3553(a) arguments or by identifying procedural errors, how the presumption fits within the Gall two-step framework (procedural then substantive reasonableness review), and how circuits differ in approach, is essential background for reading appellate opinions that address sentencing challenges.

# Nature and scope of the presumption of reasonableness

The presumption that a within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable is a rebuttable appellate presumption about substantive reasonableness. Its doctrinal origin is Rita, where the Supreme Court held that when a district court’s sentence falls within the Guidelines range, “the court of appeals may presume that the sentence is reasonable.” Gall clarified that the presumption does not eliminate the appellate court’s obligation to apply abuse-of-discretion review to sentences both inside and outside the Guidelines; it is a tool of appellate review, not a binding rule for district courts.

What the presumption means in practice depends on three features.

First, the presumption is appellate and rebuttable, not a mandate that district courts must impose within-Guidelines sentences, and not a rule that a within-Guidelines sentence is per se reasonable. District courts retain discretion to impose non‑Guidelines sentences based on individualized § 3553(a) analysis. The presumption operates at the appellate stage to inform the degree of deference an appellate court gives to the sentencing court’s selection of a particular within-Guidelines sentence.

Second, the presumption is tied to the role of the Sentencing Guidelines as the Commission’s determination of a sentence “appropriate in the mine run of cases.” Thus the presumption assumes that a Guidelines range, properly calculated and taken after a lawful process, embodies the Sentencing Commission’s empirical and normative judgments. The presumption is therefore stronger when the Guidelines calculation itself is correct and the district judge has explicitly addressed the relevant § 3553(a) factors.

Third, the presumption is not absolute in strength; appellate courts may describe or apply it with varying force. In the Eighth Circuit, the presumption is regularly invoked to give deference to within-Guidelines sentences in the substantive-reasonableness inquiry, but such deference does not foreclose reversal where the appellant shows a significant infirmity in the district court’s reasoning, a procedural error, an incorrect Guidelines calculation, or that the ultimate sentence is unreasonable in light of the record and statutory factors.

Standards tied to the presumption. The presumption lives within two distinct appellate review standards. Procedural issues—such as whether the district court correctly calculated the Guidelines range, treated the Guidelines as mandatory, failed to consider relevant § 3553(a) factors, gave an improper reason for the sentence, or relied on materially false information—are reviewed under standards appropriate to the type of error: legal conclusions (e.g., interpretation of the Guidelines) are reviewed de novo; factual findings underlying the Guidelines are reviewed for clear error; and discretionary determinations (e.g., admission of evidence, evidentiary rulings, or application of Rule 403 principles during sentencing hearings) are reviewed for abuse of discretion. By contrast, the substantive reasonableness of a sentence is reviewed for abuse of discretion after the procedural review is satisfied. The presumption affects the substantive-abuse-of-discretion inquiry by making an appellate court more likely to uphold a within-Guidelines sentence absent a convincing showing to the contrary.

# Methods of rebuttal through § 3553(a) arguments

A defendant challenging the substantive reasonableness of a within-Guidelines sentence usually seeks to rebut the appellate presumption through detailed § 3553(a) arguments. § 3553(a) sets out the statutory factors a district court must consider when imposing sentence: the nature and circumstances of the offense; the history and characteristics of the defendant; the need for the sentence to reflect the seriousness of the offense, provide just punishment, afford adequate deterrence, protect the public, and provide needed training or medical care; the applicable Guidelines range; the need to avoid unwarranted sentence disparities; and restitution. To overcome the presumption, an appellant typically marshals one or more of the following approaches.

1. Show that the district court failed to consider or inadequately considered relevant § 3553(a) factors. Rebuttal can be procedural in character if the district court neglected to address material § 3553(a) arguments that were raised or failed meaningfully to explain its reasoning. Appellate courts will scrutinize the record for a reasoned explanation. Where the sentencing judge gives only cursory justification or treats the Guidelines as binding in a way that appears to exclude meaningful consideration of alternatives, the presumption is easier to rebut.

2. Demonstrate that the district court weighed the § 3553(a) factors irrationally or that the weight it assigned to particular factors was arbitrary or based on impermissible considerations. Because appellate courts give substantial deference to the district court’s balancing, to prevail the appellant must show more than a disagreement about the proper weight; the appellant must show that the sentencing court’s balancing was outside the range of reasonable choices—i.e., it was an abuse of discretion. Examples include reliance on clearly erroneous facts, consideration of an improper factor (e.g., race or an unproven allegation treated as fact), or grossly disproportionate weighting that cannot be justified by the record.

3. Demonstrate that the Guidelines calculation itself was erroneous in a way that affected the sentence. Errors in calculating the Guidelines range—such as misapplication of an enhancement, miscounting relevant conduct, or failing to recognize an applicable safety-valve or adjustment—are procedural mistakes that can undermine the foundation of the presumption. The appellate effect depends on preservation: properly preserved Guidelines errors are reviewed and can lead to remand for resentencing; forfeited Guidelines errors are subject to plain-error review under Olano and the Supreme Court’s clarifications in Rosales‑Mireles and Molina‑Martinez. An incorrect Guidelines range weakens or collapses the presumption because the presumption presupposes a correctly determined range.

4. Present compelling policy or individual circumstances that justify a clear variance. Gall holds that extraordinary circumstances are not required to justify a variance; rather the extent of departure from the Guidelines is one factor in the appellate review. To overcome the presumption by arguing for a lower sentence, a defendant may present rehabilitation evidence, severe health problems, age, unusual family circumstances, severe disparity with similarly situated defendants that is not explained by the Guidelines, or legitimate sentencing disparities caused by the Guidelines themselves. The appellate court will ask whether the district court adequately considered these factors and whether the resulting sentence is an abuse of discretion.

5. Show that the sentence creates an unwarranted disparity. § 3553(a)(6) requires attention to unwarranted disparities. An appellant may argue that, in the mine run of comparable cases, the defendant’s circumstances do not justify the chosen within-Guidelines point, or that co-defendants with similar records received materially different sentences with no principled distinction. Because sentencing decisions are individualized, appellants must show a meaningful pattern or persuasive statistical or case-comparison basis to rebut the presumption.

Proof burdens and appellate posture. On appeal the defendant bears the burden of demonstrating that the sentence is unreasonable in light of the record; the presumption operates as an evidentiary and doctrinal headwind for that showing. Procedural errors, however, may shift the posture: if the appellant proves a significant procedural error (e.g., clear miscalculation of the Guidelines, failure to consider a nonfrivolous argument, or reliance on materially false information), the appellate court may vacate and remand without reaching a full substantive presumption analysis.

# Interaction with procedural reasonableness and Gall framework

Gall established a two-part framework for appellate review: first, the appellate court asks whether the sentence is procedurally reasonable, and second, whether it is substantively reasonable. The presumption plays its primary role in the second part but is often triggered only after a procedural check.

Procedural reasonableness. The first step examines whether the district court followed the necessary procedures before imposing sentence. Important procedural issues include: correct calculation of the Guidelines range; adequate consideration of the § 3553(a) factors; providing a reasoned explanation for the chosen sentence; addressing any nonfrivolous arguments for a differing sentence; avoiding reliance on clearly erroneous facts or improper factors; and following statutory or constitutional constraints (e.g., rights to allocution and counsel). Procedural errors can take multiple forms and have different appellate consequences:

- Legal error (e.g., misinterpreting a Guideline provision) is reviewed de novo and typically requires correction when shown.

- Factual error in the PSR or at sentencing is reviewed for clear error. Where a factual basis for an enhancement is unsupported by the record, an appellate court may set aside the enhancement and remand.

- Omission or inadequate explanation of § 3553(a) analysis can constitute procedural error when the sentence is unexplained and the court fails to respond to a nonfrivolous argument.

- Failure to correctly apply constitutional protections or statutory rules (e.g., statutory maximums) is reversible error.

Gall instructs that procedural reasonableness must be established before an appellate court considers substantive reasonableness. If a procedural error is present and material, remand is normally required so the district court may correct the procedure and explain its reasoning.

Substantive reasonableness and the presumption. If the sentence is procedurally sound, the appellate court proceeds to substantive review for abuse of discretion. The presumption that a within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable guides this inquiry: it recognizes that the Guidelines reflect the Commission’s view of an appropriate sentence and that a within-Guidelines sentence, when derived from a proper procedure, is likely to be reasonable in the mine run of cases. However, Gall emphasizes that appellate courts must still consider the totality of the circumstances; the presumption does not relieve the appellant of showing that the district court’s judgment fell outside the range of discretion.

How appellate courts apply abuse-of-discretion review varies in articulation. In substantive review the appellant must show that the sentence is unreasonable in light of the § 3553(a) factors and the record. Because sentencing involves normative judgments and fact-intensive balancing, appellate courts generally defer to the district court’s sense of appropriate punishment, and reversal on substantive grounds is uncommon. The presumption raises the bar for reversal of within-Guidelines sentences: an appellant must show that the sentence is not merely debatable but that it is unreasonable when judged against the statutory factors and the record.

Interaction with plain-error doctrine when issues were forfeited. Many sentencing challenges are forfeited by failure to object in district court. Forfeited procedural errors (including Guidelines calculation errors not preserved) are reviewed under the plain-error standard from United States v. Olano: (1) there was an error, (2) the error is plain, (3) the error affected substantial rights (usually a showing of a reasonable probability of a different outcome), and (4) the appellate court should exercise its discretion to correct the error because it seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Rosales‑Mireles clarified that when a miscalculated Guidelines range is shown to be a plain error that affected substantial rights, appellate courts should ordinarily vacate the sentence and remand in the ordinary case—that is, the fourth prong should generally be satisfied unless unique circumstances counsel otherwise. Thus a significant Guidelines miscalculation can both collapse the presumption and mandate correction even when the defendant failed to preserve the objection.

# Circuit variation on the presumption

Although Rita and Gall came from the Supreme Court and form a national framework, circuits differ in how they articulate and apply the presumption and in the strength of the evidentiary and procedural deference they afford to within-Guidelines sentences. Key areas of variation include whether the presumption is described as a “rebuttable presumption of reasonableness” or a “rebuttable presumption of substance,” how rigorously appellate courts examine the district court’s explanation for a within-Guidelines sentence, and the threshold required to rebut the presumption.

Stronger-presumption circuits. Several circuits treat a within-Guidelines sentence as presumptively reasonable and give it considerable deference on substantive review so long as a proper procedural foundation exists. Those courts often emphasize that an appellant must produce a compelling justification to overcome the presumption: mere disagreement with the district court’s weight assignment is insufficient. In these circuits, appellate reversal of within-Guidelines sentences on substantive grounds is rare and typically occurs only when the sentence is demonstrably unreasonable or based on clearly improper considerations.

More skeptical circuits. Other circuits treat the presumption more modestly or apply it more flexibly. Some circuits place heavier emphasis on ensuring the Guidelines were correctly calculated and that the district court engaged in a robust § 3553(a) analysis. These courts may be more willing to examine the district court’s reasoning and the stated bases for the sentence, particularly where systemic disparities or unusually individualized circumstances are at play.

Application to different sentencing contexts. Circuits also differ in how the presumption applies in cases involving, for example, mandatory minimums, substantial assistance departures, policy-based guideline amendments, or non-incarceration sentences. Some circuits have developed special rules or emphases—such as heightened scrutiny where a Guidelines calculation depends heavily on contested factual findings or where a within-Guidelines life sentence is imposed—while others adhere to a uniform presumption regardless of the gravity of the punishment.

Eighth Circuit posture within this spectrum. The Eighth Circuit consistently recognizes the Rita presumption and applies it within the Gall framework: the appellate court first ensures procedural reasonableness and proper Guidelines calculation; if that foundation is sound, the court affords deference to within-Guidelines sentences in substantive review. The Eighth Circuit nonetheless requires meaningful district-court explanation and will reverse where the record shows procedural errors, plainly erroneous Guidelines calculations, or a sentence that is substantively unreasonable in light of the § 3553(a) factors. Consequently, in the Eighth Circuit the presumption is an important but not dispositive part of appellate sentencing review.

Practical significance of circuit variation. Because circuits vary, attorneys litigating sentencing appeals must be attentive to local precedents about how robust a district court’s explanation must be, how heavily the presumption will weigh against reversal, and how appellate courts treat preserved versus forfeited sentencing issues. Understanding the particular circuit’s track record in reversing within-Guidelines sentences helps litigators tailor arguments—either to rebut the presumption with strong § 3553(a) evidence and demonstration of procedural infirmities or, if defending a within-Guidelines sentence, to emphasize the correctness of the Guidelines calculation and the reasoned nature of the district court’s balancing.

Conclusion

The presumption that a within-Guidelines sentence is reasonable is a central feature of modern appellate sentencing review: it reflects deference to the Sentencing Guidelines and to the district court’s application of the statutory sentencing factors, but it is rebuttable. The Eighth Circuit applies that presumption within Gall’s two-step framework—first ensuring procedural reasonableness (correct Guidelines calculation, consideration of § 3553(a), and adequate explanation), then applying an abuse-of-discretion standard to substantive reasonableness while affording deference to within-Guidelines choices. A defendant rebuts the presumption by establishing procedural error, a significant Guidelines miscalculation, or demonstrating that the district court’s weighing of § 3553(a) factors produced a sentence outside the range of reasonable choices. Because circuits vary in how strongly they apply the presumption and how readily they will reverse, careful attention to preservation, factual record-building at the sentencing hearing, and tailored § 3553(a) arguments on appeal are essential for effective appellate advocacy.
