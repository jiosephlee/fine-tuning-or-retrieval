Introduction

Presentence reports (PSRs) and the process of sentencing fact-finding are central to federal sentencing. A PSR is the primary vehicle for compiling background information, offense conduct, criminal history, victim impact, and other matters that a sentencing court uses to determine an appropriate sentence under the Guidelines and statutory sentencing objectives. Because much of the PSR consists of factual assertions drawn from a variety of sources, courts have developed procedures for resolving disputes about those assertions, allocating burdens of proof, and reviewing the sentencing court’s factual determinations on appeal. This chapter explains how PSRs are prepared and structured, how parties object and how courts resolve disputes, when courts may adopt undisputed PSR facts, the role of hearsay and reliability at sentencing, the standards of appellate review for sentencing findings, and a common type of upward adjustment—enhancement for conduct during flight under the Sentencing Guidelines.

# Preparation and contents of the PSR

A presentence report is a comprehensive pre-sentence document prepared by the probation office after a defendant has been found guilty or has pleaded guilty. The probation officer conducts an investigation that draws on the record, law-enforcement reports, victim statements, interviews with the defendant and witnesses, criminal history records, and other relevant sources. The purpose of the PSR is to supply the sentencing court with verified factual material to facilitate accurate calculation of Guidelines ranges, consideration of statutory factors (such as those in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a)), and selection of an appropriate sentence.

The typical PSR includes several discrete components:

- Offense conduct. A narrative description of the offense, focusing on the defendant’s role, the nature and extent of the criminal activity, relevant dates, victims, and the manner in which the offense was committed. This section is often the primary locus of factual disputes because it translates investigative materials into a cohesive account.

- Relevant conduct. For Guidelines purposes, the PSR identifies conduct that is relevant to offense-level calculation, which may include acts beyond the charged counts if those acts are part of the same course of conduct or common scheme or plan.

- Guideline calculations. The PSR sets out base offense levels, specific offense characteristics, adjustments (such as for acceptance of responsibility or obstruction), and criminal history category, producing an advisory Guidelines range. The report typically shows the factual bases for each calculation.

- Criminal history. Arrest and conviction history, sentences imposed, and any calculation specifics that affect criminal history points or category.

- Victim impact and restitution. Statements from victims, loss calculations, and recommendations on restitution.

- Mitigating or aggravating information. Information about the defendant’s background, employment, education, health, family circumstances, and other matters bearing on sentencing.

- Recommendations. The probation officer often provides a recommended sentence or a recommended Guidelines application, although the probation office’s recommendation is not binding on the court.

Because the PSR is both informational and often determinative of the range of possible sentences, accuracy and the opportunity to correct errors at the PSR stage are crucial. The court commonly orders disclosure of the draft PSR to the parties so that objections may be asserted before the sentencing hearing.

# Objections and burden-shifting framework

When a draft PSR is disclosed, both parties have an opportunity to object to factual statements and guideline calculations. The process for resolving those objections implicates several procedural and substantive rules:

- Timeliness and specificity. Federal practice generally requires that objections to the PSR be made in writing and filed within a prescribed time before the sentencing hearing. Many courts expect objections to be fact-specific and to identify precisely the disputed statements and the reasons for challenge (e.g., that the statement is incorrect, that the source is unreliable, or that the conduct is not relevant conduct under the Guidelines).

- The burden of production. The defendant bears the initial burden to raise a timely and specific objection to information in the PSR. A mere general denial or blanket objection will not suffice to force the court to hold an evidentiary hearing on every disputed PSR assertion. The party challenging a PSR statement must point to the factual basis for its objection or otherwise explain why the PSR is unreliable on that point.

- The burden of persuasion. Once a defendant makes a timely and specific objection, the government ordinarily bears the burden of proving disputed sentencing facts by a preponderance of the evidence. If the government does not meet its burden, the court should not rely on the disputed fact to increase the defendant’s Guideline range or to impose an upward variance. For sentencing issues that would increase the statutory maximum or add a new element of punishment, constitutional principles (e.g., those derived from Apprendi and its progeny) may require proof beyond a reasonable doubt; but for the vast majority of fact-finding relevant to Guidelines adjustments, the preponderance standard governs.

- Court procedures for resolving disputes. The sentencing court has discretion to resolve disputed facts in several ways: it can accept written proffers, hold an evidentiary hearing with live testimony and cross-examination, receive hearsay and documentary evidence, or make findings based on the PSR and other materials in the record. The court’s choice depends on the significance of the disputed facts to the sentence, the reliability of available evidence, and considerations of efficiency.

- Strategic and procedural effects. The posture of the case affects the objections process. In plea cases, the plea agreement may stipulate to certain facts or guideline calculations, narrowing disputes. When facts in the plea agreement are stipulated, courts often adopt those stipulations unless there is clear reason not to. In trial cases, evidentiary hearings may be more likely for major contested factual questions. Failure to object timely or to raise a matter at sentencing can forfeit the issue for appeal except where appellate plain-error review applies.

# Adoption of undisputed PSR facts

When parties do not object to statements in the PSR or when objections have been resolved and facts are undisputed, sentencing courts commonly adopt those facts in whole or in part. Adoption serves several functions: it provides a clear factual premise for the sentence, streamlines proceedings, and supplies a basis for the appellate record.

A court may adopt undisputed facts in any of several ways. A judge may state on the record that he or she adopts the PSR’s narrative, or the court may incorporate the PSR by reference in its oral pronouncement or written judgment. Adoption signals that the court accepts the PSR’s accuracy and intends to base sentencing determinations on those factual findings.

However, adoption is not an automatic ratification of every PSR assertion. A court should ensure that the facts it adopts are materially accurate and adequately supported. If the PSR contains implausible or contradicted material, acceptance without further inquiry risks reversible error if those facts were essential to an increased sentence. Conversely, adoption of undisputed facts does not require the court to adopt the probation officer’s legal conclusions about Guidelines application; the judge independently determines the correct application of the law.

Procedural posture matters. In a plea case where the parties have stipulated certain facts, courts often adopt the stipulation because it provides a reliable, negotiated factual basis. When facts are undisputed because the defendant fails to object, appellate courts may treat the PSR’s facts as established in the record subject to clear-error review.

# Use of hearsay with indicia of reliability at sentencing

Sentencing proceedings are not governed by the Federal Rules of Evidence in the same way as trials. The rules provide greater latitude for the sentencing judge to consider a range of information, including hearsay, so long as the information has sufficient indicia of reliability and the defendant has notice and an opportunity to contest. The rationale is that sentencing courts need a full picture of the defendant and offense conduct, and the adversarial protections of a trial are not always necessary or practical to develop reliable sentencing facts.

Key principles regarding hearsay at sentencing include:

- General admissibility. A sentencing court may consider hearsay and unsworn statements when those items bear sufficient indicia of reliability. Typical reliable sources include official records, investigative reports, sworn or recorded statements from law enforcement, victim impact statements, and corrobated admissions.

- Indicia of reliability. Reliability can be established in many ways: corroboration by other records or testimony, contemporaneous documentation, admissions by the defendant, or authoritativeness of the source (e.g., official law-enforcement records). The court should be cautious about uncorroborated or double-hearsay statements without a reliable source.

- Party control and opportunity to rebut. Even when hearsay is admissible, the sentencing court should give the parties notice of material it intends to rely upon and an opportunity to challenge the hearsay’s reliability. This might occur through pre-hearing disclosure of the PSR, evidentiary proffers at the hearing, or allowing cross-examination of witnesses whose statements are used.

- Weight, not absolute admissibility. The presence of hearsay affects the weight the court assigns rather than automatically excluding the material. A court may still consider a hearsay statement but assign it less weight if the indicia of reliability are weak.

- Limits when determining elements with significant constitutional implications. If a disputed fact would increase the statutory maximum punishment or alter a substantive right, constitutional protections may constrain the court’s reliance on untested hearsay. In such situations, due process and criminal-procedure principles may require live testimony or higher standards of proof.

In practice, courts balance the need for reliable sentencing information against the impracticality of full trial procedures. Judges should expressly state their basis for relying on hearsay and indicate how they weighed any reliability concerns.

# Clear-error review of factual findings

Appellate review of sentencing factual determinations generally applies the clear-error standard. That means an appellate court will defer to the district court’s findings of fact unless the reviewing court is left with a definite and firm conviction that a mistake has been made. The practical effect of clear-error review is that factual resolutions grounded in credibility assessments, evaluation of witness demeanor, or resolution of conflicting documentary evidence are unlikely to be reversed unless unsupported by the record.

Several points about appellate review are worth emphasizing:

- Distinction between fact and law. Appellate courts review legal questions de novo (no deference), mixed questions of law and fact often receive varying treatment depending on whether the issue is predominantly legal, and purely factual findings are reviewed for clear error. A district court’s interpretation of the Guidelines or its application of legal principles in sentencing is subject to de novo review.

- Sentencing fact-finding and reliance on PSR. When a sentence is based on PSR findings, appellate courts examine whether the district court properly resolved objections and whether the factual basis for the Guidelines calculation or enhancement is supported by the record. Courts often defer to the district court’s adoption of the PSR unless the factual basis is plainly wrong or unsupported.

- Standards for reversing. To reverse under clear-error, an appellate court must conclude that the district court’s account of the facts is implausible in light of the record or that the district court misapprehended critical evidence. Mere disagreement with how the district court weighed evidence does not suffice.

- Harmless error analysis. Even if a factual finding is erroneous, an appellate court will consider whether the error was harmless — i.e., whether the error affected the sentence imposed. If the sentence would have been the same absent the erroneous fact, reversal is not required.

- Preservation and scope of review. If a defendant fails to object to a PSR fact at sentencing, appellate review is often limited to plain-error review, which is more demanding for establishing reversible error. Preserving objections on the record and complying with procedural deadlines thus preserve full appellate scrutiny.

Overall, the combination of district court factfinding authority and clear-error appellate review reflects a policy of entrusting factual resolution about sentencing to the trial court while preserving appellate correction for clear mistakes.

# Enhancements for conduct during flight (USSG §3C1.2)

Enhancements for conduct during flight are an example of the type of sentencing adjustments that often arise from PSR allegations and factual findings. While the precise text and numeric effect of any particular guideline provision are matters of the Guidelines themselves, the doctrinal approach to such enhancements is consistent across similar provisions: the court identifies the factual predicate, determines whether the defendant’s conduct meets the guideline’s criteria, applies the appropriate standard of proof, and explains the rationale for any upward adjustment.

Key doctrinal points about flight-related enhancements include the following:

- Elements of the enhancement. A flight-related enhancement typically requires proof that the defendant engaged in willful conduct intended to avoid apprehension or prosecution. The conduct must be more than mere presence at a scene; it generally requires affirmative evasion such as fleeing from law enforcement, concealing oneself, destroying or removing indicia used to identify the defendant, or actions that impede law enforcement’s ability to effectuate an arrest. The enhancement often applies only when the flight or evasion is connected to the offense conduct—that is, when the defendant’s flight was undertaken in relation to or to avoid consequence for the charged criminal activity.

- Mens rea and causation. The sentencing court looks for evidence that the defendant acted intentionally or recklessly to avoid capture. If the conduct was motivated by an innocent reason or if the defendant did not know law enforcement was seeking to arrest, the enhancement is inappropriate. The government must show a causal link between the flight behavior and the defendant’s attempt to avoid law enforcement or prosecution.

- Standard of proof. As with other sentencing facts, the government must prove a flight enhancement by a preponderance of the evidence absent any statutory or constitutional bar calling for a higher standard. The district court may rely on the PSR, witness testimony, police reports, or other reliable evidence to make this determination. The court should explain on the record the factual basis for applying the enhancement.

- Scope and limits. Flight enhancements are not intended to punish merely for flight that is trivial, involuntary, or unrelated to the offense. For instance, a defendant who leaves a public place without knowledge of a pending arrest, or who relocates for reasons unconnected to evading prosecution, should not be subject to the enhancement. Similarly, the presence of extraordinary or overly remote conduct that bears little nexus to the charged conduct may not support the adjustment.

- Procedural considerations. When a PSR recommends a flight enhancement, the defense should lodge a specific objection identifying why the report’s account is incorrect or why the conduct does not satisfy the guideline’s requirements. If the enhancement is contested and material to the Guidelines range or sentence, the court should permit sufficient fact-finding—through testimony, documentary evidence, or proffers—to resolve the dispute reliably. If the court applies the enhancement, it should state the factual findings and their bases in order to create a clear record for any appeal.

- Sentencing discretion and proportionality. Even where the factual predicate for a flight enhancement is established, the sentencing court retains discretion to weigh that conduct among other § 3553(a) factors. The enhancement increases the Guidelines range (or functions as a factor for consideration), but the judge must still impose a sentence that is substantively reasonable in light of the totality of circumstances. Thus, an enhancement can affect the Guidelines calculation but does not dictate the ultimate sentence if mitigating factors or other considerations counsel otherwise.

Conclusion

Presentence reports and sentencing fact-finding are complex and consequential aspects of federal sentencing. The PSR consolidates the factual and legal materials that inform the Guidelines calculation and the judge’s assessment of appropriate punishment. Parties must engage with the PSR through timely, specific objections; once objections are raised, the government bears the usual burden of proof by a preponderance for disputed sentencing facts. Sentencing courts may rely on hearsay so long as it is sufficiently reliable and parties have notice and the opportunity to challenge it. On appeal, factual findings made at sentencing are reviewed for clear error, while legal conclusions receive de novo review. Specific adjustments—such as enhancements for flight—illustrate how courts apply these principles: the court must find the requisite conduct and state its factual basis, while respecting boundaries of causation, mens rea, and proportionality. Understanding these procedural and evidentiary frameworks is essential preparation for reading and evaluating judicial opinions that resolve sentencing disputes.
