Introduction

The Armed Career Criminal Guideline, USSG § 4B1.4, governs how a sentencing court calculates the offense level and criminal history consequences when a defendant is subject to an armed career criminal enhancement under federal law or is otherwise treated as an armed career criminal for guideline purposes. Section 4B1.4 does not create the predicate facts that give rise to enhanced punishment; rather, it prescribes how to translate that statutory or guideline status into the Sentencing Table calculation: which offense level to use, how to set criminal history category, and how that selection interacts with the ordinary Chapter Two offense-level calculations and Chapter Four criminal-history rules. For a law student preparing to read judicial opinions on § 4B1.4, it is essential to understand (1) how the guideline chooses an offense level when the defendant is deemed an armed career criminal, (2) how it fixes criminal history, (3) the principle that the greater applicable offense level controls when multiple provisions could apply, and (4) how these rules operate with the Chapter Two computations that would otherwise govern firearms offenses. The following sections develop each of these topics in doctrinal detail, emphasizing how courts reason and how procedural posture affects the analysis.

# Offense level determination under § 4B1.4(b)

Structure and goal. Section 4B1.4(b) is the guideline provision that prescribes which offense level the court will use when a defendant qualifies as an armed career criminal (by statute or guideline definition). The provision recognizes that there are multiple possible ways to compute an offense level: (a) the ordinary Chapter Two computation for the offense of conviction and relevant conduct; (b) an offense level associated with statutory enhancements or special offense characteristics that apply because of the defendant’s career-criminal status; and (c) any offense-level provisions within the career criminal guideline framework itself. Section 4B1.4(b) resolves potential conflicts among these alternative offense-level calculations by directing the court to adopt a single offense level: the highest (i.e., greatest) offense level that results from the applicable choices. The policy behind this approach is to ensure that the sentencing table reflects the increased culpability and danger associated with armed career criminals without creating double counting or inconsistent application of enhancements.

How the court implements the provision. In practice, the sentencing court carries out § 4B1.4(b) in a sequence:

1. Determine whether the defendant qualifies as an armed career criminal under the relevant statute or guideline definitions. That threshold question is resolved first because § 4B1.4(b) applies only if that status is established.

2. Compute the offense level under the ordinary Chapter Two rules for the count of conviction and the defendant’s relevant conduct. This means applying the applicable base offense level, specific offense characteristics, cross-references, multiple-count grouping, and any Chapter Three adjustments (such as acceptance of responsibility or obstruction) that would be appropriate absent the armed career criminal status.

3. Identify any offense levels or specific offense-level impositions that flow directly from the armed-career-criminal status itself (that is, any offense-level table or rule that the guideline system provides to reflect that status). Also identify any alternative weapon-related offense-level calculations available under Chapter Two for the particular firearm or violence offense at issue.

4. Compare the several candidate offense levels and adopt the greatest. The greatest offense level becomes the offense level used to consult the Sentencing Table (together with the criminal history category to arrive at the guideline range).

Courts’ analytic emphases. When reviewing § 4B1.4(b) determinations, courts assess whether the district court correctly identified and computed each candidate offense level and then correctly selected the highest. Appellate review scrutinizes whether the trial court adhered to the underlying mechanics of Chapter Two when calculating the ordinary offense level, whether it applied any cross-references properly, and whether it properly recognized limitations (for example, statutory minimums or maximums that cap the calculation). The burden of proof for establishing predicate convictions that trigger armed-career-criminal status is typically by a preponderance of the evidence at sentencing, and a decision that the status obtains is reviewed for clear error as to factual findings and de novo for statutory or guideline interpretation.

Common pitfalls. Typical errors in the § 4B1.4(b) context include failing to account for relevant conduct when computing the Chapter Two offense level, misapplying cross-references that would yield a higher offense level, and treating the armed-career-criminal-imposed offense level as a mandatory replacement for all Chapter Two calculations rather than as one candidate among several. Another frequent issue is double counting: a court must avoid applying the same conduct-based enhancement twice (once in a Chapter Two calculation and again under a separate career-criminal provision) unless the guideline text authorizes cumulative application.

# Criminal History Category under § 4B1.4(c)

Purpose and effect. Section 4B1.4(c) addresses criminal history scoring once the armed career criminal determination has been made. Its essential function is to ensure that a person who qualifies as an armed career criminal is treated as having a high criminal history for purposes of the Sentencing Table. The provision commonly does so by setting a floor for the criminal history category, so that no matter how the defendant’s ordinary Chapter Four calculation would come out, the criminal history category used to read the Sentencing Table is not lower than a specified level.

Implementation in sentencing. After the offense level has been selected under § 4B1.4(b), the court must determine the criminal history category. That process begins with the usual Chapter Four computation: counting prior sentences, assigning points for prior sentences and juvenile adjudications where relevant, considering the timing rules and recency of prior offenses, and applying any special rules (such as for federal, state, or foreign convictions). Once the Chapter Four calculation produces a criminal history category, § 4B1.4(c) then intervenes: if the category produced by Chapter Four is below the floor set by § 4B1.4, the court uses the higher category dictated by § 4B1.4(c). In short, § 4B1.4(c) supersedes a lower Chapter Four outcome when the defendant is an armed career criminal.

Burden and proof. The evidence necessary to trigger the criminal-history floor under § 4B1.4(c) is the same evidence that establishes armed-career-criminal status—proof of qualifying prior convictions as measured against the guideline or statutory definitions of a "violent felony" or "serious drug offense," as applicable. The sentencing record must support the conclusion that the defendant’s prior record meets the definitions and temporal requirements (for example, whether prior sentences are counted under the relevant look-back period). Procedurally, objecting to the criminal history calculation often requires challenging the factual predicate of the prior convictions or their classification; those challenges are generally resolved under the preponderance standard, with appellate review varying between clear error for factfinding and de novo for legal classification issues.

Interaction with departures and variances. A floor to criminal history does not prevent the court from considering departures or variances under other provisions, but it constrains the guideline-range computation. If a court considers and decides to impose a non-guideline sentence (a variance) based on policy disagreements with the armed-career-criminal scheme or unique mitigating circumstances, the court must explain the variance adequately. When applying § 4B1.4(c), sentencing courts should still articulate how the criminal history floor interacts with other guideline provisions and why a particular sentence is appropriate under 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a).

# Rule that the greater offense level applies when multiple provisions are implicated

Textual principle. A core rule embodied in § 4B1.4 and in similar guideline provisions is that when multiple alternative offense-level provisions are implicated by the defendant’s conduct and status, the court should apply the greatest offense level among the available alternatives. This "greater offense level" rule promotes consistency, prevents artificially low guideline calculations when a higher applicable guideline reflects the defendant’s culpability, and avoids the need for the court to engage in potentially arbitrary choices among competing guideline provisions.

How courts interpret and apply the rule. Courts apply a systematic comparison: they compute each candidate offense level following the specific instructions of the relevant guideline provisions and then select the highest. Importantly, courts do not combine levels from different provisions unless the guidelines explicitly authorize cumulative enhancements. For example, if the Chapter Two calculation yields an offense level of X, a career-criminal-specific table yields Y, and a statutory-mandatory-minimum-based enhancement would produce Z, § 4B1.4 directs selection of max{X, Y, Z} (the greatest of X, Y, and Z). Courts will carefully verify that each computed value is validly available: if a particular calculation rests on inapplicable conduct or misclassification, it is excluded from the comparison.

Analytic implications. The greater-offense-level rule affects prosecutorial charging decisions and sentencing litigation. Knowing that the maximum applicable guideline will control sometimes influences whether a prosecutor seeks enhanced statutory treatment, because triggering an enhancement may not reduce the guideline range, and may actually increase it. For defense counsel, the rule places a premium on attacking the factual or legal bases for any of the higher candidate offense levels (for example, by contesting predicate convictions, cross-references, or the applicability of particular specific offense characteristics).

Limits and exceptions. The rule does not mean that a court may ignore limitations built into the guideline system. If a candidate offense level exceeds a statutory maximum or conflicts with a statutory mandatory minimum, the court must respect statutory constraints. Moreover, if guideline text prevents cumulative application of certain specific offense characteristics, the court must follow those prohibitionary rules. Finally, when the guideline system expressly prescribes an order of operations or a mandatory substitution rather than a simple choice among alternatives, the court must follow that textual command even if it yields a lower offense level than another available computation.

# Interaction with Chapter Two calculations for firearms offenses

Chapter Two fundamentals. Chapter Two contains the offense-specific guideline provisions—base offense levels, specific offense characteristics, and cross-references—used to compute an offense level based on the offense of conviction and relevant conduct. For firearms offenses, Chapter Two often contains base offense levels keyed to the particular statutes (for example, for unlawful possession, distribution, or use of firearms) and specific offense characteristics that increase or alter the base level depending on factors such as the type of firearm, whether the firearm was discharged, whether it was possessed in furtherance of another felony, and whether the defendant was a prohibited person.

How § 4B1.4 intersects with Chapter Two. When a defendant has armed-career-criminal status, the sentencing process still must follow Chapter Two to the extent those provisions apply. Section 4B1.4(b) does not replace Chapter Two outright; rather it requires that the Chapter Two computation be performed and serve as one of the candidate offense levels in the comparison that yields the ultimate level. Thus, the Chapter Two analysis—identifying the correct base offense level, applying relevant cross-references (which may, for firearms offenses, lead to nonfirearms guidelines where appropriate), and adding or subtracting specific offense characteristics and Chapter Three adjustments—is an essential precursor to applying § 4B1.4.

Specific issues in firearms cases. Several recurring doctrinal issues arise in the interaction of § 4B1.4 with Chapter Two for firearms offenses:

- Cross-references to other guidelines. A gun offense may contain a cross-reference under Chapter Two that directs the court to calculate the offense level as if the defendant had committed another offense (for example, a drug-trafficking guideline) when certain conditions are met. The correctness of applying such a cross-reference can have a significant impact on whether the Chapter Two calculation yields the highest offense level. When applying § 4B1.4, courts must ensure cross-references are available under the guideline text and supported by the evidence of relevant conduct.

- Specific offense characteristics vs. career status. Some specific offense characteristics in Chapter Two increase the offense level for conduct that may also be implicated in the career-criminal determination (e.g., a prior use of a firearm in a violent felony). Courts must be careful not to double-count the same predicate conduct in both the career-criminal designation and as a Chapter Two enhancement, unless the guidelines expressly permit stacking. The sentencing judge should explain which enhancements are being applied and why they are not duplicative.

- Relevant conduct scope. For firearms offenses, the breadth of relevant conduct can be decisive. Chapter Two requires inclusion of all relevant conduct under §1B1.3. A comprehensive factual record at sentencing is therefore necessary to determine the full scope of relevant conduct that might be used both to calculate the Chapter Two level and to establish predicate conduct for career-criminal status. Courts scrutinize whether the evidence supporting relevant conduct satisfies the preponderance standard and whether hearsay or proffered admissions are sufficiently reliable.

- Statutory minimums and mandatory minimums. Many firearms statutes carry mandatory minimum sentences that can interact with guideline calculations. § 4B1.4’s selection of the greatest offense level must still be harmonized with statutory minima and maxima. For example, if the greatest guideline offense level corresponds to a sentencing range below a statutory mandatory minimum, the mandatory minimum controls the sentence. Conversely, if the mandatory minimum produces a higher effective offense level for table computation purposes, that must be taken into account.

Procedural posture and evidentiary considerations. Because the Chapter Two calculation and the career criminal determination both rely heavily on prior-conviction evidence and factual findings about the current offense, the procedural posture at sentencing matters. If a defendant protests the use of a prior conviction (for instance, on the ground that the record of conviction is ambiguous about whether it constitutes a qualifying predicate), courts look to the record of conviction and other relevant documents (plea agreements, judgments, charging documents) within the limits allowed by precedent. The timing of such objections also matters: a defendant who fails to raise an objection at the time of sentencing may face higher standards on appeal. When contested, district courts often hold evidentiary hearings or receive proffers; factfinding on these matters is typically reviewed for clear error, while legal classifications of prior offenses are reviewed de novo.

Sentencing calculation example—conceptual. To illustrate conceptually, imagine that after performing the full Chapter Two analysis for a firearm offense, the court arrives at one candidate offense level. The court also recognizes that the defendant’s record triggers career-criminal treatment yielding a different candidate offense level. Under § 4B1.4(b) the court compares these levels (and any statutory-minimum-influenced level) and selects the greatest. Then, under § 4B1.4(c), the court ensures the criminal history category is at least the prescribed floor. The resulting combination of chosen offense level and criminal history category is used to read the Sentencing Table and derive a guideline range, subject always to statutory limits and any permissible departures or variances.

Conclusion

USSG § 4B1.4 is a rule-of-implementation guideline: it translates a finding of armed career criminal status into concrete guiding parameters for the Sentencing Table by (1) directing that the greatest applicable offense level control, and (2) setting a floor for criminal history category. Sentencing practice under § 4B1.4 requires meticulous Chapter Two computations, careful proof and classification of prior convictions, and attention to statutory constraints and double-counting concerns. Procedurally, the defendant’s challenges to predicate convictions and to the scope of relevant conduct are central to litigating the application of § 4B1.4, and those challenges will shape the factual record that appellate courts review. Understanding how § 4B1.4 coexists with Chapter Two and Chapter Four is essential to predicting guideline outcomes and to appreciating the issues that commonly arise in judicial opinions addressing armed career criminal sentencing.
