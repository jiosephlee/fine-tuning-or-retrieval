Introduction

Federal sentencing is governed by a hybrid framework that combines statutory sentencing directives, an advisory Sentencing Guidelines regime, and institutional practice embodied in the Presentence Investigation Report (PSR). Since the era in which the Guidelines were binding, courts now treat them as advisory. Sentencing courts must calculate the guideline range, treat that calculation as an important point of reference, and then select a sentence that is sufficient but not greater than necessary to satisfy the statutory purposes of punishment. To do that, courts rely heavily on the PSR and the parties’ submitted evidence and arguments. This chapter explains the central doctrines that a reader must grasp before approaching a federal sentencing opinion: the advisory nature of the Guidelines; the role of the PSR and the mechanisms for lodging and resolving objections; how relevant conduct is defined and proved under USSG § 1B1.3; the standards of proof and the evidentiary rules that apply in sentencing; the conceptual and practical distinction between departures and variances; and the statutory sentencing factors in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) together with the enumerated purposes of sentencing. Throughout, emphasis is placed on legal reasoning—how courts apply rules to facts, allocate burdens, and explain sentences in ways that permit meaningful appellate review.

# Advisory nature of the Guidelines

The Sentencing Guidelines remain the centerpiece of federal sentencing analysis but are no longer binding in a manner that removes judicial discretion. The modern, post-binding regime is structured as follows: a sentencing court must first correctly calculate the applicable guideline range by applying the Guidelines’ rules to the offense of conviction and to the offender’s base criminal history. That range is the starting point for the court’s consideration. However, the Guidelines are advisory: after calculating the range, the district court must consider the Guidelines together with the factors set out in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), and may impose a sentence either within the guideline range or outside it, so long as it provides adequate explanation for any variance or departure.

This advisory framework requires a two-step approach in reasoning. First, accurate application of the Guidelines requires strict adherence to the applicable guideline provisions, interpretive rules in the Guidelines Manual, and controlling statutory limits (e.g., statutory maximums and minimums). Errors in calculation are reviewed on appeal for correctness, often subject to de novo review where the district court’s legal interpretation is at issue. Second, the court must consider whether to vary from the calculated range in light of the statutory factors. When imposing a sentence outside the calculated range, the court should explicitly identify the factor or factors that justify the variance and explain why a sentence outside the range is necessary to achieve the statutory purposes of sentencing.

The advisory nature of the Guidelines affects procedural posture and appellate review. Because the Guidelines are not binding, courts have discretion to impose non-guideline sentences, but that discretion is cabined by statutory requirements to consider the Guidelines and to articulate a reasoned basis for any deviation. Appellate courts assess procedural reasonableness by examining whether the district court correctly calculated the range, adequately considered the § 3553(a) factors, and provided sufficient explanation for the sentence imposed. Substantive reasonableness review then examines whether the sentence is reasonable in light of the facts and the statutory goals. The interplay of these obligations makes precise calculation of guideline factors, careful handling of objections, and clear oral and written statements of reasons an important part of sentencing practice.

# Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and objections

The Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) prepared by the probation office is central to federal sentencing. The PSR gathers factual information relevant to offense conduct, relevant conduct (as that term is defined in the Guidelines), the defendant’s history and characteristics, victims’ impact, restitution and forfeiture issues, and other facts relevant to guideline calculation and the § 3553(a) analysis. The PSR typically contains recommended guideline calculations, including offense level computations and criminal history points, as well as factual narratives that summarize the offense, the defendant’s role, and relevant conduct.

Procedurally, the PSR has a special role because courts and parties treat it as an authoritative, though not dispositive, source. The probation officer is responsible for drafting the PSR, applying guideline rules, and recommending a guideline range based on the agency’s investigation. The government and the defendant must review the PSR and are afforded an opportunity to object to any facts or guideline calculations. The timing for submitting objections is established by the local rules and the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure; parties usually must submit written objections in advance of the sentencing hearing to give the probation officer an opportunity to investigate and, if appropriate, to revise the PSR.

An objection typically contests either a factual assertion in the narrative portion of the PSR (for example, whether certain conduct occurred or whether a person was part of a jointly undertaken criminal activity) or a legal application of the Guidelines (for example, whether a particular adjustment, enhancement, or grouping rule applies). The burden of proof and the nature of supporting evidence for objections are crucial. Generally, the party advancing a disputed factual claim at sentencing bears the burden of persuading the court by a preponderance of the evidence when the disputed facts are material to the guideline calculation. The probation officer will consider documentary evidence, witness statements, evidence introduced at trial, plea colloquy statements, and submissions from the parties. The government typically submits affidavits or agency reports in support of disputed factual allegations; the defense may submit contrary affidavits or other proof. Where a defendant challenges a factual assertion, a hearing may be necessary if material facts are in dispute and the court cannot resolve the dispute on the record and affidavits alone.

At the sentencing hearing, the district court rules on unresolved objections. The court may accept, reject, or modify factual and guideline findings in the PSR, supplying reasons for its rulings. When the court finds facts by a preponderance of the evidence at sentencing, those facts inform the guideline calculation unless they would increase the statutory maximum or mandatory minimum imposed for the offense (in that narrow category the court’s power to find facts is constrained by constitutional allocation of factfinding). Once the court adopts final guideline calculations, those figures form the benchmark guide for the sentence unless the court explains a variance or departure.

The PSR also has practical consequences beyond guideline arithmetic. The narrative sections may be used by the court to evaluate the § 3553(a) factors, to assess risk and need for sentencing programs, and to make custodial designations or conditions of supervised release. Therefore careful management of PSR objections—timely, specific, and backed by evidence—is essential because unresolved or unchallenged assertions may become the basis for significant guideline enhancements or a heavier sentence.

# Relevant conduct under USSG § 1B1.3

Relevant conduct is the mechanism by which conduct beyond the offense of conviction is attributed to the defendant for the purpose of determining the offense level under the Sentencing Guidelines. USSG § 1B1.3 lays out the scope and limits of relevant conduct. The concept is broad in many contexts, and the analysis requires attention to several different categories identified in the provision: conduct that is part of the same course of conduct or common scheme or plan (including uncharged and acquitted conduct that meets the criteria), jointly undertaken criminal activity, and conduct attributable under specific definitions tied to the offense guideline.

For individual (single-defendant) offenses, relevant conduct typically includes all acts and omissions that were part of the same course of conduct or common scheme or plan as the offense of conviction, and any acts that were foreseeable in connection with jointly undertaken criminal activity. In drug distribution cases, for example, quantities delivered by coconspirators or amounts involved in the broader scheme may be counted if they are part of the same course of conduct or reasonably foreseeable and within the scope of the jointly undertaken activity.

Key components of the § 1B1.3 analysis are scope, foreseeability, and attribution. The “scope” inquiry asks whether the uncharged or additional acts were part of the same course of conduct or common scheme as the offense of conviction—i.e., were the acts sufficiently connected in time, purpose, and pattern to be treated as part of the same criminal operation. “Foreseeability” is typically used to attribute to an individual the acts of others in jointly undertaken activity: if a coconspirator’s conduct was reasonably foreseeable in furtherance of the jointly undertaken activity, it can be attributed. Attribution follows rules depending on whether the conduct was jointly undertaken or separate. The Guidelines instruct that when a defendant’s criminal liability is as a participant in a joint endeavor, the acts of coconspirators may be counted if they were within the scope and reasonably foreseeable and in furtherance of that endeavor.

Different offense guidelines provide specific instructions that refine or modify the general § 1B1.3 rules. For example, certain guidelines focus on the amounts involved in a fraud scheme, the number of victims, or the equivalency measures for valuable consideration. That means the sentencing court must consult the specific guideline governing the offense to determine how to apply relevant conduct rules.

Application of § 1B1.3 at sentencing often involves contested factual questions—whether particular acts were committed by the defendant, whether they were part of the same course of conduct, and whether coconspirators’ acts were foreseeable. The court resolves these disputes under the standard of proof applicable at sentencing (generally preponderance of the evidence), and then adjusts the guideline offense level accordingly. Because relevant conduct can have a substantial impact on the guideline range, careful attention to its elements, the required factual showing, and the permitted sources of evidence is essential.

# Standard of proof and evidentiary rules at sentencing

Sentencing proceedings differ from trials in their purpose, procedure, and rules of evidence. The applicable standards of proof and the degree to which formal evidentiary rules apply have important consequences for what the court may consider and how contested facts are resolved.

Standard of proof. The prevailing standard for proving facts at sentencing that affect the guideline calculation or the imposition of specific sentence enhancements is the preponderance of the evidence. That is, the party asserting the fact must persuade the court that it is more likely than not to be true. This standard applies to most factual findings relevant to sentencing, including relevant conduct determinations and many guideline enhancements. There are, however, constitutional constraints when a fact would increase the maximum statutory penalty authorized beyond what the jury’s verdict or the defendant’s plea established: those particular facts implicate the constitutional guarantees related to jury factfinding and the reasonable doubt standard. For instance, facts that would expose a defendant to a higher statutory maximum or a mandatory minimum not established by the jury verdict may require a jury determination or a plea admitting those facts. Outside those constitutional boundaries, sentencing judges may generally find facts by a preponderance.

Evidentiary rules. The Federal Rules of Evidence govern federal sentencing proceedings only to the extent the court elects to use them. Sentencing courts have broader discretion than juries at trials: they may consider reliable hearsay, uncontested statements in the PSR, testimony by witnesses, documentary evidence, plea statements, trial transcripts, and other forms of proof. The rationale is practical: sentencing courts need access to a full picture of the defendant and the offense to craft an appropriate sentence, and strict exclusionary rules would impede that task.

Nevertheless, courts exercise that discretion within bounds. Although hearsay is admissible, a court must be mindful of reliability. Parties frequently submit evidentiary materials (police reports, witness statements, certifications, business records, drug ledgers, expert reports). A sentencing court may accept such unsworn hearsay if it finds it reliable, but if the reliability of a critical factual dispute turns on credibility that cannot be resolved without live testimony and cross-examination, courts may hold a hearing or decline to consider the proffered evidence. The defense can object to the admission of unreliable hearsay and request a hearing under the rules of procedure and the court’s inherent authority.

Cross-examination, confrontation, and due process. Criminal defendants do not have a right to confront every witness whose statement is considered at sentencing in the same way they do at trial. The right of confrontation is primarily a trial right; sentencing courts may consider out-of-court statements where practical considerations and reliability concerns permit. If a defendant demonstrates that a party’s proffered material is unreliable or that denial of a full hearing would be fundamentally unfair under the circumstances, the court may be required to provide an opportunity for live testimony and cross-examination. Rarely, a due process problem may arise if the government intentionally denies defense counsel access to material evidence or if the only available evidence is unsubstantiated accusation.

Allocation of burdens. The party making a sentencing enhancement or asserting additional relevant conduct typically bears the burden of proof. Where the government bases a guideline adjustment on facts beyond the plea or verdict, it must prove those facts by a preponderance. Defense counsel bears the burden of persuading the court that the government’s proofs are insufficient or that an asserted enhancement is inapplicable. However, practical burdens are shaped also by what the PSR contains and whether the defendant timely objects. If the defendant fails to make a timely objection to a factual allegation in the PSR, many courts deem those allegations admitted for guideline calculation purposes, or at least treat them as unopposed. Parties should therefore pursue objections promptly and support them with evidence.

Limits on consideration of certain findings. Some categories of facts—such as prior convictions—are found through judicial notice or proof beyond a reasonable doubt as part of the criminal record. Others, such as relevant conduct that would raise a statutory minimum or maximum, raise constitutional constraints. The sentencing judge must identify the standard applied and ensure that material facts used to impose a sentence comply with both evidentiary norms and constitutional requirements.

# Departures vs. variances

Post-Guidelines sentencing doctrine recognizes two distinct mechanisms by which a court may impose a sentence different from the calculated guideline range: departures and variances. The distinction is important both doctrinally and procedurally.

Departures. A departure is a sentence outside the guideline range that is justified by the Guidelines themselves. The Guidelines Manual contains specified grounds for both upward and downward departures. These grounds may be general policy statements (e.g., “the court may depart from the applicable guideline range if the court finds an aggravating or mitigating circumstance of a kind, or to a degree, not adequately considered by the Sentencing Commission”), or they may be specific enumerated grounds (e.g., substantial assistance to authorities (Chapter 5K), aberrant behavior, diminished capacity, coercion or duress, certain extraordinary medical conditions, or post-offense rehabilitation in narrowly defined circumstances). To obtain a departure, the moving party must show that the specific departure ground applies and satisfies the thresholds and conditions stated in the Guidelines. The scope of permissible departures is defined by the Commission’s policy statements and commentary.

Departures are often governed by more detailed and particularized rules than variances. For example, the Guidelines may require a particular evidentiary showing or limit the extent of the departure. A departure that is properly authorized under the Guidelines must be clearly based on the relevant policy statement or specific guideline provision; otherwise the court cannot purport to rely on a departure theory that lacks a valid textual or policy basis within the Guidelines.

Variances. A variance is a sentence outside the guideline range justified by consideration of the statutory factors in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). Variances are not constrained by the specific departure grounds in the Guidelines; instead, they permit the sentencing court to consider the broader purposes and factors Congress specified for sentencing—such as the need to reflect the seriousness of the offense, to promote respect for the law, to provide just punishment, to afford adequate deterrence, and to protect the public—as well as individualized offender characteristics. Because variances invoke the statute rather than the Guidelines, their scope is broader and depends on judicial judgment about how the § 3553(a) factors apply to the particular case.

Procedural and practical differences. The choice between characterizing a non-guideline sentence as a departure or a variance has practical consequences. Departures require reference to specific guideline authority and usually more formalized application of Guidelines’ rules; they are reviewed on appeal against the standards that govern departures. Variances involve broader discretion and are reviewed for reasonableness in light of the § 3553(a) factors. Sentencing courts should clearly state whether a non-guideline sentence is a departure (and cite the specific guideline provision or policy statement), a variance under § 3553(a), or some combination of both. Failure to identify the basis for a sentence complicates appellate review and may render a sentence vulnerable to remand.

Substantively, both mechanisms can lead to the same numerical sentence, but the justifications and the route to appellate review differ. Defense counsel often prefer to frame relief as a variance because § 3553(a) permits wide-ranging consideration of individual circumstances; prosecutors may prefer departures where the Guidelines provide a narrow and enumerated path for relief (for example, when a defendant has provided substantial assistance, the government can file a motion under Chapter 5K, which triggers a potential departure). District courts must ensure that departures adhere to the Guidelines’ textual limits, while variances must be grounded in the statutory factors and supported by an individualized explanation.

# 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a) factors and sentencing purposes

The statutory architecture for federal sentencing is anchored in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a), which enumerates factors the court must consider in imposing a sentence and expresses the goals that sentences should serve. Understanding § 3553(a) is critical because it is the normative prescription guiding variances and the articulation of a sentence that is “sufficient, but not greater than necessary.”

Statutory factors. Section 3553(a) requires the sentencing court to consider:

1. The nature and circumstances of the offense and the history and characteristics of the defendant. This is the most fact-intensive of the factors and includes the seriousness of the offense, any mitigating or aggravating conduct, the defendant’s background (education, family circumstances, mental and physical condition, employment history), and the defendant’s role in the offense.

2. The need for the sentence imposed to reflect the seriousness of the offense, promote respect for the law, and provide just punishment for the offense. These goals emphasize proportionality and public censure.

3. The need to afford adequate deterrence to criminal conduct (both specific deterrence for the defendant and general deterrence for the public).

4. The need to protect the public from further crimes of the defendant.

5. The need to provide the defendant with needed educational or vocational training, medical care, or other correctional treatment in the most effective manner. This factor invites the court to consider rehabilitation and treatment needs when fashioning a sentence.

6. The kinds of sentences available and the applicable sentencing range established by the Sentencing Commission (i.e., the Guidelines), including any pertinent policy statements of the Commission.

7. Any pertinent policy statement by the Sentencing Commission, which the court must consider even though the Commission’s statements are advisory.

8. The need to avoid unwarranted sentence disparities among defendants with similar records who have been found guilty of similar conduct. This national perspective requires courts to be mindful of consistency and proportionality across cases.

9. The need to provide restitution to any victims of the offense.

Sentencing purposes. Section 3553(a) also directs attention to the core purposes of sentencing. There are five common purposes courts explicitly address: retribution/just punishment, deterrence, incapacitation (protecting the public), rehabilitation, and restitution/compensation. A defensible sentence will articulate how the chosen punishment serves these aims in the circumstances of the particular offender and offense.

Application and weighting of § 3553(a) factors. The statute does not prioritize the factors in a rigid hierarchy, and the relative weight the court gives each factor may vary with the case. The “nature and circumstances” factor typically receives substantial weight because it encapsulates individualized information about the offense and the offender. The guideline range also looms large because Congress directed the court to consider the Sentencing Commission’s ranges and policy statements. Thus, many courts start with the guideline range as an anchor and then examine whether § 3553(a) considerations warrant a sentence within that range or a variance.

A sentencing court seeking to justify a sentence differing from the guideline range should identify which § 3553(a) factors support the deviation and provide specific factual findings linking those factors to the sentence imposed. General assertions that a sentence outside the guidelines is necessary are insufficient; courts should explain how, for instance, a particular history of abuse, a medical condition, or extraordinary family circumstances affect the need for deterrence, rehabilitation, or proportionality.

Avoiding unwarranted disparities and recognizing distinctions. § 3553(a)(6) expresses a national concern with unwarranted disparities. Courts should strive for consistency with like cases, yet they must also recognize distinctions attributable to unique offense characteristics or personal history that justify different sentences. A sentence that departs substantially from the typical range in similar cases must have articulated reasons that reconcile the difference with the statutory goal of avoiding unwarranted disparities.

Special statutory directives. In addition to § 3553(a), sentencing courts must consider any statutory directives specific to the offense—mandatory minimum sentences, statutory maximums, available alternatives (e.g., probation, fines, restitution), and special sentencing provisions such as mandatory restitution statutes. Sentencing must be consistent with statutory constraints.

Explaining the sentence. A critical procedural requirement is that the sentencing court explain its decision sufficiently to permit meaningful appellate review. The explanation typically takes the form of a colloquy at sentencing and a written statement of reasons that identifies the calculated guideline range, recites any agreed or found facts that materially affected that calculation, states which § 3553(a) factors were determinative, and explains any departure or variance. This explanation accomplishes three goals: it grounds the sentence in law and fact, demonstrates that the sentencing authority exercised its discretion informedly, and provides a record for appellate review of both procedural and substantive reasonableness.

Conclusion

Federal sentencing requires integration of guideline calculation, evidentiary assessment, statutory consideration, and reasoned judicial explanation. The Guidelines remain an indispensable starting point, but courts exercise broad discretion under § 3553(a) to tailor a sentence to the offender and offense. The PSR and the parties’ objections frame the factual record, while USSG § 1B1.3 governs how conduct beyond the immediate offense is attributed. Sentencing factfinding typically uses the preponderance standard and allows consideration of reliable hearsay, yet constitutional limits constrain judge-found facts that increase statutory penalties. Understanding the distinction between departures (grounded in the Guidelines) and variances (grounded in § 3553(a)) is crucial to litigating and reviewing non-guideline sentences. Finally, comprehensive and specific judicial explanation is essential for a sentence to survive appellate scrutiny for reasonableness. Mastery of these doctrinal building blocks will equip the student to follow and critique the legal reasoning in federal sentencing opinions.
