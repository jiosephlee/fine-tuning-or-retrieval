Introduction

United States Sentencing Guideline § 2K2.1 governs the base offense level and many specific offense characteristics for most federal offenses involving firearms, destructive devices, and certain other weapons. Before reading judicial opinions that apply § 2K2.1, a student should understand how the guideline determines the starting point for sentencing, how prior convictions and weapon types affect the base level, how specific offense characteristics (SOCs) operate to increase offense levels, and how the guideline interacts with relevant conduct principles and cross-references to other law (including the National Firearms Act (NFA), 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a)). Equally important are the factual and legal tests courts use to decide whether enhancements apply, and the standard of proof the sentencing court must use. This chapter explains those doctrinal rules and how courts reason through typical fact patterns.

# Base offense levels and prior convictions affecting § 2K2.1

The guideline establishes different base offense levels in § 2K2.1 depending on the nature of the offense conduct and the defendant’s criminal history in certain contexts. The principal distinction is between simple possession offenses and more serious transfer/trafficking or possession after a disqualifying conviction.

Typical structure of base offense levels

In broad terms, § 2K2.1 provides a sliding scale of base offense levels. The lowest base levels apply when a defendant merely possesses a firearm without aggravating circumstances; higher base levels apply when the defendant has certain prior convictions (e.g., a felony that is a crime of violence or a controlled substance offense), when the offense involved trafficking, or when the offense involved a semiautomatic firearm capable of accepting a large-capacity magazine (depending on guideline text in effect at the time). A sentencing court first determines which statutory subsection of § 2K2.1 governs the defendant’s conduct (the "applicable provision" within the guideline) and then reads the base offense level from the corresponding table or paragraph. The guideline language often sets a baseline offense level and then provides upward adjustments for aggravating factors.

How prior convictions change the base offense level

One of the most significant drivers of the base offense level is whether the defendant was previously convicted of a felony that prohibits firearm possession. Under § 2K2.1, prior convictions can elevate base offense levels into higher starting ranges. The guideline commonly distinguishes, for example, between (A) defendants with one or more prior convictions for crimes of violence or drug trafficking offenses, and (B) defendants with no such prior convictions. The presence of a qualifying prior conviction typically raises the base offense level considerably because the Legislature and Commission view repeat or previously disqualified possessors as higher risk.

Determining what counts as a prior conviction

Sentencing courts look to the definition of "prior sentence" and "conviction" in the Guidelines Manual and to pertinent statutory definitions to decide whether a past adjudication qualifies. The timing and nature of the prior conviction matter: it generally must be a final conviction (post-adjudication) and countable under the Criminal History rules unless an exception applies. Where statutes differ in labeling (e.g., a juvenile adjudication or a foreign conviction), courts assess whether those adjudications are treated as convictions for guideline purposes. The sentencing court also considers whether the prior offense is categorized as a "crime of violence" or a "controlled substance offense" for purposes of applying the higher base level.

Procedural posture and allocation of fact-finding

When the parties dispute whether a qualifying prior conviction exists or was properly counted, the guideline requires the court to make factual findings. After the Supreme Court’s advisory-Guidelines jurisprudence, sentencing courts may find such facts by a preponderance of the evidence unless the facts were admitted by the defendant or found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt in the underlying criminal proceeding. The procedural posture—whether the contested facts were part of the charges, whether they were admitted in a plea agreement, and whether they are sentencing-only facts—affects the allocation of burdens and the degree of deference to plea colloquies.

# Enhancement for National Firearms Act weapons (cross-reference to 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a))

The Sentencing Guidelines treat certain NFA-regulated firearms and devices as aggravating because of their destructive capacity or because federal law already imposes special regulatory treatment and penalties.

What are NFA weapons?

The National Firearms Act, 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a), defines several categories of weapons regulated under the NFA. These include, among others, "machineguns," "short-barreled rifles," "short-barreled shotguns," "destructive devices" (which include certain bombs, grenades, and other explosive devices, as well as large-bore weapons), and certain other items such as silencers/suppressors. The NFA’s definitional provisions focus on the physical characteristics of the weapon and, for destructive devices, their intended use and construction.

Guideline enhancement that references § 5845(a)

Section 2K2.1 includes specific offense characteristic enhancements when the offense involves an NFA weapon as defined by 26 U.S.C. § 5845(a). To apply the enhancement, the sentencing court must find that the firearm or device involved falls squarely within an NFA category. That determination is generally a factual one—whether the accused possessed or trafficked a device that meets the statutory definition (e.g., the barrel length of a rifle is under the statutory threshold, or the device has the components and design of a silencer).

Application and evidentiary considerations

The court typically looks at the physical characteristics and any expert testimony or manufacturer's specifications to determine whether the device falls within § 5845(a). If the defendant argues the device is nonfunctional or lacks the required characteristics, the court evaluates conflicting evidence under the preponderance standard (again, unless the fact was charged and found beyond a reasonable doubt or admitted). The presence of an NFA weapon almost always produces a discrete, higher SOC because the device’s classification under federal tax/registration law signals a greater potential for harm and regulatory concern.

# Altered or obliterated serial number enhancement

The guidelines provide an enhancement when a firearm has an altered, removed, or obliterated serial number. This enhancement reflects the law’s concern about the defendant’s intent to make the firearm difficult to trace.

Elements and legal tests

To invoke the serial number enhancement, the government must prove by the applicable sentencing standard that the firearm’s identifying number was knowingly altered, obliterated, or removed. The core elements are (1) the firearm lacked an original, legible serial number (or the serial number showed signs it had been altered/obliterated), and (2) the defendant had the requisite mental state—typically knowledge of the condition of the serial number. Courts often consider whether the defendant intentionally removed or attempted to remove the number, or whether the nature of the firearm’s acquisition suggests an intent to conceal.

Proof and typical factual inquiries

Proof generally comes from forensic examination, photographs, inventory reports, law enforcement testimony, and sometimes the defendant’s statements. A crucial factual inquiry is whether the number was removed before or after the defendant’s possession; if removed before, courts will examine whether the defendant knew about the alteration. When multiple parties have access to the weapon, the court must determine which actor is responsible for the alteration or who had the requisite knowledge—a classic issue of factual causation and attribution under the Guidelines.

Interaction with intent and mens rea doctrines

Because the enhancement turns on knowledge, courts scrutinize contextual evidence indicating awareness—statements such as "I took the number off," prior purchases of unpainted or re-serialized firearms, or behavior consistent with trying to avoid tracing (packaging, attempts to sell without paperwork). When a defendant claims lack of knowledge, the sentencing inquiry resembles an evidentiary trial on a discrete factual matter: was the evidence sufficient to support a finding that the defendant knew the serial number was removed?

# Number of firearms and other specific offense characteristics

The Guidelines increase offense levels based on the quantity of firearms involved and other enumerated facts that signal greater culpability—such as possession of a firearm in connection with a drug trafficking offense or possession of a stolen firearm. Understanding how courts count firearms and apply those SOCs is essential.

Counting firearms

Section 2K2.1 typically uses the total number of firearms involved in the offense to determine part of the offense level. Courts count each weapon that falls within the guideline's definition of "firearm" and that is attributable to the defendant as part of the offense conduct. The definition of "firearm" used for counting mirrors federal statutes in many respects (firearms designed to expel a projectile by explosive force, etc.), and courts sometimes consult statutory definitions when ambiguity exists.

Aggregating firearms for a single course of conduct

Multiple firearms recovered at a single scene or in a single course of criminal activity are usually aggregated. The guideline focuses on whether the multiple weapons were part of the same overall criminal scheme—possession in the same transaction or reasonably attributable to the same criminal intent means they will be counted together. Conversely, weapons seized in entirely separate and temporally distant events may be treated as separate counts or separate offenses for grouping under §3D1. This assessment depends heavily on the facts and timing.

Other SOCs tied to firearm characteristics or conduct

Beyond the raw number of firearms, § 2K2.1 contains many SOCs that raise the offense level. Examples include possession of a stolen firearm, possession or use in connection with another felony offense (discussed in a later section), possession of a firearm with an obliterated serial number, and possession of ammunition in connection with other misconduct. Each of these SOCs requires a factual finding that the element or condition is present and attributable to the defendant.

Procedural posture and factual findings

As with other enhancements, the court must find the facts that trigger a quantity-based adjustment by a preponderance of the evidence unless the facts were proven or admitted earlier. When the number of firearms is disputed, documentation (seizure reports, inventory, photos) and chain-of-custody evidence become central. The government bears the burden of establishing by a preponderance that each weapon should be counted and that the SOCs are present.

# USSG § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B): “in connection with another felony offense”

One of the most litigated SOCs in firearms sentencing is the enhancement for possession "in connection with another felony offense." Subparagraphs within § 2K2.1 address increases when a firearm is possessed in relation to a drug trafficking offense or another felony; § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) commonly refers to conduct where the firearm is connected with another felony offense (other than a drug trafficking offense or a crime of violence specified elsewhere).

Meaning of "in connection with"

The phrase "in connection with" signals a relationship between the firearm possession and a separate felony offense. The guideline does not require that the firearm be used in the other felony; rather, it requires a sufficient nexus such that the firearm facilitated, had the potential to facilitate, or otherwise was related to the commission or furtherance of the other felony. Courts interpret the nexus requirement using various tests that assess temporal proximity, spatial proximity, and the functional relationship between the firearm and the underlying felony.

Common judicial tests for nexus

Courts typically consider several factors when deciding whether possession was "in connection with" another felony:

1. Temporal proximity: Were the firearm possession and the other felony contemporaneous, or at least close in time? If the firearm was present during the commission of the felony or reasonably available to facilitate it, this supports the enhancement.

2. Spatial proximity: Were the firearm and the other felony offense co-located—e.g., the firearm was at the scene of a drug transaction, or stored where drugs were sold? Physical accessibility is a strong indicator of connection.

3. Functional nexus: Did the firearm facilitate the felony or have the potential to do so? Courts look at whether the firearm provided protection for the criminal activity, enabled the offender to commit the offense, or was intended to be used for that purpose.

4. Purpose or intent: The defendant’s intent or purpose for possessing the firearm—was it to further the other felony? Statements, context, and the nature of the possession help determine purpose.

No single factor is dispositive; courts weigh the totality of circumstances. Importantly, merely incidental or coincidental possession of a firearm by a person who commits an independent felony may not satisfy the "in connection with" requirement unless a sufficient nexus is shown.

Examples of application

Possession of a firearm next to drugs during a drug sale, with evidence that the firearm was usable to intimidate purchasers, would typically satisfy the enhancement. Possession of a firearm in the same residence where a separate and unrelated burglary had occurred months earlier, with no evidence that the firearm was used or intended to be used to facilitate the burglary, might not.

Burden of proof and procedural implications

The government bears the burden to prove the requisite connection by a preponderance of the evidence unless the facts were pleaded or admitted. Because this SOC can substantially increase the offense level, the sentencing court carefully explains its factual findings on the record when applying it. When the defendant had possession of the firearm long before or long after the other felony, the court scrutinizes the temporal and causal links more closely.

# Application Note 14 (facilitation and potential to facilitate)

Application Note 14 to § 2K2.1 elaborates on the "in connection with" language by explaining how courts should treat firearms that facilitate, or have the potential to facilitate, other offenses. Understanding this Note helps clarify the sometimes amorphous "nexus" inquiry.

Core points of Application Note 14

Although the precise wording of the Note may evolve across guideline editions, its doctrinal function is to instruct courts that a firearm need not actually be used in the commission of another felony to trigger the enhancement; it is sufficient that the weapon facilitated or had the potential to facilitate the other felony. The Note commonly lists examples of conduct that support the conclusion that a firearm facilitated another offense: possession at the scene of a drug transaction, possession to protect a drug stash, or placement of firearms in a vehicle used for transporting contraband.

Potential to facilitate versus actual facilitation

Application Note 14 clarifies that "potential to facilitate" is a lower threshold than actual use: the firearm need only be positioned or possessed in a manner that could assist in the other felony. For example, a weapon in the trunk of a car used to transport stolen goods could facilitate the crime by deterring interference or enabling escape, even if never fired. Courts therefore examine whether the firearm was sufficiently accessible and intended for use in connection with the other offense.

Examples, counterexamples, and contextual analysis

The Note generally provides positive examples—but the absence of those facts does not preclude the enhancement if other evidence shows the potential to facilitate. Conversely, the presence of a firearm in a home where an independent felony unrelated in time or purpose occurred, without evidence that the firearm was for the felony, may not suffice. The Note encourages fact-specific analysis rather than mechanical application.

Evidentiary and mens rea considerations

Because the Note contemplates "potential" facilitation, courts often consider circumstantial evidence (e.g., the location of the weapon, the presence of contraband, the defendant’s statements) to infer the necessary nexus. As with other SOCs, the government bears the preponderance burden. When the defendant contests the finding, the sentencing court details the facts supporting the facilitation inference, often citing Application Note 14 examples.

# Interaction with relevant conduct

Sentencing under the Guidelines requires that the court account for "relevant conduct"—criminal acts that are sufficiently connected to the offense of conviction to be considered when calculating the offense level and criminal history. Sections 1B1.3 and related portions of the Guidelines govern this interaction, and they are crucial for understanding how § 2K2.1 enhancements can be applied based on conduct not charged in the conviction count.

Relevant conduct basics

Relevant conduct encompasses acts and omissions that are part of the same course of conduct or common scheme or plan as the offense of conviction, or that were within the scope of jointly undertaken criminal activity when a co-conspirator's acts are at issue. The court must determine which uncharged conduct is part of the appropriate base for sentencing and thus can trigger SOCs. This determination is both legal (applying the definitions and tests in § 1B1.3) and factual (weighing proof of the conduct).

How relevant conduct affects § 2K2.1 calculations

When a defendant is convicted under § 2K2.1 for a discrete firearm offense, the sentencing judge may consider other conduct that is relevant under § 1B1.3 to determine the applicable base offense level and any SOCs. For example, firearms possessed in the same course of conduct but not charged may be counted for the quantity-based SOCs; firearms used to facilitate an uncharged drug offense that is relevant conduct may trigger the "in connection with" enhancement; or a defendant’s prior acts could qualify as relevant conduct leading to the application of a higher base level due to a course of conduct standard.

Nexus between relevant conduct and SOCs

A common point of contention is whether uncharged activity can create the factual nexus necessary for a SOC. Under § 1B1.3, relevant conduct that is proven by a preponderance of the evidence may be used in computing the guideline range. Thus, the sentencing court can enhance under § 2K2.1 based on conduct that was not part of the indictment—so long as that conduct satisfies the relevant-conduct rules and the particular SOC’s nexus requirement (for example, that the firearm facilitated the uncharged felony). The court’s findings on such matters should be explicit because they materially affect the guideline range.

Limits and defenses

There are limits. Conduct cannot be included as relevant if it is too remote, temporally separated without a continuing scheme, or not part of a common plan. Defendants often argue that uncharged acts are insufficiently connected to the offense of conviction or that including them violates notice and fair-play concerns. The guideline framework allows such conduct to be considered, but constitutional fair notice concerns sometimes constrain how heavily courts rely on surprising, extraneous conduct to impose severe enhancements—especially when the conduct is only marginally related.

Procedural posture: objections, proof, and the sentencing record

Because relevant conduct can derive from information outside the indictment (e.g., police reports, plea colloquies, or other case exhibits), defendants commonly object to reliance on particular facts. The sentencing hearing is the event for testing such evidence: parties may present witnesses, stipulate facts, or rely on exhibits. The court then makes findings by a preponderance of the evidence. If the defendant disputes these findings, appellate review assesses whether the facts were supported and whether the application of the guideline was procedurally correct.

# Concluding integration: how courts apply rules to facts and adjust for posture

Applying § 2K2.1 and its SOCs is primarily a fact-driven exercise constrained by doctrinal rules: identify the correct guideline subsection and base offense level; determine which SOCs apply by examining the statutory and guideline definitions (e.g., NFA weapon, altered serial number, stolen firearm); assess whether firearms are aggregated for quantity-based adjustments; and determine whether any uncharged conduct counts as relevant conduct that can trigger enhancements (using § 1B1.3 and Application Note 14). At each step, the court must make factual findings, typically by a preponderance of the evidence unless admitted by a plea or previously proven to a jury.

Sentencing courts must explain how contested facts were proved and why a particular nexus or enhancement applies. Procedural posture—whether facts are contested, whether the defendant admitted them, and whether they were part of the charging instrument—shapes the evidentiary route and the allocation of burdens. For law students, mastering § 2K2.1 means appreciating both the textual elements of each enhancement and the practical evidentiary and factual inquiries courts undertake when those textual triggers are disputed. This doctrinal groundwork prepares the reader to follow and evaluate judicial opinions that apply § 2K2.1 in concrete cases.
