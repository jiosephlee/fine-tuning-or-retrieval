Introduction

Impeachment by prior inconsistent statements and the authentication of extrinsic evidence are central tools in trial advocacy and judicial factfinding. They permit parties to challenge witness credibility, to introduce documentary or recorded materials that contradict or refresh testimony, and—when tightly constrained—occasionally to admit prior statements substantively as evidence of the truth of the matter asserted. The relevant rules (particularly Rules 613, 801(d)(1)(A), 901, and 612 of the Federal Rules of Evidence, and analogous state rules) balance competing interests: allowing cross-examination and truth-seeking, protecting against unfair surprise or manipulation of evidence, and enforcing hearsay doctrine where necessary. This chapter explains the doctrinal framework that governs the use of prior inconsistent statements and the authentication of extrinsic materials used for impeachment. It focuses on when and how such evidence may be shown to the jury, the foundation a proponent must lay, the limits on substantive use, and how refreshing recollection operates in contrast to impeachment.

# Rule 613(b): foundation and fairness requirements

Rule 613 governs the use of a witness’s prior statements to impeach the witness. Subdivision (a) requires that when a party uses a witness’s prior statement during examination, the party need not show the statement to the witness to confront them; but subdivision (b) establishes procedural protections when extrinsic evidence of the prior statement will be admitted.

Rule 613(b) provides that when a party intends to admit extrinsic evidence of a witness’s prior inconsistent statement, the party must, on request, show the witness the statement and give the witness an opportunity to explain or deny it. The rule furthers two principal aims: (1) fairness to the witness, who should have an opportunity to respond to a potentially damaging alleged inconsistency; and (2) protecting the jury from speculation by ensuring the factfinder hears the witness’s explanation or denial when available.

What constitutes adequate foundation under Rule 613(b) depends on timing and purpose. A proponent usually follows these steps: (1) identify the prior statement and confront the witness with its substance or text; (2) establish the witness heard, read, or otherwise was exposed to that formulation (so the witness knows what is being alleged); and (3) give the witness an opportunity to accept, deny, explain, or clarify the prior statement. The request to show the witness the statement is not limited to written documents; it can include recorded statements or testimony from earlier proceedings. If defense counsel requests production, the proponent must show the witness the statement prior to admitting extrinsic evidence, unless the statement is already in evidence or its text has been put before the witness on the record.

Rule 613(b) is not merely a technicality. Courts assess whether failure to comply resulted in unfair surprise or prejudice. If the proponent deliberately withholds a statement to disadvantage a witness or the opponent, a court may exclude extrinsic evidence or grant a limiting instruction. Conversely, inadvertent noncompliance—when timely opportunity to respond exists—may be cured if the proponent later provides the statement and the witness can address it.

Timing is important. The requirement to show the statement is often triggered when the proponent seeks to introduce an extrinsic exhibit (e.g., a written or recorded prior statement) that contains the inconsistency. If the proponent uses impeachment via cross-examination (e.g., reading the inconsistent statement aloud) but does not intend to offer the document itself into evidence, the court still may require disclosure on request. The key equitable principle is that the witness should not be ambushed by a document they cannot address.

Finally, Rule 613(b) interacts with other rules that protect against unfair prejudice. A judge may preclude extrinsic impeachment under Rule 403 if the prejudicial effect substantially outweighs probative value—an important check when the prior statement is collateral, confusing, or cumulative.

# When extrinsic evidence of a prior statement is admissible

Extrinsic evidence means presenting a document, recording, or third-party testimony showing the prior statement, rather than relying solely on the witness’s acknowledgment on the stand. Extrinsic impeachment is useful when the witness refuses to admit the prior inconsistent statement or claims lack of recollection.

The admissibility of extrinsic evidence to prove a prior inconsistent statement is subject to several constraints.

First, the statement must be inconsistent with the witness’s in-court testimony. Courts will not permit extrinsic contradiction based on minor variation that is immaterial to the facts at issue. The inconsistency must be genuine and relevant to credibility or a material issue in the case.

Second, extrinsic evidence is ordinarily allowed only if the witness is given an opportunity to explain or deny the prior statement. This requirement is imposed both by the fairness principle in Rule 613(b) and common-law confrontation practice. The proponent must confront the witness with the alleged prior statement—either by reading it to the witness or showing it to the witness—so the witness can reconcile the two versions. If the witness admits the inconsistency, extrinsic evidence may still be admissible to prove the underlying prior statement for impeachment and sometimes for substantive purposes if other conditions are met.

Third, courts restrict extrinsic impeachment when the subject matter is collateral—that is, when contradiction would not be material to the issues at trial. The classic rule is that extrinsic evidence is not admissible to impeach on collateral matters: if the prior statement concerns an unimportant or peripheral fact unrelated to the case’s substantive issues or to the witness’s credibility in a meaningful way, the court may prohibit extrinsic proof. The rationale is to avoid mini-trials on tangential matters and to preserve juror resources. Whether a matter is collateral is often a question of degree: if the prior statement touches on something that bears on the witness’s overall credibility, bias, or recollection, extrinsic evidence is more likely to be admitted.

Fourth, procedural posture matters. During cross-examination, a party may impeach by asking the witness about the prior statement; if the witness denies it or claims no memory, extrinsic evidence is typically admissible to prove the statement. If the witness admits the prior statement, traditional rules sometimes allow the extrinsic evidence nonetheless, but courts weigh whether admission makes the extrinsic proof unnecessary or cumulative.

Fifth, extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement is inadmissible if its admission would violate other evidentiary rules—most notably hearsay prohibitions—unless an exception or a rule makes the statement nonhearsay. For example, recordings or statements offered only to show inconsistency are not being offered for the truth of the matter asserted and thus are used for impeachment; they are not hearsay for that limited purpose. However, extrinsic evidence that is itself hearsay or contains hearsay layers may require independent foundational proof or exception applicability before it may be admitted, even for impeachment.

Finally, judges exercise discretion. Even when the procedural and foundational preconditions are met, a court may exclude extrinsic impeachment under Rule 403 or for unfair surprise if its probative value is substantially outweighed by prejudicial or confusing effects.

# Rule 901 authentication requirements

Rule 901 requires that the proponent of evidence produce sufficient evidence to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it is. Rule 901(a) sets the general standard: the proponent must introduce evidence sufficient to permit a reasonable juror to conclude the exhibit is genuine. The standard is not absolute certainty; rather, the proponent must make a prima facie showing of authenticity. Once made, this showing shifts the issue to the jury, unless the court decides as a matter of law that authentication is lacking.

Common methods of authentication listed in Rule 901(b) and developed in case law include direct testimony by a witness with knowledge that the item is what it is claimed to be (for example, "I taped this call" or "I wrote this letter"), distinctive characteristics (handwriting, content, internal references) coupled with other circumstances, comparison by an expert or trier of fact with an authenticated exemplar, evidence that a document came from a particular source (chain of custody), email headers and metadata for electronic communications, or voice identification by someone familiar with the speaker’s voice.

Several practical points arise in applying Rule 901.

Burden of production and persuasion. The proponent carries the burden of producing sufficient evidence of authenticity to get the item before the jury, typically by a preponderance of the evidence standard when the court resolves preliminary questions under Rule 104(a). Many courts, however, treat the question of authenticity as one of conditional relevance: the exhibit is admissible if the court finds sufficient evidence to support a jury finding that it is authentic, leaving the ultimate resolution to the jury. The proponent need not disprove all possible explanations of forgery or tampering; it must establish a plausible chain from creation to possession in a way that makes the item likely genuine.

Chain of custody. For physical evidence and many documents, a chain of custody is often necessary to demonstrate that the evidence has not been altered, substituted, or contaminated. Chain of custody testimony describes how the item was handled from its origin to the courtroom, focusing on measures taken to preserve integrity, such as sealing, logging, or continuous supervision. The sufficiency of chain of custody evidence depends on the item’s nature and the risk of tampering. Courts balance the need for protection against the burdens of producing perfect custody records.

Documentary authentication. For writings, testimony that the author made the document or that the document bears the author’s signature suffices. Where direct testimony is unavailable, authentication may rest on distinctive characteristics, corroborating circumstances (e.g., an internal reference to contemporaneous events), or expert handwriting comparison.

Digital evidence. Electronic records raise special issues: metadata, digital signatures, server logs, and IP address records are commonly employed to authenticate emails, chat logs, social media content, and digital photographs. Authentication may require testimony explaining how the data was generated, preserved, and retrieved, and the significance of technical indicators (timestamps, headers). Courts recognize that digital content can be easily manipulated; therefore, stronger foundational proof is often necessary for such exhibits.

Voice and video. Authentication of recorded statements generally requires testimony from someone who recognizes the voice or from the person who recorded the communication and can attest to the recording’s identity and continuity. For video, testimony may be needed to link the participants and context, to confirm lack of editing, or to explain the video’s source.

Business-records and public-records foundations. For categories of documents that the rules elsewhere treat as self-authenticating or presumptively trustworthy (e.g., certain public records, certified records), the proponent must still satisfy statutory or rule-based requirements—often by producing a custodian’s certificate or testimony—though the formalities are lighter than for other items.

Conditional relevance and trial procedure. Under Rule 104(b), when the relevance of evidence depends on the existence of a fact (such as a document’s authenticity), the court may admit the evidence for the jury’s consideration if there is sufficient evidence to support a finding that the condition is satisfied. The court’s ruling is interlocutory and may be revisited as the trial record develops.

# Police reports and third-party writings: hearsay and foundation issues

Police reports and writings prepared by third parties frequently arise in impeachment contexts. They pose two distinct doctrinal problems: hearsay (admission for the truth of the matter asserted) and authentication/foundation for admission.

Hearsay concerns. Police reports normally contain out-of-court statements—observations, witness reports, admissions, or conclusions—offered to prove the truth asserted. As such, they are hearsay and are inadmissible unless an exception or exclusion applies. Several exceptions may matter in practice.

First, parts of a police report may be admissible as nonhearsay when offered only to show inconsistency between the report and a witness’s trial testimony—i.e., for impeachment rather than for the truth of the matters asserted. In that limited use, the hearsay rule does not bar admission because the report is not being offered for its substantive content. Nonetheless, courts are careful: if the proponent attempts to slip the report’s substantive assertions to the jury under the guise of impeachment, the court must gatekeep to ensure the jury understands the limited purpose.

Second, some portions of a police report may fall within hearsay exceptions. Business-records hearsay exception can apply where the officer’s report records regular, contemporaneous activities of a government entity and was made near the time of observation in the course of a regularly conducted activity, with appropriate foundation. Public records exceptions may permit the admission of records setting out activities of the office or factual findings from a legally authorized investigation, subject to limitations (e.g., investigatory findings often are excluded when they reflect opinions or conclusions unless they meet exception criteria).

Third, statements by an opposing party contained in a police report may be admissible as party admissions. If an entry documents a statement by the defendant or another party-adverse declarant, that part is not hearsay and may be used substantively, provided the proponent can authenticate the record as accurately reflecting that party’s statement. Caution is necessary because the report’s compiler must accurately attribute the words to the party.

Authentication and foundation. Even for impeachment use, the proponent must authenticate a police report to show that it is what it purports to be: a report made by a given officer, at a given time, describing a specific event. Authentication often requires testimony from the officer who prepared the report or from a custodian of records who can identify the file and explain the office’s recordkeeping practices. Where the author is unavailable, authentication may rely on distinctive characteristics, testimony about department practices, or corroborating testimony that the report was kept in the regular course of business.

If the report contains multiple layers of statements or hearsay within hearsay, each layer must meet an exception or exclusion if used substantively. Courts scrutinize police reports closely because they can include police conclusions, witness hearsay, and summaries that risk usurping the jury’s role.

Third-party writings more generally present similar issues. A letter, email, or diary entry written by a non-party who is unavailable may be admissible against a party if it qualifies as a statement of a party-opponent or if an exception applies (e.g., former testimony, dying declaration). Where the document is offered for impeachment only, authenticity must be established, and the content may often be considered nonhearsay for that limited purpose. But as always, courts must be vigilant against using impeachment to smuggle substantive hearsay before the jury.

# Substantive use under Rule 801(d)(1)(A) vs. impeachment-only use

A key distinction in treating prior inconsistent statements is between using them merely to impeach a witness’s credibility and using them substantively as evidence of the truth of what they assert. Rule 801(d)(1)(A) is a critical gateway for substantive use.

Rule 801(d)(1)(A) treats certain prior inconsistent statements as nonhearsay when the declarant testifies and is subject to cross-examination about the prior statement and the statement is inconsistent with the declarant's in-court testimony. However, the rule narrows substantive admissibility: not all prior inconsistent statements are treated as nonhearsay for their truth; the rule requires that the prior inconsistent statement have been given under oath in a formal prior proceeding (in many formulations, at a deposition or prior hearing), depending on the jurisdiction’s version of the rule.

In the Federal Rules, Rule 801(d)(1)(A) provides that a prior statement by a witness is not hearsay if the declarant testifies at the current trial, is subject to cross-examination about the prior statement, and the prior statement is inconsistent with the declarant’s testimony and was given under penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing, or other proceeding or in a deposition. In other words, to admit a prior inconsistent statement for its truth (substantively), the proponent must satisfy these formalities: the witness is testifying at trial, the prior statement was under oath in a prior official proceeding or deposition, and it is inconsistent with the current testimony.

By contrast, prior inconsistent statements not meeting these formalities are typically admissible only for impeachment, not for their substantive truth. That means they may be used to persuade the jury that the witness should be disbelieved or that the witness is unreliable, but they may not be used as independent proof of the facts the prior statement asserts. The rationale is that statements made out of court, without oath and without the opportunity for cross-examination at the time, retain hearsay dangers.

Practical consequences. Before a court will allow substantive use under Rule 801(d)(1)(A), the proponent must establish the predicate facts: production of the prior statement, proof it was given under oath (e.g., a transcript of a deposition or a transcript of prior testimony), and the witness’s present testimony and availability for cross-examination. If these predicates are met, the prior statement is admissible as nonhearsay and the jury may consider it for its truth. If not, the proponent may still be able to admit the prior statement extrinsically for impeachment purposes under Rule 613, subject to the limitations discussed earlier.

Interplay with Rule 403 and cross-examination. Even when a prior inconsistent statement qualifies under Rule 801(d)(1)(A), a court retains discretion to exclude it if its probative value is substantially outweighed by danger of unfair prejudice, confusion, or undue delay. Additionally, courts ensure the defendant or the witness has adequate opportunity for cross-examination to test the prior statement’s reliability.

Jurisdictional variations. Some jurisdictions treat a broader category of prior inconsistent statements as substantive if they are recorded contemporaneously (recorded recollection) or if the declarant was available for cross-examination at another stage. State evidentiary rules and interpretations vary; a lawyer must check local authority.

# Refreshing recollection vs. impeachment

Refreshing recollection and impeachment sometimes overlap but are distinct doctrines with different procedures and consequences.

Refreshing recollection is governed by rules like Rule 612. When a witness’s memory is incomplete or lapses, the proponent may use a writing or object to jog the witness’s present recollection. The foundational premise is that the document is not being offered as substantive evidence; rather, it is a memory aid. After refreshing, the witness’s testimony—if recollection is restored—becomes evidence. If the witness cannot recollect even after refreshing, the writing may be admissible as a recorded recollection only under the recorded-recollection hearsay exception (Rule 803(5)), which allows admission if the writing was made when the matter was fresh and accurately reflects the witness’s knowledge.

Procedural aspects of refreshing recollection. The proponent may show the writing to the witness outside the jury’s presence or in front of the jury. Courts commonly permit private review to encourage candid attempts at recollection, but defense counsel typically may request that the document be shown to the jury for impeachment purposes or seek production under Rule 612(c), which gives the adverse party certain rights to inspect and cross-examine concerning the document. If the writer’s recollection is refreshed and the witness testifies from memory, the document itself generally is not admitted into evidence unless offered under an exception.

Impeachment by prior inconsistent statement, by contrast, seeks to show that the witness previously said something inconsistent with present testimony, thereby undermining credibility. When a witness denies having made the prior statement or claims lack of recollection, impeachment techniques may include; (1) confrontation on the stand; (2) use of extrinsic evidence to prove the prior statement is genuine; or (3) introduction of a writing into evidence. The prior statement can be used only to impeach unless it meets Rule 801(d)(1)(A)’s criteria for nonhearsay substantive use.

Key distinctions and tactical considerations. The key differences are the purpose and use: refreshing recollection aims to produce accurate testimony by restoring memory, whereas impeachment aims to reduce the credibility of testimony. When an attorney shows a witness a prior statement, the tactic may serve either purpose. Courts examine the proponent’s purpose and the subsequent use of the document. If the witness’s memory is refreshed and the witness then testifies consistently with the prior writing, the writing is not used to contradict and impeach; rather, it functions as a memory aid. If the witness persists in testifying differently, the opponent may use the writing extrinsically to impeach, subject to Rule 613(b) safeguards.

Adversary rights and production. Rule 612(c) gives the adverse party rights when a writing is used to refresh memory: the adverse party may inspect the item, cross-examine the witness about it, and introduce relevant portions into evidence. Courts balance protecting the witness’s chance to recollect with the opposing party’s right to test and, if appropriate, to use the document for impeachment.

Recorded recollection exception. If a witness’s memory cannot be refreshed and the writing was made when the matter was fresh and reflects the witness’s knowledge accurately, the writing may be admitted as a recorded recollection under the hearsay exception. That allows the document to be read into evidence, but generally the writing itself is not received as an exhibit unless offered by the adverse party.

Practical interplay. A savvy advocate must choose whether to use a document to refresh a witness’s memory or to impeach. The choice affects admissibility and tactical outcomes: a successful refresh can produce favorable testimony without admitting the writing into evidence; impeachment may exclude the writing from substantive consideration unless it meets Rule 801(d)(1)(A). Moreover, showing a document to a witness as a memory aid without following Rule 613(b)’s confrontation procedures can undermine later attempts to use the same document extrinsically for impeachment.

Conclusion

Understanding how prior inconsistent statements are used—and when they may be treated as substantive evidence—requires mastery of Rules 613, 801(d)(1)(A), 901, and 612, as well as the interplay with hearsay exceptions and judicial gatekeeping. The courtroom attorney must carefully authenticate extrinsic materials, protect witness fairness by complying with confrontation requirements, and distinguish refreshing memory from impeachment. Courts weigh probative value, potential prejudice, and fairness; these considerations and procedural prerequisites shape whether a prior statement will influence the jury only as a credibility tool or will be admitted as proof of the facts asserted.
