Introduction

Evidence that a defendant engaged in other wrongful acts—commonly called "other-acts" evidence—presents a recurring and difficult question in criminal and civil trials: when does evidence of conduct outside the charged offense go to a permissible, noncharacter purpose and when is it merely evidence of propensity or bad character that must be excluded? The Federal Rules of Evidence and analogous state rules create a two-step structure to govern such evidence. First, the court must decide whether the evidence is intrinsic to the charged offense or extrinsic and therefore governed by Rule 404(b). Second, if the evidence is extrinsic, the court must determine whether it is admissible for a proper noncharacter purpose, whether it is relevant to that purpose, and whether its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice under Rule 403. Even when admissible, courts typically consider limiting instructions and other safeguards to reduce the risk of jury misuse. This chapter explains these principles in a doctrinal, practice-oriented manner so a student can understand how judges reason about other-acts evidence and how its admissibility may turn on finer distinctions of timing, similarity, and context.

# Definition and scope of intrinsic evidence tied to the charged offense

"Intrinsic" evidence refers to acts, statements, or facts that are part of the same transaction as the charged offense or are otherwise inextricably intertwined with it. The doctrine recognizes that not all evidence of other conduct is "other" in the relevant sense: some such conduct is necessary to explain the charged offense, to place the offense in context, or to provide the story of the crime in a coherent way. When other-conduct evidence is intrinsic, courts generally admit it without subjecting it to the specialized Rule 404(b) analysis because it is not being used as evidence of the defendant's propensity to commit crimes but rather to complete the narrative or to prove an element of the offense.

There are two common ways courts characterize intrinsic evidence:

1. Same transaction or occurrence. Evidence is intrinsic if it arises from the same act or transaction as the charged offense. Examples include statements made contemporaneously with the offense, acts done immediately before or after the offense that help to explain it (such as flight, concealment, or preparatory acts closely tied in time), or other conduct that forms part of a single, continuous sequence of events. The key features are closeness in time and direct connection to the charged act.

2. Inextricably intertwined with the evidence of the charged offense. Even if the conduct does not form a single temporal sequence, it may be intrinsic when it is necessary for the jury to understand the charged offense. For instance, admissions, confessions, or acts that explain the relationship between people or the motives for actions often fall into this category. The question is whether excluding the evidence would leave an incomplete or misleading story.

The line between intrinsic and extrinsic is not mechanical; it is guided by relevance and necessity. Courts ask whether the evidence is being offered to prove an element of the offense or to help the jury understand the facts, rather than to show that the defendant is a bad person who therefore committed the charged crime. Intrinsic evidence does not require a separate 404(b) justification because it is not being offered for propensity inference. However, intrinsic evidence still must satisfy ordinary rules of relevance (Rule 401) and is subject to exclusion under Rule 403 if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the risk of unfair prejudice.

Procedural posture affects this analysis. When a prosecutor offers evidence at trial, the judge may rule on admissibility in limine to preserve the trial record and guide jury instructions. On appeal, whether evidence is intrinsic or extrinsic is often reviewed for abuse of discretion because the trial judge is in a better position to evaluate the context and narrative needs of the evidence. When evidence arguably serves both narrative and propensity purposes, courts scrutinize whether the proponent's primary reason is a permissible one and whether the jury can avoid propensity reasoning.

# Rule 404(b) permissible purposes (motive, intent, identity, absence of mistake)

Rule 404(b) governs extrinsic other-acts evidence—that is, evidence of other crimes, wrongs, or acts that are not intrinsic to the charged offense. The rule begins with a prohibition: evidence of other acts is inadmissible to prove a person's character in order to show that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with that character (i.e., propensity). But Rule 404(b) also carves out a set of permissible noncharacter purposes for which extrinsic acts may be admitted, subject to further relevance and balancing analysis. The commonly recognized nonpropensity purposes include motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake or accident, and sometimes a common scheme or plan. Different jurisdictions and formulations may vary slightly, but the governing principles are consistent.

Motive: Evidence that a defendant committed other acts that show a motive for committing the charged offense is admissible under Rule 404(b). Motive evidence is probative when it links the defendant's previous conduct to a reason for acting. The court asks whether the proffered other act makes the existence of a motive more probable than it would be without the evidence.

Intent: Evidence of prior acts can be used to prove the defendant's state of mind, particularly where intent is in dispute. For example, a prior similar act may show that the defendant had the requisite specific intent or purpose when committing the charged behavior. When the element at issue is a mental state rather than an action, Rule 404(b) often plays a critical role.

Identity: Evidence of other acts can help prove identity or modus operandi—i.e., showing that the same person committed both the prior and charged acts because of signature similarities, rare techniques, or a distinctive pattern. The more unique and distinctive the pattern, the more probative the evidence is of identity. Courts require a showing of sufficient similarity to make the inference of identity reasonable rather than speculative.

Absence of mistake or accident: Where a defense of accident or mistake negates criminal liability, evidence of prior similar acts can prove that the defendant acted intentionally, not accidentally. The logic is that if the defendant has engaged in the same conduct before, a claim that the charged conduct was a fluke is less credible.

Plan, preparation, common scheme or system: Evidence of prior acts may show a common plan or scheme encompassing both the prior acts and the charged offense. The more the prior act and charged offense fit into a coherent scheme, the stronger the relevance for proving elements like preparation or intent.

Knowledge: In some cases, prior acts can show knowledge of a particular fact or procedure that bears on the charged offense. For example, prior use of a particular method may show the defendant's knowledge of how to accomplish a certain objective.

Before admitting 404(b) evidence, courts typically require that the prosecution articulate the nonpropensity purpose and that the evidence be relevant to that purpose. The prosecution must explain the logical connection between the prior act and the issue for which it is offered. Many courts insist that the relevance be established by admissible proof that the defendant committed the prior act—i.e., that there is sufficient evidence for a finder of fact to conclude the prior act occurred. The standard for that preliminary finding is discussed below.

Rule 404(b) is often described as "inclusionary" and "exclusionary": it excludes evidence offered to show propensity but includes evidence for specified noncharacter purposes. The risk in applying 404(b) lies in thinly disguised propensity arguments: prosecutors may present a string of prior bad acts that cumulatively suggest criminal disposition. Judges must vigilantly ensure that the evidence does not become proof of bad character in all but name.

# Use of prior possession of the same weapon to prove possession or control

Prior possession of the same weapon is a frequent battleground in crimes involving weapons possession, use, or identity. Evidence that a defendant previously possessed or controlled the same weapon (or a weapon with unique markings or features) can be highly probative on several issues: current possession, constructive possession, identity of the person who used the weapon, knowledge of the weapon's location, and intent to use it in a criminal manner. But whether such evidence is admissible, and under which doctrinal rubric, turns on specifics: timing, location, similarity of the weapon, the context in which the prior possession occurred, and the purpose for which the evidence is offered.

When prior possession is intrinsic. If the prior possession is part of the same transaction or forms a continuous chain of events leading up to the charged offense, it may be intrinsic. An example is where a defendant possessed the weapon immediately prior to or during the charged conduct and the prior possession explains the defendant’s access or ability to commit the charged offense. In that event, the evidence is introduced to complete the narrative and not under Rule 404(b).

When prior possession is extrinsic. If the prior possession occurred at a distinct time and place and is being used to prove knowledge, intent, identity, or lack of mistake, the material falls under Rule 404(b). For instance, proof that the defendant possessed a similar gun weeks earlier may be offered to show the defendant’s knowledge of and control over the weapon in a later incident. Here the court examines whether the prior possession is sufficiently similar and proximate to be probative, and whether its admission is for a permissible nonpropensity purpose.

Similarity and distinctiveness. The probative weight of prior possession evidence often depends on the degree of similarity between the prior weapon and the weapon at issue in the charged offense. Identical or unusually distinctive features (custom markings, unique modifications, serial numbers removed in a particular way) increase the evidentiary value because they support identity or continuity of possession. Courts are more skeptical when the weapons are common and indistinct; similarity in such cases may not significantly raise the likelihood that the defendant possessed the weapon at the time of the offense.

Temporal proximity and continuity. The closer in time the prior possession is to the charged offense, the more probative it is of contemporaneous possession or intent. A possession weeks or days before may be materially more convincing than possession months or years earlier. But temporal proximity is not dispositive: a sufficiently distinctive weapon or pattern may sustain relevance across a longer interval.

Proof of the prior act. Under federal practice, before admitting extrinsic acts, the judge ordinarily must determine that the prosecutor has produced sufficient evidence by a preponderance that the defendant committed the prior act. This preliminary finding is necessary because the jury should not be allowed to consider an unproven bad act for purposes other than propensity. The level of proof—preponderance of the evidence—reflects judicial gatekeeping; it is not the same as the prosecution’s burden of proof on the ultimate charge.

Rule 403 and the specific risks in weapon cases. Evidence of prior possession of a weapon is often emotionally prejudicial to a jury; it may suggest to jurors that the defendant is violent or dangerous and thus more likely to have possessed or used the weapon in the charged crime. Courts thus apply Rule 403 scrutiny, weighing whether admission would unfairly prejudice the defendant. Examples of mitigating steps include restricting the scope of the evidence—admitting only that a weapon was seen, not details of prior crimes—or using limiting instructions to tell the jury the specific permissible purpose for which they may consider the prior possession.

Common defenses and tactical considerations. Defenses often pivot on challenging the relevance, similarity, or proof of the prior possession. Defendants may move to exclude under Rule 404(b) and Rule 403, demand notice of other-acts evidence, and request voir dire of witnesses or pretrial offers of proof to allow the court to assess probative value. From the prosecutor’s side, framed carefully, prior possession evidence can be powerful to shore up gaps in the timeline, to show access to the weapon, or to negate claims of mistake or misidentification.

# Rule 403 balancing: probative value vs. unfair prejudice

Even when other-acts evidence is relevant and offered for a proper purpose under Rule 404(b) or is intrinsic, it remains subject to exclusion under Rule 403.
