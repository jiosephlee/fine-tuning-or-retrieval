Introduction

Appellate review of sentencing decisions occupies a central place in federal criminal practice. After the Supreme Court rendered the federal Sentencing Guidelines advisory rather than mandatory, appellate courts developed a two-part approach to reviewing sentences: first, courts examine procedural reasonableness (did the district court follow the correct law and process?), and second, they evaluate substantive reasonableness (was the ultimate sentence a reasonable exercise of discretion?). This chapter explains the doctrinal framework governing both aspects of review, the standards that apply to legal and factual determinations at sentencing, the treatment of within-Guidelines sentences (with particular attention to the approach used in the Eighth Circuit), the distinct concepts of departures and variances, and the adequacy-of-reasons requirement that drives much of procedural review. The aim is to equip a student with the doctrinal tools necessary to read and understand appellate opinions about sentencing review.

# Procedural reasonableness: correct Guideline calculation, consideration of § 3553(a), response to arguments

Procedural reasonableness focuses on whether the district court correctly followed the statutory and Guideline procedures required to arrive at the sentence. It is a threshold inquiry: procedural errors can require remand even if the court might have reached an otherwise acceptable substantive outcome.

Guideline calculation. The starting point is the court’s computation of the Sentencing Guidelines range. This computation involves mixed questions of law and fact. Legal questions—interpretation of Guideline language and which provisions apply—are reviewed de novo on appeal. Factual determinations that underlie the calculation—such as the existence or amount of a specific enhancement, the quantity of drugs, role in the offense, or victim-related facts—are reviewed for clear error. Under the clear-error standard, an appellate court will defer unless convinced that the district court’s finding is without support in the record or is implausible. Because many Guidelines issues implicate both legal and factual components, appellate opinions typically parse those components and apply the appropriate standard to each.

Objections, preservation, and burdens. Preservation of objections at sentencing affects appellate review. If a party timely objects to a particular Guidelines calculation or to a legal ruling, the appellate court applies the standards above. If, by contrast, a party fails to raise an objection in the district court, the appellate court typically reviews the unpreserved claim for plain error: the appellant must show an error that is clear or obvious and that affects substantial rights, and the appellate court exercises its discretion to correct the error only if it seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Consequently, counsel should make specific, timely objections to preserve issues for full appellate scrutiny.

Consideration of § 3553(a). Beyond computing the Guidelines range, a district court must consider the statutory sentencing factors found in 18 U.S.C. § 3553(a). These factors include the nature and circumstances of the offense, the history and characteristics of the defendant, the need for the sentence to reflect the seriousness of the offense and provide deterrence, the need to protect the public, and the kinds of sentences available, among others. Procedural reasonableness requires that the district court meaningfully consider these factors: it must demonstrate that it took them into account in choosing a sentence. This does not mean the judge must discuss each factor at length, but the record must show the judge considered the statutory list and made reasoned connections between the facts, the statutory factors, and the selected sentence.

Response to arguments. Procedural reasonableness also encompasses how the district court responds to arguments raised by the parties. When a party points to specific mitigating or aggravating circumstances, the court should address those arguments, particularly if they would reasonably affect the sentence. A failure to acknowledge or respond to important sentencing arguments can be procedural error. The level of response required will vary with the circumstances: a brief, clear statement of how and why the court accepted or rejected an argument will often suffice, whereas entirely ignoring a salient contention may render the sentence procedurally deficient.

Sentencing procedure and record. A properly developed sentencing record aids appellate review. The district court should state its Guidelines calculation, identify the facts it found and its source of authority for each enhancement or adjustment, state the applicable Guidelines range, and provide an explanation under § 3553(a) for the sentence imposed. Sentencing hearings should reflect engagement with counsel arguments, questions to the parties or to the defendant if appropriate, and sufficient factual findings to support discretionary choices. The better the trial record, the easier it is for an appellate court to assess whether procedural errors occurred.

# Substantive reasonableness and abuse-of-discretion review

Once procedural regularity is established, appellate courts ask whether the sentence is substantively reasonable. Substantive reasonableness review asks if the sentence is an unreasonable or unjustified result given the § 3553(a) factors and the unique circumstances of the case. The Supreme Court has directed that district courts possess substantial discretion in selecting a sentence within statutory limits; appellate courts must therefore apply a deferential standard.

Standard of review. The general standard for substantive review is abuse of discretion. Under this standard, an appellate court will set aside a sentence only if it is “arbitrary, capricious, whimsical, or manifestly unreasonable,” or if the district court relied on clearly erroneous facts or an erroneous view of the law. This highly deferential standard acknowledges the district court’s institutional advantages—proximity to witnesses, familiarity with local conditions, and experience with the defendant and offense—and thus limits appellate second-guessing. The appellate court will review reasoning and conclusions in light of the record and may consider whether the sentence reflects a proper balancing of § 3553(a) factors and avoids unwarranted sentencing disparities.

Substantive review in practice. In applying the abuse-of-discretion standard, appellate courts typically evaluate several indicia. First, they examine whether the sentence falls within or outside the Guidelines range; within-Guidelines sentences attract a presumptive reasonableness in many circuits (see the next section). Second, they consider the size and direction of any deviation from the Guidelines—significant deviations require a correspondingly strong justification. Third, they assess the district court’s explanation: did the court adequately articulate how the § 3553(a) factors supported the sentence? Fourth, appellate courts compare the sentence to sentences imposed in comparable cases to gauge whether the sentence is substantively disparate without justification. Finally, courts look for improper considerations—such as reliance on impermissible factors like race, religion, or personal bias—which would render any sentence unreasonable.

Allocation of the burden. The parties bear shifting burdens in demonstrating substantive reasonableness. A defendant challenging a sentence as substantively unreasonable must persuade the appellate court that the sentence is unreasonable in light of the record and the statutory factors. If the sentence is within the Guidelines, many circuits treat the sentence as presumptively reasonable, assigning the defendant the burden of refuting that presumption. If the sentence departs or varies substantially, the burden to justify the extent of the deviation rests with the sentencing court (and, practically, with the government to defend the sentence on appeal). But these allocations vary in strength and formulation among the circuits.

# Within-Guidelines presumption (Eighth Circuit approach)

The treatment of within-Guidelines sentences differs by circuit, but the Eighth Circuit has followed an approach that gives significant weight to a sentence within a properly calculated Guidelines range. Understanding the Eighth Circuit’s approach requires appreciating the balance courts strike between deference to district courts and the appellate court’s duty to correct unreasonable sentences.

Presumption of reasonableness. In the Eighth Circuit, a sentence within the properly calculated Guidelines range is generally presumed to be reasonable on appeal. This presumption is rebuttable: a defendant may overcome it by demonstrating that the sentence is unreasonable in light of the § 3553(a) factors, or by showing that the court relied on improper considerations, committed procedural error, or that the particular facts of the case make the Guidelines range inapplicable or unjust. The presumption functions as a legal shorthand: it channels appellate scrutiny toward the most plausible claims of unreasonableness (for example, procedural errors, clear misapplication of the § 3553(a) factors, or extraordinary case facts), while recognizing the Guidelines as an important frame for sentencing.

Operational consequences. Under this approach, appellate courts in the Eighth Circuit apply ordinary standards of review—de novo for legal issues and clear error for factual findings—but treat a within-Guidelines sentence as having a strong claim to reasonableness. A defendant mounting an attack on such a sentence must point to specific errors or disparities that could overcome the presumption. Appellate courts nonetheless remain attentive: the presumption does not immunize sentences from review for procedural defects (such as miscalculations, failure to consider § 3553(a), or ignoring significant arguments) or for substantive excessiveness when the record shows that the Guidelines range produces an unreasonable result in the individual case.

Limits and comparisons. The Eighth Circuit presumption aligns with the trend in several circuits to treat within-Guidelines sentences as presumptively reasonable but not conclusive. The presumption is not absolute; appellate courts continue to exercise meaningful review for abuse of discretion. Moreover, the weight of the presumption can vary with context. A modest within-Guidelines sentence will often be easier to sustain than a within-Guidelines sentence that is at the top of a range accompanying a large upward variance from typical practice. Similarly, when the Guidelines range itself is suspect because of unusual or atypical application of enhancements, the presumption may be more easily rebutted.

# Review of departures and variances

Departures and variances describe ways in which a district court may impose sentences outside the Guidelines range. They are distinct in origin and review.

Departures (Guideline-based). A departure is an adjustment from the Guidelines range authorized by the Sentencing Guidelines themselves, based on circumstances that the Guidelines identify as warranting a nonstandard sentence. Examples include recognizing certain mitigating or aggravating circumstances explicitly contemplated by the Guidelines but not captured in the range calculation. Legal questions regarding whether a particular departure ground applies are reviewed de novo; the factual predicates for a departure are reviewed for clear error. If the Guidelines prescribe a procedure for calculating and applying a departure, the appellate court will ensure that the district court followed that procedure and provided the requisite factual findings. The degree of departure—how far the sentence moves from the range—will then be evaluated for reasonableness under the abuse-of-discretion standard.

Variances (3553(a)-based). A variance is a sentence outside the Guidelines range justified not by Guideline text but by the district court’s consideration of the § 3553(a) factors. The advisory nature of the Guidelines expressly permits variances when a judge concludes that a sentence different from the Guidelines range better fits the statutory factors. The key distinction is that departures are governed by the Guidelines’ own structure and methods, while variances rely on statutory sentencing considerations that may not be enumerated in the Guidelines.

Standards for reviewing departures and variances. For both departures and variances, appellate courts review the decision for procedural and substantive reasonableness. Procedurally, the court asks whether the district court identified the correct legal basis for the deviation, considered the relevant factors, made necessary factual findings, and adequately explained its decision. Substantively, appellate courts apply abuse-of-discretion review to determine if the degree of the departure or variance is reasonable in light of the § 3553(a) factors and the record. Large departures or variances attract closer scrutiny: the more substantial the deviation from the Guidelines, the more compelling the district court’s justification must be.

Guidelines constraints and upward departures. Departures can be upward or downward. The same principles apply to both, but upward departures and large variances are often more vulnerable to appellate critique because they risk creating unwarranted disparities or exceeding what a district court may justify by reference to the statutory factors. Appellate courts will examine whether the district court relied on an impermissible double-counting of factors already accounted for in the Guidelines, or on erroneous factual bases. If the district court gives adequate reasons tied to § 3553(a), however, even a significant upward variance can survive review.

Remedial consequences. If an appellate court finds an improper departure or variance—either because the district court committed procedural error or because the sentence is substantively unreasonable—the typical remedy is to vacate the sentence and remand for resentencing. The appellate court may retain jurisdiction to fashion alternative relief in limited circumstances, but the usual path is remand so the district court can re-evaluate the sentencing decision consistent with the appellate guidance.

# Sentencing explanations and the adequacy-of-reasons requirement

A central aspect of both procedural and substantive review is the district court’s explanation for the sentence. Adequacy of reasons serves multiple functions: it assures the parties that the sentence was the product of reasoned judgment, it documents the factual and legal grounds that support the sentence, and it enables meaningful appellate review.

What constitutes an adequate explanation. The adequacy requirement is context-dependent; the court need not produce a written dissertation nor recite each § 3553(a) factor verbatim. An adequate explanation typically does three things: it discloses the Guidelines calculation and the factual bases for any adjustments or enhancements; it identifies the considered § 3553(a) factors and explains how they inform the chosen sentence; and it addresses significant arguments raised by the parties, especially those that, if credited, might lead to a materially different sentence. When the district court imposes a sentence within the Guidelines, a concise explanation that demonstrates the court considered the Guidelines and the statutory factors may suffice. When the court departs or varies, more detailed reasoning is usually required to justify the deviation and to permit appellate assessment of its proportionality.

Formalities and modes of explanation. Explanations may be given orally at sentencing and entered into the record, or the court may provide a written statement of reasons. Federal practice codifies certain formalities: the court must complete and file a statement of reasons for sentences outside a Guidelines range or where required by statute; the probation office ordinarily prepares a presentence report that informs the court’s factual determinations. An oral explanation at the sentencing hearing is acceptable but is more likely to be adequate if a contemporaneous written statement supplements it or if the hearing transcript captures the court’s reasoning clearly.

Consequences of inadequate reasons. If the explanation is inadequate in a way that prevents meaningful appellate review, appellate courts will often vacate the sentence and remand for resentencing. The severity of the procedural error dictates the remedy: if the deficiency was trivial and remand would be futile, an appellate court may nonetheless affirm, but when the record lacks essential findings or the district court failed to consider a material argument or factor, remand is typical. Inadequate reasons can also convert what might otherwise be a deferential substantive review into a broader inquiry: if the appellate court cannot discern the basis for a sentence, it cannot properly determine whether the sentence is substantively reasonable.

Avoiding boilerplate and double-counting. District courts should avoid boilerplate language that merely repeats statutory phrases without linking them to case-specific facts. Similarly, judges should explain why considerations already accounted for in the Guidelines justify additional weight if they lead to a variance or departure; failure to explain why the factor warrants further consideration can create an appearance of double-counting and may lead to reversal. Clear, candid, and fact-linked explanations reduce the risk of appellate reversal.

# Procedural posture and practical considerations

Appeals from sentencing usually follow a final judgment imposing a term of imprisonment, a term of supervised release, or other sentencing components. Understanding how appellate courts approach preserved versus unpreserved objections is critical. Preserved legal errors (e.g., statutory interpretation or incorrect Guidelines application) receive full review, factual disputes are generally reviewed for clear error, and unpreserved claims are typically subject to plain-error review. Parties should frame objections precisely at sentencing to reserve issues for appeal.

Appellate briefing should identify procedural errors and substantive unreasonableness and explain why the district court’s explanation is inadequate. For defendants challenging within-Guidelines sentences in circuits like the Eighth Circuit that apply a presumption of reasonableness, briefs should concentrate on rebutting the presumption by demonstrating either procedural shortcomings, extraordinary factual circumstances, or misapplication of the § 3553(a) factors that render the Guidelines range inapposite.

Finally, appellate courts may consider policy developments, sentencing trends, and circuit precedent in substantive review, but they do not substitute their own policy judgments for the district court’s within the abuse-of-discretion framework. The appellate role is corrective and supervisory: ensure legal correctness, fidelity to procedure, and that the chosen sentence is accountable to the statutory sentencing scheme.

Conclusion

Appellate review of sentences blends legal precision with deference. Procedural reasonableness requires correct Guideline computations, meaningful consideration of § 3553(a) factors, and responses to parties’ arguments; failures in these areas frequently lead to remand. Substantive reasonableness is governed by abuse-of-discretion review, with within-Guidelines sentences accorded presumptive reasonableness in some circuits, including the Eighth Circuit, though that presumption is rebuttable. Departures and variances are distinct pathways for sentences outside the Guidelines and attract varying degrees of appellate scrutiny depending on their magnitude and justification. Central to all review is the adequacy of the sentencing court’s explanation: clear, case-specific reasoning is the keystone that allows appellate courts to determine whether a sentence was lawfully and reasonably imposed. Armed with this framework, a student will be prepared to analyze appellate opinions that apply these principles.
