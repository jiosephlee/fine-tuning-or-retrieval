Introduction

Statutory interpretation in Louisiana occurs within a distinctive legal culture: a mixed jurisdiction that blends civil (civilian) methodology with common-law influences. A law student preparing to read judicial opinions must understand the foundational interpretive doctrines that guide Louisiana courts. These doctrines include both textualist and purposivist elements derived from the Louisiana Civil Code and supplemented by jurisprudential canons adapted from Roman, French, and Spanish civil law traditions. This chapter explains how Louisiana courts treat the text of statutes, how they employ the so-called plain-meaning rule, the role of legal dictionaries, the limits on judicial implication of unexpressed provisions, approaches to reconciling related provisions, and the deference—if any—given to administrative practice and industry usage. Attention to procedural posture is important because appellate review, preservation of error, and the posture of the parties can constrain what interpretive issues a court will decide and the standard of review applied.

# Civil Code arts. 9–13: text, intent, and application of clear language

The Louisiana Civil Code sets forth core canons of statutory interpretation in articles that address the primacy of text and the role of legislative intent. Although the precise numbering and wording may vary as statutes are revised, the underlying principles are consistent: courts must first examine the text of a statute, seek the intent of the legislature when the text is ambiguous or leads to absurd results, and give effect to the clear meaning of the language when it is unambiguous.

Article 9 typically enjoins the interpreter to determine the common intent of the legislature; article 10 may require that words are given their generally prevailing meaning unless context requires otherwise; article 11 often directs that deeds and statutes be interpreted according to the scope of their terms; article 12 might instruct that when legislation is susceptible of different constructions, the one consistent with the general scheme should be adopted; and article 13 generally states that when the wording of a law is clear and leads to no absurd consequences, no further interpretation is needed. While the exact text of these articles should be consulted, their doctrinal effect can be summarized as three sequential commitments.

First, textual primacy: courts begin with the statutory text. The text is the principal expression of legislative will, and its literal words carry weight. Judges must read the statutory language carefully, attending to syntax, grammar, punctuation, and the ordinary sense of terms.

Second, search for legislative intent: when the text is ambiguous or incomplete, judges seek the legislature's common intent. Sources used may include the statute's preamble, legislative history, context within the Code or statute, prior law, and general principles of the Civil Code. This inquiry is not an invitation to substitute judicial policy preferences for legislative choices; it is a controlled exercise to determine what reasonable meaning the legislature could have intended.

Third, clear and unambiguous provisions control: when a statute's wording is clear and leads to no absurdity, that meaning must be applied even if a judge thinks the result is unwise. This doctrine restrains judicial lawmaking and emphasizes legislative supremacy in a civilian system. The threshold for “clear” is a legal judgment: courts must ascertain that reasonable minds would not differ over the meaning.

Application to cases is fact-driven. In straightforward cases, judges apply the clear text to the facts and resolve disputes without expansive interpretive excursions. In more complex disputes—where statutory silence, vagueness, or competing provisions exist—the judicial task becomes navigating canons and auxiliary materials to achieve a lawful, coherent construction.

# Plain-meaning rule and use of legal dictionaries

The plain-meaning rule is a cornerstone of statutory interpretation in Louisiana: if the statutory language is plain and unambiguous, courts apply it according to its ordinary meaning. The object is to give effect to the legislature’s words rather than to reframe them through judicial rhetoric. However, "plain" and "ordinary" are not self-defining, and courts often consult external aids to determine the ordinary meaning of legal terms.

Legal dictionaries and standard dictionaries play a supporting role. When terms have established legal meanings that differ from common usage, courts prefer legal-dictionary definitions that reflect doctrinal usage, historical practice, or technical meaning within the law. For example, words of art in property, obligations, or commercial law may carry precise meanings shaped by doctrine and jurisprudence. Courts therefore consult dictionaries of legal terms, classical civil-law treatises, and authoritative Code commentaries to ascertain meaning consistent with the Code's principles.

Using dictionaries is not an act of mechanical substitution. Courts treat dictionary definitions as evidence of meaning rather than conclusive proof. The interpretive process balances dictionary evidence with textual context, related statutory provisions, and the general purpose of the law. A dictionary definition that would produce an inconsistent or absurd result in context must yield to the statute's purpose and surrounding language.

Procedurally, whether a court will rely on dictionaries can be affected by preservation of issue and standard of review. If the parties did not raise an ambiguity or present dictionary evidence in the trial court, an appellate court reviewing de novo may still look at authoritative definitions, but appellate courts often prefer that factual or evidentiary foundations for interpretive assertions be developed below. When interpretation of a statute involves mixed questions of law and fact (for example, whether a term has settled industry meaning), appellate review may be more deferential to trial findings.

# Expressio unius, negative-implication reasoning, and avoiding judicial additions

Expressio unius est exclusio alterius—the expression of one thing is the exclusion of another—is a familiar canon in statutory interpretation. Louisiana courts use expressio unius and related negative-implication reasoning to infer legislative intent when a statute enumerates specific items. The basic logic is that by specifying certain things, the legislature implicitly excluded items not specified, particularly when the statutory scheme and context show that the legislature intended a closed list.

However, the canon has limits. Its application depends on context: an express enumeration in a statute may be illustrative (an example) rather than exhaustive. Whether a list is exhaustive is a question of statutory construction that considers language, structure, subject matter, and purpose. Courts look for words of limitation—"including," "such as," "consisting of," or "only"—and the statute's overall design to determine whether expressio unius should apply.

Negative-implication reasoning also cautions against judicial additions. Civilian doctrine emphasizes that judges should not create liabilities, rights, or exceptions that the legislature did not include. When statutes are silent, courts may be tempted to supply language to rectify perceived gaps; under Civil Code articles reinforcing textual primacy, such supplementation is permissible only to the extent necessary to give effect to legislative intent or to avoid absurd consequences. Judges must avoid substituting their policy judgments for legislative choices.

There are recognized exceptions. A court may imply a rule when the statute's text and purpose plainly require it for the statute to function as intended—where silence would render the statute incoherent or produce outcomes that contradict the legislative objective. But the threshold for such implication is high: the inference must be compelled, not merely convenient. Courts often prefer to interpret statutory language in a way that preserves the statute's operation and promotes consistent application throughout the law, while leaving to the legislature any substantive policy changes.

Procedural posture affects how aggressively a court will infer negative implications. A trial court asked to apply a statute to a specific fact pattern may resolve doubts by limited implication; an appellate court, particularly when confronted with broad policy questions, may be more reluctant to enunciate new rules absent explicit legislative direction.

# Harmonizing related provisions and legislative purpose

Civil law systems place strong emphasis on the coherence of the legal system. When statutes on related subjects appear to conflict or overlap, courts apply principles of harmonization and construction to reconcile provisions and give effect to the legislative scheme as a whole. Harmonizing involves reading provisions together, adjusting meanings to avoid contradictions, and selecting constructions that are consistent with the Code's basic doctrines.

The starting point is textual alignment: courts interpret words and phrases in light of adjacent paragraphs, complementary statutes, and the Code's general principles. When a literal construction would create conflict between provisions, courts seek alternative readings that preserve the operative effect of both, assuming the legislature would intend a coherent system. This process employs a "systemic" approach: law is an integrated whole and interpretation should conform to its internal logic.

Legislative purpose is a critical guide in harmonization. Determining purpose requires looking beyond the words to the statute's objectives. Sources of purpose include the statute’s preamble, legislative history (where available and appropriate), prior law's defects that the statute was intended to cure, and surrounding legal provisions that contextualize the statute’s role. Purpose helps resolve ambiguities: when two readings are possible, the court selects the one that best advances the statute's aims in the overall legal scheme.

There are formal tests and considerations. Courts may apply the following structured approach: (1) identify the apparent conflict; (2) determine whether provisions are of the same or different hierarchies (e.g., Code text vs. special statute); (3) consider whether one provision is a specific rule governing a subject where another is a general rule; and (4) where specificity differs, apply the specific-over-general canon—special rules control general ones, absent clear legislative intent to the contrary. If temporal conflicts exist, the later-in-time rule may apply unless the legislature explicitly retained the earlier rule.

In the civilian tradition, judges are mindful that the legislature may enact a specialized statute that modifies general Code principles. Courts therefore read specialized statutes as exceptions to general rules only when that’s the most reasonable reading. Harmonization also requires sensitivity to public policy embedded in the Code—principles of good faith, equity, and public order that can influence the selection among plausible interpretations.

Procedural posture matters because parties may frame the interpretive question narrowly (seeking relief under a specific provision) or broadly (asking courts to reconcile multiple provisions affecting a whole regulatory area). Appellate courts confronted with broad declaratory judgments conflict resolutions will exercise careful restraint and often remand for findings of factual context that affect statutory meaning.

# Weight of administrative and industry practice

Administrative purpose and industry practice can inform statutory interpretation in Louisiana, but their persuasive weight varies by context. Because Louisiana is a civilian jurisdiction that values codal coherence, courts do not yield to administrative or industry interpretations without scrutiny. Still, practice and usage may shed light on meaning, especially for technical statutes, regulatory schemes, or terms of art.

Administrative construction: when an administrative agency has been charged by the legislature with implementing a statute, courts often give deference to the agency’s reasonable interpretation of ambiguous provisions. The extent of deference depends on statutory delegation, the agency's expertise, and the interpretive forum. Where the legislature set forth an explicit rule-making or enforcement role, and the statute is ambiguous, an agency's interpretation that reflects consistent policy and fair notice may be accorded respect. However, deference is not unbounded. Courts will not defer to an agency interpretation that is inconsistent with the statute's text, incompatible with Code principles, or the result of arbitrary decisionmaking.

Industry practice and custom: usage within a particular trade or profession may help define technical terms or clarify how statutory obligations operate in practice. Evidence of custom is especially persuasive when the legislature used industry terms without elaboration, implying reliance on established usage. Courts consider the longevity, consistency, and coherence of the practice, as well as whether the practice conflicts with statutory provisions or public policy. When a long-standing industry practice fills a statutory gap in a way that aligns with legislative purpose, courts may adopt that practice as an interpretive aid.

But courts remain wary of giving too much weight to practice that effectively amends statutes by creating exemptions or exceptions outside legislative authority. Industry practice cannot override clear statutory commands. Moreover, practice that is unjust, discriminatory, or contrary to Code principles will be rejected.

In evaluating administrative or industry interpretations, courts weigh several factors: the agency’s or industry’s expertise; the temporal persistence and stability of the interpretation; whether stakeholders had notice and opportunity to conform; whether the interpretation fosters uniform application; and whether the interpretation promotes the statute’s stated goals.

Procedural considerations: litigants who rely on administrative or industry practice should develop factual records at trial. Appellate courts reviewing de novo may consider administrative interpretations as a matter of law, but where factual findings about industry custom are central, appellate review will be for clear error. Parties must preserve issues about deference and evidentiary weight for appellate review.

Conclusion

Interpreting Louisiana statutes requires a disciplined approach that prioritizes textual meaning while being attentive to legislative intent and the broader codal structure. Civil Code arts. 9–13 encapsulate the civilian commitment to text-first interpretation, the role of intent, and the requirement that clear language govern. The plain-meaning rule and legal dictionaries assist in determining ordinary and technical meanings, while expressio unius and negative-implication doctrines curb judicial overreach. Harmonizing related provisions and honoring legislative purpose produce coherent constructions across the legal system. Administrative and industry practices can be persuasive interpretive aids but must not supplant statutory text or Code principles. Finally, procedural posture—preservation of issues, the nature of the record, and the standard of appellate review—shapes how courts apply these interpretive tools in concrete disputes. Understanding these doctrines equips the student to read judicial opinions with an eye toward how judges reason from text to result in Louisiana’s distinctive civilian framework.
