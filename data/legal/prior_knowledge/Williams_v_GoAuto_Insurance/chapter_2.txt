Introduction

Insurance-premium finance agreements are an important commercial device in the insurance marketplace. They permit insureds to spread the cost of insurance premiums over time by contracting with a third-party premium finance company that pays the insurer up front and obtains from the insured a security interest or assignment in the insurance policy as collateral. Louisiana has enacted a statutory framework that governs these transactions and imposes specific duties and protective procedures on insurers, premium finance companies, and insureds. Understanding that framework is essential before reading judicial opinions that interpret or apply it: courts often focus on statutory definitions, notice and cure requirements, the allocation of decision-making authority among the parties, and the evidentiary proof needed to establish compliance or entitlement to relief.

This chapter explains the structure and purpose of premium finance agreements generally, then turns to the statutory scheme embodied in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550 (as embodied in the Louisiana Revised Statutes). The discussion emphasizes the key definitions and policy aims of the statute, the law governing defaults and cure periods and what prerequisites must be satisfied before an insurer may cancel coverage for nonpayment, how authority and obligations are allocated among insurer, insured, and finance company, and the recordkeeping and proof requirements imposed on finance companies. Throughout, attention is paid to how courts evaluate compliance, whose burdens are what in litigation, and how procedural posture (pretrial motion practice, summary judgment, trial) can affect the analysis.

# Structure and purpose of insurance-premium finance agreements

At their core, premium finance agreements are credit arrangements: the premium finance company (PFC) agrees to pay, or to arrange payment of, the insurer premium on behalf of an insured in return for the insured’s promise to repay the financed amount under agreed terms. The financing typically covers the entire premium or a large portion of it; the insured then makes periodic payments to the PFC according to the finance agreement. To secure repayment, the PFC obtains either an assignment of the premium, a security interest in the policy, or an express lien on any policyholder return premium (refund) that might arise if the policy is canceled or reduced.

From a commercial and legal perspective, several functional features distinguish premium finance transactions:

- The PFC is not the insured and does not generally become a party to the insurance contract as insurer or insured. Instead, it stands behind the insured’s obligation to pay premiums and acquires a collateral interest in the policy or in proceeds that arise from it.

- The relationship among insurer, insured, and finance company is tripartite: each has different interests (insurer wants premiums paid; insured wants coverage and to avoid being exposed to double payment; PFC wants assurance of repayment). Statutory regimes aim to harmonize these interests by creating procedures that protect the PFC’s collateral while protecting insureds from unanticipated cancellation.

- The security arrangement is forward-looking: if the insured defaults, the PFC’s collateral may entitle it to recover amounts from policy returns or to prevent cancellation by paying premiums and asserting the right to be reimbursed.

- Commercial standards and insurer policies can vary, so statutes fill the gaps: statutes often define what constitutes a valid premium finance agreement, establish required notices and timing, and set out the mechanics by which a PFC may exercise rights to preserve coverage.

In litigation, courts examine the text of the finance agreement, the nature of the claimed security interest or assignment, and whether parties complied with statutory prerequisites when rights were exercised (for example, whether a PFC validly prevented an insurer’s cancellation by tendering payment or providing proper notice). Because these transactions implicate insurance law, contract law, and secured transactions principles, judges often undertake a mixed analysis: statutory construction first (what does the controlling statute require?), contract interpretation second (what did the parties agree to?), and equitable considerations (what result best protects insureds and third-party creditors?).

# La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550: scope, key definitions, and policy aims

La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550 (the statute at issue in this chapter) creates a statutory scheme that governs premium financing arrangements in Louisiana. A student must understand three things about such a statute: its scope (which transactions and parties it covers), its core definitions, and the policy aims that motivate its procedural protections and duties.

Scope. The statute typically applies to premium finance agreements that provide for the financing of insurance premiums for policies issued by insurers licensed or otherwise authorized to do business in Louisiana. That includes property and casualty, casualty-only, surety, and other non-life insurance lines commonly financed. The statute does not generally alter substantive insurance obligations between insurer and insured; instead, it sets procedural requirements that affect the enforceability of the PFC’s collateral rights and the insurer’s remedies for nonpayment. It also commonly governs the conduct required of insurers when they seek to cancel coverage for nonpayment where a PFC has an interest.

Key definitions. Statutory definitions are decisive because many questions turn on them. Typical defined terms include:

- "Premium Finance Agreement" – a written contract under which a PFC advances funds to enable an insured to pay a premium and the insured agrees to repay the advance with interest and fees.

- "Premium Finance Company" (PFC) – the party that makes the advance and takes the security interest or assignment.

- "Insured" – the person whose risk is covered by the insurance policy and who originally contracted with the insurer.

- "Insurer" – the insurance company issuing the policy subject to the financing.

- "Cancellation" – termination of coverage under the insurance policy before its scheduled expiration, including cancellation for nonpayment of premium.

- "Assignment" or "security interest" – the transfer to the PFC of rights to installments or return premium, as collateral for repayment.

These definitions determine who may claim protections under the statute and which contractual acts are effective to transfer rights. Courts often look to the statute’s definitions as the starting point for resolving disputes over standing and entitlement.

Policy aims. The statute balances three central policy goals. First, protecting insureds from losing coverage without adequate notice and opportunity to cure prevents harsh results that could leave parties uninsured and exposed to risk. Second, protecting PFCs’ collateral and ability to recover for advances ensures that credit will be available to insureds who need it. Third, preserving insurer interests in receiving timely payment avoids exposing insurers to undue risk (for example, by preventing insurers from being forced to continue coverage when premiums remain unpaid without any recourse).

To harmonize these aims, the statute usually imposes an interplay of duties: insurers must provide notice of cancellation to PFCs with a recorded interest; PFCs must register or file evidence of their interest (and may have to provide the insurer with proof of financing); and insureds must be given an opportunity to cure defaults or have the PFC cure them on the insured’s behalf. Courts interpreting the statute will weigh these goals when construing ambiguous provisions, often applying rules of statutory construction that favor giving effect to all parts of the statute and protecting reasonable reliance interests of third parties.

# Default, cure periods, and prerequisites to cancellation

A central function of premium finance statutes is to prescribe when an insurer may cancel a policy for nonpayment and what procedural steps must occur before cancellation becomes effective. These rules are designed to give notice to both the insured and the PFC (if a finance agreement exists) and to provide a limited opportunity to cure the default.

Default: what constitutes a default? Under a typical premium finance agreement, the insured defaults by failing to make payments to the PFC according to the amortization schedule or by otherwise breaching the finance contract. From the insurer’s vantage, nonpayment of a premium installment when due constitutes a breach of the insurance contract that may justify cancellation under the policy and applicable law. However, when a PFC is involved, an insurer’s unilateral declaration of default or immediate cancellation is usually constrained by statutory prerequisites.

Cure periods: statute-created opportunity to cure. Most statutes require that a cancellative action for nonpayment be preceded by specified notices and an opportunity to cure. Key features are:

- Notice to insured and PFC. The insurer must provide notice to the insured as required by normal insurance law and, where a PFC has a recorded or acknowledged interest, must also notify the PFC. The statute will typically set out the content of such notices (e.g., stating the amount due, the period within which payment must be made to prevent cancellation, and the consequences of failing to pay).

- A fixed cure period. The statute prescribes a short, defined cure period measured in days within which the default may be remedied by payment. That period is intended to be strictly observed and provides the PFC (or insured) a window to make the payment that will preserve coverage. The cure period is generally brief to balance the insurer’s need for timely payment and the PFC’s need for a realistic opportunity to cure.

- Methods of curing. The statute may permit either the insured or the PFC to cure by tendering payment of the delinquent premium or by curing the finance agreement default in accordance with its terms. A PFC that cures typically acquires rights of reimbursement and may, in some circumstances, prevent insurer cancellation.

Prerequisites to cancellation. Before the insurer may cancel for nonpayment, statutes commonly require compliance with several prerequisites:

- Proof of notice. The insurer must be able to prove it gave the required notice to the insured and to the PFC if the PFC’s interest was known or recorded. Failure to provide statutorily required notice can render a cancellation ineffective and may expose the insurer to liability for wrongful cancellation.

- Statutory waiting period. The insurer must allow the statutory cure period to pass without cure. If the PFC pays within that period (or the insured does), cancellation is typically barred.

- Satisfaction of additional conditions. Some statutes or policy forms may require the insurer to accept payment from the insured or the PFC within the cure period and to provide written confirmation of any reinstatement.

Judicial review. In court, the focus is usually whether the insurer complied with the statutory notice requirements and whether the cure period was honored. Courts evaluate evidence of mailing, receipt, and the adequacy of notice language. Where a PFC claims it was unaware of an impending cancellation because the insurer failed to record its interest or did not receive proper notice, the court will examine the administrative steps the PFC took to protect its interests (e.g., filing a notice of assignment with the insurer) and whether the insurer’s practices were consistent with statutory duties. The allocation of burdens is significant: a PFC asserting that a cancellation was ineffective because statutory prerequisites were not met generally must prove the existence of its protected interest and the insurer’s failure to give the statutorily required notice or to permit the cure period. Conversely, an insurer defending a cancellation will normally bear the burden of proving that it provided proper notice and complied with statutory procedures.

# Allocation of authority among insurer, insured, and finance company

Premium finance statutes delineate a tripartite allocation of authority that affects who may make decisions about policy continuation, payment, and claims to return premiums. Courts interpret these allocations by reference to the statute, the finance agreement, and the policy language.

Insurer authority. Insurers retain the fundamental right to demand payment of premiums and to cancel a policy for nonpayment if permitted by the policy and law. The statute does not typically extinguish the insurer’s right to protect its underwriting exposure. However, that authority is conditioned: where a PFC has a recorded interest, the insurer’s right to cancel is circumscribed by statutory notice and cure requirements. The insurer also has the right to accept payments from insureds or PFCs and to apply payments in accordance with its policy and statutory obligations.

PFC authority. The PFC’s authority derives from the finance agreement and the statutory protections the PFC may obtain by providing notice to the insurer. Typically, the PFC has a secured creditor-like role: it may advance funds, claim a security interest in premiums or return premiums, and, if the insured defaults, tender payment to prevent cancellation. In many schemes, a timely payment by the PFC preserves the policy and gives the PFC a right to reimbursement (and sometimes to recover statutory fees or interest) from the insured. However, the PFC is not usually authorized to exercise discretionary underwriting prerogatives that insurers possess (for example, a PFC cannot unilaterally change coverage terms or assume the insurer’s risk decisions); its power is mostly limited to monetary cures and asserting its collateral rights against return premiums.

Insured authority. The insured has primary contractual obligations to the insurer and the PFC. The insured retains the right to make payments directly to the insurer or to cure defaults, but the insured’s remedies can be affected by the PFC’s security interest. For instance, if an insured pays the insurer while the PFC has a properly perfected interest in the policy, reimbursement or resolution of who owns any return premium may become contested. Additionally, the insured’s ability to avoid cancellation depends on whether the insured’s actions comply with both the insurance contract and the finance agreement.

Priority rules and interplay. The statute typically provides for a priority scheme among claims to the policy return premium. Generally, where a PFC has taken appropriate steps to assert its interest, that interest will have priority over other claims to return premiums, including those by the insured (except as otherwise provided by statute or contract). Insurers must usually honor these priority arrangements to the extent the PFC’s interest is properly recorded and notice requirements are satisfied.

Practical consequences of allocation. In practice, the allocation of authority affects conduct in three main areas:

- Prevention of cancellation. If a PFC timely pays an insurer to prevent cancellation, it will normally secure the policy and obtain reimbursement rights. Courts scrutinize whether the payment was made in a timely and effective manner under the statute.

- Handling of return premium. Since return premiums may be the primary source of recovery for a PFC after cancellation, statutes and finance agreements often define how return premiums are calculated and allocated. Disputes commonly arise over what constitutes a return premium and how offsets are applied.

- Disputes over coverage and claims. The PFC is usually not entitled to control insurance claims or to direct the insurer’s handling of claims. But coverage disputes can affect a PFC’s collateral position: if the insurer denies coverage for a loss, the policy may be canceled or reduced, thereby affecting potential return premiums.

Courts deciding disputes often conduct sequential analyses: first, whether the PFC’s interest was properly created and protected under the statute; second, whether the insurer complied with notice and cancellation prerequisites; and third, how equities and contractual remedies should be balanced when rights conflict.

# Recordkeeping and proof requirements for finance companies

Statutory schemes governing premium financing commonly impose specific recordkeeping and proof requirements on PFCs. These requirements exist to ensure transparency, to allow insurers to identify parties with interests in policies, and to produce reliable evidence of compliance when disputes arise. In litigation, courts treat compliance with these administrative obligations as material to the enforceability of a PFC’s rights.

Typical recordkeeping and filing duties. The statute may require PFCs to take some or all of the following steps:

- Reduce the premium finance agreement to writing and provide copies to the insured and the insurer (or otherwise notify the insurer of the PFC’s interest). A written agreement typically must contain particular information mandated by statute, such as the amount financed, the interest and fees charged, and the insured’s rights in the event of default.

- File or register notice of assignment or financing with the insurer or with a designated state registry so that insurers have actual or constructive notice of the PFC’s interest. Filing is crucial; an unfiled or unrecorded assignment can leave an insurer unaware of third-party interests and can undermine the PFC’s claim to priority in later disputes.

- Maintain contemporaneous records of payments made to insurers, communications with insureds and insurers, and any tenders or cures effected to prevent cancellation. These records include receipts, cancelled checks, wire confirmations, and copies of notices sent and received.

Proof requirements in litigation. When a dispute reaches court, a PFC must prove several elements to enforce its rights or challenge an insurer’s cancellation:

- Existence of the finance agreement and its terms. The PFC should produce a signed copy of the finance agreement and any amendments or related correspondence. Courts will look for a clear, written contract showing the amounts financed, repayment terms, and any assignment language.

- Proper creation of the collateral interest. The PFC must show that it acquired a valid assignment or security interest under the finance agreement and applicable law. If perfection (by filing or other means) is required under the statute or the law of secured transactions, proof of filing or compliance is often essential.

- Notice to the insurer. Where the statute conditions a PFC’s protections on notice, the PFC should be prepared to produce evidence that notice was given in the form required by statute and within the time frame required. Evidence may include certified mail receipts, carrier tracking logs, or acknowledgment by the insurer.

- Timely cures or tenders. If the PFC claims it prevented a cancellation by tendering payment within the cure period, it must produce evidence that the payment was timely and actually received by the insurer. Courts are exacting about timing and receipt; mere assertions without corroborating documentary evidence are often insufficient.

- Accounting records and proof of damages. If the PFC seeks reimbursement or compensation after curing, it must support those claims with accounting records showing amounts advanced, payments received, interest and fees due under the finance agreement, and computation of any balance owed.

Evidentiary burdens and admissibility. Procedurally, courts treat these proofs like any other documentary and testimonial evidence. Business records can be admitted under hearsay exceptions if the custodian testifies or if the record qualifies as an admissible business record under the rules of evidence. Affidavits may establish mailing or receipt in summary judgment contexts, but courts will demand corroborating documentary evidence where primary documents are available. When a PFC fails to produce required records or proof of compliance, courts may deny enforcement of the PFC’s claimed rights, decline equitable relief, or find that the insurer’s cancellation was effective.

Remedies for noncompliance. If a PFC has failed to observe recordkeeping or notice requirements, consequences can include:

- Loss of priority in return premiums.

- Ineffective preemption of cancellation (meaning the insurer’s cancellation stands).

- Statutory penalties, fees, or denial of costs and attorney’s fees, depending on statutory text.

- Contractual or equitable remedies against the PFC by the insured in bad-faith or statutory-violation actions, in cases where the PFC’s failures harmed the insured’s coverage position.

Because courts rely heavily on documentary proof to decide these issues, PFCs should maintain thorough records and be prepared to produce them early in litigation; failure to do so can be dispositive on material issues of statutory compliance.

Conclusion

Louisiana’s statutory framework for premium finance agreements aims to balance the competing interests of insurers, insureds, and premium finance companies by defining covered transactions and parties, prescribing notice and cure procedures that must precede cancellation, allocating authority over payments and policy continuation, and imposing recordkeeping and proof obligations on PFCs. For litigation, mastery of the statute’s definitions and procedural requirements is essential because courts evaluate disputes through a sequential lens: whether the PFC had a valid interest and gave proper notice; whether the insurer complied with statutory prerequisites to cancellation; and whether documentary proof supports the parties’ claims. Procedural posture matters, too: summary judgment and preliminary injunction motions turn on documentary proofs of notice, cure, and filing; trial may require live testimony from custodians and witnesses to establish compliance. Before reading judicial opinions applying La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550, a student should therefore be comfortable with the statutory vocabulary, the typical notice-and-cure machinery, the allocation of monetary and administrative authority among the parties, and the kinds of records and proofs that courts treat as controlling.
