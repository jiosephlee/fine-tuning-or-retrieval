Introduction

Federal subject-matter jurisdiction is the first threshold question in any removed case: without a proper federal jurisdictional basis, a federal court must remand or dismiss the case. Class litigation raises additional doctrinal and procedural wrinkles. The Class Action Fairness Act (CAFA) altered the landscape for many putative class suits by broadening access to federal courts; removal mechanics and timeliness rules govern when and how defendants can invoke federal adjudication; remand practice and the allocation of burdens determine which party must justify or challenge the federal forum; and jurisdictional posture (federal vs. state) affects what law will apply and how ancillary claims or additional parties may be handled through principles of supplemental jurisdiction and joinder. This chapter sets out the foundational doctrines a law student should know before reading judicial opinions about removal in class actions: (1) the basics of diversity jurisdiction as modified by CAFA, (2) the mechanics and timing of removal, (3) remand standards and burdens, (4) how the jurisdictional posture influences applicable law, and (5) supplemental jurisdiction and party-alignment issues that frequently arise in removed class litigation.

# Diversity jurisdiction basics and the Class Action Fairness Act (CAFA)

Diversity jurisdiction traditionally rests on the requirements of 28 U.S.C. § 1332: complete diversity of citizenship between plaintiffs and defendants and an amount in controversy exceeding a statutory threshold. Prior to CAFA, the “complete diversity” rule required that no plaintiff be a citizen of the same state as any defendant, and the amount in controversy was viewed separately for multiple plaintiffs unless aggregation rules permitted otherwise.

CAFA (enacted 2005) significantly modified the federal diversity access point for many class actions. Rather than eliminating diversity jurisdiction, CAFA supplements and in many cases relaxes the traditional requirements:

- Minimal diversity. CAFA requires only “minimal diversity,” meaning that at least one member of a class is a citizen of a state different from any defendant (28 U.S.C. § 1332(d)(2), (d)(1)(B)). The reduction from complete to minimal diversity makes it easier for defendants to invoke federal jurisdiction.

- Amount in controversy. CAFA sets a lower single threshold for aggregated amount in controversy in class actions: the aggregate amount in controversy for the entire proposed class must exceed $5,000,000, exclusive of interest and costs (28 U.S.C. § 1332(d)(2), (d)(6)). That sum is measured at the time of removal and is calculated by aggregating (in the class context) the claims of individual class members; it is not limited by any one plaintiff’s claim.

- Size of class and citizenship. CAFA applies to class actions where the proposed class contains more than 100 members (28 U.S.C. § 1332(d)(5)). CAFA’s jurisdictional inquiries thus focus on the aggregate size and value of the putative class and require minimal diversity.

- Exceptions and special provisions. CAFA contains key exceptions that can defeat federal jurisdiction despite the statutory formula: notably, the “home-state exception” (also called the “local controversy” or “home-state” provision) in § 1332(d)(4) excludes certain class actions in which two-thirds or more of the putative class members are citizens of the state in which the action was filed, and at least one defendant is a citizen of that state, provided other conditions are satisfied. CAFA also includes provisions addressing mass actions, removals involving federal officers or agencies, and procedures for notifying state attorneys general upon removal.

- Removal mechanics under CAFA. CAFA provides a standalone mechanism for removal of class actions, codified in 28 U.S.C. § 1453, which simplifies and clarifies several procedural requirements compared to ordinary removal provisions. Notably, CAFA’s removal statute empowers defendants to remove class actions even where the local-defendant rule (discussed below) might otherwise bar removal, and sets out notice requirements to state attorneys general.

Understanding CAFA requires attention to statutory text and to how courts interpret “amount in controversy,” the timing for measuring the amount, whether the putative class size requirement is satisfied, and when CAFA exceptions apply. Courts typically treat CAFA removals as class-wide aggregations for the $5,000,000 inquiry and will look to the complaint and any reasonable inferences or damages estimates to determine whether the jurisdictional threshold is met.

# Removal procedure and timeliness

Removal is the unilateral statutory mechanism by which a defendant moves a state-court suit into federal court. The principal statutory scheme is in 28 U.S.C. §§ 1441, 1446, and — for class actions — § 1453. The removal process involves several distinct steps: establishing that the case is removable, filing a proper notice of removal within the statutory window, and complying with notice and pleading requirements.

- Who may remove? Generally, defendants may remove civil actions of which federal district courts have original jurisdiction. Where there are multiple defendants, all defendants who have been properly joined and served must join in or consent to removal under the “rule of unanimity,” although CAFA’s removal provision modifies the landscape for class actions. Removal is typically allowed only by defendants; plaintiffs may not remove their own case except in limited circumstances (e.g., where a third-party counterclaim raises federal issues).

- Grounds for removal. The defendant’s notice of removal must contain a short and plain statement of the grounds for removal (§ 1446(a)). The usual grounds are federal-question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 or diversity jurisdiction under § 1332 (including CAFA-based diversity through § 1332(d)). The notice must include a copy of all process, pleadings, and orders served in the state court action.

- Timing: the 30-day rule. For ordinary removals, the primary time trigger is service of the initial pleading. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1446(b)(1), a defendant has 30 days after receipt of the initial pleading (through service or otherwise) to file a notice of removal if the case is then removable. If the case is not initially removable — for example, because a pleading does not make clear that the amount in controversy exceeds the jurisdictional threshold — § 1446(b)(3) allows a defendant to remove within 30 days after it receives “an amended pleading, motion, order or other paper” from which it can first be ascertained that the case is removable. The “other paper” language has driven much litigation over what communications (e.g., settlement demands, claimant interrogatory responses) can suffice to start a new 30-day window.

- One-year limitation. Section 1446(c)(1) imposes an outer limit: when removal is based on diversity jurisdiction, a case may not be removed “on the basis of [diversity] jurisdiction more than 1 year after commencement of the action,” unless the district court finds the plaintiff has acted in bad faith to prevent removal. This one-year bar prevents belated removals in stale cases but can be avoided if the defendant can show bad-faith manipulation.

- The forum-defendant rule. Ordinarily, 28 U.S.C. § 1441(b)(2) bars removal on diversity grounds if any properly joined and served defendant is a citizen of the state in which the action was brought (the “forum-defendant rule” or “local defendant” rule). This rule preserves state-court adjudication when defendants are local citizens. CAFA’s removal provision, however, effectively supersedes the local-defendant bar for qualifying class actions by allowing removal even when certain defendants are citizens of the forum (a tension courts address by applying § 1453(b) governing CAFA removals).

- CAFA-specific procedural rules. For class actions removed under CAFA’s § 1453, the defendant’s notice of removal must contain a short and plain statement of the grounds for removal, a declaration that all required notices have been given, and a copy of all state court papers (parallel to § 1446). CAFA also requires that the removing defendant notify the state attorney general of the removal and the reasons for it. CAFA permits removal by any defendant and relaxes some unanimity and forum-defendant constraints that would otherwise apply.

- Effect of multiple defendants and late-joined defendants. The rule of unanimity ordinarily requires all properly served defendants to join a timely removal. Where additional defendants are later joined or served, the time to remove may run anew for those defendants upon service, and courts evaluate whether the later-served defendants are timely and whether their consent is required. In class litigation, CAFA’s removal scheme can affect the unanimity requirement.

Courts scrutinize removal procedural compliance: failure to include required documents, to obtain co-defendant consent, to notify state attorneys general where required, or to file within applicable time periods can lead to remand for procedural defect. But lack of subject-matter jurisdiction is not a curable procedural defect: if the federal court lacks jurisdiction, the case cannot be retained regardless of procedural compliance.

# Remand standards and burden allocations

Once a case has been removed, the plaintiff may seek remand to state court. Remand practice turns on two separate axes: procedural defects in removal and substantive defects in subject-matter jurisdiction. The statutory scheme provides deadlines and rules allocating burdens.

- Motions to remand: timing rules. Under 28 U.S.C. § 1447(c), a motion to remand the case on any basis other than lack of subject-matter jurisdiction must be made within 30 days after the filing of the notice of removal. This 30-day bar is strictly enforced by courts; a late procedural challenge is typically forfeited. By contrast, lack of subject-matter jurisdiction may be raised at any time, including on appeal or by the court sua sponte. Thus, a plaintiff who fails to timely object to a procedural defect may still challenge the court’s power to hear the case if subject-matter jurisdiction is absent.

- Allocation of burdens. The party asserting federal jurisdiction bears the initial burden of demonstrating that removal was proper. In removal litigation, defendants typically must show by a preponderance of the evidence (or at least by competent proof) that the statutory elements of jurisdiction are satisfied. For diversity-based removal, courts require a showing of complete (or minimal, under CAFA) diversity and that the amount in controversy requirement is met. Courts generally construe removal statutes strictly and resolve doubts in favor of remand: close jurisdictional questions are resolved for remand, reflecting the principle that federal courts are courts of limited jurisdiction and statutory prerequisites must be met.

- Amount in controversy proof. When the complaint does not allege an amount, the defendant may submit evidence (declarations, calculations, estimates, settlement communications) to show by a preponderance of the evidence that the amount in controversy exceeds the jurisdictional threshold. Federal courts generally accept reasonable extrapolations from representative data, but they will not accept wild speculation. In class actions under CAFA, the defendant must show that the aggregated claims of the putative class exceed $5,000,000.

- Procedural defects vs. jurisdictional defects. Procedural defects (e.g., defective removal notice, lack of unanimity, failure to notify state attorneys general, or removal after the one-year bar without a bad-faith finding) are waived if not timely raised. However, lack of subject-matter jurisdiction may be raised at any time; if it exists, it is fatal. Courts will therefore first examine subject-matter jurisdiction; if jurisdiction is present, they will consider timely procedural objections.

- Effect of remand orders. 28 U.S.C. § 1447(d) restricts appellate review of remand orders; in general, remand orders are not reviewable on appeal, subject to narrow statutory exceptions. Practically, this means that a remand order typically ends federal court involvement, and appellate review is limited, so litigants press jurisdictional and procedural arguments vigorously in district court.

Judicial practice: defendants face a rigorous initial task when removing, because courts read removal statutes narrowly and favor remand when the statutory basis is doubtful. Plaintiffs, to avoid having their case heard in federal court, will often (strategically and legitimately) draft complaints to keep amounts below federal thresholds or to join local defendants, and they may resist producing “other paper” that would permit removal.

# Effect of jurisdictional posture on applicable law

Whether a case proceeds in state or federal court can significantly affect substantive and procedural law applied to the dispute. Two principal doctrines govern: the Erie doctrine (and its progeny) on substantive vs. procedural law, and the doctrine that federal courts follow state substantive law in diversity cases but may apply federal procedural rules.

- Erie and its framework. Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins and subsequent decisions establish that a federal court sitting in diversity must apply state substantive law and federal procedural law. The key issue is whether a rule is substantive (binding federal court must apply state law) or procedural (federal practice rules apply). Courts apply a two-step approach: first determine if a federal statute or rule directly conflicts with the state law; second, if no direct conflict, apply the outcome-determinative test and consider whether applying federal rule would lead to forum-shopping or inequitable administration of law. The ultimate aim is to avoid inequities between federal and state fora and to respect state substantive policies.

- Choice-of-law and governed issues in class actions. In class litigation, the posture affects many certification and merits rules. Federal courts sitting in diversity apply state substantive law to the underlying causes of action (e.g., contract, tort, consumer-protection statutes), but they apply federal procedural rules such as Rule 23’s requirements for class certification. This can create tension: certification decisions may be influenced by the content of state law (e.g., typicality or predominance inquiries in Rule 23(b)(3)), and the federal court’s interpretation of state law for the purposes of class-wide relief often leads to substantial pretrial rulings on matters that might have been left for state courts.

- Preclusion and collateral estoppel. A federal court’s judgment will have preclusive effect under federal law, but where federal jurisdiction is based on diversity, state preclusion law governs whether a decision is given preclusive effect in subsequent state proceedings. The posture of the case thus matters for how broadly a class-wide judgment will bind nonparties and absent class members.

- Federal statutes and preemption. Removal may introduce federal defenses or preemption arguments; removal itself does not convert state-law claims into federal ones, but a federal court will resolve federal preemption arguments that might not have been decided in state court. CAFA added special federal procedural provisions and federal appellate access in limited circumstances, and federal class procedures can shape the substantive remedies that are practically available.

- Strategic implications. Because federal courts may handle class action discovery, certification, and settlement approval differently from state courts, parties often view removal as a choice of a different procedural regime. Plaintiffs may prefer state courts for perceived advantage in state-substantive claims, while defendants may prefer federal courts for a uniform national forum or perceived stricter certification scrutiny. The jurisdictional posture thus bears directly on litigation strategy, the prospect of nationwide relief, and the substantive outcomes for class members.

# Supplemental jurisdiction and party alignment issues

Supplemental jurisdiction and the rules for determining party alignment are crucial in multi-party and multi-claim litigation, particularly when plaintiffs attempt to join additional parties or claims that might remove or defeat federal jurisdiction.

- Supplemental jurisdiction general rule. 28 U.S.C. § 1367(a) grants federal courts supplemental jurisdiction over claims that are so related to the claims within the court’s original jurisdiction that they form part of the same case or controversy under Article III, and that would ordinarily be expected to be tried together. Supplemental jurisdiction allows federal courts to resolve state-law claims joined with federal claims in a single proceeding for efficiency and consistency.

- Limits on supplemental jurisdiction in diversity cases. Congress carved out limits to supplemental jurisdiction in diversity cases in § 1367(b). For cases where original jurisdiction is based solely on diversity (§ 1332), § 1367(b) precludes supplemental jurisdiction over claims by plaintiffs seeking to intervene or be joined as plaintiffs whose joinder would be inconsistent with the requirements of diversity jurisdiction (e.g., defeating complete diversity). The statute’s aim is to prevent plaintiffs from circumventing diversity requirements by adding parties through permissive joinder or interventions that would otherwise destroy diversity. Put differently, a plaintiff cannot use supplemental jurisdiction to add claimants whose citizenship would destroy complete diversity in a diversity-founded case. CAFA, however, operates in the class context and generally turns on minimal diversity and aggregate amount, so § 1367(b) has different implications for CAFA-removed matters.

- Names and alignments of parties: legal versus functional alignment. Determining a party’s alignment as plaintiff or defendant is sometimes more than a formal exercise: courts look to the real-party-in-interest and the nature of the claims and defenses. In some cases, a nominal defendant may be effectively aligned with plaintiffs; courts will realign parties for diversity-jurisdiction purposes or for removal analysis if the nominal alignment masks the substantive interests. This is especially important when parties are added or removed, and when plaintiffs attempt to name nominal or indispensable parties to avoid removal.

- Citizenship of class members vs. representatives. In class actions, determining diversity depends on which citizens count. Under CAFA, minimal diversity requires that any class member be of diverse citizenship from any defendant — courts typically look to class representatives’ citizenship for certain jurisdictional questions but will be attentive to the citizenship of unnamed class members where appropriate. For ordinary diversity cases, the citizenship of a representative plaintiff is sufficient to test diversity, but joinder rules and supplemental jurisdiction limits can complicate whether additional plaintiffs’ citizenship is considered.

- Pleading and procedural maneuvers. Plaintiffs cannot defeat removal by pleading artfully — that is, by masking federal claims or by manipulating citizenship allegations in ways that are obviously contrived. Likewise, defendants cannot manufacture diversity by misaligning parties. Courts scrutinize manipulative pleadings to determine real-party interests and to prevent jurisdictional gamesmanship.

- Indispensable and indispensable-party doctrine. Federal courts may examine whether nonjoined parties are indispensable under Rule 19 and whether their absence prohibits federal adjudication; however, the indispensable-party analysis operates after jurisdiction is established and can result in dismissal or stays rather than remand. When joining necessary parties would destroy diversity, § 1367(b) and removal doctrines limit the court’s power to exercise supplemental jurisdiction to retain claims.

Practical implications: plaintiffs may attempt to join local defendants or additional plaintiffs to keep a case in state court, while defendants might remove early or contest joinder to keep the case federal. CAFA reduces some of these tactics in the class context by permitting removal on minimal diversity and aggregate-amount grounds, but courts remain attentive to manipulative joinder and alignment.

Conclusion

Class removal doctrine is a layered body of law combining the classic jurisdictional rules with CAFA’s special provisions and the general mechanics of removal and remand. A sound understanding requires mastery of the statutory text (particularly 28 U.S.C. §§ 1332, 1367, 1441, 1446, 1447, 1453), the allocation of burdens at removal and remand, the timing rules for filing and contesting removal, and the interplay between federal and state substantive law once jurisdiction is vested. Courts will probe both the formal sufficiency of removal and the substantive indicia of jurisdictional prerequisites, and litigants should plan pleadings, joinder strategies, and evidence-gathering with an eye to how these doctrines operate in class contexts.
