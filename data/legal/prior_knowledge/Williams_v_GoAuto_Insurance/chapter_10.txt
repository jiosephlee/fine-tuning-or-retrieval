Introduction

Federal courts sitting in diversity confront a recurrent and substantial task: they must decide cases under the substantive law of the state in which they sit, yet federal procedure and federal adjudicative processes govern much of the litigation. This chapter explains the foundational principles that govern how a federal court applies, interprets, and predicts state law in a diversity case. It emphasizes the doctrine that requires federal courts to follow state substantive law, the role and hierarchy of state legal authorities—with special attention to Louisiana sources of law—and the interpretive and practical tools a federal judge uses when faced with an unsettled question of state law. The analysis ties together Erie-related principles, choice-of-law rules, the persuasive value of intermediate appellate decisions, the circumstances that prompt a federal “Erie guess,” and the scope and limits of deference when state law is unclear.

# Erie doctrine and its application to substantive state law

The Erie doctrine embodies the principle that, in diversity cases, federal courts must apply state substantive law rather than prescribe a federal common law. The doctrine rests on two interrelated policies: respect for state lawmaking authority and the avoidance of inequitable administration of the laws that would encourage forum shopping. Under this framework, the federal judge must determine whether a legal rule at issue is substantive (and therefore supplied by state law) or procedural (and therefore governed by federal law, typically the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure or federal common law governing federal jurisdiction).

At its core, the Erie inquiry requires a federal court to identify the applicable state law rule and to apply it as the state’s courts would. When a state supreme court has addressed the point, that decision is binding. When it has not, the federal court must make an informed prediction of what the state’s highest court would hold. This prediction may rely on intermediate appellate decisions, lower court decisions, statutes, state constitution provisions, and secondary authority. In making such predictions the federal court must follow the interpretive norms and methodological constraints used by the state judiciary: the federal judge should not substitute federal policy judgments for state law determinations.

A central complication is the substance-procedure distinction. While the line is not always sharp, courts ask whether applying a federal rule rather than a state rule would outcome-determinatively affect the case or promote forum shopping. If so, the state rule is more likely to be treated as substantive. Federal procedural rules that directly conflict with state law will be applied if they are valid exercises of Rulemaking power and are arguably procedural in nature, but where the federal rule is arguably substantive or conflicts in a way that would substantially alter parties’ rights, the state rule may control. Modern doctrine views the inquiry as pragmatic and guided by twin aims: preventing forum shopping and avoiding inequitable administration of the laws.

Choice-of-law rules themselves are treated as substantive state law for Erie purposes. That is, the state’s own rules for selecting governing law—relevant in multi-state disputes—must be applied by the federal court as a matter of state law. Where a choice-of-law determination is required, the federal court must use the same choice-of-law doctrine and apply it as a state court would, so that the identity of the applicable law is determined by state substantive law rather than federal free-form policy.

# Hierarchy of Louisiana authorities: statutes, Civil Code, Supreme Court, appellate courts

Every state has a hierarchy of legal authorities, and Louisiana’s is distinctive because it is a civil-law jurisdiction with a Civil Code at the center of its legal system. Understanding that hierarchy is essential for a federal court predicting Louisiana law.

At the top sits the Louisiana Constitution and the statutes enacted by the state legislature. The Civil Code plays a foundational role in private law areas—obligations, property, contracts, and successions. The Civil Code contains codal articles that establish general principles and rules; statutory enactments can modify, supplement, or provide procedural elaborations around codal norms. In interpreting codal articles, Louisiana courts use ajudication that often emphasizes codal text, legislative intent, and the civil-law tradition of general principles.

The Louisiana Supreme Court is the final arbiter of state law. Its pronouncements on legal questions bind all lower Louisiana courts and bind federal courts applying Louisiana substantive law. Where the Louisiana Supreme Court has rendered a decision on a question, that decision sets controlling state law.

Below the Supreme Court are the Louisiana Courts of Appeal. Those intermediate appellate courts decide the majority of questions that arise in litigation. A Court of Appeal’s decision is controlling on a matter within its jurisdiction only as between the parties, and it does not bind other appellate panels, other Circuits, or the Supreme Court. Nevertheless, Louisiana employs jurisprudence constante as an interpretive practice: when a consistent line of decisions from the appellate courts develops—especially when multiple circuits reach the same conclusion—such consensus can develop binding force in practice and can be treated by the Supreme Court as persuasive and oftentimes determinative of the correct interpretation of codal or statutory provisions.

Trial court opinions in Louisiana are not binding precedent, although they can be persuasive on factual or legal matters. Secondary sources—treatises, law review articles, and restatements—have heuristic value but are not authoritative unless incorporated into statutes or cited by the Louisiana Supreme Court.

For federal courts, the operational hierarchy is: applicable Louisiana constitutional provisions and statutes (including Civil Code articles), Louisiana Supreme Court decisions, persuasive and sometimes controlling lines of Court of Appeal authority (especially where jurisprudence constante exists), and then trial court decisions and secondary sources.

# Using intermediate appellate decisions as persuasive evidence of state law

When the state supreme court has not spoken on an issue, intermediate appellate decisions from state courts often provide the most direct evidence of what state law is or should be. Federal courts treating these decisions must evaluate their persuasive force. Several factors influence the weight a federal court accords to intermediate appellate authority.

First, the consistency and number of appellate decisions matter. A solitary appellate decision is less persuasive than a long, reasoned, and consistent line of appellate rulings. When appellate panels within the same state are unanimous over time—or when multiple circuits within the state reach the same conclusion—federal courts are justified in treating that body of decisions as an authoritative representation of how the state supreme court would decide. This is especially so in a civil-law jurisdiction like Louisiana, where jurisprudence constante can themselves create binding expectations.

Second, the reasoning quality in the appellate decisions matters. A decision that thoroughly analyzes statutory or codal language, considers legislative intent, and fits within the broader doctrinal context of state law is more persuasive than a poorly reasoned or cursory opinion. Federal judges should closely examine the rationale of intermediate decisions, not merely their holdings, because the rationale indicates whether the state supreme court might accept or reject the reasoning.

Third, the relevance of the facts matters. Appellate decisions that address materially similar facts, legal theories, and statutory provisions will have stronger predictive value than decisions that differ in crucial respects. A federal court should identify any factual or doctrinal distinctions and carefully assess whether those distinctions explain differences in outcome.

Fourth, temporal and doctrinal context influence persuasiveness. More recent decisions that reflect modern statutory amendments, changed social contexts, or updated precedent carry more weight. Conversely, older appellate decisions may be less persuasive if they rest on doctrinal assumptions that have been overtly or implicitly modified by the state supreme court or by legislative change.

Finally, conflicting appellate authority poses special problems. When appellate courts are divided, federal courts must make an independent judgment: they may follow the line they find better reasoned, balance the circuits’ trends, or, when practical and available, certify the question to the state supreme court. A federal court should not mechanically apply a single appellate panel’s view if the balance of authority and reasoning suggests a different outcome would better reflect the state law as the state’s highest court would pronounce it.

# When federal courts make an ‘Erie guess’

The phrase “Erie guess” denotes a federal court’s task of predicting how a state supreme court would rule on an unsettled question of state law. Making an Erie guess is an exercise in interpretive humility and careful legal analysis. The federal court must synthesize state statutory texts, codal structure, constitutional principles, intermediate appellate decisions, persuasive secondary sources, and general principles of the state’s jurisprudential approach to arrive at the best-possible projection.

The process typically unfolds in stages. First, the court identifies the governing state sources: if a codal article or statute addresses the issue, the court construes that text according to state interpretive norms—textual analysis, legislative history, and principle-based reasoning characteristic of the state’s courts. Second, the court surveys appellate decisions for guidance, paying special attention to whether there is jurisprudence constante or a majority rule among the circuits. Third, where statutes or codes are silent or ambiguous, the court looks to the underlying values and doctrines that animate the state law area (for example, contractual freedom in contract law, or protection of third parties in tort). Fourth, the court gives weight to persuasive authority from other jurisdictions only insofar as those courts share similar legal sources and reasoning methods; in civil-law jurisdictions, analogies to sister civil-law states are more predictive than comparisons to common-law states.

Several procedural and pragmatic considerations shape whether and how a federal court will make an Erie guess. If the question is dispositive and outcome-determinative, the federal court should prioritize accuracy in predicting the state rule. If the issue is collateral or unlikely to affect the ultimate outcome, the court may resolve it with less elaborate Erie analysis, aiming only to approximate state law. Where the state supreme court has a certification procedure and the question is novel and significant to state law, a federal court should consider certifying the question; certification promotes state-law uniformity and respects the state supreme court’s role in articulating its law.

Certification is not always available or appropriate. The decision to certify depends on state rules governing certification, the likelihood that the state court will accept the question, and the practicalities of the litigation (e.g., time considerations, cost, or whether the case can be resolved without the state court’s input). When certification is refused or unavailable, the federal court must proceed with its best Erie guess, documenting the basis for its prediction and explaining how it weighed competing authorities.

The federal judge making an Erie guess must also be vigilant about the limits of federal decisional power: the judge should not create a rule rooted in federal policy preferences or federal procedural advantages but should attempt to ground the decision in principles that a state court would plausibly adopt.

# Deference and limits when state law is unsettled

Deference to state law sources is a central requirement, but it is not unconditional. The federal court must balance deference with its obligation to resolve the case. There are important limits and boundaries in the Erie context.

First, federal courts must defer to the state supreme court’s decisions. If the state supreme court has decided the issue, that decision is binding even if the federal court would prefer a different rule. This creates a clear line of deference where it is strongest.

Second, when intermediate appellate courts have addressed the issue, federal courts must carefully evaluate the weight of that authority. While intermediate decisions are persuasive, they are not binding on the federal court in the formal sense—unless the state treats a consistent appellate line as effectively binding. In Louisiana, jurisprudence constante can, over time, take on a binding character: if the appellate courts have repeatedly and uniformly stated a rule, that consensus can be considered the best evidence of state law. Federal courts should recognize that dynamic and give such consistent lines of appellate authority significant deference.

Third, the federal court must avoid substituting federal judgment for state policy. Erie forbids the creation of federal general common law on matters of traditional state concern. Even when state law is unsettled, the federal court’s reasoning should be couched in terms of what the state’s judicial institutions would likely adopt, not what federal policy or federal courts would prefer.

Fourth, there are practical limits to fidelity when the state law question intersects with federal procedural rules that are valid and intended to govern practice in federal courts. Where a Federal Rule of Civil Procedure is applicable and within the scope of the rulemaking power, federal courts will apply it. The interplay between such federal procedural rules and state law can be complex; courts analyze whether the federal rule is arguably procedural and whether applying it would supplant substantive state rights in a manner that undermines Erie’s aims.

Fifth, when intermediate appellate courts conflict or the body of authority is ambiguous, federal judges must make reasoned choices. They can follow the majority of appellate courts, the opinion it believes best aligns with state law principles, or make an Erie guess. If the issue is of statewide importance or the decision is likely to cause significant divergence in how state law develops, courts should consider certification. Federal courts should explain their choices transparently so that appellate review can assess whether the prediction reasonably reflects the state law.

Finally, the deference owed to state law is constrained by the federal court’s duty to apply federal constitutional principles. If a state-law rule, as interpreted by state sources, would violate the federal Constitution, the federal court must apply constitutional limits. This tension is not unique to Erie analysis but serves as a boundary: federal courts cannot adopt a state-law rule to the extent that it conflicts with the federal Constitution.

Conclusion

A federal court sitting in diversity operates within a layered set of obligations: to apply state substantive law, to respect the hierarchy of state authorities, and to predict as accurately as possible how the state’s highest court would decide unsettled questions. In Louisiana, the Civil Code and the Louisiana Supreme Court occupy central roles, while appellate consensus—jurisprudence constante—can decisively shape the content of state law. Intermediate appellate decisions are powerful tools for prediction but must be weighed for consistency, reasoning quality, and factual alignment. When state law is unsettled, federal courts make Erie guesses guided by state interpretive practices, pragmatic concerns about forum shopping and equitable administration of the law, and a readiness to certify questions to state courts when appropriate. Throughout, the federal judge must avoid importing federal policy preferences, instead seeking the most faithful projection of state law consistent with the Constitution and federal procedural norms.
