Introduction

This chapter explains foundational principles of Louisiana agency law—particularly the law of mandate (power of attorney)—as they relate to a finance company’s authority to cancel an insurance policy under a power of attorney in a premium-financing context. Before reading judicial opinions that turn on these issues, a student must understand how mandates are created, how scope and authority are determined and proven, the legal effect of acts taken under a mandate on principals and third parties, the ways authority may be limited or revoked, and how contractual power‑of‑attorney provisions interact with statutory requirements governing insurance and other regulated transactions.

The discussion is doctrinal rather than case-specific. It emphasizes legal rules, typical tests courts apply, the allocation of burdens, and the practical consequences of procedural posture (for example, what a plaintiff must prove at summary judgment or at trial). Although many of the same underlying principles apply in other jurisdictions, the material is framed to reflect Louisiana’s civil‑law conception of mandate and principals’ obligations.

# Mandate (Power of Attorney) under the Louisiana Civil Code

In Louisiana law the institution of private agency is principally embodied in the doctrine of mandate. A mandate is a juridical act by which one person (the principal or mandator) confers authority on another person (the mandatary, agent, or attorney‑in‑fact) to transact affairs on the principal’s behalf and in the principal’s name. The mandate creates a consensual relationship: the mandatary undertakes to perform one or more acts for and on behalf of the principal, and the principal is bound by lawful acts of the mandatary within the scope of the mandate.

A mandate may be general or special. A general mandate confers broad authority to manage the principal’s affairs; a special mandate authorizes performance of a specific transaction or limited category of acts. Louisiana treats mandates as contractual in nature: they arise by agreement, whether express or implied, and carry with them duties of care and honesty owed by the mandatary to the principal.

Certain mandates must be reduced to writing under statute or the Civil Code where formality is required for effectiveness or for evidentiary purposes, particularly in transactions involving immovables or where the principal’s signature must be authenticated. Insurance cancellations and premium financing arrangements often involve consumer protection statutes or insurance code provisions that either prescribe particular form requirements for notice or acknowledge the effect of a power of attorney in relation to contractual or statutory duties. It is therefore critical to distinguish between the internal validity of a mandate as a consensual act between principal and mandatary and the externally imposed formalities required for the mandate to have legal effect vis‑à‑vis third parties or certain regulated transactions.

# Creation, Scope, and Proof of Agency Authority

Creation. The essential elements required to create a mandate are principally consent and capacity. The principal must have contractual capacity; the mandatary must accept the authority. The parties manifest consent by words or conduct. A mandate may be express—created by an explicit grant of authority in writing or orally—or implied from the parties’ conduct and surrounding circumstances when the mandatary undertakes to act and the principal acquiesces or benefits from those acts.

Scope. The scope of authority is defined by the terms of the mandate, whether express or implied, and by the nature of the transaction. Courts interpret mandates under ordinary rules of contractual interpretation: (1) words are given their general and ordinary meaning; (2) specific provisions govern general ones; (3) the intent of the parties, when properly ascertained, controls. Where a mandate is ambiguous, the ordinary presumption favors a narrow construction: ambiguous language is construed against the mandatary and in favor of the principal unless the language, the transaction, or the principal’s conduct indicates otherwise.

Authority categories. It is useful to classify agency authority into express authority, implied authority, and apparent authority.

- Express authority is the power specifically granted by the principal to the mandatary. If a mandate expressly authorizes cancellation of insurance policies or settlement of claims, the mandatary may act pursuant to that express grant.

- Implied authority includes powers reasonably necessary to accomplish express authorities. For example, where a mandatary has authority to manage an insured’s affairs and to pay premiums, a court might find implied authority to communicate with an insurer about cancellations that relate to premium funding—so long as those communications are incidental and consistent with the principal’s objectives.

- Apparent authority (authority by estoppel) arises when a third party reasonably believes, based on the principal’s manifestations, that the mandatary has authority, and the third party acts in reliance on that belief. Apparent authority focuses on the principal’s conduct toward third parties, not on the mandatary’s internal relationship with the principal.

Proof. A party asserting that an agent had authority bears the burden of proving that authority. In civil litigation the standard is the preponderance of the evidence. Proof may be documentary and/or testimonial. Documentary evidence commonly includes a written power of attorney, acknowledgment or notarial act, corporate resolutions authorizing an officer to execute a mandate, and correspondence bearing the mandator’s signature or authorization. Courts give special weight to authentic acts and notarized instruments because they provide strong evidence of the principal’s consent. Nevertheless, not every valid mandate requires notarization; authenticity primarily affects the relative strength of proof, admissibility, and third‑party reliance rather than the existence of authority.

When the authority is denied by the principal or the principal’s successors in interest, the mandatary’s routine proof is an executed power of attorney (preferably in the form required by statute or the third party’s policies), copies of written notices, and evidence that the mandatary acted within the scope of the granted authority. If the mandate is oral or inferred, proof may require testimony showing manifestations of consent and the circumstances that caused the principal to be bound by the mandatary’s acts.

Evidentiary presumptions. Louisiana jurisprudence sometimes recognizes evidentiary presumptions that an agent who holds a written power of attorney, especially an original or notarized instrument, has the authority it purports to grant. However, such presumptions are rebuttable, and opposing parties may present contrary evidence (for example, proof of revocation or mental incapacity). In litigation, the procedural posture matters: at summary judgment, convincing documentary proof of valid authority may suffice to shift the burden to an opponent to show a genuine issue of material fact; at trial the trier of fact resolves credibility and the weight of conflicting evidence.

# Acts Taken Under a Mandate and Effect on Third Parties

Binding effect on principal. When a mandatary acts within the scope of authority conferred by the principal, the principal is bound by those acts. This binding effect rests on the consensual premise that the principal authorized the mandatary to act on the principal’s behalf. The operative rule is that third parties dealing in good faith with a mandatary exercising authority within that scope are bound by the mandatary’s acts, and the principal cannot deny liability for acts falling within the grant.

Third‑party protection and good faith. Louisiana law protects third parties who reasonably rely on a mandatary’s apparent or express authority. If a third party acts in good faith, believing the mandatary has authority based on the principal’s manifestation (including delivery of a written and authenticated power of attorney), the third party generally may enforce the transaction against the principal even if the mandatary exceeded actual authority, unless the principal gave direct notice to the third party of the lack or limitation of authority.

Effect of noncompliance with formalities. In some contexts, statutes or contractual terms require specific formalities for certain acts. For example, insurance contracts or insurance regulation may require notices of cancellation to be in writing, to be signed by the insured or the insurer, or to be delivered in a specified manner. If the mandatary performs an act without satisfying form requirements that are conditions precedent to the act’s legal effect, a court may rule the act ineffective as against the principal or third parties. Conversely, if the mandate itself was executed in accordance with the formalities and the third party receives documentation that indicates the mandatary’s authorization, many courts will enforce the transaction against the principal absent clear evidence of revocation or incapacity.

Transactions with regulated third parties. When the third party is a regulated entity—such as an insurer, a financial institution, or a governmental office—the statutory scheme governing the regulated transaction may alter the usual agency rules. For example, insurance statutes often specify who may cancel policies, what notice is required to the policyholder, how refunds or return premiums are handled, and what form of authorization is necessary for an agent to act on behalf of an insured. A mandatary’s act that otherwise would bind the principal can be ineffective or subject the third party to regulatory penalties if the regulated transaction has statutory prerequisites that were not met.

Effect of exceeding authority (ultra vires acts). If a mandatary exceeds the scope of authority, the third party may not be able to hold the principal responsible for the mandatary’s ultra vires acts—unless the third party reasonably relied on the mandatary’s apparent authority and the principal’s conduct induced that reliance. But an ultra vires act that nonetheless is ratified by the principal after the fact retroactively validates the mandatary’s act. Ratification requires the principal, with knowledge of all material facts, to adopt the act in a manner sufficient to constitute assent.

Procedure and burdens in disputes. In litigation challenging the effectiveness of a mandatary’s act (for example, a cancellation of insurance coverage), the plaintiff typically must prove that the mandatary had authority and that the act complied with contractual and statutory requirements. The defendant mandatary or third party may assert defenses such as lack of authority, revocation, or defects in notice or form. If the plaintiff presents prima facie documentary proof of an apparently valid power of attorney and of the act in question, the burden shifts to the party alleging invalidity to produce contrary evidence.

# Limitations and Revocation of Authority

Limitations on authority. The mandate may contain express limitations—terms that restrict the scope or methods of the mandatary’s action. Such limitations are binding on the mandatary and generally enforceable against the principal. Judicial interpretation gives effect to express limitations unless they contravene law or public policy. In addition to express limits, common law or statutory limits may circumscribe mandatary power. For example, the law may prohibit an agent from performing certain personal acts that are nondelegable (acts that only the principal can lawfully perform), or statutes may require the principal’s signature or notice for certain transactions.

Durable and non‑durable mandates. A standard distinction is whether the mandate is durable (survives the principal’s incapacity) or non‑durable (terminates upon the principal’s incapacity). Louisiana law recognizes that a mandate ordinarily terminates upon the principal’s death or judicial declaration of incapacity, unless the parties clearly expressed an intention that the mandate continue beyond such events (a durable power). If a power is expressed to be “durable” or to continue despite subsequent incapacity or disability, the mandatary may continue to act, subject to other legal limits.

Revocation. The principal may revoke a mandate at any time, absent contractually agreed irrevocability, and revocation takes effect when communicated to the mandatary and (in some circumstances) to third parties whose rights will be affected. Revocation may be express, by written or oral communication, or implied by the principal’s conduct inconsistent with the continuation of agency authority (for example, the principal’s direct exercise of the right that had been delegated to the mandatary). Notice to third parties is critical where the mandatary has already acted or where third parties rely on existing representations of authority; a revocation not made known to third parties does not defeat the rights of a third party who already relied in good faith on the mandatary’s apparent authority.

Death and incapacity. The mandate generally terminates on the principal’s death unless the mandate expressly provides otherwise in a form valid to create a durable authority. Similarly, judicial interdiction or incapacity terminates ordinary mandates, although a durable mandate may survive if clearly drafted and permissible under statutory rules. When death occurs, the mandatary may be required to account for acts performed and to transfer any property in the mandatary’s custody back to the estate or the appointed personal representative.

Irrevocable mandates. A principal and mandatary may agree that a mandate is irrevocable, but under most circumstances such an agreement is strictly construed; the law disfavors agreements that prevent a principal from revoking because of public policy considerations protecting personal autonomy. Notwithstanding an agreement, a court may permit revocation where exceptional circumstances or statutory considerations require it.

Judicial remedies. If a mandatary acts beyond authority, wrongfully refuses to act, or misapplies funds, the principal (or the principal’s successors) may sue for breach of mandate, accounting, conversion, or other equitable remedies. Third parties who suffer injury from a mandatary’s wrongful acts may have tort or contract remedies against the mandatary and, in certain cases, against the principal if the acts were within the scope of authority or ratified.

# Interaction Between Contractual POA Clauses and Statutory Requirements

Contracts often include power‑of‑attorney clauses that purport to authorize one party (frequently a finance company or broker) to act for another in narrowly defined circumstances—for example, to cancel insurance coverage, to endorse premium refunds, or to sign notices and documents necessary to effect policy changes. These contractual clauses raise two important legal questions: (1) the extent to which a contractual POA, standing alone, creates binding authority vis‑à‑vis the principal and third parties; and (2) whether the contractual POA must comply with separate statutory or regulatory requirements for the act to have legal effect.

Contractual POA as internal authorization. A contractual POA is prima facie a valid mandate between the contracting parties. It grants internal authority to the mandatary to perform specified acts and may describe the method, scope, and conditions for exercising that authority. Courts generally give effect to a contractual POA as a consensual device: when a principal signs a contract authorizing another to cancel insurance on its behalf, the mandatary may undertake that function for the principal, and the principal is bound by acts within the contractual authorization.

Limitations imposed by statute and regulation. Regulated transactions—most notably those governed by the insurance code, consumer finance statutes, and banking law—impose requirements that can limit the effect of a contractual POA against third parties and sometimes against the principal. For example, certain statutes may require that a notice of cancellation be signed by the named insured or a named authorized agent, or that cancellation notices be delivered in a specified manner to be effective. Some statutes also prescribe consumer protections (such as mandatory notification periods, restrictions on reinstatement, or rules on premium refunds) that cannot be contracted away. If a contractual POA purports to allow a mandatary to take an act that a statute forbids or to evade statutorily mandated protections, the contractual POA will not control against the statutory policy and may be held unenforceable to the extent it conflicts with the statute.

Specificity and form. Statutes sometimes prescribe the form that a power of attorney must take to be relied upon by regulated third parties. This might include requiring an original signed document or a notarial act, requiring specific language, or requiring recorded or filed instruments where interests in immovable property are affected. When a regulated party (such as an insurer) conditions its acceptance of a mandatary’s authority on strict compliance with particular formalities, the absence of those formalities may allow the insurer to refuse to recognize the mandatary’s acts without violating agency doctrine. Consequently, careful scrutiny of both the contractual POA’s language and the statutory requirements for the pertinent transaction is necessary.

Third‑party policies and industry practices. Industry practices and third‑party policies also shape how contractual POAs function. Insurers and financial institutions often adopt internal procedures—such as requiring verification of POAs, requiring original documents, or insisting on specific notices—that reflect regulatory compliance and risk management. A mandatary who fails to satisfy a third party’s verifiable procedural requirements may find the third party declines to recognize the POA, even if the internal contract authorizes the mandatary. Courts will look to whether the third party’s requirements were reasonable, consistently applied, and in accordance with statute or the contract’s terms.

Conflicts between contract and statute: priority. When contractual POA language conflicts with controlling statutes, the statute governs. A contractual POA cannot create rights that contravene statutory mandates or impute authority that the law reserves to the principal. In some situations, statutes may nonetheless permit limited delegations that are binding on third parties when the delegation meets statutory criteria—for example, when the law specifically authorizes cancellation by a designated agent or when the statute provides a safe harbor for actions taken by an agent who presents an authenticated power of attorney.

Interaction with assignments and subrogation. Contractual POAs in premium‑financing arrangements are frequently part of a web of collateral and assignment provisions—finance agreements, escrow arrangements, endorsements, and subrogation rights. Whether a mandatary may cancel coverage or receive premium refunds depends on how those related agreements allocate rights. A typical premium finance agreement may assign the financed premiums and authorize the finance company to pay premiums or cancel policies upon default. Courts must examine these documents in concert with agency law and statutory obligations. Assignments of proceeds or endorsements may enable a finance company to assert a right to return premiums; however, the enforceability of those rights often depends on strict compliance with statutory notice and form requirements.

Practical considerations for litigants. In litigation, parties disputing the scope or effect of a contractual POA should preserve documentary evidence of the POA itself, any relevant statutory texts, communications related to the exercise of authority, and the third party’s internal policies or requirements. Because courts give considerable weight to formalities and to the presence or absence of notice to third parties, litigants should be prepared to show how the mandatary's acts conformed to both the contractual language and the statutory-regulatory framework governing the transaction at issue.

Conclusion

Understanding mandate law in Louisiana requires attention to the consensual basis of agency, the categories of authority (express, implied, and apparent), the standards for proof, and the practical effects of acts taken under a mandate on principals and third parties. Students should appreciate that while a contractual power of attorney creates internal authority, its external effectiveness can be limited by statutory requirements, transaction‑specific formality rules, industry practices, and public policy. In procedural settings, documentary proof (especially notarized instruments) substantially influences the outcome, but such proof is rebuttable; revocation, incapacity, or statutory noncompliance are common grounds for contesting a mandatary’s acts. These doctrinal points form the necessary background for analyzing judicial opinions that address whether a finance company’s exercise of a POA to cancel an insurance policy was valid, effective, or enforceable.
