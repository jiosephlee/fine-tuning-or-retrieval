Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Jaison Coleman

### Is the scope of consent a factual question reviewed for clear error, a legal question reviewed de novo, or mixed—and how does Jimeno fit with Seventh Circuit precedent?
Question:
The opinion states that both voluntariness and scope are reviewed for clear error. But other cases treat the interpretation of consent’s scope as an objective legal question (subject to de novo review) applied to historical facts. In the Seventh Circuit, what is the controlling standard for reviewing scope determinations, and how does the court reconcile that with Jimeno’s "objective reasonableness" formulation?
Answer:
Short answer
In the Seventh Circuit (as shown in this opinion) both voluntariness and the scope of consent are factual findings reviewed for clear error. Jimeno supplies the legal standard—an objective-reasonableness test for what a typical person would have understood—but applying that test to the particular exchange is a factbound inquiry for the district court. The panel states both points explicitly: Jimeno gives the “proper inquiry,” and “our role as a court of review” is to review scope and voluntariness “for clear error.” (See II.A).

Why and how the two fit together
1. Two different levels of analysis, both present in the opinion
   - Legal standard: The opinion quotes Jimeno for the governing legal test: the scope question asks “what would the typical reasonable person have understood by the exchange between the officer and the person giving consent?” (II.A). That is the legal definition of the scope inquiry — an objective test.
   - Factual application and review: Immediately after stating Jimeno, the opinion says the court “review[s] a district court’s finding of voluntary consent and any challenge to its scope for clear error.” (II.A). Thus the legal standard is Jimeno’s objective test, but the district court’s ultimate conclusion about what that standard produces on the facts is a factual finding.

2. How that plays out in practice
   - The district court hears the evidence (here, body-cam video), makes the primary finding whether the exchange would have been understood to permit the officers’ actions, and resolves credibility/emphasis/totality-of-the-circumstances questions (e.g., tone, prior denial, timing).
   - On appeal the Seventh Circuit asks whether the district court’s resolution of those factual matters — and its ultimate finding that the scope was not exceeded — was clearly erroneous. In this opinion the panel applied Jimeno’s objective test to describe the right legal lens, then deferred to the district court’s factual finding that, under that test, the officers acted within the consent’s scope. (II.B).

Practical takeaway for Seventh Circuit appeals
   - You should treat the underlying legal standard as Jimeno’s objective-reasonableness test, but expect that disputes about whether a particular exchange satisfied that test will be reviewed for clear error. Appellate argument thus should focus on showing the district court’s factfinding was implausible in light of the record (e.g., the record cannot support the judge’s view of what a reasonable person would have understood), because pure de novo re-interpretation of the scope is not the controlling Seventh Circuit approach.

Relevant citations from the opinion
   - “The proper inquiry for evaluating the scope of consent is ‘objective reasonableness’… Florida v. Jimeno….” (II.A).
   - “As a court of review, our role is more limited. We review a district court’s finding of voluntary consent and any challenge to its scope for clear error.” (II.A).
   - Application: the court applied Jimeno’s standard to the video record but affirmed the district court’s factual finding that the officers “respected the consent” and did not exceed its scope. (II.B).

That is the controlling Seventh Circuit position reflected in this opinion: Jimeno supplies the legal rule; the district court applies it to the facts; the circuit reviews the resulting factual determination for clear error.
