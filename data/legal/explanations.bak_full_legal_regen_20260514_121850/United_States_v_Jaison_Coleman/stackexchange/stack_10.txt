Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Jaison Coleman

### Was a protective sweep under Maryland v. Buie necessary, or is consent alone sufficient to justify the officers’ canvass to locate Coleman and the children?
Question:
Once inside, the officers located occupants and asked questions. The panel frames this as within the scope of consent for a welfare check, not as a Buie protective sweep incident to arrest. Do officers need a Buie justification to move room-to-room if consent was given for a welfare check, or does consent independently authorize that limited canvassing? What doctrinal distinctions matter between a consent-based walk-through and a protective sweep?
Answer:
Short answer: Consent alone was sufficient here; the panel treated the officers’ room‑to‑room canvass as within the scope of Lisa’s voluntary consent to a welfare check, so the court did not need (and did not require) a Buie protective‑sweep justification.

Why — doctrinally and as the opinion explains

- Consent is an independent Fourth Amendment exception to the warrant requirement. The opinion applies the ordinary consent rules (Matlock, Bumper, Schneckloth) and asks whether Lisa’s agreement to let officers inside was freely and voluntarily given (see II.A, citing Schneckloth and the Figueroa‑Espana factors). The court upheld the district court’s finding of voluntary consent based on the video: Officer Kizer’s tone, Lisa’s initial refusal and later affirmative “Okay,” lack of custody or coercion, and the brief interaction (II.B).

- Scope of consent is an objective‑reasonableness inquiry. The panel relied on Jimeno’s “what would a reasonable person have understood” test: because the stated purpose of entry was a welfare check for the children and to determine whether Coleman posed a danger, a limited scan to locate people and ask basic questions “necessarily required the police to scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions” (II.B). On that basis the court concluded the officers stayed within the scope of Lisa’s consent.

- Buie is a different doctrine with a different trigger. A Buie protective sweep is justified when officers, incident to an arrest, have a reasonable belief based on specific and articulable facts that the area harbors an individual who poses a danger to those on the scene; its scope is limited to places where a person might be hiding. The opinion does not perform a Buie analysis because it did not need to — the court found consent sufficient and explicitly declined to reach the government’s alternative arguments relying on exigent circumstances and inevitable discovery (II.B, last paragraph).

What doctrinal distinctions matter (concise):

- Source of authority: consent is a third‑party waiver of Fourth Amendment protection; Buie is an exception to the warrant requirement tied to officer safety incident to arrest.
- Proof/standard: consent is judged on voluntariness under the totality of the circumstances; Buie requires reasonable suspicion/specific and articulable facts that someone dangerous may be present.
- Scope: consent’s scope is what a reasonable person would have understood (can be broader or narrower depending on words and circumstances); Buie is strictly limited to places where a person posing danger could be found.
- Who can authorize: a co‑occupant may validly consent to entry (Matlock/Cellitti); Buie is an officer‑safety doctrine and does not turn on third‑party authorization.
- Remedies/role: if consent is valid and within scope, no Buie showing is necessary; conversely, absent consent, a Buie/safety justification might still permit a limited sweep.

Bottom line: Under the facts the panel reviewed, the officers needed no separate Buie justification because the district court reasonably found (and the panel affirmed) that Lisa voluntarily consented to a welfare‑check entry and that the officers’ scanning of rooms to locate the children and Coleman was within that consent (II.B). The court therefore did not treat the entry as dependent on Buie.
