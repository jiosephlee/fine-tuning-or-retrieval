Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Jaison Coleman

### Does Coleman have Fourth Amendment standing to challenge a search consented to by his spouse, and how do Matlock and Randolph apply when the objecting co-occupant is in another room?
Question:
The opinion says a third party (Lisa) may validly consent to entry (citing Matlock) and that the defendant may challenge the validity of that consent. But what about Georgia v. Randolph, which restricts third-party consent when a physically present co-occupant expressly objects? Coleman was in a back bedroom and never objected at the door. Does being "present" elsewhere in the home trigger Randolph, or does his silence mean Lisa’s consent controls under Fernandez v. California?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes—Coleman had standing to challenge a third party’s consent (as the opinion itself acknowledges), but Randolph does not help him here because he was not “physically present” and did not expressly object at the door. The Seventh Circuit applied Matlock’s rule (a co‑occupant can consent and a defendant may challenge that consent), found Lisa’s consent voluntary on the totality of the circumstances, and upheld the entry. Because Coleman was in a separate bedroom and never asserted an on‑the‑spot objection, the court treated Lisa’s second, affirmative “Okay” as controlling.

Why Coleman can challenge consent (standing)
The opinion explicitly recognizes the settled rule that a defendant may challenge a third party’s consent. See II.A: “a third party may give consent to search a place in which both she and the defendant have legitimate expectations of privacy, and the defendant can challenge the validity of the consent given by the third party” (citing Matlock). That language establishes Coleman’s Fourth Amendment standing to contest the validity of Lisa’s consent.

When Randolph applies (and why it did not here)
Georgia v. Randolph is a narrow rule: when two co‑occupants are both physically present and one expressly refuses consent, the police may not rely on the other occupant’s consent to justify a warrantless entry. By contrast, Matlock recognizes that one co‑occupant can consent in the absence of an unambiguous, on‑the‑spot objection by the other.

The court’s opinion applies that Matlock framework and the voluntariness/totality‑of‑the‑circumstances test. It stresses two facts critical to its ruling: (1) the video shows Officer Kizer’s tone and demeanor were noncoercive and Lisa was capable of denying consent (she initially did so), and (2) Lisa later affirmatively said “Okay” and opened the door. See II.B: “Two aspects of the interaction stand out… Officer Kizer’s tone and demeanor… Lisa showed that she had the ability to deny consent… She chose to let Officer Kizer enter after he asked again.” The court also notes Coleman was located in a separate bedroom (II.A factual recitation), and there is no finding that he made any contemporaneous objection at the door.

Put simply: Randolph requires the objector be physically present and to refuse consent at the time the other occupant gives consent. The Seventh Circuit found those conditions absent here, so Randolph was not triggered and Matlock (and the voluntariness inquiry) governed. The district court’s finding that Lisa voluntarily consented was not clearly erroneous, so the entry was lawful.

Practical takeaway
- Coleman had standing to challenge Lisa’s consent (Matlock).
- Randolph limits third‑party consent only where an objecting co‑occupant is physically present and expressly refuses at the scene; mere presence elsewhere in the home without an on‑the‑spot objection does not invoke Randolph.
- Because Coleman was in a back bedroom and did not object at the door, the court treated Lisa’s consent as controlling and upheld the search.
