Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Jaison Coleman

Chapter 6: # From Entry to Evidence: Warrants, Plain View, and Fruits

The factual scenario in United States v. Coleman, No. 24‑3051 (7th Cir. Oct. 7, 2025), provides a compact and instructive prism through which to study how the law treats evidence gathered after an initial entry into a home — especially entries that are claimed to rest on third‑party consent. Much of Fourth Amendment practice turns on the fine lines between a lawful encounter that yields admissible evidence, and an unlawful intrusion that taints everything that follows. This chapter explains the doctrinal rules that govern when observations made during an initial entry may be used to secure a warrant or admitted under the plain‑view doctrine, and when those observations must be suppressed as “fruits” of an unlawful invasion. Coleman illustrates several key themes: the totality‑of‑the‑circumstances test for consent, the two‑prong plain‑view rule, how later warrants must be insulated against taint, and the alternative doctrines that may validate otherwise tainted evidence (independent source, attenuation, inevitable discovery).

## Using observations from a lawful consent entry to establish probable cause for a warrant

Probable cause for a search warrant requires a fair probability that contraband or evidence of a crime will be found in a particular place, viewed through the totality of the circumstances. See Illinois v. Gates, 462 U.S. 213 (1983). When officers enter a home with valid consent, observations they make within the lawful scope of that entry may furnish the probable cause needed to obtain a subsequent warrant. Coleman is straightforward on this point: the Seventh Circuit accepted the district court’s clear‑error finding that Lisa had validly consented to entry, and that the information acquired inside (children’s statements that their father pointed a gun, observed or described guns, the father’s aggressive conduct, plus the officers’ location of Coleman in a bedroom) supported the magistrate judge’s probable‑cause determination for a search warrant.

Three lessons flow from Coleman’s application of the probable‑cause principle.

First, the source and reliability of the information matter. Courts assessing an affidavit for probable cause look to the content and reliability of the factual assertions supplied to the magistrate. In Coleman, children provided consistent descriptions that their father had pointed a gun at their mother and threatened violence; those statements were corroborated by the scene (a domestic disturbance, the father found in the house). Corroborated, contemporaneous observations by police are probative, and those observations are routinely treated as reliable under Gates.

Second, there is no requirement that a warrant affidavit be built only from pre‑entry facts. When the initial entry is lawful, facts learned during that entry — for instance, seeing or being told about a weapon — can be incorporated into an affidavit. But the officer must describe in the affidavit how the information was obtained, so that the magistrate can perform the Fourth Amendment’s independent assessment. Where credibility of the source is at issue, corroboration (e.g., multiple witnesses, physical indications consistent with the account) strengthens the probable‑cause showing.

Third, appellate review of the sufficiency of probable cause and the district court’s factual findings about consent are distinct. The existence of probable cause to issue a warrant is reviewed for clear error as to historical facts and de novo as to the ultimate legal conclusion; district court findings about witness credibility and voluntariness of consent are reviewed for clear error. The Coleman court repeatedly emphasized that videotaped evidence (body camera footage) can eliminate disputes over “what happened,” making the factual inquiry less contestable. See Scott v. Harris, 550 U.S. 372 (2007).

For students: when analyzing a warrant affidavit grounded on a prior consensual entry, identify (1) the facts observed during the entry, (2) whether the observations were made within the entry’s lawful scope, (3) the level of corroboration for testimonial claims, and (4) whether the magistrate had a substantial basis to conclude a fair probability exists.

## Plain view during consent entries: lawful vantage point and immediately apparent incriminating nature

The plain‑view doctrine admits into evidence contraband or instrumentalities that officers discover while lawfully in a place where they have a right to be and where the incriminating nature of the object is immediately apparent. The doctrine has two essential prongs: (1) the officer must lawfully be in the location from which the object is seen; and (2) the incriminating character of the object must be immediately apparent — i.e., the officer must have probable cause to believe the item is contraband or evidence without additional searching. Horton v. California, 496 U.S. 128 (1990), summarizes the doctrinal core.

Coleman neatly exemplifies the prerequisites. Because the Seventh Circuit concluded that Lisa validly consented to the officers’ entry, those officers had a lawful vantage point to “scan the home, locate everyone, and ask some basic questions” in pursuit of confirming the children’s welfare. When officers encounter a firearm in plain sight under those circumstances, firearms are typically contraband for a convicted felon and readily recognizable as instruments that could facilitate violent crime; their incriminating character often is “immediately apparent.” Thus, if an officer lawfully sees a firearm sitting on a table or bed while conducting a welfare check consistent with consent, the plain‑view doctrine will frequently justify seizure or reliance on that observation in a subsequent affidavit.

Caveats and nuanced issues. The “immediately apparent” requirement can be complex when the object requires contextual inference (for example, a bag containing unknown items). The officer must be able to articulate a basis for concluding the item is contraband or evidence without conducting a further search. Likewise, the lawful vantage point can be narrow: if the consent extended only to being escorted to a bedroom to check on a child, an officer who opens drawers or moves objects to reveal contraband exceeds the scope of that consent and may not rely on plain‑view capture of items discovered as a result of that additional intrusion. The Jimeno standard — objective reasonableness of what a typical person would have understood consent to permit — governs the scope inquiry.

For practice: when making or evaluating a plain‑view seizure arising from a consent entry, document (a) the nature of consent and its scope; (b) the precise location and manner in which the item was observed; and (c) the factual basis for why the officer reasonably believed the item was incriminating without further manipulation.

## Fruit of the poisonous tree if entry is unlawful; attenuation and independent source

If the initial entry is unlawful — e.g., consent was coerced, or the person lacked authority to consent — then evidence discovered as a result of that entry is presumptively tainted and subject to exclusion as the “fruit of the poisonous tree.” See Wong Sun v. United States, 371 U.S. 471 (1963). The exclusionary rule and the fruit‑of‑the‑poisonous‑tree doctrine attenuate the government’s incentives to violate the Fourth Amendment by suppressing evidence that stems from such violations.

But suppression is not absolute in every case. Two well‑established doctrines can permit admission of evidence that the defense claims is tainted: attenuation and independent source.

Attenuation asks whether the connection between the illegal conduct and the challenged evidence has become so attenuated that the taint has dissipated. Courts consider factors such as temporal proximity, intervening circumstances, and the presence of flagrancy or purposeful misconduct by police. See Brown v. Illinois, 422 U.S. 590 (1975). If, for example, an officer illegally enters but then immediately obtains an unrelated, voluntary statement after a substantial break, a court might find attenuation. Attenuation is fact‑intensive and often difficult to establish if the challenged evidence was discovered during or as a direct consequence of the unlawful entry.

The independent‑source doctrine (Murray v. United States, 487 U.S. 533 (1988)) permits the admission of evidence initially discovered during an unlawful entry if the government can show that the evidence later was obtained by means wholly independent of the primary illegality — typically by a warrant supported by information untainted by the unlawful intrusion. The government must demonstrate that the later legal avenue to the same evidence was not prompted or influenced by the earlier illegality.

How these doctrines would operate in a Coleman‑style case. Had the district court concluded Lisa’s consent was coerced, the initial entry and any plain‑view observations would be suspect. The government could attempt to show attenuation (unlikely where the contested items were located and relied upon immediately) or an independent source (by demonstrating that a magistrate’s probable‑cause finding rested on untainted facts — e.g., independent witness reports or physical evidence obtained before the entry). Because the Seventh Circuit found consent valid in Coleman, it did not reach these doctrines; but they are the natural defenses in any appeal where consent is challenged.

For students: to assess whether the independent‑source doctrine is available, identify the precise facts in the warrant affidavit that derive from the initial entry, and ask whether the magistrate’s decision was based on an independent body of facts that would have supported issuance absent the tainted evidence.

## Inevitable discovery (Nix v. Williams) as an alternative basis to admit evidence

Nix v. Williams, 467 U.S. 431 (1984), carved out another exception to the exclusionary rule: evidence that would have been discovered inevitably by lawful means may be admitted even if its actual discovery stemmed from illegal conduct. The Nix doctrine is distinct from independent source: inevitable discovery asks whether the government can prove by a preponderance that lawful procedures already underway would necessarily have resulted in the evidence’s discovery.

Important elements of inevitable‑discovery analysis include: (1) proof by a preponderance that the discovery was truly inevitable, not merely speculative; (2) the existence of an independent, active investigative plan that would have uncovered the evidence; and (3) that the lawful plan would have proceeded without being derailed by the police misconduct. Nix typically requires concrete demonstration — such as an ongoing search plan, interviews that were already scheduled, or authorized searches that were about to be executed — rather than bare conjecture that the police “might” have eventually stumbled on the evidence.

In practice, inevitable discovery is a high bar. The court in Coleman did not decide whether inevitable discovery would have validated any evidence because it concluded the consent was valid and therefore admission of the guns was lawful on that ground. But in other cases where consent or a warrant is challenged, a government seeking to invoke Nix must produce documentary or testimonial proof of an independent, imminent lawful strategy that would have led to the same evidence absent the constitutional violation.

## Particularity and scope in follow‑on warrants; avoiding taint in affidavits

Once officers rely on observations from an initial entry to seek a warrant, the Fourth Amendment’s particularity and probable‑cause demands require careful drafting. Particularity demands that a warrant describe with specificity the place to be searched and the items to be seized. Probable cause requires the affidavit to supply sufficient facts to show a fair probability of finding those items at the premises.

Two drafting risks are common after a contentious initial entry:

1. Overreliance on contested observations without establishing their provenance. If the affidavit incorporates statements or physical observations acquired during an entry whose legality is contested, the affiant should explain how those observations were made, by whom, and whether any corroborating facts exist. Omitting these details invites suppression motions and appellate reversal if the court later finds the entry unlawful.

2. Vague recitals that elide the temporal or causal connection between the observed facts and the items sought. For instance, an affidavit that simply states “guns were observed” without specifying where they were seen, whether they were in plain view, and whether they were seized or merely described can make it difficult for a magistrate to assess probable cause and for a reviewing court to parse taint issues.

Practical drafting strategies. To minimize taint and to satisfy the magistrate, an affiant should:

- Explicitly state the basis on which the officer was in the place where the observation occurred (consent, exigency, warrant already in effect), and attach or summarize any documentary evidence of consent if available (e.g., signed consent forms or body‑cam footage).
- Describe the specific observations (who said what; where an item was seen; the appearance of items) and any corroboration (multiple witnesses, physical evidence, or prior reports).
- Separate in the affidavit those facts that would have been known independent of the contested entry from those that derive from it. If the warrant relies partly on questionable facts, include additional, untainted facts that can support probable cause.
- If the government anticipates a suppression motion, contemporaneously preserve any recordings (body cam, radio transmissions) and document officers’ contemporaneous notes.

Finally, the particularity of the items sought must be tailored to the probable cause. If the affidavit shows a fair probability that firearms used in a domestic threat are present in the home, the warrant should describe “firearms” and related indicia (ammunition, holsters, etc.) rather than a catchall that could extend beyond what the magistrate authorized.

Conclusion

Coleman reinforces fundamental propositions: a valid consent entry opens a lawful path to observations that can justify seizure or a follow‑on warrant; plain‑view seizures require lawful presence and an immediately apparent incriminating character; unlawful entries threaten suppression of the “fruits,” subject to narrow exceptions (attenuation, independent source, inevitable discovery); and careful affidavit drafting is essential to insulate warrants from constitutional challenge. For practitioners and students alike, the Coleman facts remind us that the Fourth Amendment turns on context — the officer’s demeanor, the voluntariness of consent, the scope of what the occupant reasonably understood, and the contemporaneous record that memorializes the encounter. These are the elements that separate admissible evidence from the forbidden fruit.
