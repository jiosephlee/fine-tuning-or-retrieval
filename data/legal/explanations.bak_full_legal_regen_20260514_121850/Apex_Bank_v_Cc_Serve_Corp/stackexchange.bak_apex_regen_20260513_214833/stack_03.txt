Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Why is likelihood of confusion treated as a question of law while each DuPont factor is a question of fact?
Question:
The court says likelihood of confusion is a question of law reviewed de novo, but each DuPont factor presents a question of fact reviewed for substantial evidence. What is the doctrinal basis for classifying the ultimate LoC determination as legal while the component factors are factual, and how does that classification shape what the Federal Circuit can and cannot do on appeal (e.g., reweighing vs. remanding)?
Answer:
Short answer
The court follows the familiar two-step structure: likelihood of confusion (LoC) is a legal conclusion drawn from subsidiary factual findings. The DuPont factors are those subsidiary factual findings. So on appeal the Federal Circuit (1) reviews the Board’s DuPont fact-findings for substantial evidence, and (2) reviews the Board’s ultimate LoC determination de novo by applying the law to those facts. That classification means the court may correct legal errors and remand for further factfinding when the Board misapplies a legal standard (as it did here), but it may not overturn or “reweigh” properly supported factual findings under the guise of de novo review.

Why the court treats them that way (doctrinal basis)
The opinion states the governing rule succinctly: “Likelihood of confusion is a question of law, based on findings of relevant underlying facts, namely findings under the DuPont factors.” M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1381 (quoted at J.A. 2–3 of the opinion). It then says that “each of the [thirteen] DuPont factors presents a question of fact, findings with regard to which we test for substantial evidence when called into question on appeal.” Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002) (quoted at J.A. 3).

That allocation tracks ordinary appellate practice: the Board (an administrative factfinder) determines the evidentiary facts (e.g., scope of services, number/nature of third‑party uses, how the marks look/sound), and the court makes the ultimate legal determination—whether those facts, viewed through the correct legal lens, support a finding that confusion is likely.

How that shapes what the Federal Circuit can and cannot do on appeal
1) What the court can do
- Correct legal errors de novo. If the Board applied an incorrect legal standard—e.g., used an improperly narrow definition of “similar” marks under the sixth DuPont factor—the appellate court may reverse or vacate the Board’s legal conclusion and remand. That is exactly what the court did: it concluded the Board’s limitation of third‑party marks to only “credit card services” was a legal error inconsistent with its earlier factual finding (J.A. 47, 27–28), so the court vacated the sixth‑factor finding and remanded for reconsideration (J.A. 7–8).
- Reconcile inconsistent findings. If the Board’s factual determinations conflict (or the Board applies different scopes/standards to different factors), the court may remand for the Board to apply a consistent legal standard to the facts (again what happened here: the Board found services “highly similar” in factor two but then narrowed the universe of third‑party marks for factor six, prompting vacatur and remand, J.A. 7).

2) What the court cannot do (absent insufficiency of evidence)
- Reweigh evidence or substitute its own factual findings where the Board’s findings are supported by substantial evidence. The opinion affirms the Board’s second‑factor finding because “substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar” (J.A. 6). The court does not overturn that factual finding; it treats it as settled for purposes of the legal LoC analysis unless the evidence is legally insufficient.
- Make credibility judgments or choose between competing inferences when the record permits reasonable minds to differ. The substantial‑evidence standard demands deference: “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” (Quotation from Consol. Edison cited at J.A. 3.)
- Treat factual DuPont findings as legal questions simply to reach a different outcome. If the Board’s factual findings are supported by substantial evidence, the Federal Circuit must accept them and can only decide if those facts, under the correct legal standard, lead to likelihood of confusion.

How that played out in this opinion (concrete application)
- The court affirmed the Board’s second DuPont factor because the Board’s factual work (defining credit‑card services and concluding the services were “highly similar”) was supported by substantial evidence (J.A. 24–28; affirmation at J.A. 6).
- The court vacated the sixth factor because the Board made a legal error in limiting the universe of third‑party marks—an error of law that the court can correct—given the Board’s own factual finding of high similarity under factor two (J.A. 7). The court remanded rather than substitute its own factual assessment, precisely because the proper scope for considering third‑party uses is a legal question that must be applied to the factual record.
- The court also vacated the first factor (similarity of the marks) for reconsideration because the commercial‑strength analysis under factor six could change the “commercial impression” analysis under factor one (J.A. 7–8). Again, the court corrected legal error and remanded to the Board to apply the law consistently to the facts.

Bottom line
The Federal Circuit accepts the Board’s DuPont factfindings unless they lack substantial evidentiary support, but it independently decides the legal question whether those facts amount to a likelihood of confusion. That means the court can (and will) correct legal misapplications and remand for new fact‑sensitive analysis, but it will not reweigh or replace factual findings that are supported by substantial evidence. The opinion is a textbook application of that division of labor: affirm factual findings where supported (factor two), vacate where the Board misstated or misapplied the law (factor six and, consequentially, factor one), and remand for further fact-application consistent with the correct legal standard.
