Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act and TTAB Appellate Review

Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d), provides a statutory bar to federal trademark registration when the mark sought by an applicant is likely to cause confusion with a previously used or registered mark. The bar is prophylactic: it prevents registration of marks that are likely to confuse consumers as to the source of goods or services, thereby protecting both the public and senior users. Oppositions brought under § 2(d) are decided at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) by applying the multifactor likelihood-of-confusion framework established in Application of E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973), and refined in later Federal Circuit decisions. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 WL ___ (Fed. Cir. 2025), illustrates how that framework operates at the TTAB and how appellate courts review the Board's mixed legal and factual work.

This chapter explains the statutory text and policy of § 2(d), maps the procedural posture and mechanics of TTAB opposition practice, sets out the parties’ burdens and the DuPont analysis, and describes the standard of appellate review that governs Federal Circuit review of TTAB decisions. It also explains several practical and doctrinally important points that arose in Apex Bank—how identifications of goods and services are construed, the treatment of standard-character versus design marks, use of the Trademark ID Manual and dictionaries, the evidentiary role of third-party registrations and use under the sixth DuPont factor, and why industry-specific facts (for example, the roles of banks, card issuers, and program managers in the financial-services marketplace) matter in relatedness analyses.

## Section 2(d): text, purpose, and policy

Section 2(d) states that the PTO shall refuse registration of a mark that so resembles a previously used mark as to be likely to cause confusion, mistake, or to deceive. The statute does not require actual confusion—likelihood of confusion is sufficient—and it focuses on the effect of concurrent use in the marketplace. The policy objectives are twofold: (1) to prevent consumer confusion about source, sponsorship, or affiliation; and (2) to protect senior users from dilution of their source-identifying function when newcomers use confusingly similar marks. The TTAB—and courts—implement § 2(d) through the DuPont multifactor test, a pragmatic balancing inquiry that weighs facts about marks, goods and services, channels of trade, evidence of actual confusion, and marketplace conditions.

Apex Bank v. CC Serve exemplifies § 2(d) enforcement where a registered senior user (CC Serve) opposed registration of a later applicant’s mark (Apex). The Board applied DuPont to determine whether consumers would likely mistake Apex’s ASPIRE BANK marks for CC Serve’s earlier ASPIRE registration. The Federal Circuit’s disposition—affirming some aspects of the Board’s analysis, vacating others, and remanding—shows the care courts exercise in ensuring the Board applies the DuPont factors consistently and within the bounds of competent factfinding.

## Anatomy of TTAB proceedings

Opposition proceedings begin after an application is examined and published for opposition in the Official Gazette. For intent-to-use applications—commonly used by applicants who have not yet put the mark into commerce—the applicant files an application under Section 1(b), and after substantive examination the mark is published. Interested third parties may file letters of protest during examination or oppose registration within the statutory window after publication. If an opposer files, the matter proceeds to the TTAB, which functions as an administrative tribunal, conducting discovery, receiving testimony, and making findings of fact and law.

A typical sequence is: (1) filing of an intent-to-use or use-based application; (2) examination and, if approved, publication; (3) potential letter(s) of protest during examination; (4) filing of an opposition after publication; (5) TTAB discovery and evidentiary hearings; (6) a written decision by the Board; and (7) appeal to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit on questions of law and fact. In Apex Bank the applicant filed intent-to-use applications, CC Serve submitted a letter of protest during prosecution, the marks nevertheless published, and CC Serve formally opposed on § 2(d) grounds. The Board sustained the opposition; Apex appealed to the Federal Circuit (jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B)).

Two practical points are worth noting. First, letters of protest to an examining attorney are discretionary and do not substitute for an opposition at the TTAB—opposition offers a full adversarial record. Second, an appeal to the Federal Circuit challenges the Board’s application of law and its factual findings; how the court reviews the Board’s mixed determinations is central to the outcome.

## Party roles and burdens at the TTAB

In a § 2(d) opposition, the opposer bears the burden of proving priority and likelihood of confusion. Priority is ordinarily established by earlier use in commerce, but a federal registration, depending on its status, may provide a constructive priority date back to a filing or priority date (and thus is often dispositive on priority). The TTAB’s task is then to determine whether confusion is likely, weighing the DuPont factors. The applicant may rebut by showing distinguishing facts—differences in marks, dissimilar goods, distinct channels of trade, third-party evidence of weakness, or other indicia militating against confusion.

Evidence the parties may rely on includes registrations, specimens of use, advertising and marketing materials, declarations and testimony regarding channels of trade and consumer sophistication, third-party registrations and usage, and market surveys or evidence of actual confusion. The TTAB evaluates credibility and weighs competing evidence; not every DuPont factor must be analyzed—only those relevant to the dispute. Apex Bank demonstrates these dynamics: CC Serve relied on its 1998 registration (priority date 1996) and evidence about how its ASPIRE mark was used in connection with credit-card programs; Apex attempted to show differences in services and pointed to third-party Aspire-formative uses to argue weakness.

## The DuPont framework and its application

DuPont identifies a non-exhaustive list of factors for assessing likelihood of confusion: similarity of the marks (appearance, sound, connotation, commercial impression); similarity of the goods or services and channels of trade; the conditions under which sales are made (impulse vs. careful purchasing); the fame and strength of the prior mark; the number and nature of similar marks in use (third‑party evidence); evidence of actual confusion; the nature and extent of concurrent use without confusion; whether the marks were used in good faith; and other miscellaneous considerations. Courts and the TTAB treat the first (marks) and second (goods/services) DuPont factors as central; the interplay among factors, and the relative weight given to each, depends on the record.

Several points of doctrinal practice are important. The similarity-of-goods/services inquiry does not require identity; it is enough that the goods or services are related so that consumers may think they come from the same source. Likewise, the sixth DuPont factor—third-party evidence of similar marks—addresses commercial weakness: when many third parties use similar marks on similar goods, the senior mark’s scope of protection is narrowed. But third-party marks must be used on similar goods or services to be probative. The TTAB need not consider every DuPont factor, but it must consider relevant evidence and explain its reasoning in a way that the Federal Circuit can review.

Apex Bank is instructive in two ways. First, the Board carefully construed the services at issue and found CC Serve’s “credit card services” and Apex’s “banking and financing services” to be highly similar, even partially legally identical, based on dictionary definitions and the parties’ evidentiary showings; the Federal Circuit affirmed that finding as supported by substantial evidence. Second, the Board erred in how it treated third-party Aspire-formative marks under the sixth DuPont factor: it limited consideration to marks used specifically for credit-card services even though it had already found the services to be highly similar (and thus within the same relevant marketplace). The Federal Circuit vacated and remanded the sixth-factor analysis, instructing the Board to adopt a consistent scope of relatedness across factors—the universe of similar services examined for third-party use should match the Board’s factual finding about what services are similar. The Court also remanded the first-factor analysis because commercial strength assessed under the sixth factor can affect the overall commercial impression of the marks.

## Standards of appellate review

The Federal Circuit applies a bifurcated standard of review to TTAB decisions. Legal conclusions—including the legal standard applied and the ultimate determination whether likelihood of confusion exists—are reviewed de novo. By contrast, factual findings—including subsidiary DuPont factfindings such as similarity of the goods, channels of trade, actual use evidence, and the weight accorded to particular pieces of evidence—are reviewed for substantial evidence. Substantial evidence means more than a scintilla; it is such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion. The DuPont factors themselves present factual questions for the Board; thus, a reviewing court will disturb them only if they are not supported by substantial evidence or if the Board committed legal error in applying the governing legal standards.

Apex Bank illustrates these principles. The Federal Circuit upheld the Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services were highly similar (substantial-evidence review). But the Court reviewed de novo whether the Board correctly applied the sixth DuPont factor and whether it applied a legally permissible scope in evaluating third-party evidence; finding legal error, the Court vacated and remanded that part of the decision. The Court also explained that an error on one DuPont factor (here, the sixth) can require reconsideration of another (here, the first) because the factors are interdependent.

## Identifications, priority, and their legal significance

A trademark application includes an identification of the goods or services for which registration is sought. These identifications are important because the TTAB compares the applicant’s identified goods/services to the opponent’s identified goods/services as registered or used. Courts construe identifications according to their ordinary meaning and the record; the Trademark ID Manual is a tool the TTAB and examining attorneys use to determine acceptable identifications and to understand how the PTO treats particular terms. If an identification term is deleted from the ID Manual (as happened in Apex for “credit card services”), the Board may still construe the term using dictionary evidence and other sources.

Priority is critical in a § 2(d) dispute. A registrant with a valid registration generally has priority as of its filing date or effective priority date, and an opposer who owns such a registration typically carries the day on priority. In Apex, CC Serve’s registration issued in 1998 with an effective priority date of 1996; that seniority was dispositive on priority and allowed CC Serve to press the § 2(d) claim. An applicant with an earlier priority claim could rebut by showing prior use, but absent that evidence the registered mark’s priority governs.

Identifications can be broad, and breadth cuts both ways. A broad identification may bring more goods/services within the comparison universe and may make it easier for an opposer to show relatedness, but a broad identification can also invite more third-party evidence of similar uses that could show weakness. Practitioners must draft identifications carefully and anticipate how the TTAB will construe ordinary-language terms, the ID Manual, and dictionary definitions.

## Standard-character versus word/design marks

A standard-character (word) mark registration claims rights in the word(s) regardless of font, style, size, or color. That breadth increases the scope of comparison because a standard-character mark covers the mark in any presentation. A design or composite mark (word plus stylized logo) is limited to the particular stylization shown in the registration; courts compare the overall commercial impression of the applicant’s mark and the registrant’s mark, taking into account both word and design elements.

In Apex Bank the registrant, CC Serve, owned a standard-character ASPIRE registration while Apex sought registration for ASPIRE BANK word and design marks. The TTAB and the Federal Circuit focused on the overall commercial impression of the marks, but CC Serve’s standard-character mark meant that its word ASPIRE could be presented in many forms and was compared on that broad basis. This doctrinal distinction matters because a senior owner of a standard-character mark can sometimes claim a broader scope of rights against stylized or composite later marks that incorporate the same word element.

## Use of the Trademark ID Manual and dictionaries to construe identifications

When an identification is ambiguous or when a term has been added or removed from the ID Manual, the TTAB and courts commonly rely on dictionary definitions and extrinsic evidence to construe the scope of the goods or services. The Board may define the relevant public and the ordinary meanings of the terms in the identifications; those definitions, in turn, shape the relatedness inquiry under the second DuPont factor and the pool of comparable third-party marks under the sixth. In Apex the Board used dictionary definitions of “banking” and “finance” to conclude that those terms encompass extending credit by issuing credit cards, which supported its determination of high similarity between the parties’ services.

Practitioners should therefore expect the TTAB to consult the ID Manual, dictionaries, and the record when construing identifications and should tailor their evidence accordingly. If a contested term is removed from the ID Manual, submit dictionary evidence and real-world examples to show the ordinary commercial meaning and the channels of trade.

## Third-party registrations and use; the sixth DuPont factor

Third-party registrations and evidence of third-party use play a limited but important role. Under the sixth DuPont factor, a showing that numerous third parties use similar marks on comparable goods or services indicates the senior mark may be weak and entitled to a narrow scope of protection. The Board considers both the number and the nature of such uses, and whether the evidence shows “ubiquity” or at least enough marketplace presence to have trained consumers to distinguish between similar marks.

A key legal point, emphasized by the Federal Circuit in Apex, is that third-party evidence must be evaluated against the same scope of relatedness adopted elsewhere in the analysis. If the Board finds the parties’ services are “highly similar” or partly identical under the second factor, it is inconsistent to consider only a narrower subset of third-party marks for the sixth factor. The sixth factor does not require identical goods; similarity suffices. Apex required the Board to reassess its sixth-factor evidence in light of its earlier finding of broad relatedness and to reweigh the mark’s commercial strength accordingly.

Finally, quantity matters. Courts have said that a handful of third-party uses will not demonstrate market-wide weakness, while dozens may. But the inquiry is qualitative as well as quantitative—the prominence of third-party use, overlap in channels of trade, and the extent to which consumers have learned to distinguish similar marks all matter.

## Financial-services landscape and relatedness analyses

Trademark relatedness analyses in the financial-services space require industry context. The financial ecosystem includes banks that directly issue credit cards, card issuers and program managers that operate card programs on behalf of banks, processors that handle transaction clearing and authorization, and service providers that manage accounts, billing, and customer service. A company that “offers credit card services” may do so by partnering with a bank (serving as a program manager or processor) rather than being a bank itself, but to consumers the service—issuance, account management, authorization—may appear as a bundled credit-card service. Thus, marks used by different types of financial actors can be considered related if consumers reasonably could perceive a single source providing a continuum of financial services.

In Apex the Board found that CC Serve’s credit card services and Apex’s broader banking and financing services were highly similar because dictionaries and the record showed banking often encompasses credit-extension functions. Apex argued that CC Serve only managed card programs for banks and did not itself provide banking services; the Board recognized that practical distinction but concluded that the services were related in commerce and could emanate from the same source. The Federal Circuit affirmed the relatedness finding. This highlights that courts will look beyond legal form (who holds a bank charter) to the practical relationships among services and how consumers experience them.

## Practical implications for practitioners

Apex Bank instructs several practical strategies. First, draft identifications with care: broad claims can help establish relatedness but can also invite third-party weaknesses. Second, collect and present third-party evidence thoughtfully; show similarity of goods/services and overlap in channels of trade if you argue weakness, and do not segment the universe of relevant third-party marks inconsistently with your own positions on relatedness. Third, when opposing or defending marks in regulated industries (like finance), present evidence explaining the marketplace structure (banks, program managers, processors) so the TTAB can properly assess consumer perceptions. Fourth, anticipate appellate review: factual findings are reviewed for substantial evidence, so develop a record that supports your factual assertions; legal arguments (including whether the Board applied DuPont correctly) can be reviewed de novo on appeal.

Conclusion

Section 2(d) litigation before the TTAB is a fact-intensive comparison governed by the DuPont factors. Apex Bank v. CC Serve demonstrates how the TTAB’s construal of identifications, its handling of third-party evidence, and its articulation of the marks’ commercial impressions can determine outcomes and how appellate review polices legal consistency while deferring to reasonable factual findings. Success in the TTAB requires both careful record-building on factual matters (use, channels of trade, consumer perception) and clear legal argument about the proper application of DuPont and the scope of protection accorded to the marks at issue.

# The DuPont Likelihood-of-Confusion Framework

The Lanham Act forbids registration of marks that are likely to cause confusion as to source. That legal rule is implemented in practice by the DuPont factors, a flexible multi-factor inquiry developed by the Court of Customs and Patent Appeals and applied by the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) and the federal courts. The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. (Fed. Cir. 2025) supplies a useful, concentrated lesson about three of the most frequently dispositive DuPont factors—Factor 2 (similarity of the goods/services), Factor 6 (number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods), and Factor 1 (similarity of the marks)—and about the essential principle that the DuPont factors must be applied consistently and interdependently.

This chapter first sketches the DuPont framework and the courts’ approach to weighting and relevance. It then explains Factor 2, Factor 6, and Factor 1 in depth, shows how courts evaluate evidence under those factors, and draws doctrinal lessons from Apex Bank about how these factors interact and must be analyzed in a coherent way.

## Overview of the thirteen DuPont factors and the interdependence principle

The DuPont test requires the factfinder to consider a set of factors relevant to the question whether confusion is likely when two marks are used on (or for) their identified goods or services. The factors include, among others: the similarity of the marks (appearance, sound, connotation, commercial impression), the similarity of the goods/services and their channels of trade, the strength of the prior mark (including third‑party uses), the degree of care likely to be exercised by consumers, evidence of actual confusion, the number and nature of similar marks in use, intent in adopting the mark, and so on. The Board and courts do not have to address every DuPont factor; they must address those that are relevant and supported by the record. The ultimate question—likelihood of confusion—is a legal conclusion based on subsidiary factual findings developed under the DuPont factors.

A crucial feature of the DuPont framework is its contextuality: factors are interdependent and their significance varies with the circumstances. Some factors commonly carry more weight (e.g., similarity of the marks, similarity of goods/services, and the strength of the prior mark), but none is mechanically dispositive in every case. Because certain factual findings (for example, that goods are highly similar) inform the evaluation of other factors (for example, which third‑party uses are “similar”), courts must apply the same definitions and factual scope consistently across the analysis. Apex Bank illustrates the consequences when the Board narrows its scope under one factor after making a broader factual finding under another.

## Weighting and flexibility: not all factors are relevant or equal in every case

DuPont is not a checklist with fixed weights. The Board and courts exercise judgment about which factors matter given the marks and marketplace at issue. For example, a field crowded with similar marks (Factor 6) will reduce the commercial strength of any single mark and therefore diminish the probative force of similarity under Factor 1. Conversely, a finding of virtual identity of goods or services (a strong Factor 2 result) can make the similarity of the marks (Factor 1) more likely to cause confusion because consumers encountering the marks are buying closely related offerings through overlapping channels.

Practically, boards and courts often focus their opinion-writing on the subset of DuPont factors that prove decisive, explaining why other factors are not implicated. But they must do so coherently: if a factual finding under one factor narrows or broadens the universe of relevant goods, services, or consumers, that scope should govern the evaluation of other factors unless the record supports a reasoned difference.

## Factor 2 (similarity of the goods/services): relatedness, partial legal identity, channels of trade, classes of consumers

Factor 2 asks whether the goods or services of the parties are related such that consumers would be likely to believe they come from the same source. The similarity requirement is not strict identity: courts have long held that goods or services need only be related in some manner or marketed in such a way that confusion as to source is possible (see Coach Servs. v. Triumph Learning, 668 F.3d 1356 (Fed. Cir. 2012)). Two important points govern this inquiry.

First, the identification of goods/services in the application or registration is itself dispositive for the purposes of the Board’s analysis. The Board compares the parties’ identifications as written; if one identification encompasses the other, that finding can support a conclusion of legal identity (or partial legal identity). Partial legal identity means that some of the applicant’s identified services are legally identical to the registrant’s services even though other identified services might be different. An applicant cannot avoid an adverse Factor 2 finding by pointing to differences in actual, non‑identified uses when the identified descriptions overlap.

Second, courts look beyond labels to marketplace realities: channels of trade, classes of consumers, and typical marketing practices matter. If the same consumers buy both goods or services, if the goods/services travel in the same channels (e.g., issued by the same kinds of providers or accessible on the same platform), or if suppliers commonly offer both kinds of services under a single mark, those facts support relatedness.

Apex Bank applied these principles. The Board examined the parties’ identified services—credit card services (registrant) and banking and financing services (applicant)—and concluded that the descriptions overlap because dictionary definitions of “banking/finance” encompass activities connected with credit cards, and because third‑party registrations show entities providing both services under single marks. The Board characterized the parties’ services as “highly similar” and partially legally identical. The Federal Circuit affirmed that Factor 2 finding as supported by substantial evidence.

## Evidence under Factor 2: third-party registrations showing single-source offerings; marketplace practices

Two common categories of evidence inform Factor 2:

- Third‑party registrations and uses showing that the same source commonly offers both types of services (e.g., registrations covering “credit card services” and “banking services” under one mark). Such evidence demonstrates that consumers might reasonably expect a single source to provide both services under a single mark.

- Marketplace evidence: contracts, promotional materials, partnerships (e.g., affinity or co‑branding arrangements), actual business models showing how services are delivered and marketed (for example, payment processors managing card programs for banks). Where a company like CC Serve provides outsourcing and servicing for credit card programs issued by banks, the Board must assess whether consumers perceive the card brand, the servicing firm, or the issuing bank as the source; however, the legal test looks to the identified services and the reasonable consumer’s expectations.

When presenting evidence under Factor 2, it is important to (1) supply registrations or declarations that map specific services to the same commercial source and (2) describe typical channels of trade and target consumers so the Board can see overlap in who buys and how.

## Factor 6 (number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods): crowded field doctrine; conceptual vs commercial strength

Factor 6 evaluates the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” Its central function is to inform how much scope of protection a mark deserves. If the marketplace is crowded with similar marks used on similar goods, consumers become accustomed to distinguishing similar marks, and any one descriptive or weak mark merits narrower protection (Palm Bay Imps. v. Veuve Clicquot, 396 F.3d 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2005)). By contrast, a strong, distinctive mark in an uncrowded field gets broader protection.

Courts distinguish conceptual strength (how distinctive the mark is in meaning—e.g., coined or arbitrary vs descriptive) from commercial strength (extent and prominence of use). Factor 6 is principally concerned with commercial strength shown by third‑party marketplace use and registrations: how many other parties use similar marks on similar goods, how prominent or voluminous those uses are, and whether consumers are likely trained to distinguish among them.

Apex Bank teaches two lessons about Factor 6. First, similar does not mean identical: Factor 6 requires consideration of third‑party marks used on similar—not necessarily identical—goods or services (Olde Tyme Foods v. Roundy’s, 961 F.2d 200 (Fed. Cir. 1992); Juice Generation v. GS Enters., 794 F.3d 1334 (Fed. Cir. 2015)). Second, and equally important, once the Board has found the parties’ services to be highly similar under Factor 2, it must carry that scope into its Factor 6 analysis. The Apex Board narrowed its Factor 6 review to third‑party marks used only on “credit card services,” excluding marks used on other banking and financing services; the Federal Circuit held that narrowing was legally erroneous because the Board had already determined services were highly similar and thus relevant third‑party marks extend across that broader field.

## What counts as “similar goods”: identical vs similar; Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation guidance

The line between “identical” and “similar” goods is factual, but the legal rule is clear: identical goods/services make confusion more likely because consumers encountering the same goods under different marks are especially likely to assume common source. However, non‑identical but related goods marketed in the same channels to the same classes of consumers can create an equal or higher risk of confusion depending on their relationship.

Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation emphasize that Factor 6 evidence need not match the registrant’s goods word‑for‑word. Juice Generation, for example, treated evidence of multiple third‑party marks across a field as demonstrating crowding even though the third‑party registrations were not identical in every respect to the contested goods. The practical implication: when a Board has found relatedness or partial identity under Factor 2, Factor 6’s universe of relevant third‑party uses should encompass the same related goods and services.

## Evidence under Factor 6: third-party registrations vs marketplace use; the role of quantity and context (Jack Wolfskin, Juice Generation)

Not all third‑party registrations carry equal weight. Courts distinguish registrations alone from documented marketplace use. A disparate collection of registrations, some for different services or with limited use, may not establish a crowded field. Board and court precedents illustrate thresholds for “considerable” evidence of crowding. In Jack Wolfskin the court noted “voluminous evidence” (including many examples of both registrations and uses) supporting weakness; in Juice Generation, 26 third‑party marks were deemed “a considerable number.” By contrast, the Apex Board found only nine third‑party Aspire‑formative marks for credit‑card‑related services and concluded that was insufficient to show commercial weakness.

Key points for litigants:

- Quantity matters, but so does quality: the number of similar marks must be considered alongside their prominence, duration, geographic scope, and whether they actually operate in the same channels and target the same consumers.

- Registrations demonstrating common single‑source offerings across related services are persuasive but should be supplemented by evidence of actual marketplace use when possible.

- The field of comparison must be the set of goods/services found relevant under Factor 2; excluding related but nonidentical uses without justification risks error.

## Factor 1 (similarity of the marks): appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression; the effect of mark strength on this analysis

Factor 1 assesses the marks themselves—visual appearance, phonetic sound, meaning or connotation, and the marks’ overall commercial impression. The analysis is holistic: no single element (e.g., an exact word match) automatically resolves the factor; the question is whether the marks, when viewed in their commercial context, are similar enough to cause confusion.

Mark strength (conceptual and commercial) modulates Factor 1. A weak or descriptive mark warrants narrower protection and requires a greater degree of similarity to the applicant’s mark before confusion is likely. Conversely, a strong, arbitrary, or fanciful mark commands broader protection; a fairly small degree of similarity in appearance or sound may suffice for confusion. Evidence developed under Factor 6 is thus relevant to the legal weight the Board will give to observed similarities under Factor 1.

Apex Bank demonstrates this interdependence. The Board concluded that CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark should receive the normal scope of protection, in part because it found insufficient third‑party use to show weakness. The Federal Circuit held that the Board’s Factor 6 analysis was legally flawed and remanded Factor 6; because Factor 6 bears directly on how much weight to assign the similarity of the marks under Factor 1, the court also vacated the Board’s Factor 1 analysis and remanded it for reconsideration in light of any revised findings on commercial strength.

## Interplay among Factors 1, 2, and 6: how strength and marketplace context modulate perceived similarity

The most important practical rule flowing from Apex Bank and DuPont jurisprudence is that these factors must be analyzed together, not in isolation. Typical dynamics include:

- If Factor 2 shows the goods/services are identical or highly similar, the universe of relevant consumers and channels is broad and therefore the Board should consider third‑party uses across that broad field under Factor 6.

- Factor 6 evidence of a crowded field reduces the commercial strength of the prior mark and narrows the protective scope under Factor 1; that in turn may make otherwise noticeable similarities less likely to cause confusion.

- Conversely, a finding of an uncrowded field or dominant commercial strength supports a broader reading of Factor 1: even modest similarities may create a likelihood of confusion when goods are identical or closely related.

When a board finds inconsistency across the factors—e.g., a broad finding of relatedness under Factor 2 but a narrow universe for third‑party marks under Factor 6—courts will scrutinize the explanation and may remand for consistent application, as happened in Apex Bank.

## Practical guidance for practitioners and students

- Define goods/services consistently. If you argue that services are highly similar under Factor 2, be prepared to show third‑party uses across that same related field for Factor 6. Conversely, if you seek to limit the scope at Factor 6, explain why the broader related services (found under Factor 2) are not comparable for purposes of third‑party evidence.

- Build the record on commercial use, not just registrations. Registrations are useful, but marketplace evidence—web captures, advertising, press releases, sales figures, partnership agreements—better demonstrates commercial strength or crowding.

- Document channels of trade and classes of consumers. Declarations and samples showing who buys the services and how they are marketed help the Board decide both relatedness (Factor 2) and whether consumers are likely trained to distinguish marks (Factor 6).

- Be explicit about the interdependence of factors. If you win on one factual point (e.g., relatedness), show how that point should anchor analysis of other factors rather than allowing the Board to recalibrate scope in an inconsistent way.

- Prioritize dispositive factors in briefing, but do not ignore others. The record should anticipate that the Board or court will assess the overall commercial impression (Factor 1) in light of mark strength (Factor 6) and relatedness (Factor 2).

## Conclusion

DuPont’s multi‑factor likelihood‑of‑confusion test is capacious and fact‑intensive. Apex Bank v. CC Serve reaffirms two stable doctrinal lessons: (1) the Board’s findings of fact—such as that goods or services are highly similar—must govern the scope of relatedness in the consideration of other factors, and (2) because Factors 1, 2, and 6 are tightly interlinked, error in considering one can require revisiting the others. Successful advocacy, and sound adjudication, depend on building and evaluating a coherent record that ties together who the consumers are, what goods/services are at issue, which marks are in the field, and how consumers perceive and distinguish those marks.

# Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.: A Case Study in Applying DuPont

This chapter uses the Federal Circuit’s 2025 decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. to illustrate how courts and the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB or “the Board”) apply the DuPont likelihood-of-confusion framework when a registrant opposes an applicant’s mark. The opinion is particularly useful because it shows (1) a clear application of the “relatedness” inquiry (the second DuPont factor) that is sustained on appeal under the substantial-evidence standard, and (2) an error of law in the Board’s handling of third‑party‑mark evidence (the sixth DuPont factor) that required vacatur and remand — with a knock‑on effect on the first DuPont factor (similarity of the marks in their entireties). The decision also displays how the Federal Circuit uses precedent (Coach Services, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin, Olde Tyme Foods, I.AM.Symbolic) to shape the analysis.

## Facts and industry context

CC Serve owns a registered, standard‑character mark ASPIRE for “credit card services,” a registration dating to the 1990s. CC Serve provides credit card programs by partnering with banks: banks issue ASPIRE‑branded cards and accounts, and CC Serve services the accounts. Apex Bank is a small retail bank that does not issue credit cards but filed intent‑to‑use applications to register ASPIRE BANK (word and design) for “banking and financing services,” intending to use the marks for an online bank. CC Serve protested during examination; the marks nevertheless published and CC Serve opposed registration under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, alleging a likelihood of confusion. The TTAB sustained the opposition; Apex appealed to the Federal Circuit.

This posture captures a common fact pattern: an established payment‑service/processor mark (credit card services) vs. a bank’s use of a similar mark for banking services. Whether consumers will be confused depends on a multi‑factor DuPont analysis.

## Procedural posture and standard of review

Procedurally, Apex filed intent‑to‑use trademark applications, the examining attorney allowed publication despite CC Serve’s letter of protest, CC Serve filed an opposition at the TTAB, and the Board sustained the opposition. On appeal, the Federal Circuit reviews legal conclusions de novo and factual findings for substantial evidence. That dual standard is decisive here: a factual finding supported by substantial evidence will stand even if another reasonable factfinder might disagree; legal errors (misapplication of legal standards) require correction and remand.

The DuPont framework governs likelihood‑of‑confusion cases before the TTAB. The Board evaluates the relevant DuPont factors (not all thirteen must be discussed, but those that are relevant and supported by the record). The key DuPont factors implicated here are factor 2 (relatedness of goods/services), factor 6 (third‑party use of similar marks), and factor 1 (similarity of the marks in their entireties).

## Factor 2 affirmed: construing the services and finding partial legal identity

The second DuPont factor asks whether the goods or services are related such that consumers could reasonably believe they come from the same source. The principles are twofold: (1) the goods or services need not be identical, and (2) the Board may rely on the plain meaning of identifications in applications/registrations and on evidence (e.g., third‑party registrations or marketplace practice) showing they are commonly provided together or by a single source. See Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC (relatedness can be shown by the circumstances of marketing or natural relationship).

In Apex, the Board construed CC Serve’s “credit card services” identification expansively (issuing cards, account management, processing, authorization, monitoring, counseling, access to credit scores). Because dictionary definitions and common industry practice show that banking and financing services often include issuance of credit and credit cards, the Board concluded the services are “legally identical, in part,” and otherwise highly similar. The Board also relied on third‑party registrations showing providers that offer both credit‑card‑type services and broader banking/finance services under the same mark. On appeal the Federal Circuit held that substantial evidence supports the Board’s factor‑2 finding.

Lessons for application:
- Begin factor‑2 analysis by construing the identifications in the applications/registrations, giving each term its ordinary meaning and considering the Trademark ID Manual and dictionary or industry evidence where necessary.
- Remember that legal identity in part is sufficient: if some aspects of the identified services are identical (e.g., both identifications cover issuance or servicing of credit accounts), that supports a finding of relatedness.
- Third‑party registrations and marketplace evidence showing common provision under a single mark are persuasive to show consumers might think services emanate from the same source.

## Factor 6 vacated: scope of “similar goods” and third‑party evidence

DuPont factor six considers “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods” — i.e., whether third‑party use or registration of similar marks on related goods indicates that the mark is weak and entitled to a narrower scope of protection. Courts and the TTAB treat such evidence as relevant to commercial strength: a crowded field of similar marks tends to teach consumers to distinguish between marks and thus undermines the senior user’s exclusivity. See Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin.

In Apex the Board acknowledged Apex’s submissions of many third‑party marks containing “Aspire.” But the Board limited its analysis to only those third‑party marks shown to be used in connection with credit card services (a narrow slice), excluding marks used for other banking and financing services. Because the Board had already determined under factor 2 that the parties’ services were highly similar (indeed partially legally identical), the Federal Circuit concluded that it was legally erroneous to adopt a narrower definition of “similar services” for factor 6 than the one used for factor 2. The sixth factor does not require identical goods; similar is enough. See Olde Tyme Foods, Juice Generation. Because the Board’s prior factual finding of substantial similarity should have governed which third‑party marks were “similar” enough to count, excluding marks for broader banking/finance services was inconsistent and legally flawed. The Federal Circuit vacated the factor‑6 ruling and remanded for reconsideration with the appropriate scope of similar services.

Practical and doctrinal points:
- When a Board finds services highly similar under factor 2, it must remain consistent in treating which third‑party marks are “on similar goods” for factor 6. You cannot narrow the universe for factor 6 in a way that conflicts with your factor‑2 finding.
- Third‑party registrations and evidence are relevant to show weakness, but there is no strict numerical threshold; courts look to whether the evidence shows a field “crowded” with similar marks (cases sometimes reference 14, 26, or a “voluminous” number as guides, but these are case‑specific).
- Distinguish between registrations and marketplace use: active marketplace use tends to carry more weight than registrations alone (but both are admissible and probative).

## Consistency principle: a scope adopted for one factor should inform others

A central teaching of Apex is a consistency principle. DuPont calls for flexible, fact‑specific inquiry, but it does not permit self‑contradictory factfinding across factors. If the Board determines that the services are highly similar or partially legally identical under factor 2, it must use that scope when evaluating third‑party marks under factor 6 (and should generally be consistent across other related factors). Inconsistent narrowing or expansion of the relevant marketplace or consumer class between factors risks legal error and remand. The Federal Circuit’s vacatur on factor 6 reflects the need for coherence: relatedness is not a factor‑specific silo.

For students and practitioners: explicitly state the scope of the relevant goods/services at the outset of the analysis and carry that scope through the other DuPont factors unless the record provides a reasoned, evidence‑based justification for altering it. If you change the scope, explain why and point to record evidence supporting a different definitional line.

## Factor 1 vacated: commercial impression depends on mark strength and must be revisited

Factor 1 requires assessing the marks’ similarity in appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression. The commercial impression question is factladen but must account for the senior mark’s commercial strength. The Federal Circuit recognized that a proper re‑evaluation of factor 6 could change the perceived commercial strength (narrow vs. normal scope of protection) of CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark. Because that reassessment could materially affect the analysis of the marks’ similarity and the likelihood of confusion, the court vacated the Board’s factor‑1 finding and remanded so the Board could reexamine the marks’ appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression in light of its corrected factor‑6 analysis. The court cited I.AM.Symbolic for the proposition that commercial impression and factor‑1 analysis are informed by other DuPont findings, including strength/weakness assessments.

Teaching points:
- Factor 1 is not evaluated in a vacuum; the commercial impression analysis must be informed by the mark’s commercial strength.
- A finding that a senior mark is conceptually or commercially weak (because of many third‑party uses) will typically narrow its protectable scope and can reduce the likelihood of confusion even if marks are visually or phonetically similar.
- Conversely, if factor 6 shows limited third‑party use, the senior mark’s strong commercial presence may weigh more heavily in favor of confusion under factor 1.

## Standards of review illustrated: substantial evidence vs. legal error

Apex is a clear demonstration of the interplay between standards of review:
- The Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar (factor 2) was reviewed for substantial evidence and affirmed. The Federal Circuit deferred to the Board where the evidentiary record supported the finding.
- The Board’s narrower conceptualization of “similar goods” for factor 6, when that concept conflicted with its factor‑2 finding, was a legal error. Legal errors (misapplication of legal standards or inconsistent reasoning) are reviewed de novo and may require vacatur and remand.

For exam answers or appellate practice, highlight what is a factual finding (typically entitled to substantial‑evidence deference) and what is a legal conclusion (reviewed de novo). Inconsistency across factual predicates and legal frameworks will be treated as a legal problem.

## How Apex used precedent

The court relied on and cited several instructive precedents:
- Coach Services: confirms that goods/services need not be identical to be related; marketplace context and marketing channels are relevant.
- Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation: establish that similarity for the sixth factor does not require identity and that substantial third‑party evidence can show a crowded field.
- Jack Wolfskin: demonstrates that “voluminous” third‑party evidence can show weakness; contrast the facts where few examples do not suffice.
- I.AM.Symbolic: articulates that factor 1’s commercial‑impression inquiry is informed by other DuPont findings, particularly strength.

These cases sketch the boundaries of what evidence is probative for relatedness and weakness and underscore the centrality of consistent, record‑based reasoning.

## Outcome and remedy

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding on factor 2 (services highly similar), vacated the Board’s findings on factors 6 and 1 (for legal error and inconsistency), and remanded for the Board to reconsider those factors using the same scope of related services determined under factor 2. The final judgement was affirmed‑in‑part, vacated‑in‑part, and remanded; costs were awarded to the appellant.

## Practical takeaways for students and practitioners

- Start with clear, consistent definitions of the goods/services and the relevant consumer class. Use that scope throughout the DuPont analysis unless record evidence compels a change.
- Treat the DuPont analysis as an integrated exercise: findings on relatedness and mark strength will influence the weight given to similarity in appearance, sound, and commercial impression.
- Make your record: if you wish the Board to consider or exclude particular third‑party marks, submit evidence of actual marketplace use and registrations that match the scope of services you contend are relevant.
- Know the standards: factual findings supported by substantial evidence will likely survive appeal; legal misapplications or inconsistent reasoning will not.
- There is no magic number for third‑party marks that transforms a mark into a weak one; the Board and courts evaluate the number, nature, and quality of evidence holistically.

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. thus offers a compact lesson in DuPont practice: be precise and consistent in defining similarity; third‑party evidence must be considered against that definition; and mark strength and commercial impression are interdependent inquiries that must be reasoned about together.

# Proving and Refuting Likelihood of Confusion at the TTAB

This chapter explains how litigants build and attack the evidentiary record on the key DuPont factors at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB), with Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025) used as a recurring roadmap. The Federal Circuit’s disposition in Apex—affirming the Board on relatedness of services (DuPont factor two), but vacating and remanding the Board’s treatment of third‑party use (factor six) and the marks’ overall commercial impression (factor one)—illustrates two enduring rules: first, the Board must apply consistent legal scope across related DuPont inquiries once it has fixed the universe of similar goods/services; and second, the probative value of third‑party evidence depends on both quantity and quality of marketplace exposure, not merely on registration listings. The practical guidance below explains what to gather, how to authenticate it, and how to tie it to each DuPont factor so the Board (and a reviewing court) can and will reach a reasoned result.

DuPont and the TTAB’s task. A Section 2(d) opposition turns on a likelihood of confusion determination informed by relevant DuPont factors. The Board need only consider factors that are relevant and in the record, but each factual finding is reviewed for substantial evidence on appeal, while legal determinations are reviewed de novo. Apex underscores that factual findings about the similarity/relatedness of services (factor two) control the correct scope of analysis under other factors that depend on similarity—most notably factor six and the first‑factor commercial impression analysis.

Building Factor 2 evidence: showing relatedness or partial identity. Factor two asks whether the goods/services are related such that consumers might think they come from the same source. Proof need not show identical offerings; it is enough to show overlap in channels of trade, typical purchasers, or that the services are complementary and commonly offered together. Practical, reliable avenues to establish relatedness include: dictionary definitions and official industry glossaries to define terms invoked in the identification of goods/services; trade and industry reports explaining how services are marketed and bundled; contracts or agreements that demonstrate actual co‑provisioning or joint offerings (for example, bank‑processor arrangements or service‑provider partnerships); advertising showing cross‑promotion or co‑branding; and declarant testimony from company witnesses or industry experts explaining routine business practices and customer behavior.

Apex shows the value of careful textual work: the Board began its analysis by construing the parties’ identifications (e.g., “credit card services” and “banking and financing services”) and relied on dictionary and industry meanings to find partial legal identity. Practitioners should make that textual work explicit in the record—submit the definitions you rely on, the TESS identification rules and any prior USPTO guidance (Trademark ID Manual entries are useful), and a witness who explains how the parties’ actual services operate in the marketplace. If a party provides services by partnering with banks (like CC Serve’s relationship with issuers), introduce the agreements and descriptive testimony that explain that relationship so the Board can appreciate how consumers encounter the marks in context.

Third‑party registrations and relatedness: when and how they matter. Third‑party registrations that claim both types of services (or show common ownership of registrations for related services) are legitimate and commonly used indicators that goods/services may emanate from a single source. Such registrations are admissible through notice of reliance on the USPTO registration records. But registrations standing alone are weaker than evidence of market practice: a registration shows an owner thought the mark could be used on a set of services, but not that consumers have actually encountered the mark on those services. For this reason, pair registrations with corroborating evidence—website screenshots, marketing materials, press releases, or specimens showing real use—to show that these third parties actually offered the listed services and that consumers had exposure to the marks in a way that could educate the marketplace.

Factor 6: assembling marketplace use and distinguishing registration from use. DuPont factor six asks how many and what nature of similar marks are in use on related goods or services. The core issues under factor six are twofold: (1) is the relevant field crowded with similar marks such that the claimant’s mark is comparatively weak and entitled to a narrow scope of protection, and (2) is the evidence sufficient to show that the field is crowded?

Quantity alone is not determinative; courts look at both quantitative and qualitative metrics. The Board and courts have compared what counts as “a considerable number” of similar marks in different contexts—cases have cited as examples numbers like 14 or 26 where the evidence was deemed voluminous. But Apex shows that nine third‑party marks confined to only one subcategory may not be sufficient to demonstrate crowding, particularly if the Board had earlier concluded the parties’ services were highly similar across a broader range. That decision therefore instructs practitioners to think broadly when gathering factor six evidence: do not rely only on registrations confined to a narrow sub‑category; gather evidence of uses across the entire range of services the Board has found similar.

What to collect and how to authenticate. The most persuasive factor‑six evidence is actual marketplace use: screenshots of third‑party websites that show the mark used with the relevant services; archived webpages (e.g., from the Wayback Machine) to show duration; specimens such as brochures, app screens, or co‑branded materials; trade press articles and marketing emails; domain name registrations; and sales or distribution data showing the geographic scope and volume. For each item, build a foundation for admissibility: authenticate with a sponsoring witness or use business‑records foundation, use preserved discovery responses or custodial declarations, and use notices of reliance where TTAB practice permits. The TTAB accepts printed publications under notice of reliance; it also permits submission of third‑party registrations and certain discovery testimony via notice of reliance. But be mindful that a mere printout of a registration or a web search result without foundation may be given little weight. If opposing party authentication would be easy, anticipate and cure that by obtaining a witness declaration from the third party (if cooperative) or by offering corroborating contemporaneous materials.

Quantitative and qualitative showings under factor 6. Plan to quantify and qualify the field: count the number of distinct third‑party marks in use on the same or similar services; identify which are national brands versus local or niche uses; document the duration of use (how long each mark has been in commerce); map geographic reach (local, regional, national); and show consumer exposure (advertising spend, website traffic metrics, social‑media impressions, third‑party news coverage). Provide context: a dozen small, local uses do not typically debase a mark’s commercial strength in a national market; conversely, a handful of large national competitors or dominant uses in a single category may be material. Use comparative prominence—market share and media presence—to demonstrate whether consumers are likely to know and distinguish among the marks. Apex’s holding teaches that nine entries limited to a narrow subcategory were not enough to show a crowded field when the Board had already found broader relatedness.

Defining the relevant public and the universe of comparison; avoid artificial narrowing. An initial factual determination about the relatedness of services controls the definition of the relevant public and the appropriate universe of comparable marks. Apex illustrates the mistake of narrowing the relevant universe in one factor after construing it broadly in another: the Board found the services to be highly similar (factor two) but then limited factor six comparison to just credit‑card services, excluding other banking and financing uses. The Federal Circuit rejected that inconsistency and remanded. Practitioners should therefore ensure that the scope of goods/services and the definition of the relevant consumer are consistent across DuPont factors. If you want the Board to consider only a narrow class of consumers or goods for strategic reasons, make that argument at the stage when the Board defines the goods/services; if the Board makes a broader factual finding, preserve your objection and show why the narrower lens is still appropriate as a legal matter. Always tie your formulation to concrete evidence about channels of trade, class of purchasers, and manner of marketing.

Conceptual vs commercial strength: building both sides of the strength inquiry. Strength divides into conceptual (where the mark sits on the distinctiveness spectrum: generic, descriptive, suggestive, arbitrary/fanciful) and commercial (how strong the mark is in the marketplace due to use and consumer recognition). Proving conceptual weakness is often a matter of dictionary definitions, common usage evidence, and statutory registrations that identify the mark as merely descriptive or laudatory. For instance, a term like “aspire” may be shown to have a common dictionary meaning suggesting ambition, which bears on conceptual distinctiveness. To prove commercial weakness—or rebut it—produce market‑level evidence: sales figures, advertising expenditures, consumer survey evidence demonstrating low recognition, third‑party use data demonstrating a crowded field, and evidence that consumers routinely distinguish similar marks (trade press testimony, long coexistence without confusion).

Surveys and actual consumer recognition evidence are particularly powerful for demonstrating commercial strength; conversely, extensive third‑party use and consumer familiarity with multiple similar marks argue for narrow protection. Apex shows the practical interplay: the Board found neither side had established a decisive commercial strength or weakness; on remand, parties should present robust metrics that demonstrate where on the spectrum their mark sits.

Factor 1 proof: showing appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. The first DuPont factor asks whether the marks are similar in their entireties. The TTAB evaluates appearance, sound, connotation, and overall commercial impression from the vantage of ordinary consumers of the identified goods/services. Practical proof includes high‑quality specimens (web pages, packaging, app screens) showing marks as used in commerce; side‑by‑side visual comparisons; testimony explaining how the mark is presented with house marks or corporate indicia; audio recordings if phonetic impression matters; and survey evidence testing consumer perceptions of similarity. Remember that the first factor is not decided in a vacuum: it must be read alongside evidence of mark strength and the competitive context. A weak mark receives a narrower scope of protection, and small differences between marks are more likely to prevent confusion where the junior mark is weak. Apex makes clear that a reexamination of factor six (strength) can—and should—prompt reconsideration of factor one because commercial strength affects the weight given to similarities.

Procedural mechanics: notices of reliance, testimony declarations, websites, hearsay, and preservation. The TTAB’s rules and practice on evidence differ from district courts. Notices of reliance are your workhorse for documentary evidence available in printed or electronic form (USPTO records, registrations, printed publications, discovery deposition transcripts). They provide a streamlined route to put documents into the record without live witness testimony but must be used in accordance with the Board’s rules and TBMP guidance. Testimony declarations and deposition testimony are the best way to authenticate materials, provide foundation for business records, and present witness explanations of marketing and consumer behavior.

Web evidence is ubiquitous but requires careful authentication. Preserve web captures contemporaneously, include URL, capture date, and, where possible, a sponsoring witness who can testify that the captures reflect the website content and that it was used in commerce. Archive.org captures can establish historical use and duration. For business emails, marketing databases, and internal documents, use custodial declarations and business‑records foundations to avoid hearsay objections. If you rely on third‑party registrations or printed materials, use notice of reliance on official USPTO copies or certified printouts and be prepared to authenticate aspects of online third‑party claims that bear on consumer exposure.

Preserve objections and set forth precise legal grounds. If you oppose evidence on hearsay or foundation grounds, make those objections in the record, not merely in a later brief. When the Board rules, it will explain the basis for crediting or discounting evidence—important for appeal. If the Board excludes or downweights critical evidence, contemporaneously seek reconsideration or present alternate authenticated proof.

Maintaining consistency across factors; avoiding the Apex error. Apex’s core error lesson is a methodological one: once the Board has made a firm factual finding about the relatedness and typical channels of the parties’ services, that finding becomes the operative lens for evaluating factors that depend on similarity—most notably factor six and the first‑factor commercial impression. Litigants should ensure their arguments and proffers are internally consistent and should press the Board to use a single, coherent definition of the relevant universe. If the Board narrows the universe in a way inconsistent with its own earlier factual findings, preserve the objection and explain precisely how the inconsistent scope affects the weight of third‑party evidence and commercial impression. On appeal, courts will look for such internal inconsistencies and may remand if the Board’s analysis is legally inconsistent.

Appellate preservation: tying the record to DuPont factors and distinguishing legal vs factual challenges. The Federal Circuit reviews the Board’s legal conclusions de novo and factual findings for substantial evidence. To preserve both types of arguments on appeal, parties must do three things well at the TTAB: (1) develop a record that directly ties each piece of evidence to the DuPont factor(s) it is meant to prove or disprove, (2) obtain clear Board findings on contested factual issues so that any challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence is well framed, and (3) articulate discrete legal objections to the Board’s methodology (for example, an assertion that the Board applied an inconsistent scope across factors or applied an incorrect legal standard). Apex succeeded on remand because the Federal Circuit identified legal error (inconsistent scope of similarity across DuPont factors) and vacated the Board’s factor‑6 and factor‑1 findings, while affirming the factor‑2 factual finding supported by substantial evidence. Parties should therefore preserve their legal arguments separately from factual attacks so an appellate court may grasp the nature of the claimed error.

Concluding checklist for practitioners

Gather full documentary proof of relatedness: IDs, definitions, industry reports, contracts, advertising, co‑brand specimens, and witness declarations that explain how consumers encounter services. For third‑party evidence, seek both registrations and proof of actual use, and authenticate marketplace evidence with business records, archived captures, and witness foundation. Quantify and qualify third‑party use: count, measure prominence, demonstrate duration, and document geographic reach and consumer exposure. Use surveys and market metrics to prove commercial strength or weakness. When framing the relevant public, be consistent across factors; if the Board makes a broad relatedness finding, insist that the same universe govern factor six and factor one. Use notices of reliance and testimony declarations intelligently, and preserve foundation and hearsay objections in the record. Finally, tie every major piece of evidence to a particular DuPont factor in your briefs and requests for findings so that, on appeal, you can point to clear factual findings or legal errors—exactly the path used to win vacatur on the narrow issue in Apex.

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. gives a practical road map: get the definitions and marketplace facts right up front; collect widespread, authenticated use evidence if you argue a field is crowded; and insist on internal consistency in the Board’s analytical scope. Do that and you will give the Board—and a reviewing court—the factual and legal structure necessary to decide, and to sustain on appeal, a well‑supported likelihood‑of‑confusion ruling.

# Implications and Applications After Apex

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a short but important Federal Circuit decision that clarifies how the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) must treat the concept of “similarity” once it has made a finding about the relationship between the parties’ goods or services. The court affirmed the Board’s factfinding that credit-card services and banking/finance services were highly similar, but vacated the Board’s handling of two other DuPont factors—the sixth (number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods) and the first (similarity of the marks in their entireties)—because the Board used a narrower definition of “similar” in considering third‑party marks than it had used in finding the services similar. The holding is doctrinally modest but practically significant: once the Board determines that the parties’ services are highly similar (or partially identical), it must apply that same scope of similarity when it evaluates other related DuPont factors that ask whether the marks coexist with similar marks or how consumers perceive the marks in context.

The remainder of this chapter draws out the doctrine established by Apex, explains how to apply it to evidence and argument in TTAB proceedings (and to appeals), and offers practical strategies and classroom hypotheticals that highlight its application—especially in the financial‑services context where house marks and industry descriptors (like “BANK”) are common.

## Key takeaway: uniform scope of “similarity” across DuPont factors

DuPont’s multifactor test governs TTAB likelihood‑of‑confusion determinations. The second DuPont factor concerns the similarity of the parties’ goods or services; the sixth asks whether there are many similar marks in use on similar goods, and the first compares the marks themselves in their entirety. Apex reiterates an important, commonsense rule of analytic consistency: when the Board makes a factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar (or partly legally identical), it cannot then constrict “similarity” for purposes of other DuPont factors to a narrower category that would exclude third‑party uses or registrations that lie within the scope of the Board’s own finding.

Practically, this means that similarity is not a free‑floating term that the Board may define one way for factor two and another way for factor six. If factor two treats “banking and financing services” and “credit card services” as overlapping or highly similar, then factor six must consider third‑party marks that are used in any of those related services when evaluating crowding and commercial strength. The same point is true for factor one: commercial impression (which depends on how the mark appears in the marketplace) must be assessed with the same market scope in mind.

This is not a change in the elements of the DuPont test; it is a constraint on inconsistent application. Apex thus enforces internal logical coherence in the Board’s multifactor analysis and gives practitioners a predictable basis for marshaling—or challenging—the scope of third‑party evidence.

## Predicted impact on TTAB practice: expanded third‑party consideration under Factor 6

Apex will push the Board toward considering a broader universe of third‑party uses in crowded‑field analyses whenever it has already found services to be related. Two concrete consequences follow.

First, opponents and applicants who rely on evidence of third‑party registrations or marketplace use should expect the Board to treat that evidence more expansively. A party asserting that a mark is weak because it is used widely across the financial services sector can no longer be confined to a subcategory (for example, “credit card services”) if the Board has already deemed broader categories (like “banking” or “financial services”) sufficiently related. Conversely, a party trying to insulate its mark by arguing that third‑party uses are in a different, excluded subcategory will face a higher burden where the Board has previously found similarity.

Second, submissions that show third‑party use should be organized to make the cross‑category connections clear: include registrations and specimens across banking, lending, credit card programs, account servicing, and ancillary payment or account management services; show how third parties market the services together; and provide evidence (e.g., industry materials, trade publications, partnerships) that consumers commonly encounter these services under the same house mark or in bundled offerings. The Board will expect a realistic map of the market that aligns with its factual finding about service similarity.

## Financial services implications: house marks, “BANK,” and crowded‑field showings

Financial services present common factual patterns that illuminate Apex’s import.

House marks plus industry descriptors. Many financial marks combine an arbitrary or suggestive house word with a descriptive industry term (e.g., ASPIRE BANK). When the house portion is the dominant distinctive element, the presence of a generic or descriptive element like “bank” can mitigate but not eliminate a likelihood‑of‑confusion finding. Apex does not change that basic calculus. What Apex does require, however, is that the Board assess commercial impression and the field’s crowding with consistent market boundaries. If the Board finds credit card and banking services are related, third‑party uses of “Aspire” with “Card,” “Bank,” or “Financial” across those services should be weighed together when evaluating the mark’s strength and the marks’ overall impression.

Crowded‑field showings may narrow protection. Evidence of many third‑party uses of a formative term across the financial services field tends to show that consumers are accustomed to distinguishing similar marks and therefore that a particular mark is entitled to a narrower scope of protection. Apex increases the importance of assembling comprehensive crowded‑field evidence where services overlap. But quantity alone is not dispositive; courts and the Board still evaluate the nature of the third‑party uses (how prominent the shared element is, whether the third parties are geographically or channel‑restricted, the length and prominence of use, and consumer recognition).

## Reconciling ID Manual changes with marketplace evidence

Apex arose in part because the Board had to determine the proper meaning for services after the Trademark ID Manual entry for “credit card services” had been deleted. That procedural point highlights two complementary rules about identifications of goods and services.

First, the literal phrasing of the identification matters a great deal; a narrowly drafted ID that excludes certain activities can limit the overlap an opposer can rely upon. Applicants and counsel should draft IDs carefully: broad identifications invite broader overlap findings, but narrow IDs can be attacked as merely limiting rights while leaving a playing field for others.

Second, changes in the ID Manual do not displace evidence of actual marketplace practices. Even if a Manual entry is absent, the Board will interpret the services at issue using the record—dictionary definitions, industry materials, and evidence of how services are marketed and provided. Apex confirms that the Board must be rigorous and faithful to its factual findings about marketplace similarity when deciding how to treat third‑party evidence. Practitioners should therefore marshal both carefully worded identifications and contemporaneous marketplace evidence (specimens, joint offerings, partnerships, trade press) to control how the Board defines the relevant scope.

## Strategy checklist for opposers and applicants post‑Apex

Opposers (owners of earlier marks) should:
- Build a record demonstrating the relatedness of the services across categories: show joint marketing, common channels, partnerships (e.g., banks issuing branded cards serviced by a third party), and consumer exposure.
- Assemble comprehensive third‑party evidence across all related services once similarity is established—registrations, specimens, screenshots, advertising spend, and news items that show common house marks or the same formative word across banking, credit, lending, and payment services.
- Emphasize commercial strength of the mark where appropriate: long use, consumer recognition, marketplace prominence.
- Anticipate the applicant’s narrow‑ID strategies and counter with marketplace evidence that the narrowed services are commonly bundled with or subsumed by the opposer’s services.

Applicants (those seeking registration) should:
- Draft IDs tightly where desirable, and, when possible, limit the identification of services to reduce overlap; but understand a narrow ID may be scrutinized for bona fides.
- Submit evidence showing differences in channels, consumers, and trade channels (e.g., retail branches vs. back‑end card program management), and demonstrate that consumers do not encounter the marks as emanating from the same source.
- Provide co‑existence evidence or third‑party examples showing no actual confusion where appropriate.
- If facing a crowded‑field challenge, present evidence showing that the shared formative element is conceptually weak (dictionary meanings, diverse usages) and that third‑party use is limited or qualitatively different.

## Classroom hypotheticals

Hypothetical 1: Extensive cross‑category use. Suppose the Board finds that “online banking services” and “credit card account services” are highly similar. The opposer submits 40 third‑party marks using the word “PIVOT” across online banking, credit cards, loans, and payment processing. The applicant points to only 3 uses in the credit‑card subcategory. How should the Board apply DuPont factor six? Under Apex, the Board must consider the full set of 40 uses because they fall within the Board’s own finding of relatedness. Students should analyze whether quantity plus prominence of the third‑party uses demonstrate conceptual weakness and whether that narrows the opposer’s protection.

Hypothetical 2: Narrow identification. An applicant files for ASPIRE for “online savings accounts only.” The opposer’s registration is ASPIRE for “credit card services,” and the Board finds the services related in part. Does Apex permit the Board to ignore third‑party uses in other banking categories? No. Apex requires consistent scope. But students should also evaluate whether the applicant’s narrow ID meaningfully reduces overlap under factor two (it might) and whether the third‑party evidence still tends to show crowding of ASPIRE across the relevant, overlapping services.

Hypothetical 3: Stylized vs. standard character marks. Applicant seeks a stylized mark ASPIRE BANK (stylized) and opposer owns a standard character mark ASPIRE for financial services. The Board finds related services. Under factor one, how should the Board assess commercial impression? Apex instructs the Board to assess commercial impression with the same market scope used for factor six: consider third‑party uses and whether the stylization meaningfully distinguishes consumer perception. Students should analyze whether a stylized element sufficiently transforms the commercial impression in light of crowded usage of the word ASPIR E.

Each hypothetical invites students to identify what evidence would be dispositive on the record, which DuPont factors are primarily implicated, and which appellate standard (de novo or substantial evidence) would control on appeal.

## Comparative notes: TTAB appeals to the Federal Circuit versus civil actions under § 1071(b)

Apex was resolved on appeal to the Federal Circuit, where the court reviewed the Board’s legal conclusions de novo and its factual findings for substantial evidence. That is the usual route: an appeal from a final TTAB decision goes to the Federal Circuit (28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(4)(B)), and the Federal Circuit affords the Board deference on factual determinations while reviewing legal errors without deference.

There is a procedural alternative in some cases: a party may bring a civil action in district court pursuant to 15 U.S.C. § 1071(b) (where permitted by statute and timing), obtaining a trial de novo in an Article III forum. The district court path allows fuller evidentiary development (live testimony, broader discovery), which may be advisable when the adverse TTAB decision rests on factual findings the party believes are weak or incomplete. By contrast, an appeal to the Federal Circuit limits review to the administrative record and is more focused on whether the Board applied correct legal standards and supported its factual findings with substantial evidence. Choosing between the two remedies involves strategic tradeoffs: speed and precedent (appeal to the Federal Circuit) versus opportunity for a new trial and fuller factual development (district court action).

Practitioners should expect Apex to be cited in appeals that assert inconsistent scope of similarity: a legal error of that sort will be reviewed de novo and is an attractive target for appellate correction.

## Exam and practice takeaways: structuring a DuPont analysis and spotting appeal issues

Structure your DuPont analysis so that the factual predicate is clear and consistent across factors. Begin by defining the relevant goods/services and the relevant consumer and trade channels; explain the factual bases for any relatedness finding; then, when you reach factors that hinge on relatedness (such as factor six and factor one), explicitly state that you are using the same market boundaries. Apex shows that failing to do so exposes the Board’s analysis to reversal.

When preparing for appellate review, separate legal questions (e.g., what legal standard governs relatedness?) from factual ones (e.g., whether the services are related as a matter of fact). Apex is an example of a court reversing because of an internally inconsistent legal application of “similarity” rather than overturning the Board’s factual conclusion on relatedness. Legal errors—misstating or misapplying the applicable standard—are reviewed de novo and are the most promising grounds for reversal. Factual inadequacy claims require a showing that the Board’s conclusions lack the substantial evidence support required on appeal.

Finally, integrate factor interdependence in both litigation and classroom responses. DuPont factors are not independent silos; many overlap and should be analyzed together. Apex enforces coherence among those overlapping factors.

## Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. does not rewrite the DuPont landscape. It makes a narrower but critical point about analytic consistency: once the Board finds the parties’ services related (or partially identical), it must maintain that scope when evaluating other DuPont factors that depend on similarity. For practitioners, the decision underscores the need to litigate (and document) the market definition issue early and clearly, and to present comprehensive third‑party evidence that aligns with the market definition the Board adopts. For students, Apex is a useful exercise in spotting internal inconsistencies in multi‑factor analyses and in understanding how appellate review differentiates legal mistakes from factual adequacy.
