Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### How should evidence of third-party marks be weighed: does marketplace ‘use’ matter more than ‘registration’?
Question:
The opinion cites Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin, which looked at volumes of third-party uses/registrations to show weakness. The Board here discounted 42 non–credit card uses and only credited 9 credit-card uses. On remand, what kinds of third-party evidence (registrations, websites, advertising, consumer exposure metrics) are most probative of conceptual vs. commercial weakness, and is there any principled threshold for what counts as a “considerable” number?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Sixth DuPont factor asks whether a field is crowded with similar marks; both registrations and evidence of actual marketplace use can show weakness, but they are not interchangeable. Registrations tend to show conceptual (relative) weakness; actual use (websites, ads, sales, consumer exposure metrics) is far more probative of commercial weakness — i.e., that consumers have been trained to distinguish similar marks.  
- Apex requires the Board on remand to count third‑party marks across the same scope of services it found under the Second DuPont factor (banking/financial services broadly), not just credit‑card labels. See Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., slip op. at III.B (vacating Board’s narrow scope).  
- There is no fixed numerical cutoff for what is “considerable.” Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin are examples where courts found 26 and a comparable “voluminous” set persuasive; but whether a number is “considerable” depends on the marketplace, similarity of goods/services, and quality of evidence (use v. mere registration, consumer exposure).

Explanation and guidance for the remand analysis

1) Legal framework (as the opinion applies it)
- The Sixth DuPont factor looks to “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods” to assess whether a mark is relatively weak and entitled to a narrow scope of protection. Apex cites Palm Bay, Juice Generation, and Jack Wolfskin for this point. Apex Bank, slip op. § III.B (citing Palm Bay Imps., Juice Gen., Jack Wolfskin).  
- Where the Board has already found the parties’ services to be highly similar (here, partially legally identical banking/finance services), it must use that same scope when identifying third‑party marks to consider under the Sixth factor. Apex Bank, slip op. at III.B (vacating because Board restricted universe to credit‑card uses only).

2) Types of third‑party evidence and what they prove
- Registrations (USPTO registrations or applications) — primarily show conceptual/commonness of a term or element across the field. Multiple third‑party registrations using the same formative (e.g., “Aspire”) support a finding that the word is relatively weak as a source identifier for the field (conceptual weakness). The Board and courts have treated registrational evidence as relevant (see Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation cited in Apex).  
- Actual marketplace use (websites, specimens, advertising, sales materials, contracts, screenshots, trade press) — these show commercial strength or weakness: how frequently consumers actually encounter the term, who uses it in practice, whether channels overlap, and whether consumers have been “educated” to distinguish similar marks. Apex and the cited authorities emphasize that use (not just registration) is persuasive of consumer exposure and thus of a narrowed scope. See Apex Bank, slip op. § III.B (discussing use versus registration in context of Jack Wolfskin/Juice Gen.).  
- Consumer‑facing metrics (search volume, website traffic, ad impressions, sales figures, geographic distribution) — these can be powerful evidence of consumer exposure and marketplace prominence, and thus of commercial strength (or the opposite, if many active competitors dilute distinctiveness). But their probative value depends on linking the metrics to the relevant consumer universe and time period. Apex requires treating the relevant consumer class consistently with the Second factor finding (consumers of similar banking/finance services). Apex Bank, slip op. § III.B.

3) How the Board should weigh these categories on remand
- Use the same scope for third‑party mark collection as the Second factor: because the Board found the services highly similar, include marks used for banking and finance generally (not only narrow “credit card” listings). Apex Bank, slip op. at III.B.  
- Differentiate registrations from use: registrations alone have less weight than registrations plus evidence of use. If a registration is accompanied by evidence that the registrant actually uses the mark in the marketplace (web pages, specimens, sales), it should be given greater weight (as in Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation, which relied on voluminous registration/use). Apex Bank, slip op. § III.B.  
- Look to consumer exposure/channel overlap: evidence that consumers in the relevant market actually encounter multiple “Aspire” marks (ads, card offerings, bank websites, joint marketing, media coverage) supports a finding that consumers are trained to distinguish and that the mark is commercially weak.  
- Consider qualitative aspects: geographic spread, length of use, prominence in the relevant channels, degree of similarity of the third‑party marks to the marks at issue, and whether the third parties operate in the same channels (online, branch networks, co‑branding with banks) will all affect weight.

4) No strict numerical threshold — context matters
- The Apex opinion rejects the Board’s paring down of the universe rather than endorsing a numeric test. The opinion cites Juice Generation’s characterization of 26 third‑party marks as “a considerable number,” and Jack Wolfskin’s discussion of “voluminous evidence” and 14 notable examples, but does not establish any bright‑line count. Apex Bank, slip op. at III.B (citing Juice Gen., Jack Wolfskin).  
- Practically, the Board should explain why the number and nature of third‑party marks it finds are (or are not) sufficient to show weakness, tying the analysis to (a) the breadth of the market for the parties’ services, (b) whether those third‑party marks are actually used and seen by the relevant consumers, and (c) how many such marks are needed to support a conclusion given the field’s size and concentration. In short: quantify if useful, but justify qualitatively.

5) What to expect on remand
- The Board must reassess the Sixth factor using the broader universe of banking/finance marks. It should catalogue registrations and actual uses, separately note registrations with no evidence of use, and explain how many and what kinds of uses support (or do not support) a finding of relative weakness. Because the First factor (commercial impression) was tied to the Sixth, the Board should revisit that factor after reweighing third‑party evidence. Apex Bank, slip op. at III.C.

Bottom line
On remand the Board should (1) consider third‑party marks across the same, broader service scope it already found similar, (2) treat registrations and marketplace use differently — giving greater weight to evidence of actual consumer exposure (websites, ads, sales, metrics), and (3) decline to apply any bright‑line numerical cutoff; instead explain why the quantity and quality of the third‑party marks it finds are sufficient (or insufficient) to show conceptual or commercial weakness in context. The Apex opinion instructs the Board precisely to correct its overly narrow universe and then reweigh the Sixth (and, in consequence, the First) DuPont factors.
