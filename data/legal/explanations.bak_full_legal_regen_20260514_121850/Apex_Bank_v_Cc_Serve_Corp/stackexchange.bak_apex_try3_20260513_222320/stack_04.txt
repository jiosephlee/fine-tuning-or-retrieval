Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### What is the legal justification for requiring a consistent scope of “similar goods” across DuPont factors two and six?
Question:
The court faults the Board for defining similarity broadly under factor two (services are highly similar/legally identical in part) but narrowly under factor six (only credit card services). Is there a doctrinal rule that the scope of “similar goods/services” must be consistent across factors, or is the court creating a harmonization principle here? How should the Board determine the correct breadth of the relevant marketplace for third-party-use evidence post–Apex Bank?
Answer:
Short answer
The Court’s holding in Apex Bank is not inventing a new substantive test for DuPont factor six; it is insisting on reasoned consistency between the Board’s factual findings. Because factor six only requires “similar” (not identical) goods/services, once the Board has already found the parties’ services to be highly similar (indeed “legally identical, in part”) under factor two, it must use that same scope when deciding which third‑party marks count under factor six—unless it gives a reasoned, record‑based explanation for departing from that scope. Apex Bank thus enforces a harmonization/consistency principle grounded in the existing DuPont precedent the Board already applies.

Why the Court says this
- DuPont factor two asks whether the parties’ goods/services are related; factor six asks whether there are many similar marks in use on similar goods/services. The Court recalled that factor six “does not require identical goods—only similar ones” (citing Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation). Apex Bank, slip op. § III.B.
- The Board found the parties’ services “highly similar” (even partially “legally identical”) under factor two. The Court held it was error for the Board then to restrict the universe of third‑party marks considered under factor six to a narrower category (credit‑card services only), saying “the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors.” Apex Bank, slip op. § III.B (emphasis supplied).
- The Court framed this as an error of analysis, not a new substantive limitation: “We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.” Id.

What this means doctrinally
- There is no freestanding rule that every DuPont factor must use the exact same phrasing in every case. Rather, Apex Bank requires internal consistency: factual findings about what services are “similar” (and the marketplace/consumer class that follows from that finding) should inform which third‑party uses are “similar” for factor six.
- Put differently: factor six still asks about marks on “similar goods/services,” but the Board cannot bootstrap a broad similarity finding under factor two and then narrow the relevant universe under factor six without explaining why the narrower definition is supported by the record.

How the Board should approach the scope of the relevant marketplace for third‑party‑use evidence post‑Apex Bank
Follow a two‑step, record‑based approach:

1) Fix the baseline similarity/factual finding under factor two.
   - Use the pleaded/identified services, dictionary/identifications, third‑party registrations, channels of trade, classes of customers, and marketing evidence to determine how broadly the services are related (as the Board did when it found banking/finance services encompass credit‑card activity). Apex Bank, § III.A.

2) Use that baseline to define which third‑party marks are “on similar goods/services” for factor six.
   - Include third‑party uses and registrations that fall within the scope of similarity established under factor two (the Court said the Board “should retain the same scope” when considering factor six). Apex Bank, § III.B.
   - Analyze those third‑party examples to assess commercial strength (how many, how widespread, how prominent), remembering that factor six does not require identical goods—only similarity (Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation). Apex Bank, § III.B.
   - If the Board wants to consider a narrower universe than the factor‑two finding would suggest, it must explain why the record supports a different scope (for example, evidence that consumers treat a subset of services as distinct, distinct channels of trade, different classes of buyers, or other record evidence justifying a narrower marketplace).

Practical consequences
- A Board finding that services are “highly similar” (or “partially identical”) will ordinarily require the Board to consider third‑party marks across that same breadth when evaluating factor six.
- Reconsideration of factor six may change the commercial‑strength analysis and therefore may require reexamination of factor one (as the Court ordered here). Apex Bank, § III.C.

Bottom line
Apex Bank enforces internal consistency across DuPont factors: once the Board determines the relevant scope of similarity under factor two, it should carry that scope forward to factor six (and explain any departure). This is a harmonization requirement grounded in existing precedent that factor six looks to “similar” goods/services—not a new, more demanding rule.
