Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# Standards of Review Matter: Parsing Legal Error from Substantial Evidence in TTAB Appeals

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a tidy reminder of a basic appellate truth in trademark oppositions: whether an error is reversible often turns on whether it was one of law or one of fact. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar under the second DuPont factor but nonetheless vacated the Board’s analysis of the sixth DuPont factor (and, because of its interdependence, the first factor) for legal error. The decision shows how a legal misstep—here, an inconsistent narrowing of the universe of third‑party marks—can contaminate the Board’s reasoning even where other findings enjoy substantial‑evidence support. For practitioners, the opinion is a useful lesson in appellate focus: preserve precise legal objections to the Board’s methodology, and build the record and argumentation to make those legal errors readily reviewable.

## What the court did

The Federal Circuit applied the familiar bifurcated standard of review: legal conclusions de novo, factual findings for substantial evidence. In this case the Board found that Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK mark and CC Serve’s earlier ASPIRE registration covered highly similar services—indeed, the Board concluded the services were “legally identical, in part” under the second DuPont factor, and the Federal Circuit affirmed that factual conclusion as supported by substantial evidence.

Where the Board ran into trouble was the sixth DuPont factor, which asks whether there are numerous third‑party uses of similar marks on similar goods or services (evidence that can show a mark is weak and entitled to a narrower scope of protection). Having already concluded the parties’ services were highly similar, the Board nevertheless restricted its sixth‑factor inquiry to third‑party uses tied narrowly to “credit card services,” excluding third‑party ASPIRE uses in other banking and financing contexts. The Federal Circuit held that restriction was legally erroneous because the sixth factor asks about similar—not identical—goods and services, and the Board’s own second‑factor finding required it to consider a broader set of third‑party uses. Because the sixth factor bears directly on the mark’s commercial strength—and commercial strength in turn figures heavily in the first‑factor comparison of overall commercial impression—the Court vacated the Board’s sixth‑factor and first‑factor findings and remanded for reconsideration consistent with the correct legal standard.

## Why the legal/factual distinction matters here

Two principles are on display. First, substantial‑evidence review is deferential. If the Board’s factual conclusions have record support, an appellate court will generally leave them undisturbed. That explains why the Federal Circuit affirmed the second DuPont factor: the Board examined the service descriptions, relied on dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations, and reached a reasonable factual conclusion about overlap.

Second, legal errors are reversible. The scope-of-similarity test the Board applied under the sixth factor was legal in nature—essentially a misconstruction of the relevant legal standard—and the Federal Circuit corrected it de novo. Where the Board applied a stricter standard for similarity under the sixth factor than it had for the second, the Court treated that as a legal inconsistency that could not be glossed over simply because other findings were supported by evidence.

The opinion thus underscores that an appellate court will not simply sanitize an internally inconsistent administrative analysis by picking and choosing findings to uphold. When a legal error infects the analytical framework—particularly in areas where factors are logically interlinked—it can force recalibration of other parts of the decision.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Apex Bank cements some pragmatic rules about DuPont practice. Most important is the expectation of coherence across factors: once the Board establishes a scope of similarity between services for one factor, it should not narrow its lens under related factors in a way that defeats that earlier finding. The sixth factor asks about “similar goods” and need not be limited to identical ones; a Board that treats it as requiring identity risks reversal.

The decision also reiterates the continuing importance of third‑party use evidence in assessing commercial strength. But it reminds practitioners that the persuasive force of such evidence depends on how broadly the Board defines the relevant field. A Board’s artificial narrowing of the reference class for third‑party marks can undercount relevant uses and overstate a registrant’s strength.

Finally, the case highlights that the first DuPont factor—overall commercial impression—is not insulated from error in a companion factor. When commercial strength is put in doubt by a legally flawed sixth‑factor analysis, the first factor must be revisited, because commercial impression is informed by strength of the mark.

## Practical takeaways for litigants and counsel

For TTAB litigants and their appellate counsel, the decision translates into several concrete strategies. First, preserve specific objections to legal standards and definitions the Board uses—especially the definition of the relevant public and the scope of “similar goods or services.” A clear record showing that the Board applied an inconsistent or too‑narrow legal standard will be the fastest route to reversal on legal grounds.

Second, when building the sixth‑factor record, submit third‑party registrations and use evidence framed broadly across the relevant field of services, not just the narrow service label the registrant used. If the parties’ services overlap in the second‑factor analysis, make sure the Board does not cabin the sixth factor to a narrower conception that undermines the connection.

Third, appreciate the uphill nature of substantial‑evidence challenges. If you can show error of law—misstatement of the governing legal test, inconsistent scope across factors, or an impermissible requirement of identity—you stand a far better chance than by arguing that the Board’s factfinding was just wrong. The Federal Circuit’s deference on facts means that appellate strategy should foreground legal arguments.

Finally, anticipate remediation on remand: if the Board is ordered to re‑examine the sixth factor under an appropriately broad conception of similar services, expect it to re‑weigh the mark’s strength and then to revisit first‑factor comparisons and any downstream DuPont balancing that depends on strength. Parties should be prepared to supply supplemental evidence of third‑party use and consumer perception, and to brief clearly how those materials bear on commercial strength and confusion likelihood.

## Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve is not novel as doctrinal law, but it is pedagogically useful. It shows the different bite that legal error and factual insufficiency have on appeal, reminds practitioners that DuPont factors are interdependent, and reinforces the importance of consistent scope across the Board’s analyses. For lawyers, the practical lesson is straightforward: build the record broadly where similarity is contested, preserve precise legal challenges to the Board’s standards, and aim your appellate brief at legal errors that will survive de novo review rather than relying solely on an argument that the Board got the facts wrong.
