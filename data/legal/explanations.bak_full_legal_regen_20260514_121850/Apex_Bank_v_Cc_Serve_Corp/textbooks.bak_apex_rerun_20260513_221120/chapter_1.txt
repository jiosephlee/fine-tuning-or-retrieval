Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Chapter 1: # Foundations of Likelihood of Confusion: Section 2(d), TTAB Practice, and Appellate Review

This chapter sets out the doctrinal foundation and practical architecture governing refusals under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, the procedural posture and evidentiary dynamics of Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) oppositions, and the appellate review rules that shape what issues survive to the Federal Circuit. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., 2025 WL ___ (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025), supplies a recent and instructive example: the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that the parties’ services were highly similar (the second DuPont factor), but vacated and remanded the Board’s treatment of third‑party use (the sixth DuPont factor) and the marks’ overall commercial impression (the first DuPont factor) because the Board applied inconsistent scopes of similarity across factors. That outcome illustrates three persistent themes: (1) the DuPont framework is flexible but must be applied consistently; (2) the TTAB’s findings rest on the evidentiary record made below; and (3) appellate courts review legal questions de novo but defer to the Board’s factual findings when supported by substantial evidence.

## Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act: text, purpose, and where refusals fit

Section 2(d) provides that the Patent and Trademark Office shall refuse registration if a mark “so resembles” a previously registered mark “as to be likely, when used on or in connection with the goods or services of the applicant, to cause confusion, or to cause mistake, or to deceive.” 15 U.S.C. § 1052(d). The statutory scheme balances two policy goals: protecting consumers from confusion about source and protecting owners’ goodwill by granting a right to exclude confusingly similar marks in the registration context. A Section 2(d) refusal is a refusal of registration—it does not by itself adjudicate infringement in the marketplace—but registration is valuable (constructive notice, prima facie evidence of validity and ownership), so disputes over registration often determine whether new entrants can obtain those benefits.

Oppositions and cancellation proceedings before the TTAB are the routine vehicles for resolving Section 2(d) claims in the administrative process. The Board applies a multi‑factor framework to determine whether confusion is likely; registration will be refused if the Board finds a likelihood of confusion under that framework.

## TTAB practice: from filing to opposition to decision

The administrative path begins with an application filed in the USPTO. Many applicants file intent‑to‑use (ITU) applications under Section 1(b); the mark is published for opposition before use is required. During prosecution, third parties may file letters of protest, which can bring evidence to the examiner’s attention but do not substitute for a formal opposition. If the mark survives examination, it publishes in the Official Gazette, triggering a statutory 30‑day window (extendable) for any party to oppose. An opposition is litigated before the TTAB under Board rules: pleadings, discovery, testimony periods with written direct and rebuttal evidence (declarations and exhibits), and an oral hearing.

Practical implications visible in Apex: Apex filed ITU applications for ASPIRE BANK; CC Serve submitted a letter of protest in prosecution, but the examiner nevertheless published the mark. CC Serve then filed an opposition asserting Section 2(d). The Board heard evidence and made findings about the parties’ services and third‑party uses from materials in the record. Those materials—what was introduced into evidence—proved decisive on appeal. That procedural chain highlights two rules every practitioner must respect: (1) the letter of protest can alert the examiner but cannot substitute for a fully litigated opposition; and (2) evidence not put into the TTAB record will generally not be entertained on appeal.

## The DuPont framework: flexibility, relevant factors, and the Board’s obligations

The Board assesses likelihood of confusion using the factors laid out in In re E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973) (the “DuPont factors”). There are thirteen listed factors, including: similarity of the marks (factor one); similarity of the goods/services and channels of trade (factor two); conditions of purchase and consumer sophistication (factor four); evidence of actual confusion (factor six is number and nature of similar marks? — note that courts and the Board sometimes enumerate differently; the controlling principle is that the Board should consider relevant DuPont factors). The DuPont test is not a mathematical checklist; the Board need not discuss every factor, only those that are relevant and supported by the record. M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378, 1382 (Fed. Cir. 2006).

Two points about DuPont application recur in appellate opinions and in practice:

- The factors are flexible and contextual. Some factors may be dispositive in the right case (e.g., identical marks for identical goods), while others may be neutral. The Board’s task is to analyze the factors supported by evidence and to explain its reasoning.

- Consistency in scope matters. When the Board defines the scope of relevant goods, channels of trade, and consumers in analyzing one factor, it must reasonably retain that scope (or explain why it changes) when analyzing related factors. Apex demonstrates why: the Board found Apex’s “banking and financing services” and CC Serve’s “credit card services” highly similar (factor two). But in assessing third‑party use (factor six)—which tests the mark’s practical strength by surveying similar marks—the Board limited the pool of third‑party Aspire marks to those expressly used for “credit card services,” excluding other banking or financial uses. The Federal Circuit found that inconsistent. If the services are “highly similar,” then third‑party uses in the same broader field (banking/finance) are probative of weakness; restricting consideration to one subset without justification risks error.

## Identifications of goods and services: the written description controls, and default presumptions

The Board compares the parties’ legally defined goods and services as they appear in the application or registration. The test is not whether, in a particular market, the parties actually compete, but whether the goods/services as identified could be related in commerce. See, e.g., Coach Servs. Inc. v. Triumph Learning LLC, 668 F.3d 1356, 1369 (Fed. Cir. 2012). Where an identification is broad (e.g., “banking and financing services”), the Board often interprets that scope through dictionary definitions, the Trademark ID Manual, evidence of industry practice, or third‑party registrations that show how the descriptive terms are commonly used. The Board also applies the presumption that the channels of trade and classes of consumers identified in a registration are those contemplated by the identifications; absent limiting language, the Board assumes that the registrant will use the mark on the full sweep of the identified services and in all normal channels of trade for them. This is why meticulous drafting of identifications matters.

In Apex, the Board construed “credit card services” broadly—beyond merely issuing a physical card to include account servicing, processing, authorization, monitoring, and counseling. The Board then concluded that dictionary and regulatory definitions of “banking” and “finance” encompass issuance of credit (including through credit cards), and therefore the services overlapped and were, in part, legally identical. That construction of the identifications controlled the second‑factor analysis and should have controlled related factor analyses absent a reasoned departure.

## Third‑party use and the sixth DuPont factor: crowding, commercial weakness, and proper scope

Third‑party registrations and marketplace uses of similar marks are relevant to show that a mark is conceptually or commercially weak—that is, entitled to a narrower scope of protection. See Palm Bay Imps., Inc. v. Veuve Clicquot Ponsardin Maison Fondee En 1772, 396 F.3d 1369, 1373 (Fed. Cir. 2005); Juice Generation, Inc. v. GS Enters. LLC, 794 F.3d 1334, 1338–39 (Fed. Cir. 2015). The Board may rely on samples of third‑party registrations, advertisements, and other evidence of use to support a finding that a mark is not strong. But the probative set of third‑party uses is those that are similar in mark and used on goods or services that are similar to those at issue. Similarity here does not require identity. See Olde Tyme Foods, Inc. v. Roundy’s, Inc., 961 F.2d 200, 204 (Fed. Cir. 1992).

Apex’s appeal turned on whether the Board properly defined the universe of third‑party uses to consider. Apex introduced a broad array of third‑party uses of “Aspire” on banking and finance services (42 marks), while the Board limited consideration to nine marks used explicitly for credit card services, because it viewed the relevant consumer as the credit card user. The Federal Circuit held that this narrowing conflicted with the Board’s own finding—made in the second‑factor analysis—that the parties’ services were highly similar, even partially legally identical. Once the Board has found such similarity, third‑party uses across the broader class of banking/finance services are relevant to show weakness. The lesson: when arguing weakness through third‑party use, show (with evidence) how those third‑party goods/services relate to your opponent’s identifications and to the identifications at issue. Conversely, when the Board finds relatedness, it should not arbitrarily narrow the scope of third‑party evidence it will consider.

## The first DuPont factor and commercial impression: why strength and weakness interact

The first DuPont factor—the similarity of the marks in their entirety as to appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression—asks whether, viewed as a whole in marketplace context, the marks are likely to be confused. Commercial impression is not static; it is informed by the mark’s conceptual distinctiveness and by its commercial strength in the marketplace. If a mark is shown to be weak because many third parties use the same or similar terms for related goods, the scope of protection narrows and the first‑factor analysis may tilt differently.

Apex illustrates this interaction. The Board assessed the parties’ marks and concluded they were similar enough to weigh in favor of CC Serve on the first factor. But because the court vacated the Board’s sixth‑factor analysis for improperly narrowing the universe of third‑party uses, it also remanded the first factor: reassessment of third‑party evidence could change the mark’s commercial strength and thus the marks’ overall commercial impressions. That is a routine pattern: when an error in assessing evidence of weakness is corrected, the entirety‑of‑mark analysis frequently must be revisited because commercial impression is interdependent with evidence of marketplace crowding.

## Standards of review on appeal: de novo law, substantial evidence fact

Two interconnected review rules govern appeals from the TTAB:

- Questions of law—primarily the legal conclusion whether a likelihood of confusion exists—are reviewed de novo. Likelihood of confusion is a legal question informed by subsidiary factual findings. See M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382.

- Factual findings, including determinations under individual DuPont factors (e.g., the factual scope of the parties’ services, credibility of witnesses, and whether third parties have used similar marks), are reviewed for substantial evidence. Substantial evidence is “such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support a conclusion.” Consol. Edison Co. of N.Y. v. NLRB, 305 U.S. 197, 229 (1938); Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367, 1370 (Fed. Cir. 2002).

Why the distinction matters: appellate courts will overturn a Board’s legal conclusions when the Board applied the wrong legal standard or failed to make the required findings; but they will sustain factual conclusions if reasonable evidence supports them, even if the appellate court might have reached a different factual conclusion. In Apex, the Federal Circuit found the Board’s factual finding that the services were highly similar to be supported by substantial evidence and therefore affirmed the Board on the second DuPont factor. But the court exercised de novo review of whether the Board’s legal approach to the sixth DuPont factor was correct in light of its earlier factual finding, and found error—thus vacating and remanding.

## The record rule: build the case before the TTAB

A central administrative‑law limitation is the record rule: the Federal Circuit reviews the administrative record compiled before the Board; new evidence cannot be introduced on appeal. M2 Software, 450 F.3d at 1382; 37 C.F.R. § 2.142(d) (motion practice for reopening record before the Board in limited circumstances). Thus litigants must assemble their proof at the TTAB: declarations, third‑party registrations and samples of use, advertising and sales materials, consumer surveys, expert testimony, and any pertinent dictionary or regulatory definitions.

Apex underscores this imperative. Both parties presented evidence—definitions, third‑party examples, prosecution history, and a description of how CC Serve’s business model uses bank partners to issue ASPIRE‑branded cards—and the court’s review turned on what the Board had actually considered. On remand, the Board will act on the same record (plus any additional evidence properly admitted during remand proceedings), not on new materials offered first to the Federal Circuit.

Practical consequences: identify early what you must prove at the TTAB and collect that evidence. If you intend to show relatedness across product lines, put in third‑party registrations and use examples demonstrating the interrelatedness. If you intend to show weakness, include representative third‑party uses across the whole field. If you intend to show consumer sophistication or limits on channels of trade, present evidence of how the goods/services are marketed and sold.

## Anchoring precedents: DuPont, M2 Software, Bose, and modern refinements

Several precedents frame modern Section 2(d) practice:

- In re E. I. DuPont DeNemours & Co., 476 F.2d 1357 (C.C.P.A. 1973): established the multi‑factor likelihood‑of‑confusion test and the principle that courts need only address relevant DuPont factors.

- M2 Software, Inc. v. M2 Commc’ns, Inc., 450 F.3d 1378 (Fed. Cir. 2006): clarified standards of review for Board decisions and reiterated the record rule.

- Bose Corp. v. QSC Audio Prods., Inc., 293 F.3d 1367 (Fed. Cir. 2002): reaffirmed that DuPont factors are findings of fact reviewable for substantial evidence.

- Palm Bay Imps., Juice Generation, and Jack Wolfskin: each addresses third‑party use and how the “crowded field” argument affects a mark’s scope. Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin show that the quantity and quality of third‑party uses matter; a handful of similar third‑party marks may be insufficient to show weakness, whereas voluminous and probative examples may.

- Olde Tyme Foods and cases like Coach Services: reinforce that similarity of goods/services does not require identity—relatedness for likelihood‑of‑confusion purposes can be established by evidence showing that the goods/services are complementary, marketed in the same channels, or otherwise connected in consumers’ minds.

Apex fits within this lineage. The Federal Circuit relied on DuPont and the standards articulated in these cases to review whether the Board applied the right legal approach and relied on substantial evidence for its factual findings.

## Applying these principles: what Apex teaches practitioners

Apex offers several concrete takeaways:

- Define and prove relatedness. To prevail on relatedness (factor two), demonstrate with evidence—dictionary definitions, third‑party registrations, industry materials, or business practice descriptions—how the identified goods/services overlap in function, channels, or commercial purpose. In Apex the Board’s careful parsing of the identifications and the dictionary/regulatory definitions supported its finding that banking/finance and credit card services are highly similar.

- Be comprehensive with third‑party evidence. If you will argue that a mark is weak, submit a representative cross‑section of third‑party registrations and actual uses across the related field. Don’t assume the Board will limit its inquiry to a narrow slice—if the services are related, third‑party uses across the broader field are relevant. Apex required remand because the Board selectively considered third‑party uses inconsistent with its own finding of relatedness.

- Preserve the record. Because appeals lie to the Federal Circuit under substantial‑evidence review, evidence not introduced below is generally lost. Use TTAB discovery rules, and plan early for the kinds of proof the Board treats as dispositive: third‑party use, consumer sophistication, channels of trade, and actual confusion evidence (if available).

- Think about scope consistency. Once the Board defines the relevant consumer and channels of trade for one factor, it should justify any departure when treating another factor. The Federal Circuit will scrutinize inconsistencies that change the probative universe—especially when the Board’s own factual findings make a different universe reasonable.

- Anticipate interplay among factors. Commercial strength and commercial impression (factor one) depend in part on evidence adduced under the sixth factor. When you develop your case, present both conceptual and commercial evidence of strength or weakness so the Board can evaluate the marks’ entirety in context.

## Remedies and appellate posture: affirmance-in-part, vacatur-in-part, remand

When the Federal Circuit finds that some Board findings are supported by substantial evidence and other parts suffer from legal error or lack support, it commonly issues an affirmance‑in‑part, vacatur‑in‑part, and remand. That is what happened in Apex: the court affirmed the finding that the services were highly similar, vacated the Board’s constrained treatment of third‑party uses, and remanded factors one and six for reconsideration under a coherent scope. Remand is a common remedial posture when further factfinding or reanalysis by the Board is needed to apply the correct legal standard or to integrate a corrected evidentiary scope into the remaining factors.

On remand, the Board may: (1) reconsider the sixth DuPont factor using the broader set of third‑party uses; (2) revisit the first DuPont factor in light of any altered view of the mark’s commercial strength; and (3) make any consequential adjustments to the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion conclusion. Parties can seek to introduce additional evidence during remand proceedings only under the Board’s procedural rules; usually, remand operates on the existing record unless the Board exercises its discretion to reopen for limited purposes.

## Practical checklist for TTAB practitioners (derived from Apex)

- Draft identifications carefully. Remember that the comparison is based on the identifications in the application or registration.

- Anticipate relevant channels of trade and consumers, and put evidence into the record that demonstrates who the consumers are and how the goods/services are marketed.

- Assemble robust third‑party evidence if you will argue weakness: registrations plus samples of actual marketplace use across all related goods/services.

- If you challenge relatedness, put in documentary evidence (industry materials, contracts showing partnerships, testimonial evidence) to show that services are not just theoretically related but are marketed or provided in complementary ways.

- Use dictionary definitions and the Trademark ID Manual to support construction of terms, but also be ready to supplement with real‑world evidence showing how terms are used.

- Preserve every piece of relevant evidence at the TTAB; do not expect to add new proof on appeal.

- If the Board reaches an adverse legal conclusion, consider whether any factual findings underlying that conclusion lack substantial‑evidence support or whether the Board applied an inconsistent legal standard across factors—those are the typical grounds for vacatur and remand.

Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. reinforces familiar but important lessons. The DuPont framework demands fact‑driven, evidence‑based analysis; the Board’s findings are entitled to deference when supported by substantial evidence; and legal errors or inconsistent scope applications will lead to vacatur and remand. For practitioners, the lesson is clear: build a complete, coherent record at the TTAB and make the Board’s job straightforward by supplying the evidence and reasoned arguments needed to support each DuPont factor you rely on.
