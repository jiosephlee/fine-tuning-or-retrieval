Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

Chapter 11: # Channels of Trade, Consumer Sophistication, and Conditions of Purchase

This chapter examines three interrelated strands of the likelihood-of-confusion inquiry that often decide trademark disputes in the financial-services sector: the channels of trade and the presumptions that attach to broad identifications; the role of consumer sophistication and the conditions of purchase (the care with which consumers make selecting decisions); and how online delivery, price, and a crowded marketplace interact with mark strength and similarity. The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., decided in 2025, provides a concrete vehicle for showing how these doctrines operate in practice and how errors in applying them can require remand. Read together, these themes demonstrate that seemingly small choices about how parties frame their services and how the Board defines the relevant consumer and channels of trade can have outsized effects in financial-services cases.

## Presumptions for unrestricted identifications: all normal channels and classes of consumers

When an applicant or registrant describes its goods or services broadly—without limiting channels of trade, classes of purchasers, or types of marketing—the Board and courts apply a familiar evidentiary presumption: the identified goods or services encompass all of the normal channels of trade and all classes of consumers for those goods or services. That presumption is practical: a trademark owner who chooses a broad identification cannot later argue that its mark is limited to a narrow market when asserting a likelihood of confusion. Conversely, an applicant defending against an opposition cannot rely on an examiner’s or registrant’s broad identification to narrow the field unless the record contains evidence to justify a narrower scope.

In Apex Bank, the Board made a key factual finding—based on the parties’ identifications and meanings of the services—that Apex’s banking and financing services and CC Serve’s credit card services were “highly similar,” and in part “legally identical.” That finding carried consequences. The Federal Circuit relied on it to reverse the Board’s narrow consideration of third-party marks under the sixth DuPont factor. The Board had limited its inquiry to marks used for credit card services, excluding marks that used “Aspire” in connection with other banking and financial services. The court faulted that approach: if the parties’ services are found to be highly similar (or overlap), the universe of comparable third-party marks must be at least as broad as the scope used to determine similarity. In short, you cannot find services overlapping for one DuPont factor and simultaneously treat them as narrower for another factor without reasoned explanation.

Practically, the presumption of all-normal-channels means a party should:

- Pay attention to the pleaded identifications. If defending an application, consider proposing reasonable limitations to channels or classes of purchasers early in prosecution or show evidence that the marketplace is narrower. If pursuing an opposition, exploit the breadth when arguing overlap of channels.
- Anticipate that a finding of similarity for the second DuPont factor will broaden the universe of third-party uses and channels relevant to other factors (for example, the sixth factor on third-party marks and the consideration of channels of trade under the overall likelihood analysis). Apex teaches that the Board must retain a consistent scope unless record evidence supports a narrower focus.
- Introduce concrete record evidence (e.g., marketing materials, distribution agreements, third-party registrations that themselves include explicit channel limitations) if you want the Board to treat a service as reaching only a subset of the usual channels or consumers.

## Consumer sophistication in banking and credit contexts: when it helps and when it does not

Consumer sophistication is one of the recurrent contextual considerations in a DuPont analysis. The basic intuition is straightforward: a more sophisticated or discriminating purchaser is less likely to be confused by similar marks. But sophistication is neither a talisman nor a one-size-fits-all defense. Courts look to actual marketplace realities, not labels. The critical questions are: who is the relevant purchaser? How complex or expensive is the transaction? How much time and attention does a typical purchaser expend when choosing between the services at issue?

In financial services, “sophistication” can mean different things depending on the product and relationship. Institutional purchasers selecting a commercial banking partner, or a corporation negotiating a private-label credit-card program, typically exercise high sophistication — they conduct due diligence, compare competitors, and negotiate lengthy contracts. On the other hand, an ordinary consumer choosing a retail bank or a consumer credit card often makes a quicker, lower-effort choice influenced by convenience, advertising, or a branch location. Even within consumer markets there are gradations: someone opening a mortgage may be more deliberate than someone signing an instant online deposit account.

Apex illustrates how the Board framed the relevant class of consumers. Because it concluded that the parties’ services were highly similar and overlapped in the credit-card/financial-services space, the Board limited its view of the “relevant public” to consumers of credit card services. That narrowed the Board’s focus for the sixth DuPont factor (and, in turn, its inquiry into mark weakness). On appeal the Federal Circuit faulted the Board’s subsequent narrowing of third-party comparisons—holding that the Board could not narrow the consumer universe for one factor after finding broad overlap in another. Apex thus underscores two lessons:

- Precisely identifying the relevant consumer class is essential. A party that convinces the Board that its services are narrowly targeted may obtain the benefit of a sophisticated-purchaser analysis for some factors; conversely, an opponent that secures a broad overlap finding may force a broader consumer lens.
- Evidence of sophistication must be specific and tied to the product at issue. General assertions that “bank customers are sophisticated” will not be enough. Courts want evidence showing how consumers make the particular purchasing decision: typical search and selection behavior, use of brokers or intermediaries, necessity of contractual review, or presence of custom underwriting. Affidavits, industry surveys, consumer-decision studies, and example agreements can all show higher sophistication.

When sophistication helps: where the record shows that the relevant purchasers are institutions, businesses, or consumers engaged in careful comparative shopping (for example, negotiated business banking relationships, syndicated loans, or large commercial lending arrangements), courts will often give weight to the “conditions of purchase” and find a reduced likelihood of confusion.

When sophistication does not help: for common consumer financial products that are relatively low-commitment or purchased impulsively (e.g., quick consumer checking accounts, standard credit cards marketed broadly), courts frequently treat purchasers as less careful. The mere complexity of financial services as a sector does not automatically mean purchasers are sophisticated with respect to brand source.

## Price and care: credit products versus deposit accounts

Price and the resulting degree of consumer care are closely connected. Higher-priced goods or services generally invite more careful consideration, while lower-priced or routine transactions tend to be made quickly and with less brand scrutiny. In financial services, however, price is a complex metric: some high-dollar products (mortgages, commercial loans) involve careful shopping; other significant-value products (credit-card accounts that carry large revolving balances) can be obtained with minimal friction via online sign-up or in-branch offers. Similarly, deposit accounts—while often of modest initiation cost—may have long-term implications for consumers’ relationship with a bank, suggesting potential for more careful selection in some contexts.

Applying these ideas: when a party argues that consumers will exercise heightened care, they should show that the purchase involves substantial monies, binding contractual commitments, time-consuming underwriting, or negotiation. For example:

- Mortgages and commercial credit facilities: high stakes, multi-stage underwriting, counsel involved — these tend to support an inference of more careful purchasing and may reduce the chance of confusion.
- Credit cards and consumer checking accounts: despite potential long-term impact, initial acquisition is often low-friction (online apps, point-of-sale offers). Many consumers accept cards or open accounts quickly, which diminishes the protective value of “sophistication” arguments.

In Apex, the Board recognized the variety of services grouped under “credit card services” (issuing cards, processing, authorization, account management). The Board explicitly described the credit card services domain as including both account management and issuance—services that consumers may experience in different ways and with different levels of attention. This points to a practical approach: break “financial services” into the discrete transactions that consumers actually perform and tie evidence of care (or lack of it) to the discrete transaction. An opposition should not rely on a generic statement that “banking customers are careful” but instead show the actual purchasing steps and the salience of brand at each step.

## Online versus brick-and-mortar delivery of financial services

The delivery channel matters. The proliferation of digital banking and fintech platforms has changed how consumers discover, compare, and select financial services. Courts increasingly recognize that online channels can amplify some kinds of confusion (similar logos appearing in search results or app stores) while attenuating others (clear web pages and disclosures that make source distinctions obvious). Whether online delivery increases or decreases the likelihood of confusion depends on how consumers encounter marks in practice.

Key dynamics to consider:

- Visibility and juxtaposition: online marketplaces, app stores, and search results often present multiple providers in proximity. A consumer seeing two “Aspire” marks in a single search results page may be more prone to confusion than a consumer visiting a single branch location where in-person signage and attendant conversation provide multiple cues.
- Control and richness of presentation: registrants and applicants can control their own web pages and app listings to emphasize distinctive design elements, disclaimers, and corporate identity. High-quality brand presentation can reduce confusion when consumers land on a company’s dedicated property.
- Speed of selection: on the web, consumers often click through quickly, increasing the risk of confusion where marks are similar and the browsing experience does not immediately make source differences obvious.
- Cross-channel exposure: many financial-services consumers interact with firms both online and offline. Where consumers begin their relationship online but later complete transactions in branch (or vice versa), brand confusion can propagate across channels.

For litigants, practical rules of thumb follow: gather evidence about how consumers typically encounter and select the relevant service. This can include analytics demonstrating pathways to account opening, shopper-experience studies, screenshots of search results and app-store presentations, and affidavits from customers describing their experience. In Apex, although the opinion did not focus on online versus brick-and-mortar as a dispositive point, the Board’s initial narrowing to credit-card consumers implicitly recognized the concrete ways consumers encounter credit-card products (often via partnerships between banks and processors and via online offers). A properly developed record showing the predominant channels for acquisition—online sign-up pages, in-branch applications, co-branded card offers at merchants—would have helped the Board and the court define the right universe of channels and consumers.

## Interplay with mark strength and similarity: sophisticated consumers in crowded fields

The sixth DuPont factor—evidence of third-party use of similar marks on similar goods—operates at the intersection of channels of trade and consumer sophistication. Evidence of widespread third-party use of a word or device in a product category tends to show the mark is conceptually or commercially weak and thus entitled to a more limited scope of protection. The rationale is that when many competitors use similar marks, consumers are trained to attend to subtle differences, not to assume common source.

Apex exposes how calibrating the scope of comparison influences the assessment of mark strength. The Board, after finding the parties’ services highly similar, nevertheless limited its sixth-factor analysis to third-party marks used only for credit card services; it excluded marks in other banking and financing contexts. The Federal Circuit held that limitation inconsistent: once the Board determined that banking and credit-card services were highly similar (and thus within the same relevant market), it had to treat third-party marks across the broader banking and finance spectrum as relevant evidence of crowdedness. Put differently, the field against which you measure “crowdedness” must match the field in which you assert similarity.

Sophistication modifies this relationship. In a crowded field of consumer-facing, low-commitment products with unsophisticated purchasers, even a sizable number of third-party uses may not inoculate a mark from protection: consumers are less likely to devote effort distinguishing numerous similar marks if they make quick choices. Conversely, in markets where purchasers are sophisticated and make careful comparisons between suppliers, evidence of crowdedness can be more powerful in narrowing the scope of protection.

Thus, consider the following when arguing mark strength or weakness:

- Define the competitive field carefully and consistently. If you argue that your services overlap with a broad banking field, you cannot ignore evidence of third-party “Aspire” uses in wider banking contexts.
- Link consumer sophistication evidence to the crowdedness argument. Demonstrate that purchasers operating in the crowded field are the same purchasers whose restraint or discriminating behavior matters.
- Produce market evidence that shows real-world third-party co-existence without confusion: co-branded marketing, contemporaneous adverts showing multiple “Aspire” marks in use in the same channels, and consumer awareness studies can all be persuasive.

In Apex, the court vacated the Board’s sixth-factor ruling because the Board’s narrower definition artificially minimized the impression of a crowded field and thus might have overallocated protective scope to CC Serve’s mark. That decision highlights the importance of consistent analytical scope across DuPont factors.

## Developing evidence for or against heightened care in financial decision-making

Because the DuPont analysis and the inquiry into consumer sophistication are so fact-sensitive, litigants and counsel should plan tailored evidentiary programs. The following categories of evidence are particularly relevant in financial-services disputes:

1. Transactional and procedural evidence
   - Typical account opening flows (online and in-branch): screenshots, step-by-step guides, click-through paths.
   - Documentation requirements and underwriting processes: required documentation lists, time-to-approval metrics, and whether underwriting is automated or manual.
   - Length and nature of contracts and termination rights.

2. Pricing and economic indicators
   - Fee schedules, APRs, initiation fees, and balances to show how economically significant the purchase is.
   - Comparative tables showing differences in what consumers pay or get under the two services.

3. Marketing and channels of distribution
   - Advertising materials, co-branding agreements (for example, bank-processor contracts for credit cards), and placement evidence (search results, app-store screens, merchant point-of-sale visuals).
   - Distribution agreements indicating whether services are sold primarily through third parties, brokers, a bank’s branch network, or direct-to-consumer online channels.

4. Consumer behavior and surveys
   - Trade or independent consumer surveys tailored to the precise question of source confusion (with careful survey design and expert testimony).
   - Analytics and consumer journey studies demonstrating how consumers discover and choose services.

5. Industry practices and testimony
   - Declarations from industry participants explaining how products are selected, who makes the decision, and how brand identity functions in vendor selection.
   - Evidence of standard market practices (e.g., whether customers typically rely on price, rewards, or brand for card selection).

6. Evidence of marketplace coexistence and actual confusion
   - Instances of actual confusion (complaints, misdirected calls, customer communications) can be powerful, though their absence is not dispositive.
   - Evidence of long-term, peaceful coexistence in the same channels can support arguments of low likelihood of confusion.

When planning discovery and building a record, parties should be mindful that general industry evidence has less probative force than evidence tied to the actual product and consumer decision point at issue. Apex shows that once a tribunal determines services are substantially similar, the breadth of the record of third-party uses and channels becomes highly material to both the sixth and first DuPont factors.

## Closing practical guidance

- Be consistent about market definition. If you win a finding that services overlap broadly, expect your opponent to insist that third-party evidence be drawn from the same broad universe.
- Develop evidence about how consumers encounter and choose the specific service at issue—pricing, process, channels, and marketing—rather than relying on general statements about banking customers.
- Use surveys and user-experience analytics to demonstrate (or refute) sophistication and conditions of purchase; ensure survey design is tailored and defensible.
- For applicants, consider narrowing identifications during prosecution if you can show the real-world market is more specific than the broad class asserted in your application. For opposers, leverage broad identifications to capture third-party evidence showing crowdedness.
- Remember that online presentation and cross-channel shopping are increasingly central in financial services: document how the marks appear in search, app-stores, and co-brand offers, and evaluate how those presentations affect the salience of mark differences.

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. illustrates the cost of inconsistent analysis: a finding that services are highly similar requires a commensurate, broad approach to factors that measure crowdedness, commercial weakness, and the marks’ overall commercial impression. Thoughtful, targeted evidence about channels, purchaser care, and the actual conditions of purchase will often determine which way that analysis tips.
