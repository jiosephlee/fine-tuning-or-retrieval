Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### Must the scope of “similar goods/services” be consistent across different DuPont factors?
Question:
The Board found under DuPont factor 2 that Apex’s “banking and financing services” are highly similar (even partially legally identical) to CC Serve’s “credit card services.” But for factor 6, it limited the relevant universe to only credit card services and excluded other banking/financing uses of ASPIRE. The Federal Circuit says that was error. Is there a doctrinal requirement that once the Board defines the parties’ goods/services as similar for factor 2, it must use the same scope when assessing third-party use/registrations under factor 6? Or is this more about avoiding an inconsistent application of the similarity standard rather than a strict rule?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes—at least in the circumstances of this case. The Federal Circuit held that where the Board has already made a factual finding that the parties’ services are “highly similar” (indeed, “partially legally identical”) under DuPont factor 2, the Board must not then narrow the universe of “similar” goods it examines under factor 6. That is because factor 6 requires consideration of third‑party marks used on similar (not identical) goods, and the Board cannot apply a more stringent scope for factor 6 than it applied for factor 2 without explanation.

Why (doctrinally) — the court’s reasoning
- DuPont factor 2 asks whether the parties’ goods/services are related; the Board found Apex’s “banking and financing services” and CC Serve’s “credit card services” to be highly similar and partially legally identical. The court affirmed that finding. (Opinion §III.A, J.A. 27–28, 33–34.)
- DuPont factor 6 asks whether there are many similar marks in use on similar goods; that factor is important to assess commercial strength and scope of protection. The Federal Circuit reiterated that factor 6 does not require identical goods—only similar ones (citing Olde Tyme Foods and Juice Generation). (Opinion §III.B.)
- Because the Board had already concluded the services are highly similar under factor 2, the court held the Board “should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors” and that there was “no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.” The Board’s decision to restrict factor 6 to only credit‑card uses while excluding other banking/finance uses was therefore error. (Opinion §III.B, J.A. 6–7.)

Practical rule to take from the opinion
- The opinion does not announce a hyper‑technical, rigid rule that the identical wording must be used across every DuPont factor in every case. Rather, it enforces logical consistency: once the Board makes a factual finding about the scope of similarity between the parties’ goods/services, it must apply that same factual scope when evaluating evidence that depends on that similarity (e.g., third‑party registrations/uses under factor 6), unless it provides a reasoned, evidence‑supported basis for a different scope.
- In short: inconsistent narrowing of the universe of “similar goods/services” between factors, without explanation, is reversible error; a different scope can be used only if the Board explains and supports why the context of the other factor justifies a different (narrower or broader) definition.

Citation (from the opinion)
- “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar—in fact, partially legally identical—in its analysis of the second DuPont factor . . . the Board should retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors. We see no reason to impose a different and more stringent legal standard for similarity under the sixth DuPont factor.” (Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., §III.B.)

### How does the TTAB interpret legacy identifications when the ID Manual entry has been deleted?
Question:
The Board had to construe CC Serve’s 1998 registration for “credit card services” because that exact ID Manual entry had been deleted. It parsed the term broadly to include issuing cards, processing payments, authorization, alerts, account management, etc. What guides the TTAB’s interpretation of older identifications when the current ID Manual no longer contains that wording? Does deletion imply a narrower scope today, or does the Board default to ordinary meaning/dictionaries for the registration’s original identification?
Answer:
Short answer: Deletion of an ID Manual entry does not automatically narrow what a live registration covers. When the Manual no longer supplies the wording, the TTAB construes the registration’s actual identification by reference to the wording of the registration and ordinary-meaning evidence (dictionaries, the parties’ pleaded/actual services, and marketplace/third‑party evidence in the record).

Why: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. illustrates the point. The Board confronted a 1998 registration that identified “credit card services” after the ID Manual entry for that phrase had been deleted. Rather than treat deletion as dispositive, the Board “first determined the meaning of the identified services,” and construed “credit card services” broadly to include “issuing credit cards…counseling regarding credit card debt, processing credit card payments and transactions, credit card authorization, credit card monitoring and alerts, managing credit card accounts, and providing access to credit scores.” J.A. 25. The Board then relied on dictionary definitions to compare “credit card services” to “banking” and “finance,” concluding the services were “legally identical, in part.” J.A. 27–28. It also looked to third‑party registrations in the record to show the services may emanate from a single source. J.A. 33–34.

Practical takeaways for your drafting/analysis:
- The Board looks to the registration’s actual wording and interprets it using ordinary‑meaning tools (dictionaries), the evidence of how services are marketed or used, and third‑party registrations in the record. See J.A. 25, 27–28, 33–34.
- Deletion of an ID Manual entry doesn’t shrink the registration by operation of PTO policy; it simply removes a ready reference so the Board must construe the term from the record and common sources.
- Once the Board fixes a scope for the identified services, it should apply that same scope consistently across the DuPont analysis (the Federal Circuit faulted the Board for using inconsistent scopes when considering third‑party marks). See III.B (discussion and remand) and J.A. 27–28, 47.

So, in short: deletion -> TTAB constructs the original identification (ordinary meaning + record evidence); deletion does not automatically imply a narrower scope today.

### What counts as evidence under DuPont factor 6: actual marketplace use vs. third-party registrations?
Question:
Apex submitted multiple third-party uses and registrations of ASPIRE-formative marks in financial services. The Board seemed to focus on the number of uses specifically in credit card services (nine) and treated other financial-services uses as irrelevant. On remand, how should the TTAB weigh third-party registrations compared to evidence of actual marketplace use when assessing the weakness of the ASPIRE mark? Does Federal Circuit precedent (Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin) require actual use, or can registrations alone show conceptual weakness in a crowded field?
Answer:
Short answer
On remand the Board must consider third‑party marks across the broader class of “banking and financing” services (not just marks tied to credit cards) because it already found the parties’ services highly similar. DuPont factor six looks to “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods,” and the Federal Circuit’s precedents (Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin, Palm Bay) treat evidence of third‑party use as the most persuasive proof that a mark is relatively weak. Registrations can be considered, and the Board has used registrations elsewhere in its analysis, but registrations alone are generally less probative of commercial weakness than evidence that consumers actually encountered and learned to distinguish the marks.

Relevant law and how the opinion applies
- The Sixth DuPont factor asks for “[t]he number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., slip op. at §III.B (quoting DuPont). The opinion repeats the familiar principle that “evidence of third‑party use of similar marks on similar goods is relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection” (citing Palm Bay), and explains that when a field is crowded “customers will be more adept at distinguishing marks” (citing Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin).

- Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin in the opinion are cited for the proposition that actual marketplace exposure and use (often shown together with registrations) demonstrate that consumers have been taught to distinguish marks. The opinion notes those cases relied on “voluminous evidence” of registration and use (Jack Wolfskin) or many third‑party marks (Juice Generation’s 26 marks) to support a finding of weakness.

What the Board must do on remand
1. Keep the same scope of similarity used for the second DuPont factor. Because the Board found the parties’ services “highly similar” (indeed partially legally identical), it must evaluate third‑party marks across the broader banking/finance field—credit‑card‑only evidence is too narrow. Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., §III.B.

2. Consider both registrations and evidence of marketplace use, but explain the weight given. The opinion treats third‑party use as the paradigmatic evidence of commercial weakness; registrations were already used by the Board in the second‑factor relatedness inquiry, but the Court vacated the sixth‑factor finding because the Board limited which third‑party marks it would consider, not because registrations are categorically irrelevant. Thus:
   - Actual use (webpages, advertising, sales, screenshots, specimens) is most probative of the DuPont‑6 inquiry because it shows consumer exposure and the likelihood that consumers have learned to distinguish similar marks.
   - Third‑party federal registrations are relevant and admissible evidence that similar marks exist in the marketplace, but absent evidence of use they are weaker proof of conceptual or commercial weakness. The Board should state the weight it assigns to registrations versus use.

3. Assess quantity and quality in light of precedent. The Board should measure whether the number and nature of similar marks is “considerable” or “ubiquitous” (the kinds of descriptions the Court relied on in Jack Wolfskin and Juice Generation). Nine credit‑card marks were held by the Board to be insufficient under its narrower approach; on remand the Board must reassess that count using the expanded universe and by evaluating whether the evidence (especially use) rises to the level of the examples in those precedents.

Practical guide for the remand
- Aggregate all third‑party evidence across banking/finance (not just credit cards).
- Distinguish registrations that are merely live on the register from third‑party marks actually used in commerce (include dates, geographic reach, channels, samples).
- Explain how many marks amount to a crowded field in this context and whether consumers are likely trained to distinguish ASPIRE‑formative marks.
- If registrations dominate the record and use is thin, articulate why registrations alone do or do not establish conceptual or commercial weakness.

Bottom line
The Federal Circuit did not command that only registrations be ignored; it reaffirmed that evidence of third‑party use is the classic, most persuasive way to show a mark’s weakness and that the Board must consider an appropriately broad set of similar marks given its finding of service similarity. Registrations are admissible and can support the analysis, but the Board should weigh actual marketplace use more heavily and must apply the broader scope required by the Court on remand.

### Why did the Federal Circuit affirm factor 2 despite Apex not issuing credit cards?
Question:
Apex is a retail bank that doesn’t offer credit cards, while CC Serve provides ASPIRE-branded credit card services with partner banks. Yet the Board (and the Federal Circuit) treated the services as highly similar and even partially legally identical. What’s the doctrinal basis for finding similarity when the applicant doesn’t currently offer the exact service (credit cards)? Is it because Apex’s ID covers broad “banking and financing services,” which can encompass extending credit via cards, so the comparison is based on the identification rather than current marketplace use?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the DuPont second factor compares the services as they are identified in the parties’ registrations/applications (and asks whether those services are related or marketed in a way that would cause source confusion), the Board reasonably concluded — on the basis of dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations — that Apex’s identified “banking and financing services” encompass activities equivalent to “credit card services.” The Federal Circuit found that conclusion supported by substantial evidence and therefore affirmed the Board’s finding that the services are highly similar (partly “legally identical”), even though Apex did not yet issue credit cards in the marketplace.

Why that is the correct doctrinal approach (as applied in the opinion)

- The governing inquiry under the second DuPont factor is whether the parties’ goods/services are related or marketed such that consumers might mistakenly think they come from the same source. The opinion quotes the standard: services need not be identical; it is enough that “the respective products are related in some manner and/or the circumstances surrounding their marketing are such that they could give rise to the mistaken belief that they emanate from the same source.” (Opinion citing Coach Servs., J.A. 24–25.)

- The Board relied on the identifications at issue and ordinary–meaning sources to define those services. It determined that “credit card services” includes issuing credit cards, processing transactions, account management, etc., and that dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” include extending credit or providing funds via credit cards. On that basis it concluded the services were “legally identical, in part.” (J.A. 25, 27–28.)

- The Board also relied on third‑party registrations showing credit‑card and banking/finance services often appear under a single mark, supporting relatedness and the possibility that the same source would offer both. (J.A. 33–34.)

- The Federal Circuit reviewed for substantial evidence and held that the Board’s factual findings about the scope and overlap of the identified services were supported and therefore affirmed the second DuPont factor. (Affirmance of factor two, J.A. 35–36.)

Putting it together: the analysis compares what Apex applied to register (“banking and financing services”), not only what Apex currently does in the marketplace. Because the Board found — using dictionary definitions and registration evidence — that those identified services encompass credit‑card functions, the Board (and the Federal Circuit) could properly treat Apex’s services as highly similar to CC Serve’s credit card services for DuPont factor two despite Apex’s lack of a present credit‑card product. The opinion’s reasoning and affirmance rest on that identification/relatedness approach and the supporting evidence cited above.

### How does third-party use affect the first DuPont factor’s ‘commercial impression’ analysis?
Question:
The court vacated the Board’s factor 1 analysis because reconsideration of factor 6 (marketplace crowding) could change the mark’s commercial strength and therefore the overall commercial impression. Mechanically, how does evidence of a crowded field (many ASPIRE-formative marks) feed into factor 1? Does it reduce the source-indicating power of the shared term and shift the analysis toward the differences (e.g., the word BANK, design elements), or is that effect formally captured under factor 6 and only indirectly influences factor 1?
Answer:
Short answer
Evidence of third‑party use (a “crowded field”) is assessed under DuPont factor 6, and the Board’s finding on that factor (whether the mark is relatively weak or strong) must be carried into the DuPont factor 1 inquiry because “commercial impression” includes commercial strength. In other words, third‑party use is formally considered under factor 6, but the result of that inquiry changes how much weight the shared term has in the factor 1 comparison — a crowded field reduces the source‑indicating power of the shared term and therefore tends to shift the factor‑1 analysis toward the marks’ differences.

Relevant law from the opinion
- The court explains factor 6’s function: third‑party use is “relevant to show that a mark is relatively weak and entitled to only a narrow scope of protection.” (Opinion §III.B (quoting Palm Bay, Juice Generation, Jack Wolfskin).)
- The court then ties that directly to factor 1: “Because commercial impression informs the analysis under the first DuPont factor, we must similarly vacate the Board’s analysis here because reconsideration of the sixth DuPont factor may result in a different determination of the mark’s commercial strength or weakness and affect the overall commercial impression.” (Opinion §III.C.)

Mechanics — how to proceed on remand (and in practice)
1. Take factor 6 first (or at least resolve factor 6 issues before finalizing factor 1). The Board must define the relevant universe of third‑party uses (consistent with its finding on goods/services similarity) and determine whether those third‑party uses demonstrate that the shared element is commercially or conceptually weak and thus entitled to a narrow scope of protection. (Opinion §III.B.)
2. Translate factor 6’s result into factor 1. If factor 6 shows a crowded field:
   - The shared term’s source‑indicating power is diminished (less distinctiveness/strength).
   - That diminution should be reflected in the factor‑1 “commercial impression” analysis: the shared element will carry less weight toward likely confusion, and differences (e.g., BANK, design features, other words) will accordingly be more significant in judging the overall impression.
   Conversely, if factor 6 does not show crowding, the shared term retains stronger source‑indicating power and factor 1 will give greater weight to the common element.
3. Explain the interplay on the record. The Board should make explicit findings showing how it relied on factor 6 evidence to adjust the commercial‑strength component of factor 1, rather than treating factor 6 and factor 1 in isolation. The Federal Circuit criticized the Board for narrowing the scope of third‑party marks when it had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar; consistent scope is required. (Opinion §III.B.)

Takeaway
Third‑party use evidence “lives” in factor 6, but its effect is not merely theoretical: it changes the commercial‑impression inquiry under factor 1 by altering how much source‑indicating power the shared term has. The Apex Bank opinion requires the Board to reassess factor 6 (with an appropriately broad universe of similar services) and then re‑weigh factor 1 in light of that reassessment.

### Who are the ‘relevant consumers’ under factor 6 when services are highly similar but not identical?
Question:
The Board limited the relevant public to consumers of ‘credit card services’ and excluded broader banking/financing consumers. The Federal Circuit criticized that narrowing after the Board had already found the services highly similar under factor 2. When services are ‘legally identical in part’ and otherwise highly similar, how should the TTAB define the relevant consuming public for factor 6? Should it encompass the overlapping market of banking/financing consumers, or be limited to the exact identification of the opposer’s services?
Answer:
Short answer
When the Board has already found the parties’ services to be “highly similar” (indeed “legally identical, in part”) under DuPont factor 2, the relevant consuming public for DuPont factor 6 must be defined consistently with that finding. In other words, the Board should not confine factor‑6 consideration to only consumers of the opposer’s narrowly‑worded services (here, “credit card services”); it must include the overlapping banking/financing market that the Board itself found to be similar and therefore relevant. See Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., slip op. at §§III.A–B.

Why (the legal standard)
- DuPont factor 6 asks about “the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” It does not require identical goods or services—only similarity. The Federal Circuit reiterated that similarity for factor 6 may be satisfied even where services are not verbatim identical: “the sixth DuPont factor does not require identical goods—only similar ones.” (citing Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation) (Apex ¶ III.B).
- Where the Board makes a factual finding under factor 2 that services are highly similar or partially legally identical, the Court held the Board must “retain the same scope in its consideration of similarity under the other factors.” The Board erred by narrowing the universe of third‑party marks to only those tied to “credit card services” after finding the services highly similar (Apex ¶ III.B).

How to apply that here (practical guidance)
- Define the relevant consuming public for factor 6 to match the universe of purchasers/users implicated by the factor‑2 finding. Because the Board found credit‑card services to include issuing, processing, account management, etc., and found that “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” encompass the issuance of credit cards (J.A. 25–28), the relevant public must include consumers of banking/financing services who would encounter or reasonably associate those services with credit‑card offerings.
- On remand the Board should therefore consider third‑party uses of “Aspire” (and Aspire‑formative marks) across both credit‑card and broader banking/finance services when assessing whether the mark is crowded/weak and how many similar marks are in use.
- That does not mean the Board must always adopt the broadest possible market description as a matter of law. If the record shows a narrower relevant public (e.g., separate distribution channels, distinct consumers, specialized purchasers), the Board can account for that—but it must justify any narrowing consistently with its factor‑2 findings rather than contradict them.

Bottom line
Factor 6’s “relevant consumers” are those in the marketplace for the services the Board already found similar under factor 2. Where services are legally identical in part or otherwise highly similar, the Board should include the overlapping market (here, banking/financing consumers) in its factor‑6 inquiry, and consider third‑party marks used across that overlapping market. See Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., §III.B.

### Does partnering with a bank to issue ASPIRE-branded cards qualify as offering ‘credit card services’ for Section 2(d)?
Question:
CC Serve ‘joins with a bank’ and the bank issues ASPIRE-branded credit cards; CC Serve services the accounts. Apex argued CC Serve isn’t providing banking services itself. For Section 2(d) comparisons, does it matter that CC Serve isn’t a bank? Is providing and servicing an ASPIRE-branded credit card program with a bank partner still considered ‘credit card services’ such that it can conflict with an applicant’s broader ‘banking and financing services’?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes. Under the Federal Circuit’s opinion in Apex Bank v. CC Serve, a company that “joins with a bank” to have the bank issue ASPIRE‑branded cards while the company services the accounts falls within “credit card services” for purposes of a Section 2(d) (likelihood‑of‑confusion) analysis, and those services can overlap or be legally identical (in part) with broader “banking and financing services.”

Why — the legal test and how the opinion applied it
- Trademark likelihood‑of‑confusion turns on the DuPont factors, including similarity of the parties’ goods/services (the second DuPont factor). The services need not be identical; they need only be “related in some manner” or marketed in a way that could cause consumers to think they come from the same source. (citing Coach Servs., quoted in the opinion at III.A.)
- The Board defined “credit card services” to include issuing credit cards, processing and authorizing transactions, managing credit card accounts, monitoring/alerts, counseling regarding credit card debt, and access to credit scores. (J.A. 25.)
- The court accepted the Board’s factual findings that CC Serve “joins with a bank, and the bank issues ASPIRE‑branded credit cards and associated accounts to customers. The accounts are serviced by CC Serve and its affiliates.” (J.A. 6930.) The court held Apex’s argument that CC Serve is “not providing banking services” was “unavailing” and that “substantial evidence supports the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar.” (III.A; J.A. 27–28, 35.)
- Because “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” can encompass extending credit through issuance of credit cards, the Board (and the court) treated the parties’ services as at least partially legally identical. (J.A. 27–28.)

Practical takeaway
- For Section 2(d) comparisons, the critical question is what services are actually being offered and how they relate, not whether the service provider itself is a chartered bank. A non‑bank that partners with a bank to issue branded credit cards and then services those accounts can be treated as offering “credit card services” that overlap with an applicant’s “banking and financing services,” and thus may create a likelihood of confusion depending on the rest of the DuPont analysis. (Apex Bank v. CC Serve, III.A.)

A short caveat: this holding is fact‑specific to the Board’s service definitions and the evidentiary showing here; a different record or different identifications of services could lead to a different outcome.

### Standard of review check: Which issues were factual vs. legal in this likelihood-of-confusion appeal?
Question:
The Federal Circuit reviews TTAB legal conclusions de novo and factual findings for substantial evidence. Here, it affirmed the Board’s factor 2 finding (substantial evidence), but vacated factor 6 for legal error in the scope of ‘similar goods’ and also vacated factor 1 as dependent on factor 6. Can someone parse precisely which parts of the factor 6 analysis are treated as legal vs. factual (e.g., defining the comparison scope vs. counting/assessing third-party uses), and why the Federal Circuit could vacate for legal error rather than reweighing the evidence?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Federal Circuit treated the Board’s decision about the proper legal scope of “similar goods” for DuPont factor 6 as a legal error (de novo review). That is, defining which third‑party marks are even eligible to be considered was a legal/standards question.
- By contrast, the Board’s factual determinations about the marketplace — e.g., how many Aspire‑formative third‑party uses exist within whatever scope is chosen, and whether those uses are “considerable” or “ubiquitous” — are factual findings (substantial‑evidence review).
- The court vacated rather than reweighing because the Board applied the wrong legal standard (narrow scope), so the Board must reapply the correct standard to the evidence on remand and then make the factual assessments anew.

Why (with citations to the opinion)
1. Governing review rule. The opinion recalls that legal conclusions are reviewed de novo and factual findings for substantial evidence. (Op. § II (citing M2 Software and Consol. Edison).) The ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion is a legal question based on subsidiary DuPont findings. 

2. What the Board did wrong (legal issue). The Board had already found in factor 2 that the parties’ services are “highly similar” (indeed “partly legally identical”). Op. § III.A (J.A. 27–28). Despite that factual finding, the Board “restricted the universe of marks it considered to only those relating to credit card services and excluded marks related to other banking and financing services.” Op. § III.B. The Federal Circuit held that restricting the scope in that way was a legal error: “That was an error. … the Board’s definition of similarity [was] too narrow.” Op. § III.B. The court therefore vacated the factor‑6 finding and remanded for consideration under the appropriate scope. 

3. What is factual. The Board’s counting and characterization of third‑party uses — e.g., “there were nine Aspire‑formative marks for credit card services” and that those nine “did not rise to the level of ‘considerable’ or ‘ubiquitous’ use” — are classic fact findings about the record text and marketplace evidence. Op. § III.B (J.A. 47–51). Those assessments will be tested on appeal for substantial evidence, not redecided de novo.

4. Why the court vacated rather than reweighing evidence. The court could not simply accept the Board’s factual characterizations because the Board had applied an incorrect legal standard (it artificially narrowed the class of “similar goods” to credit‑card‑only uses). Because the error was in the Board’s legal framing of which third‑party marks qualify for consideration, the correct remedy is to vacate and remand so the Board can apply the proper legal scope (consistent with its factor‑2 finding of high similarity) and then re‑evaluate the same evidence. See Op. § III.B: “When the Board has already made a factual finding that the services are highly similar … the Board should retain the same scope … We thus vacate … and remand for reconsideration of the appropriate scope of third‑party marks eligible for consideration in view of the Board’s factual finding….” Once the Board applies the correct scope, it may (as a factfinder) count and weigh the third‑party uses differently; the Federal Circuit will review those factual determinations for substantial evidence on any future appeal.

Practical map for remand
- Legal question for the Board to fix: determine the correct universe of “similar goods/services” to examine under factor 6 (must be consistent with the factor‑2 factual finding that services are highly similar). (De novo on appeal.)
- Factual tasks for the Board on remand: within that universe, identify and count third‑party Aspire usages/registrations, describe their “nature,” and assess whether the field is crowded (e.g., whether uses are “considerable” or “ubiquitous”). Those findings will stand unless not supported by substantial evidence.

Takeaway
The Federal Circuit split the analysis: scope‑setting for what counts as “similar goods” under DuPont 6 is a legal framing question (court corrected it); the counting and qualitative assessment of third‑party marks within that scope are factual questions (for the Board to decide and for the court to review under substantial‑evidence standards).

### Is there a numeric threshold for third-party uses to show weakness under Juice Generation/Jack Wolfskin?
Question:
The Board thought nine third-party ASPIRE-formative uses in credit card services were insufficient compared to the ‘considerable’ or ‘ubiquitous’ showings in Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin (26 uses; numerous registrations and uses). Does Federal Circuit precedent set any rough threshold for how many third-party uses suffice to show commercial weakness? Or is the key inquiry the nature and proximity of those uses to the relevant services and consumers rather than a raw count?
Answer:
Short answer: No. The Federal Circuit has not adopted a bright‑line numeric threshold. Apex Bank makes clear that the relevant inquiry under the sixth DuPont factor is qualitative — the “number and nature” of third‑party marks used on similar goods — and that courts must match the universe of third‑party uses to the scope of services found similar, not simply count marks.

Why (doctrine and what Apex says)
- The sixth DuPont factor asks about the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods.” As the opinion explains, third‑party use is relevant because it can show a mark is relatively weak and entitled only to a narrower scope of protection (citations in Apex to DuPont and Palm Bay/ Juice Generation). Apex repeats and applies that principle. J.A. 6–7 (discussion of sixth DuPont factor).
- Apex criticizes the Board for focusing only on nine third‑party “Aspire” uses limited to credit‑card services and treating nine as not “considerable” or “ubiquitous.” The Federal Circuit vacated that analysis because the Board had already found the parties’ services to be highly similar (indeed, partially legally identical) and therefore improperly narrowed the universe of third‑party marks to a tighter category than its second‑factor finding supported. The court remanded so the Board could consider third‑party marks covering the broader, similar banking/financial services category. J.A. 33–35, 47–51; see also J.A. 27–28 (second DuPont factor finding).  

What the prior cases show (Juice Generation / Jack Wolfskin)
- Apex cites Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin as examples of courts calling particular showings “considerable” or “voluminous” — Juice Generation discussed 26 third‑party marks as “a considerable number,” and Jack Wolfskin pointed to many registrations/uses and “fourteen ‘notable examples’” among voluminous evidence. Apex repeats those characterizations but does not treat them as numerical rules. J.A. 47 (collecting Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin).  

Practical takeaways for litigation/analysis
- Don’t rely on a magic number. The Federal Circuit treats the inquiry as fact‑specific: quality and relevance of third‑party uses (are they on truly similar services? do they reach the same consumers/channels?), the scope of services the Board has found similar, how widely the third‑party uses are commercialized and recognized, and whether consumers have been “educated” to distinguish marks are the critical factors — not a raw count.
- Use Apex’s instruction: make sure the universe of third‑party uses you present matches the scope of services the Board (or court) has found similar under the second DuPont factor. If services are broad or overlap, document third‑party uses across that broader field; if services are narrowly defined, focus the evidence accordingly. J.A. 27–28, 33–35, 47–51.

Bottom line: Federal Circuit precedent gives helpful examples of what has been persuasive (e.g., 26 marks in Juice Generation; many registrations in Jack Wolfskin), but it does not establish a numeric threshold. Apex emphasizes aligning the comparison set with the services found similar and assessing the nature, reach, and relevance of third‑party uses.

### Why were ‘Costs to appellant’ awarded after an affirm-in-part/vacate-in-part outcome?
Question:
The judgment line reads ‘Costs to appellant,’ even though the court affirmed the Board on factor 2 but vacated and remanded on factors 6 and 1. Under Federal Circuit Rule 39 or FRAP 39, how are costs allocated in a mixed outcome like this? What types of costs are we talking about (e.g., filing fees, transcript costs), and does ‘to appellant’ mean CC Serve must pay Apex’s taxable costs despite the partial affirmance?
Answer:
Short answer
The single line “Costs to appellant” means the Federal Circuit treated Apex as the prevailing party for purposes of taxable costs and awarded those costs to Apex. The court exercised its rule-based discretion after a mixed result (affirming the Board on DuPont factor 2 but vacating and remanding factors 6 and 1), and therefore directed that the clerk award taxable costs to the appellant. See the opinion’s final judgment line: “COSTS — Costs to appellant.”

Why that result is legally sensible (and how FRAP 39/Fed. Cir. practice works)
- Rule framework: Federal Rule of Appellate Procedure 39 (and the Federal Circuit’s local rules implementing it) governs taxation of costs on appeal. FRAP 39 starts from the default that costs are “allowed to the prevailing party,” but it leaves courts discretion to determine who is a prevailing party in cases with mixed outcomes and to apportion costs if appropriate. The clerk taxes costs pursuant to the rules and statutes (and by local practice).
- “Prevailing party” in a mixed result: A party need not win every issue to be the prevailing party. When an appeal produces meaningful judicial relief (here, vacatur of two DuPont-factor determinations and remand for reconsideration), the court may treat the appellant as the prevailing party even though one Board finding was affirmed. The court’s judgment shows it reached that conclusion: although the Court “affirm[ed]-in-part, vacat[ed]-in-part, and remand[ed],” it separately directed “Costs to appellant.” That directive operates as the court’s determination of who gets taxable costs.

What “costs” covers (and what it does not)
- Taxable costs (typical items recoverable): under the rules and 28 U.S.C. § 1920 (and implemented by FRAP 39), taxable appellate costs commonly include items such as the clerk’s docketing/filing fees, reporter’s transcript costs, printing/copying/reproduction costs for briefs and appendices, and shipping or delivery charges related to filing the record. The clerk will tax only those items authorized by the rules/statute.
- What is not covered by a simple “Costs to appellant”: attorney’s fees and other litigation expenses (e.g., expert fees except as statutorily authorized) are not part of the routine “costs” award unless a statute or contract separately authorizes fee-shifting. The single-line award in this opinion is a costs award, not an award of attorney’s fees.

Practical effect in this case
- “Costs to appellant” means Apex is entitled to seek taxation of its allowable appellate costs; CC Serve (the appellee) will be the party against whom the clerk will tax those awarded costs unless the court or clerk later modifies the award. The normal post-judgment procedure is for the prevailing party to submit a bill of costs (and for the opposing party to object if appropriate) under FRAP 39 and the Federal Circuit’s local rules; the clerk then taxes the allowable items.
- Because the court did not apportion costs in its judgment, it did not split taxable costs between the parties; it directed that costs be awarded to Apex in whole (subject to clerical taxation and any objections).

Bottom line
The Federal Circuit concluded that Apex obtained sufficient relief on appeal (vacatur and remand on important DuPont factors) to be treated as the prevailing party for taxable costs, and therefore entered the concise directive “Costs to appellant.” That entitles Apex to seek taxation of the ordinary appellate costs allowed by the rules and statute; it does not automatically include attorney’s fees unless some separate legal basis exists.
