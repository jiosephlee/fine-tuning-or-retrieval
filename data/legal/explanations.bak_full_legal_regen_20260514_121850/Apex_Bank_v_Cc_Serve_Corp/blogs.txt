Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# Keeping DuPont Factors in Sync: The Federal Circuit Demands a Consistent Scope of “Similarity”

## Why this case matters

In Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., the Federal Circuit confronted a methodological mistake that routinely crops up in TTAB proceedings: treating one DuPont factor as if it answered a different question than another. The court’s core holding is straightforward but consequential for trademark practice before the Board. Once the Board found the parties’ services “highly similar” (even “partially legally identical”) under the second DuPont factor, it could not then narrow the universe of third‑party marks considered under the sixth factor to a subset of services. Doing so distorted the inquiry into commercial strength and the marks’ overall impressions. The court vacated the Board’s analyses of factors six and one and remanded for reconsideration in light of the Board’s finding on factor two. That methodological clarification will reshape how the TTAB defines relevant services, curates third‑party evidence, and articulates the relationships among DuPont factors.

## The facts and the panel’s holding

The appeal arises from an opposition to Apex Bank’s intent‑to‑use applications to register ASPIRE BANK (word and design) for “banking and financing services.” CC Serve owns a long‑registered ASPIRE mark used in connection with credit‑card services (and partners with banks that issue ASPIRE‑branded cards). The Trademark Trial and Appeal Board sustained the opposition under Section 2(d), concluding a likelihood of confusion.

On appeal, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding under DuPont factor two—that Apex’s banking and financing services and CC Serve’s credit‑card services are highly similar and, in part, legally identical. But the court vacated the Board’s treatment of the sixth DuPont factor (third‑party use) and, because factor one (similarity of the marks in their entireties) depends on the mark’s commercial strength and impression, vacated factor one as well. The error: after finding the services highly similar, the Board limited its sixth‑factor inquiry to third‑party marks used only for “credit card services,” excluding marks used in the broader banking/finance field—even though the Board had already determined that the broader category was relevant. The Federal Circuit held that the Board must retain a consistent scope when it considers similarity across factors; it may not constrict the definition of “similar” from factor to factor.

## The reasoning in plain terms

Two basic principles underlie the court’s decision. First, the DuPont analysis is multi‑factorial but coherent: findings about the nature and scope of the goods or services inform other factors. If the Board determines that the relevant consumers and services cut across a certain market, that factual finding governs which third‑party uses are probative of commercial weakness. Second, the sixth DuPont factor requires consideration of third‑party use on similar—not strictly identical—goods and services. Precedent the court cited (Olde Tyme Foods; Juice Generation) supports a flexible, not hypertechnical, approach to similarity under factor six.

Here the Board’s own factor‑two finding that banking/finance and credit‑card services are highly similar made it arbitrary to treat only credit‑card third‑party uses as relevant to commercial strength. Excluding Aspire‑formative marks used across the banking and finance spectrum distorted the sixth factor and, in turn, the first factor, which depends on the mark’s marketplace strength and the marks’ overall commercial impression. The Federal Circuit’s remedy was surgical: affirm the factor‑two finding, vacate factors one and six, and remand for a reweighing that uses a single, coherent scope of similarity.

## Why the decision is doctrinally important

The Federal Circuit did not announce a new test for DuPont, nor did it expand what counts as similar goods. Instead, it emphasized methodological integrity in the Board’s analysis: fact‑findings must be applied consistently across the DuPont factors. That sounds obvious, but it has practical doctrinal bite.

First, this opinion clarifies that the Board cannot adopt a broader definition of similarity to support a finding of likely confusion under factor two and then cabin the inquiry under factor six to a narrower slice to avoid third‑party evidence of weakness. Courts have long looked to third‑party registrations and uses to assess a mark’s commercial strength; a Board that defines “similar” differently depending on which DuPont factor it prefers risks reversible error.

Second, by linking factor one to factor six through the commercial strength analysis, the court reinforces that the DuPont evaluation is integrated. A mark’s conceptual and commercial weakness or strength (partly evidenced by third‑party uses) affects whether two marks’ overall impressions are confusingly similar. The decision therefore demands coherent reasoning that explains how factual findings about services inform the universe of relevant third‑party uses and ultimately the assessment of likelihood of confusion.

## Practical implications for TTAB practice and litigators

Practitioners should take three immediate lessons from Apex Bank:

1) Build the record across the entire universe of services the Board has found relevant. If you press for or concede a broad scope of services under factor two, expect that same scope to be used when the Board evaluates third‑party uses under factor six. Don’t assume the Board will slice the category in your favor on a subsequent factor.

2) Curate third‑party evidence deliberately. Applicants and opposers alike should collect and document third‑party uses and registrations not only in narrowly framed categories but across all services the Board may deem similar. The Federal Circuit’s ruling invites broader searches and argues against selective sampling that excludes otherwise similar uses.

3) Anticipate and address “relevant public” issues early. The Board’s definition of the relevant consumer group can shape the universe of similar goods and the probative weight of third‑party uses. Make the link explicit in briefs and testimony: show why the consumers identified for factor two are the right ones to consider under factor six, and explain how that affects commercial strength and the marks’ overall impressions.

Finally, for Board adjudicators, Apex Bank is a reminder to articulate clearly the scope of services and the reasoning that ties that scope to other DuPont factors. The Federal Circuit did not prescribe a single level of breadth for the “similarity” inquiry; rather, it demanded that whatever scope the Board finds be applied consistently.

## Limits and open questions

The Federal Circuit left room for discretion. It did not hold that all third‑party uses across an industry will always be relevant, nor did it adopt a rigid formula for when goods are “similar enough” under factor six. The opinion is fact‑dependent: a finding that services are highly similar requires the Board to treat third‑party evidence across that same span as potentially probative, but the Board retains the discretion to weigh the quantity, quality, and marketplace relevance of that evidence in the usual substantial‑evidence way.

The decision will likely spur more extensive third‑party searches and expanded evidentiary submissions on commercial strength—raising costs and evidentiary burdens. It also may change litigation posture: parties who prefer a narrow universe may push for narrower factor‑two findings early in proceedings; parties who want a broad inquiry will press for broad findings and then leverage third‑party uses across that broader space.

## Conclusion

Apex Bank is an important procedural correction: DuPont factors are distinct analytical lenses, but they look at the same marketplace landscape. Once the Board fixes the view of that landscape—by finding the services to be highly similar—it cannot then peer through a narrower window to exclude otherwise relevant third‑party uses. The Federal Circuit’s remand forces the Board to harmonize its inquiry, which should produce more coherent, and ultimately more reviewable, liability determinations under Section 2(d). For practitioners, the ruling underscores the strategic importance of defining and documenting the scope of the relevant services at the outset of TTAB disputes.



# How Much Third-Party Use Is Enough? Recalibrating “Crowded Field” Evidence After Apex Bank v. CC Serve

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful corrective on how the TTAB should assess DuPont factor six—the “number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods”—when it has already made a finding about the similarity of the parties’ services under DuPont factor two. The Federal Circuit did not decide whether nine third‑party Aspire-formative uses are enough to show a crowded field; it did something more basic but important: it made clear that the Board cannot constrict the universe of third‑party marks in analyzing factor six after having defined the relevant services broadly for factor two. That principle will matter in many disputes—especially in crowded industries such as financial services—because it aligns the scope of inquiry across DuPont factors and requires the Board to treat “similar” consistently.

The decision thus prompts two immediate takeaways for practitioners: first, ensure that the Board’s service/goods scope under factor two is what you want, because it will control what third‑party uses are relevant under factor six; and second, build factor‑six records that combine quantity and quality—because courts look to both when assessing commercial weakness.

## How the court reasoned

The underlying facts are straightforward. CC Serve owns an ASPIRE registration for credit card services (priority 1996). Apex sought to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services. The Board found the parties’ services “highly similar” and, in part, legally identical, so it concluded factor two weighed heavily for likelihood of confusion. But when the Board reached factor six, it limited the universe of third‑party evidence to marks used on “credit card services” only, excluded 42 Aspire‑formative uses that fell outside that narrow slice of the marketplace, and concluded that only nine credit‑card uses existed—insufficient, in the Board’s view, to show commercial weakness. The Federal Circuit held that this was legal error: once the Board found the services highly similar (indeed, partially legally identical) for factor two, it could not narrow the scope of similar marks for factor six to a tighter category. The sixth factor looks to “similar” goods/services, not only strictly identical ones. Because the Board applied an inconsistent and unduly narrow definition of similarity when assessing third‑party uses, the court vacated the factor‑six finding (and remanded factor one, the marks‑as‑a‑whole inquiry, because commercial strength feeds into overall commercial impression) and affirmed the factor‑two finding.

In short: define the goods/services broadly for factor two, and keep that same scope when you evaluate third‑party marks under factor six.

## Putting the holding in doctrinal context

Apex reinforces several preexisting threads in DuPont jurisprudence. First, factor six is properly used to measure commercial weakness: evidence of a crowded field (many similar marks in use) can defeat or narrow the registrant’s protection. Palm Bay and Jack Wolfskin reiterate that third‑party use and registrations are probative here; Juice Generation illustrates that courts sometimes treat a relatively large set of examples (26 in that case) as “considerable.” But none of those precedents say the Board can cherry‑pick a narrower class of services for factor six after doing the opposite for factor two. The Federal Circuit’s decision therefore harmonizes the practical function of factor six—measuring industry saturation and consumer sophistication—with the Board’s earlier factual finding on relatedness.

Second, the opinion reminds practitioners that the similarity threshold for factor six is not identity. Olde Tyme and Juice Generation were cited for that point: third‑party marks on similar (not identical) goods can show weakness. That means, in many service‑heavy markets like banking, payments, and fintech, third‑party marks used across adjacent financial services (credit cards, deposit accounts, loan products, payment processing) legitimately belong in the factor‑six inquiry.

Third, the remand of factor one is a sensible doctrinal consequence. Whether a mark is commercially strong affects its overall commercial impression. If the Board, on remand, treats the additional 42 Aspire‑formative uses as relevant, it may reach a different conclusion on CC Serve’s mark strength, and that in turn could alter the first‑factor comparison.

## Practical implications and downstream effects

1. Coherent scope across DuPont factors. The Board must be careful to maintain a consistent scope of goods/services across DuPont factors, or explain why a narrower or broader scope is appropriate for a particular factor. If the Board finds high relatedness under factor two, litigants should expect factor six to pick up third‑party uses across that same relatedness band.

2. Litigation posture will shift. Applicants who want a narrow universe for factor six should resist broad findings under factor two. Conversely, registrants facing crowded‑field evidence want factor two findings as narrow as possible. Expect fights over initial claim construction and the Trademark Identification Manual language to intensify—those definitions can determine what third‑party evidence is admissible and meaningful.

3. Heavier emphasis on the quality, not just quantity, of third‑party uses. The opinion does not prescribe a numeric threshold; prior panels have treated numbers like 14 or 26 as dispositive in context, but the Federal Circuit’s focus here is on consistency and relevance rather than a magic number. That means litigants need to supply both breadth (show many users across similar services) and depth (show prominent use, consumer recognition, co‑branding, or long‑standing market presence).

4. Financial services are a prime battleground. Bank partnerships, white‑label card programs, and service outsourcing create a lattice of uses across “banking,” “card issuing,” and “processing.” Showing third‑party “Aspire” uses for deposit accounts, digital banking, payment processing, or co‑branded card programs is sensible when the Board has already found the parties’ services highly similar.

## What kinds of third‑party evidence matter (and how to present it)

Quantity matters, but context matters more. Here are categories of evidence that courts and the TTAB find persuasive when assembled thoughtfully:

- Live use examples: screenshots, marketing materials, press releases, product pages, and archived pages (Wayback) showing contemporaneous use of the third‑party marks on services within the defined relatedness band. For financial services, include partnership announcements (bank + processor), card program pages, and platform descriptions.

- Registrations coupled with use: registrations alone are weaker than evidence of ongoing commercial use, but registrations can supplement live‑use evidence—especially where registration date and specimen show contemporaneous use.

- Market prominence and longevity: evidence that a third party is a major brand or has promoted the mark widely (ad spend, media mentions, user numbers) can make fewer examples go further.

- Channels of trade and consumer: show that the same consumer classes encounter the third‑party marks (retail customers, small businesses, online banking users). Consumer overlap matters more than the label on the ID Manual.

- Consumer perception: surveys, declarations, and third‑party consumer comments can demonstrate whether consumers distinguish marks in the marketplace.

- Functional/weak or descriptive nature of the formative: evidence that “aspire” is used generically or descriptively in the industry reduces commercial strength.

- Geographic and temporal spread: multiple users across regions and over time suggests industry-wide use rather than localized coincidence.

- Design elements and compound marks: if many third‑party marks add dissimilar house marks or design elements to "Aspire," that can both show crowding and inform the likelihood that consumers will distinguish marks despite the shared word.

Be organized. Present a clear chart (for the Board) that ties each third‑party use to the relevant service category you established under factor two, and explain why each example is probative of weakness for the contested mark.

## Tactical tips for opposers and applicants

- If you represent an opposer (registrant), avoid conceding broader relatedness on factor two unless you are prepared to accept broader third‑party evidence under factor six. If the services are plausibly narrower, push for a narrow factual finding; but if similarity is unavoidable, marshal evidence to show the registrant’s commercial strength (years of use, advertising, consumer recognition).

- If you represent an applicant (the challenger seeking registration), collect exhaustive third‑party use data across the full range of services the Board might find similar—don’t limit your collection to the narrowly labeled ID Manual entry. Preserve contemporaneous web evidence and registration/specimen pairs.

- Anticipate the Board’s coherence requirement: if you win on factor two, be ready to show how that victory helps your factor‑six case (i.e., why the same universe contains many third‑party uses). If you lose on factor two, point out the inconsistency if the Board then narrows factor six.

- Use expert declarations to link third‑party evidence to consumer behavior and industry practice, especially in complicated service networks (bank processors, co‑branding).

- Think conceptually and commercially. Courts care about both conceptual weakness (the mark’s inherent distinctiveness) and commercial weakness (how the marketplace is actually saturated). Evidence should address both.

## Bottom line

Apex Bank v. CC Serve is not a numbers decision; it is a structural one. The Federal Circuit’s remand does not say “nine uses are too few” or that “42 is enough.” It says the Board must be consistent: once it defines the universe of related goods or services in evaluating relatedness, it must use that same universe when gauging whether the field is crowded. For practitioners, that means building DuPont factor six records that reflect the full, properly defined competitive landscape—both in breadth (multiple users across similar services) and in quality (prominence, longevity, consumer exposure). In crowded industries like financial services, the judges and the Board will be less impressed by neat categorical labels and more attentive to the real‑world market map you present.



# Commercial Impression Is Not Static: Why Vacating Factor Six Forced a Do-Over on Factor One

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025) is a useful reminder that likelihood-of-confusion analysis under the DuPont framework is not a checklist of independent, isolated factors. The Federal Circuit’s decision to affirm the Board on similarity of services (DuPont factor two) but to vacate the Board’s conclusions on marketplace crowding (factor six) — and, consequentially, on overall commercial impression (factor one) — illustrates the practical and doctrinal interdependence of those factors. When the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board narrows its universe of third‑party uses too tightly, it risks understating or overstating a mark’s commercial strength, and that misstatement can materially alter how two marks are compared as wholes. The court’s remand forces the Board to reexamine not just how many other “Aspire” marks exist in the marketplace, but also how that crowded field affects what consumers actually perceive when they encounter ASPIRE and ASPIRE BANK.

## What the court did

The underlying dispute is straightforward: CC Serve owns a 1998 registration for the word mark ASPIRE for credit card services. Apex filed intent‑to‑use applications for ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services.” The Board found the services highly similar (indeed, partially legally identical) and sustained CC Serve’s opposition under Section 2(d). On appeal, the Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding on factor two but found legal error in its factor‑six analysis. The Board had restricted its review of third‑party “Aspire” uses to marks actually used in connection with credit card services, excluding “Aspire” marks used in other banking and financing contexts. Because the Board had already determined the parties’ services were highly similar, the court held that the Board’s scope should not have been narrower for factor six than it was for factor two. Vacating factor six, the court also vacated the Board’s analysis of factor one — the appearance, sound, connotation and commercial impression of the marks — and remanded for reconsideration in light of any revised finding on mark strength.

## The doctrinal link between factor six and factor one

DuPont factor six (the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods or services) is essentially a proxy for commercial strength or weakness. A mark used in a crowded field of similar marks is less distinctive in practice; courts treat such marks as entitled to a narrower scope of protection. That, in turn, affects factor one because the “entirety” comparison — appearance, sound, connotation, commercial impression — cannot be abstracted away from how consumers actually process marks in the marketplace.

If the field is crowded with other “Aspire”‑formative marks across banking and finance, consumers may be primed to look past the shared formative element and rely on other components of the mark (such as “BANK” or a design element) to distinguish sources. Conversely, if “Aspire” remains relatively uncommon in closely related financial services, its prominence as a source identifier will carry greater weight in the DuPont one‑factor analysis. That is why the Federal Circuit was right to say that a retooling of the strength finding (factor six) can — and often should — trigger a do‑over on commercial impression (factor one).

Put simply: commercial impression is not static. It depends on marketplace context, and marketplace context is what factor six measures.

## How the Board should approach the remand

The Federal Circuit’s direction is not merely semantic: it requires the Board to harmonize the scope of “similar goods/services” across factors. Practically, that means several actions on remand.

First, the Board should expand its third‑party mark inquiry to cover banking and financing services in addition to credit card services, consistent with its earlier finding that the parties’ services are highly similar. This will likely broaden the universe of relevant “Aspire”‑formative uses.

Second, the Board should weigh not only the existence of third‑party registrations but the nature and prominence of their use: how long they have been used, the channels of trade, and the degree to which those third parties market to the same consumers. The court’s citations to Palm Bay, Jack Wolfskin, and Juice Generation emphasize that both registration/use quantity and consumer familiarity matter when assessing commercial strength.

Third, in revisiting factor one the Board should analyze how any changed conclusion on commercial strength affects the relative dominance of the shared element “ASPIRE” versus the distinguishing element “BANK.” If “ASPIRE” proves to be a weak, commonplace formative across the relevant financial services, the Board may give greater weight to “BANK” (or to design elements) when assessing appearance, sound, and commercial impression. Conversely, if “ASPIRE” retains distinctiveness, the shared term may substantially support a finding of likelihood of confusion.

Fourth, the Board should justify how its conclusions about consumer perception follow from the evidence. Surveys, specimens showing branding, advertising channels, and third‑party use evidence are all probative — but they must be analyzed together. The Board should avoid treating factor six as a freestanding tally and instead explain how crowding (or lack thereof) alters what the average consumer is likely to notice and use as a source cue.

## Downstream implications

A few practical consequences flow from this opinion.

For practitioners, Apex underscores the importance of comprehensive third‑party evidence when prosecuting or opposing marks, especially in industries like financial services where product lines (credit cards, banking, lending) can overlap in consumers’ eyes. Narrowly tailored third‑party searches that exclude close cousins of the identified goods risk being discounted; conversely, registrants should push the Board to explain why third‑party uses outside a narrow subcategory are irrelevant when services are found similar.

For the Board and examiners, the decision is a reminder to keep consistency across DuPont analyses. If you find legal or factual identity (or high similarity) of goods/services under factor two, you cannot then treat the universe of relevant third‑party marks more narrowly for factor six without explaining why. Doing so invites reversal and remand.

For doctrine, the opinion reinforces a theme the Federal Circuit has developed in recent years: DuPont factors are interrelated, and assessments of distinctiveness and likelihood of confusion should reflect marketplace realities, not artificial compartmentalization. The treatment of formative elements like “ASPIRE” will often turn on empirical questions about consumer attention and the degree of marketplace crowding.

## Bottom line

Apex Bank v. CC Serve is a tidy lesson in methodological consistency: the Board’s finding that the services are highly similar expanded the relevant marketplace, so the Board could not lawfully shrink that marketplace when counting third‑party “Aspire” marks. Because commercial impression is context‑dependent, vacating the strength assessment required a fresh look at the marks' overall appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. On remand the Board must show how the scope and nature of third‑party uses change what an ordinary consumer would take away from ASPIRE versus ASPIRE BANK — and explain how that revised consumer perception affects the ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion determination.



# Defining “Credit Card Services” After the ID Manual Deletion: Implications for Fintechs and Bank Partners

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025) is an important decision for trademark practitioners in financial services and fintech. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s broad construction of “credit card services” — a construction made necessary because that entry was deleted from the ID Manual — and accepted a textual/dictionary-driven approach tying together “credit card services” with broader “banking and financing” services. For fintechs that provide back‑end services to banks, and for banks that license or brand customer‑facing products, the decision signals that seemingly different operational roles (a bank issuing cards vs. a third‑party managing card programs) may be treated as highly similar services for likelihood‑of‑confusion purposes. That in turn affects registration strategy, enforcement risk, and how parties present evidence about channels of trade and consumer perception.

## What the court did

The underlying dispute was straightforward: CC Serve owns an ASPIRE registration (for credit card services) dating to 1996; Apex sought to register ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services.” The Board construed what “credit card services” means after that term was removed from the ID Manual, reading it to encompass activities such as issuing cards, processing payments and authorizations, managing accounts, providing alerts and credit‑score access, and counseling on card debt. The Board then compared those functions with Apex’s broadly phrased “banking and financing services” and — relying in part on dictionary definitions that link banking/finance to issuance of credit and similar activities — found the services “highly similar” and “legally identical, in part.” The Federal Circuit affirmed that conclusion under the second DuPont factor, holding that the Board’s factual finding of high similarity was supported by substantial evidence.

The court did, however, vacate and remand two related parts of the Board’s analysis (DuPont factors six and one). The Board had limited its sixth‑factor inquiry (third‑party marks in use) to marks actually used for credit card services, excluding third‑party uses of “Aspire” in other bank/finance contexts. Because the Board had already found the services highly similar, the Federal Circuit said the Board must apply a consistent scope when evaluating third‑party use and the marks’ commercial strength; it remanded so the Board can reassess the number and nature of similar marks across the full, now‑established field of similar services and then revisit the overall commercial impression (the first factor) in light of any change in perceived strength.

## My reading of the court’s interpretive move

Two features of the decision stand out. First, the Board’s and the court’s willingness to construe an ID‑Manual‑deleted term by reference to ordinary‑usage definitions and to operational functions reflects practical trademark adjudication. The deletion of standardized IDs forces adjudicators to look to the real‑world activities a term encompasses; in this case, that process produced a relatively expansive meaning of “credit card services.” Second, the Federal Circuit’s insistence on doctrinal consistency across DuPont factors is a salutary reminder that findings of relatedness for one factor cannot be shrugged off when assessing third‑party mark evidence and commercial strength under another factor.

Put plainly: if the Board says two services are highly similar, it must then consider third‑party uses across that same field when deciding whether a mark is weak or narrowly protectable. That consistency requirement produced the partial win for Apex — the court did not upend the Board’s relatedness finding, but it forced a reexamination of how crowded the field is and what that crowding means for ASPIRE’s scope.

## Practical implications for fintechs and bank partners

For fintechs that operate behind the scenes and for banks that partner with them, the decision raises several concrete considerations.

First, broad identifications like “banking and financing services” are risky when there are prior registrations covering credit‑card programs or card‑issuer services, even if the applicant does not itself issue cards. The Board and the court treated operational activities typical of third‑party card servicers (processing, account management, authorization) as within the “credit card services” sphere and therefore potentially overlapping with general banking services.

Second, evidence about the commercial realities of a relationship — e.g., that a fintech provides program management exclusively to issuing banks that brand the cards — may not be dispositive unless it is presented in the right form. The Board’s analysis focused on the identifications of services and dictionary meaning; parties will need to supplement legal argument with evidence showing that consumers identify the bank (not the backend provider) as the source of the card service, or that the relevant consumers transact in different channels and make decisions based on different marks.

Third, third‑party registration/use evidence matters more than ever. If a field is crowded with similar formative marks, a defendant can argue its mark is weak and entitled to a narrower scope of protection — a fact that can neutralize similarity on the marks themselves. The Federal Circuit’s remand emphasizes that Board examiners and litigants must evaluate third‑party uses across all services that the Board has found sufficiently similar, not cherry‑pick a narrow subset.

## Drafting and prosecution strategy

Practitioners can take several concrete steps to reduce risk:

Avoid generic, catch‑all identifications when you can. If an entity does not issue credit cards, explicitly say so in the identification of services; instead of “banking and financing services,” consider more specific wording (for example, “online deposit accounts and related account management services” or “card program administration services provided to issuing banks”). Narrower, more precise identifications constrain the Board’s scope analysis.

When you do use broader terms, be proactive in the record. During prosecution, submit declarations and samples showing who the customer is, how the services are marketed, and who appears on the consumer‑facing brand. Provide specimens or evidence that the applicant is not an issuer if that fact differentiates the services.

Build third‑party evidence now. If your mark is likely to collide with prior registrations, compile and present third‑party registrations and marketplace examples showing whether the term is used pervasively across banking and finance or is relatively rare and strong. Crowding across the full field (not just a subset) helps establish weakness and a narrower scope of protection.

Consider contractual fixes. Where possible, negotiate coexistence or consent agreements with prior registrants, or use licensing arrangements that clarify source attribution for consumers.

Finally, litigate cohesively across DuPont factors. If you win on relatedness, you must be prepared for the Board to apply that scope elsewhere. Do not compartmentalize your arguments in a way that invites later remand or reversal for inconsistent scopes.

## Looking ahead

Apex Bank confirms that post‑ID Manual deletions will push the Board and courts to do more interpretive work, and that such interpretive work can have significant consequences for fintechs and banks. The Federal Circuit’s demand for internal consistency across DuPont factors should improve doctrinal coherence, but it also raises the evidentiary bar for applicants who want to distinguish their services from legacy registrations. In a crowded, fast‑evolving financial marketplace, trademark counsel will need to be more granular and evidence‑driven than ever when defining services and defending registrations.



# From Publication to Opposition: What Apex’s Path Teaches About Letters of Protest, ITUs, and the Limits of Examiner Approval

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is an instructive trip through the trademark registration pipeline: an intent‑to‑use (ITU) applicant clears examination and is published despite a letter of protest, only to have the mark opposed at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB) and then partially succeed on appeal. The Federal Circuit’s decision—affirming the Board’s finding that the parties’ services are highly similar, but vacating and remanding the Board’s analyses of the first and sixth DuPont factors—highlights several recurring themes for practitioners: examiner approval is provisional and narrow, letters of protest rarely carry dispositive weight, oppositions let parties build a fuller DuPont record than the PTO’s office action process permits, and appellate review will treat legal conclusions and factual findings differently. For ITU filers in crowded fields like financial services, Apex is a useful reminder that publication is not the finish line.

## The procedural arc in short

Apex filed ITU applications in 2019 for ASPIRE BANK word and design marks for “banking and financing services.” CC Serve owned a prior federal registration for ASPIRE covering “credit card services” (priority date 1996) and submitted a letter of protest during examination. The examining attorney nonetheless approved Apex’s applications for publication. After the marks published, CC Serve opposed registration at the TTAB under Section 2(d), and the Board sustained the opposition, finding a likelihood of confusion. Apex appealed.

The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s factual finding under the second DuPont factor that the parties’ services are highly similar—even “partially legally identical.” But the court vacated the Board’s treatment of the sixth DuPont factor (number and nature of similar marks in use) and, because commercial strength feeds the overall commercial impression, remanded the first DuPont factor for reconsideration in light of a properly scoped sixth‑factor analysis. The upshot: Apex won a partial victory on appeal, but the Board’s ultimate likelihood‑of‑confusion determination remains to be revisited with a broader third‑party‑mark inquiry.

## Examining letters of protest and the thin reed of examiner approval

One immediate lesson is procedural: letters of protest are a tool, not a trump card. A letter of protest can alert the examining attorney to potential problems and supply evidence, but the examining attorney acts (or declines to act) based on the statutory standards and the evidence in the prosecution record. Here, despite CC Serve’s protest, the examiner approved Apex’s ASPIRE BANK applications for publication. Publication under the Lanham Act is not an adjudication on likelihood of confusion; it merely opens a 30‑day window for oppositions (extendable). Examiner approval is inherently constrained by the prosecution record and the “paper” nature of examination; it does not replicate the adversarial, fact‑building character of an opposition proceeding.

That reality is why ITU applicants should treat publication as provisional. An examiner’s decision is a prima facie green light for notice and publication, but it will not insulate an applicant from a thorough DuPont analysis if a prior registrant or user brings an opposition. The TTAB evaluates likelihood of confusion based on the DuPont factors using a fuller evidentiary record—testimony, third‑party use evidence, marketplace documents, and sometimes expert affidavits. The Federal Circuit’s opinion underscores that gap: the Board could and did reach a different outcome than the examining attorney because it had a broader evidentiary canvas.

## The DuPont terrain and the Federal Circuit’s review

The opinion is also a tidy illustration of how the DuPont framework operates and how appellate review divides labor. Likelihood of confusion is a legal conclusion derived from underlying factual findings on the (up to) thirteen DuPont factors. The Federal Circuit reviews the Board’s legal conclusions de novo and its factual findings for substantial evidence. That split explains the disposition in Apex: substantial evidence supported the Board’s factual finding that “credit card services” and Apex’s identified “banking and financing services” are highly similar, so the court affirmed the second DuPont factor. But the Board’s treatment of the sixth factor was legally defective because it contracted the universe of third‑party uses to only those labeled “credit card services,” even though it had already determined the parties’ services were highly similar—or partially legally identical. The Federal Circuit correctly flagged that inconsistency: once the Board adopts a scope for similarity in one factor, it may not legally narrow the field in another factor in a way that conflicts with its prior finding.

That logic led to vacatur of the sixth factor and a remand because third‑party evidence can materially affect the commercial strength of the prior mark, which in turn bears on the first‑factor analysis of appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression. In short, the court enforced methodological consistency across DuPont factors and vindicated the idea that third‑party use evidence should be considered for similar—not only identical—goods and services.

## Doctrinal implications and what to watch for going forward

Apex reinforces and refines a few doctrinal points already familiar to practitioners. First, the sixth DuPont factor requires consideration of marks used on similar, not identical, goods and services. This court made explicit what prior precedent suggested: once similarity of services is established, the Board cannot constrict the universe of third‑party marks to an artificially narrow label and ignore marks used across the broader, previously found‑to‑be‑similar field. That has practical consequences for how both opposers and applicants present third‑party evidence. If the Board finds broad relatedness between fields (for example, credit cards and banking generally), parties should be prepared to handle third‑party registrations and marketplace uses across that broader space.

Second, the case highlights the interdependence of DuPont factors. Commercial strength or weakness (informed by the sixth factor) affects the weight given to visual and aural similarity (the first factor). The Board must articulate how those factors inform each other; an isolated, inconsistent treatment invites remand.

Third, Apex is a reminder that ITU status does not prevent a likelihood‑of‑confusion challenge. The TTAB compares the registered mark and the identification of services in the application, without requiring actual use by the ITU applicant. Thus, an applicant cannot rely on nonuse as a shield; carefully tailored identifications of services and trade channels can matter a great deal.

## Strategic takeaways for ITU applicants in crowded fields

For applicants launching ITU filings in crowded industries—financial services is a paradigmatic example—Apex suggests several practical steps.

First, do not assume publication equals safety. Use the prosecution window to negotiate with prior rights holders: explore coexistence agreements, consent letters, or narrowing the identification of services to reduce overlap with earlier registrations. An examining attorney is limited in the remedies they can impose; negotiated resolution before opposition can avoid the resource‑intensive DuPont fight.

Second, be surgical in drafting identifications. Broader identifications invite broader comparisons. If you can credibly and lawfully narrow channels of trade, consumer classes, or the specific services covered, you reduce the spectrum of third‑party evidence an opposer can marshal.

Third, build a defensive record early. For ITU filers, consider submitting evidence that shows how your actual intended uses (channels of trade, consumer sophistication, branding context) distinguish your mark in practice. While this evidence may not be considered in prosecution when not yet in the record, being prepared with declarations, mockups, and market‑entry plans can help if an opposition arrives.

Fourth, monitor and, where feasible, control the narrative about third‑party use. Because third‑party registrations and marketplace evidence can show that a term is weak (entitling a registrant to narrower protection), both sides should assemble persuasive third‑party portfolios. Applicants should be ready to rebut claims of pervasive third‑party use; opposers should document that the prior mark is strong and not part of a crowded field.

Finally, be realistic about costs. Examiner approval can lull applicants into thinking the legal fight is over; the TTAB process (and potential Federal Circuit appeal) can be costly and protracted. Early negotiation may be the most economical option.

## Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a practical, doctrinally useful decision. It underscores that examiner approval and publication are only intermediate steps, that oppositions enable a fuller DuPont inquiry, and that the Board must remain consistent in defining the relevant scope of goods and services when weighing DuPont factors. For ITU applicants—especially in financial services where service lines and branding overlap—this case counsels careful drafting, early outreach to prior rights holders, and an expectation that the real contest over registrability may well occur after publication, not before.



# “We Don’t Issue Credit Cards” Isn’t a Shield: Services Overlap in the Banking‑as‑a‑Service Era

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. (Fed. Cir. Sept. 25, 2025) is a reminder that in today’s fragmented fintech marketplace, saying “we don’t issue credit cards” will not necessarily defeat a trademark opposition based on relatedness of services. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board’s finding that Apex’s proposed ASPIRE BANK marks and CC Serve’s long‑registered ASPIRE mark presented a likelihood of confusion as to services, but it also vacated parts of the Board’s analysis (DuPont factors one and six) and remanded for reconsideration. The opinion makes clear three critical points for brand owners and practitioners: the content of a mark’s recited goods/services drives the relatedness inquiry; modern bank/fintech partnerships can make otherwise different commercial actors appear to consumers as offering the same or related services; and rigorous, consistent treatment of third‑party evidence and overall commercial impression is essential.

## What the court held and why

The Board had sustained CC Serve’s Section 2(d) opposition, finding a high degree of similarity between Apex’s recited “banking and financing services” (ASPIRE BANK) and CC Serve’s registered “credit card services” (ASPIRE). The Federal Circuit affirmed that core factual finding: the Board’s interpretation of “banking” and “finance” to include activities that overlap with credit card issuance and management was supported by substantial evidence, and third‑party registrations corroborated relatedness. In short—the parties’ services were “highly similar,” and that conclusion survives appellate review.

But the court also identified analytical slippage in the Board’s treatment of two related DuPont factors. For the sixth DuPont factor (the number and nature of similar marks in use on similar goods), the Board had limited its universe of comparison to marks used only on “credit card services,” excluding third‑party uses of “Aspire” across other banking and finance categories. The Federal Circuit rejected that artificially narrow scope because it had already found the services highly similar under factor two; when services are similar or overlap, evidence of third‑party uses across the broader field is relevant to assessing the mark’s commercial strength and appropriate breadth of protection. Because the sixth factor could change the assessment of the mark’s strength, the court also vacated the Board’s first DuPont analysis (similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression) and remanded both factors for reconsideration in light of a properly scoped third‑party evidence universe.

## The lesson about services and marketplace realities

At bottom, Apex’s factual point—that it is a chartered bank that does not itself issue credit cards, while CC Serve is a servicer that partners with banks for card programs—did not immunize Apex’s application. The Board and Federal Circuit looked to what each party’s identifications of services actually encompassed and to the practical marketplace: financial services are often provided through layered relationships—an issuing bank, a program manager, a card servicer, co‑branding arrangements—so consumers may plausibly assume a common source or relatedness when marks are similar. The opinion underscores that courts and the PTO do not take formal corporate labels (bank vs. servicer) at face value when the recited services and marketplace realities point toward overlap.

This is particularly resonant in the “banking‑as‑a‑service” and “cards‑as‑a‑service” world: fintechs, white‑label providers, program managers, and banks routinely share brand space and customer touchpoints. Even if a company performs only backend functions, courts recognize that consumers’ perception and the nature of services (e.g., account management, authorization, customer access to credit) can create a connection in the public mind between different providers operating under related marks.

## Practical implications for practitioners and applicants

Apex should be a prompt for practitioners to rethink both prosecution drafting and opposition defense strategies in the financial services space.

First, tailor identification language with care. Broad identifications such as “banking and financing services” are easy hooks for challengers; when an applicant truly does not provide a particular subset of services (e.g., issuing credit cards), consider narrowing the recitation (for example, “retail banking services excluding credit card issuance and related services”)—recognizing, of course, that examiners and the PTO may resist overly narrow exclusions depending on practice and policy. Precise identifications can make the relatedness analysis more defendable.

Second, build the factual record early. If you are an applicant that does not issue cards, compile contemporaneous evidence showing how your service is marketed, to whom, and through what channels; contractual arrangements that separate your offering from card issuance; and evidence that consumers do not associate your brand with credit card services. Conversely, registrants asserting relatedness should marshal evidence of market overlap: co‑branded cards, bank‑fintech partnerships, third‑party program management relationships, overlapping customer bases, and how customers interact with the relevant brands.

Third, use third‑party evidence strategically. The Federal Circuit’s rebuke of the Board’s overly narrow third‑party analysis signalizes that the universe of similar marks should track the Board’s own relatedness finding: if services are found similar or legally identical, third‑party uses across that broader field matter. Applicants should collect relevant registrations and use examples across the full range of financial services implicated by the recitations. Likewise, defendants should be prepared to show that third‑party evidence is either insubstantial or confined to genuinely different categories.

Fourth, think holistically about commercial impression. Because the court remanded factor one for reconsideration, the case reminds us that commercial strength, third‑party evidence, and the similarity of marks inform each other. Distinctive stylization, house marks, or extensive branding that alters public perception may mitigate confusion; these arguments must be substantiated.

Finally, be mindful of survey and consumer perception evidence. Where marketplace distinctions are subtle, well‑designed surveys demonstrating consumer associations (or lack thereof) can be persuasive. Surveys are costly and fact‑specific, but this opinion shows how pivotal the consumer perception question is in financial services disputes.

## Doctrinal ripple effects and what to watch next

Apex reinforces two doctrinal constants: first, the primacy of the recited goods/services in likelihood‑of‑confusion analysis; second, the importance of consistency across DuPont factors. Practically, this decision will make it harder for firms in adjacent segments of financial services to rely on corporate form alone to escape confusion findings. It also clarifies that the sixth DuPont factor must be treated with the same breadth as the second when the Board has found overlap—boards and courts cannot pick a narrower universe for third‑party comparisons without explanation.

Watch for (1) PTO examiners and the TTAB leaning more readily to relatedness findings in bank/fintech oppositions; (2) an uptick in parties trying to narrow identifications or add limiting language during prosecution; and (3) more detailed third‑party evidence submitted by both sides to establish either a crowded field or a distinct marketplace. Trademark counsel for banks, fintechs, and service providers should assume that co‑branding and layered service models will be salient to a confusion analysis and prepare their filing and evidence strategies accordingly.

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is not a radical doctrinal shift, but it is a practical wake‑up call: in the age of program managers, white‑label banks, and shared customer interfaces, “we’re just the servicer” is an insufficient shield unless the record and the recitations squarely support that separation in the eyes of consumers.
