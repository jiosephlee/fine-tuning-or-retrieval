Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

### What evidentiary weight do third-party registrations vs. third-party use carry in proving weakness under factor six, and is there any quantitative threshold?
Question:
The Board discounted broader third-party 'Aspire' uses and found only nine relevant credit-card uses, concluding that number did not show weakness. The Federal Circuit doesn’t set a number but flags the Board’s too-narrow scope. Practically, how should parties prove weakness—do registrations alone help, or is marketplace use necessary? Are cases like Juice Generation (26 marks) and Jack Wolfskin (14 notable examples) setting any benchmark, or is the inquiry more qualitative (e.g., proximity of services, geographic spread, consumer exposure)?
Answer:
Short answer
- The Federal Circuit in Apex Bank does not adopt any numeric cutoff. The court criticized the Board for using an unduly narrow scope of “credit card” marks and for treating only nine third‑party credit‑card uses as insufficient. It directed the Board to consider all third‑party marks for similar services consistent with its finding on the second DuPont factor.
- Quantitative examples in cases such as Juice Generation (26 third‑party marks) and Jack Wolfskin (14 “notable” examples) are persuasive illustrations of a “considerable” number, not formal benchmarks. The inquiry remains both quantitative and qualitative: number matters, but so do nature, geographic spread, consumer exposure, and closeness of the services.
- Practically, registrations help but are weaker proof than marketplace use. Parties should present a mix: registrations plus concrete evidence of third‑party commercial use (advertising, sales, websites, customers, geographic reach, duration) tied to the same relevant consumer universe the Board adopts under the second factor.

Explanation and authorities (grounded in the Apex opinion)
1) Apex’s holding about scope and numbers
- Apex vacated the Board’s sixth‑factor finding because the Board considered only marks tied narrowly to “credit card services” even though it had already found the parties’ services “highly similar” and partly legally identical under the second DuPont factor. The court held that once the Board finds high similarity, it must use that same scope when evaluating third‑party marks under factor six. (Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp., J.A. 27–28, 33–34, 47–48.)
- The court did not create a numeric rule. It remanded for the Board to reassess factor six with the appropriate scope rather than substituting any specific numeric threshold. (Apex opinion, vacating factor six and remanding.)

2) Role of quantity vs. quality
- The opinion expressly cites Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin as examples of courts describing “a considerable number” of third‑party marks (26 in Juice Generation, 14 notable examples in Jack Wolfskin), but treats those numbers as descriptive precedent, not as a rigid threshold. (Apex, J.A. 47 (citing Juice Generation, 794 F.3d at 1337 n.1; Jack Wolfskin, 797 F.3d at 1373).)
- The Federal Circuit’s precedent (also cited in Apex: Palm Bay, Olde Tyme) shows that factor six is about whether the field is “crowded” enough that consumers are accustomed to distinguishing similar marks. That is inherently a mixed quantitative/qualitative inquiry: number of marks is relevant, but so is the nature of the marks and how consumers encounter them. (Apex at J.A. 46–47; Palm Bay Imps., 396 F.3d at 1373; Olde Tyme Foods, 961 F.2d at 204.)

3) Registrations vs. marketplace use
- Registrations are admissible and relevant evidence of third‑party adoption of a mark (Apex itself relied on third‑party registrations in its second‑factor analysis, J.A. 33–34), but registrations alone are weaker proof of consumer exposure and commercial weakness. Courts often give greater weight to evidence of actual marketplace use (sales, advertising, consumer recognition) because factor six asks about “marks in use on similar goods.”
- Apex reflects this balance: the Board relied on a count of nine credit‑card uses and treated them as insufficiently “considerable,” yet the court faulted the Board for limiting scope rather than for relying on registrations or uses per se. The court’s remedy was to require the Board to consider a broader set of marks (those for similar banking/financial services) and reassess their weight. (Apex at J.A. 46–48.)

4) Practical proof strategy (what to submit and how to frame it)
- Match the scope to the second DuPont finding: if the Board (or you) establishes that services are highly similar or overlap, include third‑party marks for the full range of similar services (not just a narrow subcategory).
- Combine evidence types:
  - Registrations covering the relevant services (shows third parties chose similar marks).
  - Concrete marketplace use: screenshots, websites, advertising, catalogs, sales figures, press releases, dealer networks, dates of first use, and geographic distribution.
  - Evidence of consumer exposure/recognition: web analytics, media impressions, trade press, or (if available) survey evidence.
  - Evidence on closeness of services: how consumers purchase/use the services, channels of trade, and whether the same consumers encounter the third‑party marks.
- Emphasize qualitative factors in briefing: similarity of goods/services, consumer overlap, duration and prominence of third‑party use, multi‑jurisdictional reach, and whether third parties operate in the same channels where confusion would occur.
- Use Juice Generation/Jack Wolfskin as persuasive analogues: point to their numbers and show how your combination of quantity + quality compares, but do not treat those numbers as required thresholds.

Conclusion
Apex confirms there is no magic numeric cutoff; courts look at both how many similar marks exist and how meaningful those marks are in the marketplace. Registrations matter and should be submitted, but they are most effective when paired with evidence of actual use, consumer exposure, and service proximity consistent with the second DuPont factor. Juice Generation and Jack Wolfskin illustrate when courts have found the field sufficiently crowded, but the determination remains fact‑specific and qualitative as well as quantitative.
