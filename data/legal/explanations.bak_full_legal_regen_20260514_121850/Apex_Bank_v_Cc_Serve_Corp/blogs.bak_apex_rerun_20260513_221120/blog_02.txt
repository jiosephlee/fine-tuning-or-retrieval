Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# When Credit Cards Are “Banking”: Affirmance on the Second DuPont Factor and the Breadth of Financial Service IDs

## Why this case matters

Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a useful reminder that, at the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board and on appeal, the textual breadth of an identification of services can be dispositive. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s finding that “credit card services” and “banking and financing services” are highly similar—and in part legally identical—based on dictionary definitions and third‑party registrations. That holding matters for banks, fintechs, and any applicant using broad service descriptions: the way you describe your services on an application can collapse seemingly different businesses into the same commercial universe for likelihood‑of‑confusion purposes, even if one party currently does not offer the other’s services in fact.

## The court’s reasoning, in short

The Board concluded, and the Federal Circuit affirmed, that the parties’ services were highly similar. Apex applied to register ASPIRE BANK for “banking and financing services” (intent‑to‑use), while CC Serve owned an earlier registration for ASPIRE for “credit card services.” The Board relied on dictionary definitions of “banking,” “bank,” and “finance” that encompass extending credit and issuing credit cards, and also on third‑party registrations that list both credit card services and broader banking/finance services under the same marks. Because the identifications overlap, the Board found partial legal identity and weighed the second DuPont factor heavily for likelihood of confusion. The Federal Circuit found substantial evidence supported that conclusion and affirmed.

At the same time, the court vacated and remanded the Board’s treatment of the first (similarity of marks/ commercial impression) and sixth (number and nature of similar marks in use) DuPont factors. The court reasoned that the Board’s narrow construction of the relevant third‑party marks under the sixth factor (limiting consideration only to marks expressly tied to “credit card services”) was inconsistent with its broader finding under the second factor that the services are highly similar; the same scope of services should inform related DuPont inquiries. Because reconsideration of third‑party uses could affect the perceived commercial strength of CC Serve’s ASPIRE mark, the court also remanded the first factor.

## What the opinion teaches about identification breadth

There are three lessons for practitioners and registrants:

First, identification language can determine whether services are treated as similar or even legally identical. The Board and the Federal Circuit relied on the plain meanings of “banking” and “finance” to encompass credit card issuance and account servicing. That is classic TTAB/Trademark Office reasoning: registration rights are defined by the operative identification submitted to USPTO, not by the registrant’s current business model. In practice, this means an applicant that uses broad IDs—“banking services,” “financial services,” “consumer lending services”—may find those IDs sweep in adjacent offerings such as credit cards, payment processing, or account servicing when a conflict arises.

Second, actual non‑competition today does not control. Apex does not currently issue credit cards, but its application for “banking and financing services” was vulnerable because the ID encompasses credit card issuance and related services. The inquiry under DuPont focuses on the nature of the goods/services as identified, and on the possibility of overlap in channels, consumers, or source confusion, not solely on present marketplace overlap. That doctrinal point is old hat in trademark law, but Apex underscores its bite in the fintech context, where partnerships and program relationships make service boundaries porous.

Third, third‑party registrations and definitions matter. The Board relied on third‑party registrations showing that marks have been registered for credit card and banking services under the same or similar marks. Such evidence helps establish that consumers may deem the services emanating from a single source under one mark. Conversely, ample third‑party use of a formative term on similar services can narrow a mark’s scope of protection (the sixth DuPont factor), but the Federal Circuit here required the Board to use a consistent scope when assessing that evidence.

## Implications for fintech-bank partnerships and applicants

Fintechs that partner with banks to issue co‑branded or bank‑issued credit cards, issuing banks that outsource program management, and applicants planning to enter adjacent financial markets should take away a cautionary practical point: marks used for one slice of financial services are not insulated from claims by owners of marks used in related financial offerings simply because different entities operate different pieces of the stack.

A fintech that offers card program management but does not issue cards might nonetheless be vulnerable if its mark overlaps with an earlier mark used on card issuance, because consumer confusion can arise from the source‑perception of the financial product (the card and account) rather than the internal business arrangements. Likewise, a bank that later decides to offer cards under a brand might find prior users in the card‑services space blocking registration.

For applicants, narrow and particularized identifications can be defensive. Where possible, tailor identifications to the specific services actually offered (e.g., “program management services for bank‑issued credit card accounts” rather than the broad “banking and financing services”), or prepare to argue why specific services fall outside the ordinary meaning of a broad term. But be mindful: narrowing during prosecution can trigger other tradeoffs, such as limitations on potential future expansions or arguments about intent‑to‑use scope.

## Doctrinal and downstream effects

Doctrinally, the Federal Circuit’s decision reaffirms two well‑established principles: (1) the legal significance of the identification language in an application/registration, and (2) the importance of consistent scope when applying multiple DuPont factors. Practically, the opinion signals to the TTAB and practitioners that when the Board finds services similar or partially identical under DuPont factor two, it must carry that scope into analyses under factor six (third‑party usages) and factor one (commercial impression), lest its analysis be internally inconsistent.

The remand on factors one and six is meaningful. If a fuller accounting of third‑party uses shows that “aspire” is widespread across the broader constellation of banking and finance services, CC Serve’s claim to exclusive protection may weaken and the first‑factor comparison may tilt differently. Conversely, if third‑party uses are limited when measured across the appropriate broader scope, CC Serve stands to benefit from normal protection for an inherently distinctive mark. The remand keeps the ultimate ambiguity alive and highlights that broad IDs both help and hurt: they can pull in a challenger’s services, but they also expose the mark to dilution arguments across a wide field.

For examining attorneys and applicants alike, the case reinforces careful ID drafting and thoughtful use of third‑party evidence. Examiners and the Board should avoid artificially constraining the universe of comparable services or third‑party marks in a way that contradicts their own prior findings about service similarity.

## Bottom line and practical tips

Apex Bank affirms the old maxim that words matter in trademark practice. Broad identifications like “banking and financing services” commonly encompass credit card issuance and account servicing. Applicants should (1) draft IDs to reflect actual services and foreseeable brand plans, (2) be prepared to justify narrow IDs if seeking to avoid conflicts, and (3) gather third‑party evidence that is measured to the full scope of service similarity the Board uses. Banks and fintechs engaged in program partnerships should also recognize that the source perception of financial products (cards, accounts) can create trademark exposure even when backend roles are split.

The Federal Circuit’s remand on commercial strength and third‑party use means this is not the last word on ASPIRE’s scope of protection—only a firm reminder that the identification box you check on your application can be as consequential as the business plan you execute.
