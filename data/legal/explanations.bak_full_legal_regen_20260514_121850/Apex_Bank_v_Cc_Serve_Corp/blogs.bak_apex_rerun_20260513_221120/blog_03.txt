Title: Blog about the opinion: Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp.

# Strength Begets Impression: Why the First DuPont Factor Falls with the Sixth

## Why Apex Bank v. CC Serve matters

The Federal Circuit’s decision in Apex Bank v. CC Serve Corp. is a crisp illustration of an important, sometimes underappreciated point in Lanham Act doctrine: the evidentiary showing of third‑party use (DuPont factor six) does not live in isolation — it directly shapes the commercial impression (DuPont factor one) and therefore the overall likelihood‑of‑confusion inquiry. By affirming the Board’s finding that Apex’s and CC Serve’s services are highly similar but vacating the Board’s treatment of factor six and, consequentially, factor one, the court made plain that a re‑calibration of a mark’s commercial strength can change how similarity of the marks is assessed. That ripple effect matters for litigants and practitioners: building or rebutting a case of crowding in a mark space can narrow or expand the scope of protection and materially alter the outcome of an opposition or infringement claim.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The Board had sustained CC Serve’s opposition to Apex Bank’s application to register ASPIRE BANK for banking and financing services, finding a likelihood of confusion in light of CC Serve’s earlier ASPIRE registration for credit card services. The Federal Circuit affirmed the Board’s pivotal factual finding that the parties’ services are highly similar — even “partially legally identical” — which supports treating third‑party marks for both credit‑card and broader banking/finance services as relevant.

Where the Board went astray, the court held, was in its limited view of the universe of third‑party uses under the sixth DuPont factor. Although the Board determined the parties’ services to be highly similar when analyzing factor two, it restricted factor six to third‑party uses tied narrowly to “credit card services,” excluding marks used in connection with other banking and financial services. The Federal Circuit rejected that inconsistent narrowing. Because factor six is meant to account for similar marks used on similar goods or services — not strictly identical ones — the Board should have retained the scope it adopted in factor two when evaluating third‑party evidence. The court therefore vacated the Board’s factor‑six finding and remanded.

Critically, the court also vacated the Board’s analysis of the first DuPont factor — similarity of the marks in appearance, sound, connotation, and commercial impression — explaining that the strength (or weakness) shown by a proper factor‑six analysis can alter the “commercial impression” that the Board draws. In short: if a mark is shown to be weak or crowded, its owner is entitled to a narrower scope of protection and the comparison of marks must proceed with that narrowed lens. Because the Board’s factor‑six error could change its evaluation of ASPIRE’s commercial strength, the factor‑one assessment had to be revisited too.

## Why commercial strength and commercial impression are linked

The decision rests on a straightforward doctrinal premise with real analytic bite: commercial strength is not an isolated attribute that only affects damages or entitles a markholder to bolder relief; it is a contextual fact that informs how confusing two marks will appear to the ordinary consumer. When many competitors use ASPIRE‑formative marks across closely related financial services, consumers are presumably conditioned to distinguish among slightly different uses of “aspire.” That conditioning reduces the likelihood that consumers will attribute a new ASPIRE‑derived branding to the single source who owns a prior registration. Consequently, evidence of crowding under factor six diminishes the “commercial impression” that factor one seeks to capture.

The Federal Circuit’s remedial step — vacating factor one rather than treating it as untouched by a revised strength finding — is doctrinally sound. The Board’s commercial‑impression determination cannot be insulated from evidence that undermines a mark’s distinctiveness or suggests a crowded marketplace. Practically, that means the trademark comparison is iterative: findings under one DuPont factor can and should influence how other factors are weighed.

## Doctrinal and practical implications

This opinion underscores several rules of thumb for practitioners:

- Consistency of scope across factors matters. If the Board finds services to be “highly similar,” it should use that same breadth when identifying third‑party uses that are “similar” for factor six. Narrowing scope midstream invites reversal.

- Factor six is potentially dispositive on scope. A robust showing of third‑party use across the relevant range of services can shrink the protective bubble around a mark, changing the baseline against which the competing mark is judged under factor one and beyond.

- Commercial impression is pliable. It’s not a fixed snapshot of the mark in isolation; it’s a judgment shaped by marketplace realities. Evidence that a mark is common in a field may convert ostensibly similar marks into lesser risks of consumer confusion.

Downstream, remands like this will produce Board decisions where once‑routine comparisons become more nuanced: boards will need to assemble a record of third‑party use that reflects the full range of “similar” services they have already found similar under factor two. Petitioners opposing a mark — or applicants defending registration — should anticipate more careful, record-anchored analyses of market crowding and its effects on the marks’ commercial impressions.

## Practical guidance: building (or defeating) a third‑party‑use record

For parties looking to influence the remand, here are practical steps grounded in the opinion’s logic:

- When you assert similarity of services, be prepared to accept a broader universe for third‑party evidence. If you argue that banking and credit card services are highly similar, gather third‑party examples not only of credit‑card marks but of marks used for general banking, lending, payments, and related financial services. The Federal Circuit called for precisely that consistency.

- Don’t rely solely on registrations. The Board (and courts) care about actual marketplace use as well as registrations. Collect evidence of active use: screenshots of live websites, advertisements, marketing materials, customer-facing accounts, and screenshots showing co‑brand or co‑service offerings (e.g., banks issuing credit cards under partner marks).

- Quantify and contextualize. The Board distinguished “nine” Aspire uses in a field from the kinds of voluminous third‑party use that demonstrate weakness (the court cited precedents turning on larger numbers). Help the factfinder understand whether alleged uses are isolated, regional, or national; whether they represent actual consumer exposure; and whether they are contemporaneous.

- Show consumer education, if available. If there is evidence consumers routinely distinguish among ASPIRE‑formative marks (advertising that clarifies different providers, marketplace comparisons, or consumer surveys), that supports a finding of narrow protection. Conversely, absence of such education strengthens the owner’s position.

- Tailor arguments to consumer class. The Board erred by narrowing the public to credit‑card consumers while treating services more broadly elsewhere. Be explicit about who the “ordinary consumer” is and how their purchasing context affects the weight of third‑party evidence.

## How a recalibrated strength finding could shift the confusion analysis

A remand that finds the ASPIRE family is widely used across banking and payment services could have several ripple effects. First, it could narrow the scope of ASPIRE’s protection, making ASPIRE BANK less likely to be held confusingly similar because consumers are more accustomed to distinguishing among ASPIRE‑formative brands. Second, a weaker mark diminishes the force of other DuPont factors — not by eliminating them, but by changing the baseline for what constitutes a confusing overlap in commercial impression. Third, the relative weakness could free an applicant to register more nuanced variations (design or stylized marks) that previously looked perilously close.

Conversely, if the Board’s expanded review still shows only modest third‑party use concentrated in niches or at a small scale, CC Serve may retain a robust scope of protection and the original likelihood‑of‑confusion finding could survive. The Federal Circuit’s remand is thus not pro‑applicant in the abstract; it simply requires a legally consistent, record‑based treatment of how crowded the ASPIRE space actually is.

## Conclusion

Apex Bank v. CC Serve is a timely reminder that trademark law demands coherence across its multiple, interacting inquiries. Evidence that a mark is crowded in the marketplace does more than affect abstract questions of dilution or scope — it reshapes the commercial impression against which similarity is judged. For litigants, that means building the right record matters: a well‑documented showing of third‑party use can narrow protection and tilt the likelihood‑of‑confusion balance, while poor indexing of the competitive landscape risks letting a single board finding dominate an otherwise multifaceted inquiry. The Federal Circuit’s ruling brings that interaction into sharper relief and gives practitioners a clear map for how to marshal — or rebut — evidence of crowding on remand.
