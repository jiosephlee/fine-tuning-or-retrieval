Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

# Conspiracy in the Crowd: Group Liability for Online Pump‑and‑Dumps Under Section 1349

## Why this case matters

United States v. Constantinescu (5th Cir. Oct. 2, 2025) is the first major appellate decision to squarely confront how traditional fraud and conspiracy doctrines apply to coordinated securities schemes run through modern social‑media networks. The Fifth Circuit reversed a district court’s dismissal of an indictment charging a band of well‑known online traders with conspiracy and substantive securities fraud for allegedly “pumping” stocks via false posts and then “dumping” shares for profit. In doing so the court relied on the Supreme Court’s recent clarifications in Ciminelli and Kousisis about what counts as protected “property” and what the Government must prove under the fraudulent‑inducement theory. The practical stakes are high: the remand will force the district court to grapple with how to prove agreement and attribution among multiple influencers, how far coconspirator (Pinkerton) liability reaches, and how to manage the avalanche of cross‑platform digital evidence without prejudicing defendants or drowning jurors in noise.

## What the Fifth Circuit held (and did not)

The Fifth Circuit’s holding is narrowly framed but consequential. It reversed dismissal because the indictment, read in the light most favorable to the Government, adequately alleged a fraudulent‑inducement scheme: defendants allegedly used materially false and misleading social‑media communications to induce followers to buy securities, thereby parting with money or property that the defendants sought to obtain. That aligns the indictment with Kousisis’s distinction between a forbidden “right‑to‑control” theory and an adequately protected fraudulent‑inducement theory that targets money or property obtained through deceit. The panel also relied on Kousisis to reject the claim that the Government must show the victims were left economically worse off; obtaining money or property under materially false pretenses suffices.

The court did not resolve how to prove conspiracy in decentralized online markets, did not announce rules for attributing one influencer’s statement to another, and did not map the contours of Pinkerton liability in this particular context. Those are the issues the remand will now surface.

## Agreement in decentralized online spaces: the evidentiary challenge

Conspiracy under 18 U.S.C. § 1349 requires an agreement to commit an offense and, ordinarily, an overt act in furtherance. Proving a meeting of the minds among a loosely connected set of social‑media personalities will be tougher than proving fraud by a single actor. Unlike a classic corporate or organized‑crime setting, influencers often coordinate without formal meetings, using ephemeral DMs, encrypted chat groups, shared schedules, or merely tacitly mimicking one another. On remand the Government will need to show enough of a link—communications, a common plan, shared timing, or coordinated financial benefit—to permit a reasonable jury to infer agreement rather than parallel self‑interested conduct.

Practical evidence that courts will expect to see includes time‑stamped group chats or DMs (Telegram, Discord, WhatsApp), direct messages arranging coordinated posting times or narratives, contemporaneous spreadsheets or deal‑slips showing shared purchase/sale plans, and records of profit‑sharing or centralized token/crypto flows. Absent such documentary breadcrumbs, prosecutors can turn to circumstantial proof: strikingly synchronized posts, identical false narratives appearing across multiple accounts in short order, a pattern of correlated purchases followed by coordinated sell orders, and the unmistakable economic fingerprint of one party buying large positions and later realizing outsized gains while followers transact in the opposite direction.

Defense counsels’ strongest answer will often be that contemporaneous coordination is plausibly explicable by ordinary social dynamics—shared sources, copying popular takes, or independent trading strategies reacting to the same catalyst. The district court will need careful jury instructions distinguishing lawful coordination or parallel intent from conspiratorial agreement.

## Attribution and the problem of “whose speech counts”

A difficult evidentiary and doctrinal question is attribution: when does one influencer’s post become the act of another defendant for purposes of conspiracy and substantive liability? The Fifth Circuit’s opinion rests on the fraudulent‑inducement theory—that victims were induced to buy securities by materially false statements. But in a group case, victims may have seen and acted on posts from different defendants. The Government will therefore need to show that each charged defendant either made (or directed) materially false representations that induced purchases, or that defendants knowingly participated in a common scheme where other coconspirators’ posts were the means of inducement.

Attribution will turn on classic agency and aiding‑and‑abetting principles, plus an assessment of how followers consume content. Evidence that one defendant wrote copy that another reposted, shared drafts across accounts, or coordinated messaging strategies strengthens the Government’s case. So does proof that defendants benefitted from one another’s audience overlap—e.g., that one defendant’s post reliably moved the volume in accounts concentrated among another defendant’s followers. Courts may also scrutinize platform mechanics and analytics: who posted first, the velocity of reposting, whether accounts were linked by management services, and whether defendants used common broker accounts or trading bots.

The district court will also face authentication and hearsay questions when the Government relies on screenshot captures, saved posts, or third‑party records of deleted material. Robust metadata and platform records will be essential to authenticate posts and show timing and reach.

## Pinkerton liability and foreseeability: how broad will coconspirator exposure be?

If the Government proves a conspiracy under § 1349, Pinkerton principles mean coconspirators can be responsible for substantive securities‑fraud acts committed by their partners if those acts were in furtherance of the conspiracy and reasonably foreseeable. In social‑media pump‑and‑dumps, that could sweep broadly: a defendant who agreed to a plan to “pump” could be held liable for another conspirator’s posts or sales that induced purchases, even if the defendant did not personally author them or execute the particular trades.

Two limits will matter. First, foreseeability: courts will ask whether the specific type and timing of the acts were a natural and probable consequence of the agreement. Evidence that defendants expected coordinated multi‑account postings, reciprocal amplification, or shared sell‑off timelines will make foreseeability easy to show. Second, in furtherance: courts will require proof that the substantive acts advanced the common scheme, not mere collateral or independent trading.

Defendants will argue that the online ecosystem makes neither element a straightforward fit for Pinkerton. They will stress individualized causation—followers who saw and followed only one influencer, or trades motivated by market news rather than coordinated messaging. Prosecutors, conversely, will emphasize the economics of audiences, the design of amplification strategies, and empirical patterns tying particular posts to follower behavior.

## Trial management: severance, spillover, and jury complexity

Multi‑defendant trials present real risks of spillover prejudice in this context. Influencers differ in prominence, audience size, and conduct. A star defendant’s inflammatory posts or a coconspirator’s post‑arrest admissions could poison jurors’ views of less‑culpable codefendants. On remand, defense teams will likely press for severance, limiting instructions, and targeted motions to exclude highly prejudicial evidence that is not directly attributable to individual defendants.

From the court’s perspective, practical case management will matter. Staging evidence by defendant or by discrete conspiratorial acts, using limiting instructions, and considering separate trials where one defendant’s conduct far outstrips the others may be necessary to protect fairness. The prosecution will counter that severance may impair proof of the coordinated scheme; courts will have to balance judicial economy against the risk of unfair prejudice.

## The evidentiary toolbox: chat logs, cross‑platform posts, and follower trading data

Proving a conspiracy and causation in online pump‑and‑dumps will be an exercise in forensic synthesis. Chat logs (DMs, group chats) can provide the smoking‑gun evidence of agreement; but such logs must be authenticated, and their potentially voluminous and exculpatory content may complicate trial narratives. Cross‑platform posts—text, video, and audio—show the content and timing of alleged misrepresentations; platform metadata and archived copies will be essential to proving what was said, when, and to whom.

Perhaps most novel will be follower trading data. The Government will likely seek brokerage records (order books, timestamped trades, and account identifiers) and platform analytics (impressions, demographics, click‑through and follow‑through metrics) to show that posts caused spikes in buying among followers and that those spikes preceded defendants’ sell transactions. Correlating social‑media reach with microstructure movements will require expert testimony from data scientists and market‑structure specialists. That evidence raises its own questions: matching anonymous or pseudonymous brokerage accounts to real‑world followers will involve subpoenaing platform and broker records and wrestling with privacy and Fourth Amendment issues. Defense experts will challenge causal inferences, identifying confounders such as contemporaneous news, algorithmic amplification independent of defendants’ intent, or wash trades.

Courts will also confront common evidentiary objections: hearsay (when a witness recounts a post), business‑records exceptions, and authentication of screenshots and archived web content. Judges should expect extensive pretrial litigation over the admissibility and use of such materials.

## Doctrinal ripple effects and open questions

Constantinescu signals that traditional fraud and conspiracy frameworks can reach coordinated social‑media schemes, but it leaves open several doctrinal questions that could shape future litigation. First, how granular must proof of inducement be—must the Government tie specific purchases to specific misrepresentations, or is statistical proof of market‑wide inducement enough? The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on Kousisis suggests that a showing the defendants intended to obtain money via inducement will survive sufficiency challenges, but trial proof will need to bridge from large datasets to individualized causation at least insofar as the jury must find that victims were induced.

Second, the case invites further delineation of the boundary between lawful persuasive speech (including bullish advocacy) and actionable deception when multiple actors amplify messages. That boundary will implicate First Amendment concerns—though neither the Fifth Circuit opinion nor Kousisis upended long‑standing doctrine that knowingly false statements of fact intended to induce financial transactions can give rise to liability.

Third, Constantinescu raises questions about the scope of coconspirator liability when participants occupy different roles: content creators, account managers, promoters, and traders. Courts will need to parse role‑specific culpability and ensure that Pinkerton exposure is not limitless.

Finally, the decision will spur tactical changes in both prosecution and defense practice. Prosecutors will be more likely to indict coordinated influencer networks and will invest in cross‑platform forensic capabilities. Defendants and their counsel will emphasize decentralization, independent motives, and the lack of formal agreement, and will press for early discovery of platform analytics to test causation theories.

## Conclusion

Constantinescu is a pragmatic application of Kousisis and Ciminelli to a modern fraud modality: the social‑media pump‑and‑dump. By reversing dismissal, the Fifth Circuit permits the Government to try to prove that a group of influencers agreed to a fraudulent plan and used social media to obtain money under false pretenses. The remand will be a proving ground for how courts translate ancient conspiracy and Pinkerton doctrines into a world of DMs, cross‑posted clips, and algorithmic amplification. How district courts manage attribution, foreseeability, and the admission of complex digital evidence will determine whether prosecutions succeed without unfairly sweeping in independent actors or suppressing lawful speech. Practitioners on both sides should expect protracted pretrial skirmishing over coordination evidence, severance, and expert analysis of the social‑media‑to‑trade causal chain.
