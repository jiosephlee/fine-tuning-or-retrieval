Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### Does § 1348(1) require that obtaining money or property be an object of the scheme after Kousisis?
Question:
The opinion says securities fraud under § 1348 requires engaging in deception and that obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the fraud (citing Kousisis). But § 1348(1) textually prohibits schemes "to defraud any person in connection with" a security, while § 1348(2) expressly mentions obtaining money or property. Is the Fifth Circuit reading a "money or property" requirement into subsection (1) via mail/wire fraud principles, or is it applying subsection (2) only? How should prosecutors charge/argue under § 1348 post-Kousisis with respect to the property-object requirement?
Answer:
Short answer
The Fifth Circuit in Constantinescu applies Kousisis’s fraudulent‑inducement framework to § 1348(1): if the government is alleging a fraudulent‑inducement theory (it tricked victims into buying/selling securities), the indictment must allege that obtaining the victims’ money or property was an object of the scheme. The court treats that money/property object as an element of the charged scheme (consistent with Kousisis), and it rejects pleading a conviction based only on deprivation of valuable economic information (Ciminelli).

Why — the governing doctrine in the opinion
- Constantinescu treats § 1348 as borrowing the mail/wire fraud concepts and applies the two basic elements: a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” The court explains the “scheme” element requires a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money” (quoting its Greenlaw/Evans formulation).  
- The opinion relies on Kousisis for the proposition that, under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, liability attaches where the defendant “uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over her money or property.” The Fifth Circuit therefore says a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’” even if the victim is not left economically worse off. (Constantinescu quoting Kousisis, and distinguishing Ciminelli’s rejection of a right‑to‑control/info‑only theory.)
- Applied here, the court concluded the indictment alleged a fraudulent‑inducement scheme (inducing followers to purchase securities) — “that is, to part with their money or property” — so the requisite object (obtaining money/property) was alleged.

What the opinion means for § 1348(1) versus § 1348(2)
- The court does not treat subsection (1) and subsection (2) as wholly distinct lines immune from each other; rather it analyzes § 1348 through the lens of Supreme Court fraud doctrine. Where the government advances a fraudulent‑inducement theory under § 1348(1), Constantinescu requires pleading facts showing the defendant sought to obtain victims’ money or property (i.e., induced purchases).  
- The decision does not invent a novel extra‑textual element beyond what Kousisis and Ciminelli require; it applies those precedents to § 1348(1). Put differently: Kousisis supplies the substantive rule that a fraudulent‑inducement scheme must have obtaining money/property as an object, and the Fifth Circuit applies that rule to § 1348(1) when the alleged fraud is an inducement to trade.

Practical charging/argument advice post‑Kousisis (per Constantinescu)
- If you rely on a fraudulent‑inducement theory (classic pump‑and‑dump or misrepresentations that cause purchases), plead that the defendant “induced” victims to purchase (or sell) securities and that the object of the scheme was to obtain money/property (e.g., profit from secret sales). Explicitly allege: misrepresentations, victims’ purchases, defendant’s sales/profits, and the intent to induce purchases to realize gains. Constantinescu affirmed dismissal where the indictment only alleged deprivation of information; it reversed where the indictment alleged inducement to part with money.  
- Alternatively or in addition, plead a § 1348(2) count if the misconduct squarely fits its text (a scheme “to obtain … money or property in connection with the purchase or sale of … any security”). Subsection (2) expressly names the money/property objective and thus is a natural fit for inducement‑to‑purchase theories.  
- Avoid resting a § 1348 charge solely on a “right‑to‑control” or information‑deprivation theory: Constantinescu reiterates Ciminelli’s rule that depriving victims only of valuable economic information is not a protected property interest and cannot support a fraud conviction.  
- Plead elements and overt acts with particularity sufficient to show the object (obtaining money/property) and intent to obtain it; the Fifth Circuit emphasized that alleging defendants “had money in mind” (profits from dumping) was dispositive.

Bottom line
Post‑Kousisis (as applied by Constantinescu), a § 1348 prosecution grounded in fraudulent inducement must allege that obtaining the victim’s money or property was an object of the scheme. You can (and often should) charge that theory under § 1348(1) by pleading the inducement → victim purchase → defendant profit sequence, or charge § 1348(2) which explicitly references obtaining money or property. Either way, do not rely on an information‑only/right‑to‑control theory.
