Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### How does aiding-and-abetting liability under § 2 apply to collaborators who amplify others’ misstatements?
Question:
Some defendants might not author false posts themselves but could retweet, echo, or coordinate timing while buying and selling. Under § 2, what acts and mens rea are sufficient to make someone liable for securities fraud if their role is primarily amplification and coordinated trading rather than making the initial misrepresentation?
Answer:
Short answer
Under Constantinescu, someone who does not author the original misstatement can still be criminally charged under § 2 (as an aider/abettor or coconspirator) so long as the indictment alleges that the person (1) performed acts that were part of the scheme to induce purchases (for example, retweeting/echoing, coordinating timing of posts and trades, or otherwise amplifying the misstatement in a way alleged to have caused others to buy), and (2) had the requisite mens rea — i.e., the intent to deceive and to obtain money or property through that deception. The Fifth Circuit reversed dismissal because the indictment alleged that defendants used misrepresentations to induce followers to purchase securities (a fraudulent‑inducement theory) and that obtaining money was the object of the scheme. See Constantinescu, slip op. §§ III–IV.

Why — the doctrinal rules from Constantinescu
- Elements the court required the indictment to plead: a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud.” The court treated the “scheme” element as requiring a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money” (quoting Evans) and the “intent” element as an intent (1) to deceive and (2) to cause some harm to result from the deceit. Constantinescu § III (citing Greenlaw and Evans).  
- The court relied on the Supreme Court’s fraudulent‑inducement line (Kousisis) to hold that a fraud charged under § 1348 may be based on inducing victims to hand over money or property even if the victims receive market‑value goods in return and even if the defendant’s intent was to profit rather than to render the victim worse off. Constantinescu § III.A–B (discussing Ciminelli and Kousisis).

What that means for amplifiers under § 2
- Acts that the government can (and in this opinion did) allege as part of the scheme: amplifying false statements (retweeting, echoing, reposting), coordinating the timing/content of messages, and coordinating trading activity so that followers are induced to buy while the defendants plan to sell. The opinion treats those kinds of dissemination and coordinated trading as the factual means by which the defendants “induced their followers . . . to purchase the security,” which is the core allegation that made the indictment sufficient. See Indictment ¶¶ 13–14 and Constantinescu § I–III.  
- Required mens rea: the opinion makes clear the government must allege that the amplifier acted with the intent to deceive and with the object of obtaining money or property (i.e., obtaining money was “in mind”). Constantinescu § III (drawing on Evans and Kousisis). Thus mere republication absent an allegation that the publisher intended to deceive and to cause purchases (or otherwise further the fraud) would not support a fraud indictment on the facts the court reviewed. The court distinguished that situation from Ciminelli’s “right‑to‑control” theory where the only alleged injury was loss of information; here the indictment alleges the amplifier helped induce purchase (money/property) so Ciminelli does not bar the charge. Constantinescu § III.A.

Practical takeaway for pleading § 2 liability
- To charge a retweeter/echoer/coordination player under § 2, the indictment should allege (and Constantinescu endorses the sufficiency of such allegations): (a) specific acts of dissemination or coordination tied to the fraud (e.g., republishing false posts at specific times, coordinating messages among multiple accounts, timing posts to coincide with sales), and (b) that those acts were done with knowledge of the falsity (or at least with deceptive intent) and with the object of inducing purchases so the defendant (or the group) would obtain money or property. Constantinescu treats such allegations as enough to plead a scheme and intent to defraud. See Constantinescu §§ I–III; see also Kousisis discussion in § III.B.

Caveat
- Constantinescu resolved indictment sufficiency; it did not resolve guilt or delineate all contours of § 2 proof at trial. The court did not attempt to catalog every possible fact pattern that will or will not sustain accomplice liability, only that an indictment alleging amplification tied to fraudulent inducement and a money‑obtaining objective states an offense. Constantinescu § IV.

Bottom line: amplifiers can be liable under § 2 when the indictment ties their amplification/coordination to a fraudulent‑inducement scheme and alleges they acted with intent to deceive and to obtain money/property. Constantinescu reversed dismissal on that ground.
