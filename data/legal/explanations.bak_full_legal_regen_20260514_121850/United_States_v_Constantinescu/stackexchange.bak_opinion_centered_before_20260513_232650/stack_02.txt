Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### How is "intent to defraud" satisfied if followers received market-value securities?
Question:
The court says intent to defraud requires intent to deceive and to cause some harm, but then relies on Kousisis to say the statutes don’t require a net pecuniary loss and that providing something of equal value "does not obviate" fraud. How do we reconcile the Fifth Circuit’s prior formulation (intent to cause harm) with Kousisis’s statement that no economic loss is required? In practice, what counts as the requisite "harm" if victims bought securities at then-prevailing market prices?
Answer:
Short answer
The Fifth Circuit reconciles the two formulations by treating the “harm” element as the induced deprivation of money or property — i.e., the act of causing victims to part with money or property through materially false pretenses — not a requirement that victims be left economically worse off on net. So followers who buy securities at then‑prevailing market prices after being fraudulently induced still suffer the legally cognizable “harm” required for intent to defraud: they were tricked into transferring money/property. Kousisis (as applied in Constantinescu) squarely supports that view.

Why (doctrinally)
- Two different but compatible ideas appear in the opinion:
  1. The court (quoting Evans/Greenlaw) says intent to defraud requires an intent (1) to deceive and (2) to cause some harm to result from the deceit. United States v. Constantinescu, slip op. at III (quoting Evans, 892 F.3d at 712).
  2. The Supreme Court in Kousisis held that the fraudulent‑inducement theory covers schemes in which the defendant obtains the victim’s money or property by false pretenses “regardless of whether the fraudster . . . seeks to cause the victim net pecuniary loss.” Constantinescu III.B (quoting Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1388–92).

- The reconciliation made by the Fifth Circuit: the “harm” the defendant intends to cause can be the very deprivation of money or property brought about by fraudulent inducement. In other words, the causation‑type harm is the transfer itself (the victim handing over money or property because of the deception), not a separate requirement that the victim’s bottom line be reduced after all transactions settle. Constantinescu III.B; see Kousisis, 145 S. Ct. at 1392–94.

How that applies to followers who bought at market prices
- If the indictment plausibly alleges (as Constantinescu does) that defendants posted materially false information to induce followers to purchase securities, those followers were induced to part with money/property. Under Kousisis (applied in Constantinescu), that induced transfer is a legally cognizable injury even if the purchaser received market‑value securities or the defendant provided something that appears equal in value. Constantinescu III.B (quoting Kousisis: defendant may be liable “even when he ‘provides something—be it money, property, or services—of equal value in return’”).

- Put more concretely: a follower who buys a stock because a social‑media influencer falsely says insiders are buying has been deprived of money (the purchase price) by the fraud. Whether the stock’s market price at the time equals the value the buyer got is not fatal to the fraud charge under the fraudulent‑inducement theory embraced by Kousisis and applied in Constantinescu.

Limits and practical points
- Ciminelli still bars a prosecution based solely on deprivation of valuable economic information (a “right‑to‑control” theory). Constantinescu III.A (distinguishing Ciminelli). The government must allege a fraudulent‑inducement (or other traditional property‑interest) theory — here, that victims were induced to hand over money/property.
- At indictment stage the court assumes the allegations true; factual issues such as whether a particular follower actually relied on the misrepresentation, whether causation is established, or whether the follower in fact suffered any ultimate loss, are jury/trial issues. Constantinescu II (indictment adequacy standard).
- The court also cites Baker to reject a “mirror image” requirement — the defendant’s gain need not exactly mirror the victim’s loss. Constantinescu III.B (citing United States v. Baker, 923 F.3d 390, 403–05 (5th Cir. 2019)).

Bottom line
“Intent to cause some harm” is satisfied when the defendant intends to deceive others so they will hand over money or property — i.e., fraudulent inducement. Under Kousisis and Constantinescu, that induced transfer is a cognizable injury even if victims received securities at market price or something of equal value; the law does not require proof of a net pecuniary loss to allege intent to defraud.
