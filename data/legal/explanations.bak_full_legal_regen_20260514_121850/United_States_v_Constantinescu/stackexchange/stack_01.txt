Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### How did the indictment satisfy § 1348’s “money or property” requirement without invoking the discredited right-to-control theory?
Question:
The panel says Ciminelli bars convictions based solely on depriving victims of valuable economic information, but upholds this indictment because it alleges fraudulent inducement to part with money. I’m struggling to see the property interest here: the followers bought market-traded securities (arguably at then-market prices), and there was no alleged right-to-control theory. Is the idea that the victims’ cash used to buy shares is the protected property, even if they received securities of equal value at the time? What makes this different from simply providing (allegedly false) information that affects someone’s decision-making, which Ciminelli seemed to reject?
Answer:
Short answer: The indictment pleaded a fraudulent‑inducement theory, not the right‑to‑control theory Ciminelli rejected. It alleges defendants used material misrepresentations to trick followers into buying securities — i.e., to “part with their money or property” — and the Court relied on Kousisis to hold that obtaining that money/property is all § 1348 requires, even if the victims received securities of (alleged) market value in return.

Why that matters (doctrinal explanation tied to the opinion)

- Ciminelli stands for a narrow point: a fraud conviction cannot be based solely on depriving someone of “valuable economic information” (the right‑to‑control theory) because that right is not a traditional property interest. As the opinion summarizes, “[t]he right to valuable economic information . . . is not a traditional property interest,” so the right‑to‑control theory “cannot form the basis for a [fraud] conviction.” (Opinion § III.A, quoting Ciminelli.)

- Kousisis, by contrast, recognizes the fraudulent‑inducement theory: a defendant uses a material misstatement to trick a victim into a contract that requires handing over money or property. The Constantinescu panel quotes Kousisis: the fraudulent‑inducement theory “is not a repackaging of the right‑to‑control theory” and “protects money and property.” (Opinion § III.A.)

- Critically, Kousisis also makes clear that § 1348 does not require the victim to be left economically worse off. A fraud is complete when the defendant induces the victim to part with money or property under materially false pretenses, even if the fraudster provides something of equal value in return. The Fifth Circuit relied on that point when rejecting the defendants’ argument that receiving market‑value securities defeats § 1348 liability. (Opinion § III.B, discussing Kousisis and quoting that the statutes reach schemes to “obtain” the victim’s “money or property,” “regardless of whether the fraudster . . . seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.”)

Application to these facts (as alleged in the indictment)

- The indictment alleges defendants “post[ed] false and misleading information … so that other investors were induced to purchase the security.” The Court reads that allegation as alleging that defendants induced followers to “part with their money or property” by buying the stock — i.e., the cash (or other consideration) used to buy the shares is the protected property. (Opinion § III.A.)

- The panel treats this as materially different from Ciminelli because the harm here is not mere informational deprivation; it is an alleged scheme whose object was to obtain money (the alleged $114 million profit is cited as showing they “had money in mind”). Under Kousisis and the authorities the panel cites, that suffices for § 1348.

Bottom line

The indictment survived because it alleges fraudulent inducement to buy (a transfer of money/property), not merely withholding of information. Under the Court’s reading of Kousisis (as applied in Constantinescu), obtaining the buyer’s money/property through lies satisfies § 1348 even if the buyer receives securities of apparent market value in return.
