Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

### Why proceed under § 1348 instead of § 10(b)/Rule 10b-5, and what elements differ in the criminal context (e.g., reliance, loss causation)?
Question:
The conduct sounds like classic pump-and-dump manipulation often charged under § 10(b) and Rule 10b-5, but this case proceeds under § 1348. Does § 1348 avoid civil-law concepts like reliance and loss causation that sometimes appear in § 10(b) cases? In a criminal § 1348 prosecution, what must the government prove about materiality, causation, or market impact compared to a Rule 10b-5 theory?
Answer:
Short answer
- The government proceeded under 18 U.S.C. § 1348 because this is a criminal securities‑fraud statute that targets schemes to obtain money or property by deception. As the Fifth Circuit explains in United States v. Constantinescu, § 1348 borrows the mail/wire‑fraud framework and asks whether the indictment plausibly alleges (1) a scheme to defraud (a material misrepresentation intended to deceive to obtain something of value) and (2) an intent to defraud (an intent to deceive and to obtain money/property or cause harm).  
- That criminal framework does not require the civil Rule 10b‑5 proof problems that often arise—most importantly, it does not rest on the “right‑to‑control” theory condemned in Ciminelli and, under Kousisis as applied in Constantinescu, does not require proof that the victim suffered a net pecuniary loss. But the government still must allege inducement to part with money or property (here, that followers were induced to buy securities) and that obtaining money/property was an object of the scheme.

What the opinion actually holds (doctrine and proof)
- The Fifth Circuit frames § 1348 in the mail/wire fraud mold: securities fraud under § 1348 requires (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” A “scheme to defraud” means a material misrepresentation “intended to deceive others in order to obtain something of value, such as money, from the [entity] to be deceived.” An “intent to defraud” requires an intent both to deceive and to cause some harm or to obtain money/property. (Constantinescu, § III; quoting Greenlaw and Evans.)
- Constantinescu distinguishes two theories: the rejected “right‑to‑control” theory (depriving someone of valuable economic information alone is not a protected property interest — Ciminelli) and the “fraudulent‑inducement” theory (inducing someone to hand over money or property through a lie). The indictment here alleged fraudulent inducement (followers were induced to purchase securities), so it did not run afoul of Ciminelli. (Constantinescu § III.A.)
- The court also relies on Kousisis to reject the notion that the government must prove a victim was left economically worse off. Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, a defendant violates the fraud statutes when he “schem[es] to ‘obtain’ the victim’s ‘money or property,’ regardless of whether he seeks to leave the victim economically worse off.” The indictment’s allegation that defendants “pumped and dumped” and thus sought money was sufficient to allege intent to defraud. (Constantinescu § III.B; citing Kousisis.)

What the government must (and need not) prove in a criminal § 1348 prosecution, per Constantinescu
- Must prove (allegations the indictment must plausibly make):  
  - Material misrepresentations or deceptive conduct (the “scheme to defraud”); and  
  - Intent to deceive and to obtain money/property (or to cause harm) — i.e., the object of the scheme was to obtain money (here, by selling after pumping). The indictment should allege the deceptive communications induced victims to part with money/property (e.g., that followers bought the stock because of the misrepresentations). (Constantinescu §§ II–III.)  
- Does not require (as an element of the criminal charge, per the opinion):  
  - Proof that the victims were deprived only of “valuable economic information” (the right‑to‑control theory) — that cannot by itself support fraud under Ciminelli; and  
  - Proof that the victims suffered net pecuniary loss or that the fraudster intended to leave victims worse off — Kousisis makes clear the government need not show net loss. (Constantinescu §§ III.A–B.)

Where “reliance,” “causation,” and “market impact” fit in practice
- Reliance/inducement: Constantinescu treats the fraudulent‑inducement theory as focused on whether the defendant used a material misstatement to trick victims into a transaction that required handing over money/property. So while the government need not plead the private‑law elements of a Rule 10b‑5 civil claim, it must plausibly allege that victims were induced to act (i.e., that the misrepresentations caused followers to buy). The indictment’s allegation that defendants “induced their followers to purchase securities” supplies that element. (Constantinescu § III.A.)  
- Loss causation/ net loss: The court says the government need not prove the victims were made worse off overall; obtaining money/property for the defendant suffices as the object of the fraud even if the victim received market‑value securities or some offsetting benefit. (Constantinescu § III.B; citing Kousisis and Baker.)  
- Market impact: Allegations that the deception “artificially inflat[ed]” the price and enabled defendants to sell at a profit are offered in the opinion as one plausible way to show how the scheme produced the defendants’ ill‑gotten gains. Proving market impact or price inflation will be one evidentiary path to proving inducement and that the object was to obtain money/property, but the opinion does not adopt an independent extra element requiring market‑wide impact distinct from the scheme/intent inquiry.

Practical takeaway for a pump‑and‑dump prosecution
- Prosecutors use § 1348 when they want to criminally punish schemes to obtain money/property by deception. Under Constantinescu they can proceed on a fraudulent‑inducement theory: allege material misstatements, allege the defendant intended to deceive and obtain money, and allege that victims were induced to purchase (i.e., parted with money or property). They do not have to prove the narrower civil elements of reliance and loss causation in the same way civil plaintiffs must, nor can they rely solely on a right‑to‑control theory based on withholding information. (Constantinescu §§ III.A–B.)

If you want to work through how an indictment should be pleaded in this posture, you can map each required allegation in Constantinescu to the facts the government will need to allege (material false statements; specific acts showing intent to deceive and to obtain money; facts showing followers were induced to buy; and allegations explaining how defendants realized gains).
