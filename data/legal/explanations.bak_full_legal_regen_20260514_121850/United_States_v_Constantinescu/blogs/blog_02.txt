Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

## Intent Without Loss? Mens Rea Under § 1348 After Kousisis and the Fifth Circuit’s Take

Why this matters: The Fifth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Constantinescu is an important early test of how lower courts will apply the Supreme Court’s recent fraud decisions—most notably Kousisis v. United States and Ciminelli v. United States—when prosecuting modern, social-media-driven “pump and dump” schemes under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. At issue is a basic doctrinal question with big practical consequences: what must the government prove about a defendant’s mens rea when the alleged scheme enriches the promoter but leaves victims holding marketable securities (or otherwise receiving value) so that they may not show a net loss? The Fifth Circuit says that, consistent with Kousisis, a profit motive can satisfy the required intent to defraud so long as the indictment alleges a fraudulent inducement to part with money or property.

How the panel reasoned: The panel began by situating § 1348 within the broader fraud jurisprudence, borrowing concepts developed under the mail- and wire-fraud statutes. That framing set up the two core elements: (1) a “scheme to defraud” involving a material misrepresentation and (2) an “intent to defraud.” Defendants conceded deception, so the dispute focused on whether the indictment alleged both a scheme to obtain money or property and the requisite intent.

The opinion leans heavily on two Supreme Court benchmarks. First, Ciminelli foreclosed convictions premised solely on a “right-to-control” theory—that is, a theory that treats the victim’s right to valuable information as the protected property interest. The Fifth Circuit was careful to distinguish Constantinescu: the indictment here was not trying to criminalize withholding or manipulating informational control alone; instead, it alleged a classic fraudulent-inducement scheme in which influencers made false or misleading statements to induce followers to buy securities (i.e., to transfer money or property).

Second, and crucially, the panel relied on Kousisis for the proposition that the fraud statutes do not require evidence that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off. Kousisis accepts the fraudulent-inducement theory: a fraud is complete when a defendant induces a victim to transfer money or property by material misrepresentations, even if the defendant provides something of equal value in return or the victim does not suffer a net pecuniary loss. Applying that principle, the Fifth Circuit concluded the indictment adequately alleged defendants “had money in mind,” that they used deception to induce purchases, and that obtaining money was an object of the fraud. Those allegations suffice to plead intent to defraud under § 1348.

Putting the decision in context: The panel’s approach is straightforward in one sense: it follows Kousisis’ textual logic and rejects the district court’s dismissal that depended on a hyper-technical reading of “property” that would exempt many modern frauds built on inducement rather than pure information control. But the opinion also highlights a doctrinal seam running through recent fraud law. Prior decisions (and traditional formulations) describe intent to defraud as requiring an intent both to deceive and to cause some harm to result from the deceit. Kousisis narrows what “harm” means for the fraudulent-inducement theory—harm is realized through the induced transfer of money or property, not necessarily through a showing of net loss. The Fifth Circuit embraces that refinement while acknowledging Ciminelli’s limiting principle: information alone isn’t a “protected” property interest absent a showing that deception produced a transfer of money or property.

Why “having money in mind” matters—and what it doesn’t: The phrase “had money in mind,” drawn from Kousisis and echoed here, is doing real doctrinal work. It signals that the prosecution’s theory must show the defendant sought to obtain money or property as an objective of the scheme. That is different from prosecuting someone merely for hyping a stock or making optimistic predictions. To survive the kinds of pretrial challenges before the district court here, an indictment must allege (and later the government must prove) purposeful deception aimed at eliciting purchases or transfers. Secret selling after pumping, coordinated timing, demonstrable profits tied to misleading posts, and instructions that targets act now are the kinds of facts that make the “money in mind” inference plausible.

Defense levers and practical fallout: The Fifth Circuit’s ruling will sharpen defense strategies in several predictable ways. First, defendants will press Ciminelli’s boundary—arguing that alleged misconduct is really only about manipulation of information or opinion, not inducement to transfer money. When conduct looks more like puffing or opinion-based promotion, defendants will urge dismissals or favorable jury instructions insulating speech and predictive statements. Second, defendants will litigate mens rea vigorously: they will press evidentiary challenges to show lack of specific intent to deceive (e.g., that posts were careless, optimistic, or non-actionable opinion) or that defendants lacked intent to obtain money (arguing instead motives like self-promotion, platform growth, or liquidating positions for unrelated reasons). Third, defenses will emphasize materiality—if alleged statements were non-actionable hype rather than materially false representations about a company’s business or trading positions, the fraudulent-inducement theory is weaker.

For prosecutors and courts, the ruling foreshadows practical conflicts at the jury-instruction stage. Judges will need to craft instructions that reflect Kousisis’ principle—explaining that a defendant can be guilty even when a victim receives something of value in return—while also making clear that mere profit-seeking without deception, or mere expression of opinion, does not constitute fraud. Trial courts will face the hard task of explaining where persuasive advocacy or puffery ends and criminal deception begins. That balance will be especially fraught in cases involving social-media influencers whose business model blends commentary, self-branding, and trading.

Doctrinal implications and what may come next: Constantinescu reinforces a post-Kousisis landscape in which prosecutors can pursue criminal liability for inducements that produce transfers of money or property even if victims are not left economically worse off. At the same time, the decision preserves the Ciminelli guardrail: courts must be vigilant to ensure prosecutions allege an actual transfer of money or property induced by deception rather than mere manipulation of information or discretionary choice.

Expect several downstream effects. First, the decision invites more criminal enforcement against social-media pump-and-dump schemes; indeed, indictments that properly plead inducement and “money in mind” will be harder to dismiss. Second, defense-oriented litigation will increasingly target the line-drawing between puffery and materially false statements, and between information-control theories barred by Ciminelli and inducement-based theories permitted by Kousisis. Finally, this area is ripe for further appellate guidance—either clarifying what quantum of proof is needed to infer intent to obtain money from a promoter’s conduct or further refining how courts should instruct juries about “harm” and “value” in fraudulent-inducement cases.

Bottom line for practitioners: For prosecutors, the lesson is to plead and prove inducement—tie false statements to induced transfers and to demonstrable profits. For defense counsel, the path forward is to keep pressing Ciminelli’s limits, attack proofs of deliberate deception, and demand careful jury instructions that distinguish criminal deception from risky speech or harmless market activity. Courts will be the arbiters of that line, and Constantinescu shows how lower courts are beginning to reconcile the modern realities of online trading fraud with the Supreme Court’s recent doctrinal recalibration.
