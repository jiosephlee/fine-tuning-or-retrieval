Title: Blog about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

# Influencers, Pump-and-Dumps, and the Next Wave of ‘FinTok’ Prosecutions

## Why Constantinescu matters

The Fifth Circuit’s reversal in United States v. Constantinescu is one of the clearest signals yet that federal prosecutors have a viable path to use the securities-fraud statute, 18 U.S.C. § 1348, against social-media “influencers” who tout stocks and then quietly sell into the induced demand. At issue was nothing exotic: the government’s superseding indictment alleges a classic pump-and-dump in which a group of well-followed posters bought securities, posted false and misleading statements to inflate prices, and then “dumped” their positions for roughly $114 million in alleged proceeds. The district court had dismissed the indictment on the ground that what was alleged was only a deprivation of “valuable economic information,” a theory the Supreme Court rejected in Ciminelli. The Fifth Circuit reversed, framing the allegations as a permissible fraudulent-inducement theory under Kousisis: defendants are accused of tricking followers into parting with money or property (by buying securities), not merely withholding or controlling information.

This is not a novelty prosecution shoehorned into an ill-fitting statute. Rather, Constantinescu shows how recent Supreme Court decisions have clarified the doctrinal lines prosecutors must clear—and the contours of what is still protected speech.

## What the court held and why its reasoning matters

The panel took doctrinal care in two ways. First, it distinguished the indictment from the “right-to-control” theory invalidated in Ciminelli. In Ciminelli the fraud prosecution depended on the idea that depriving a public actor of valuable economic information about contracting opportunities could constitute the kind of traditional “property” the federal fraud statutes protect; the Court said it could not. Here, by contrast, the Fifth Circuit emphasizes that the indictment alleges active inducement to buy securities—that is, the followers parted with money (or property) as the direct object of the deception. Under the Court’s later guidance in Kousisis, that fraudulent-inducement theory is a proper basis for a § 1348 prosecution because it protects money and property, not a amorphous right to control information.

Second, the panel relied on Kousisis to reject the defendants’ argument that fraud requires an intent to leave the victim economically worse off. Kousisis made clear that obtaining money or property by deceit is the relevant object, regardless of whether the fraudster provides something of equal value or whether the victim’s net position is ultimately harmed. The indictment’s allegation that the influencers posted falsehoods to induce purchases and then sold for profit was therefore enough, at the pleading stage, to allege intent to defraud.

Put succinctly: the Fifth Circuit treated on-platform touting as classic fraudulent inducement when the government alleges concrete misrepresentations about positions and prospects designed to make followers buy—and when the defendant secretly plans to exit.

## Drawing the line from puffery to criminal fraud

A central practical question is where the boundary lies between actionable fraud and non-actionable opinion or “sales puffery.” The opinion leaves room for the usual First Amendment and fraud-law protections. Materiality and deception remain essential: blanket cheerleading—“this stock’s a rocket!”—is qualitatively different from a knowingly false statement about one’s trading position (“I just bought 10,000 shares and I’m holding for months”) or a dishonest claim about imminent corporate developments. The court’s reasoning tracks that conventional divide. The fraudulent-inducement theory requires a material misrepresentation intended to cause the victim to part with money or property; mere enthusiasm or hyperbole without a false factual predicate will not meet that standard.

But Constantinescu places particular weight on lies that affect a follower’s transactional choice—claims about current holdings, purchase history, timing, or plans to sell. An influencer who explicitly promises not to sell before followers accumulate positions, or who claims to be buying when in fact offloading, risks fitting squarely within the fraudulent-inducement frame the court endorsed.

## Historical context: scalping, modern amplification, and enforcement appetite

The decision fits into a longstanding enforcement concern—“scalping” or undisclosed self-dealing in market promotion—that predates social media. Regulators have long been wary of coordinated market promotion where the promoter secretly profits from the very demand they create. What’s new is scale and velocity: platforms, algorithmic feeds, and private channels like Discord and Telegram allow promoters to reach large, receptive audiences quickly and to coordinate narratives in near real time.

Constantinescu gives the Department of Justice a doctrinally grounded lever to pursue these modern scalpers under § 1348 when the allegations allege deceptive inducement to transact. That does not make every misstatement a crime, but it does sharpen prosecutorial focus on demonstrable falsehoods about trades, positions, exit plans, and undisclosed incentives.

## Spillover beyond equities and interaction with other statutes

Although Constantinescu resolves the § 1348 question for securities, its practical reach will depend on whether the marketed asset is legally a “security.” For equities the fit is obvious. For many digital assets—crypto tokens, NFTs, certain ICOs—the securities analysis is fact-intensive (Howey and its progeny), and some tokens will fall outside § 1348. In those cases prosecutors still have options: traditional wire and mail fraud statutes, 18 U.S.C. §§ 1341 & 1343, share many doctrinal ancestors with § 1348 and can be used when the nexus to a “security” is frail or absent. The Fifth Circuit itself acknowledged the circuit’s tendency to borrow mail/wire fraud concepts when construing § 1348.

That prosecutorial flexibility means promoters of non-equity assets are not in a safe harbor merely because an instrument isn’t a security. Deceptive inducements to hand over money in exchange for any asset may attract wire-fraud prosecutions, and regulatory referrals to the SEC can bring parallel civil enforcement and disgorgement claims if securities laws are implicated.

## Practical takeaways for influencers and community operators

For individuals with sizable followings and for those who operate or moderate investor-oriented communities, the Constantinescu decision crystallizes several practical compliance points. First, avoid specific factual lies about your holdings, transactions, or plans: false statements about purchases, holdings, or intended exit points are the sort of allegations that sustain fraudulent-inducement counts. Second, be transparent about compensation or conflicts of interest. While the opinion does not rest on statutory disclosure regimes, undisclosed paid promotion or secret coordination is exactly the kind of fact that makes a misrepresentation material. Third, documentation and contemporaneous records matter: prosecutors often rely on messaging histories, transaction records, and platform analytics at the investigative stage, so maintaining clear business practices can be protective. Fourth, private, invitation-only channels raise risks: Discord and Telegram groups that facilitate coordinated buying and rapid dissemination magnify the chances that a prosecutor will find an intent-to-defraud inference.

For platforms and counsel, the ruling argues for clearer policies on financial recommendation labeling, paid-promotion disclosure, and retention of messages and payment records that could be relevant to investigations.

## Bottom line

Constantinescu does not criminalize enthusiasm, nor does it turn every influencer’s off-the-cuff market take into a federal offense. But it draws a consequential doctrinal line: knowingly false, material misstatements intended to induce followers to part with money can be prosecuted under § 1348 as fraudulent inducement. In an era of FinTok, pump-and-dump conspiracies can be scaled with unprecedented speed, and the decision hands prosecutors a coherent theory—rooted in Kousisis and careful to avoid Ciminelli’s forbidden “right-to-control” framing—to pursue those who allegedly manufacture demand by deception. For influencers, community operators, and would-be promoters of emerging assets, the prudent course is transparency and restraint; for enforcement watchers, Constantinescu is likely the opening chapter in a new wave of cases targeting online market manipulation.
