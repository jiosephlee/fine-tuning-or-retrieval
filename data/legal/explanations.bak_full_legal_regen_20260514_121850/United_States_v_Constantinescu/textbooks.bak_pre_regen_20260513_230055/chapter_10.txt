Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

Chapter 10: # Comparative and Emerging Authority

This chapter surveys recent circuit decisions and prosecutorial trends that shape criminal securities-fraud practice in the wake of the Supreme Court’s decisions in Ciminelli v. United States and Kousisis v. United States. Those cases clarified the limits of fraud prosecutions built on theories that deprive victims of merely “valuable economic information” (the so‑called right‑to‑control theory) and reaffirmed the viability of a fraudulent‑inducement theory that targets schemes aimed at obtaining money or property through material misrepresentations. The post‑Ciminelli/Kousisis landscape is best understood as a recalibration: prosecutors must frame securities fraud charges to allege the obtaining of money or property by deceit, and courts must distinguish between cognizable property deprivations and informational injuries that fall outside the reach of the criminal fraud statutes. The leading circuit authorities, recent applications to inducement cases (including social‑media “pump and dump” prosecutions), the Department of Justice’s evolving charging practices, the interplay between civil and criminal securities doctrines, and the particular evidentiary and jurisdictional problems raised by technology‑driven schemes are all developing in response to those doctrinal guardrails.

## Fifth Circuit guidance: Greenlaw; Evans; Baker

The Fifth Circuit has been the principal source of circuit guidance in this area, and its jurisprudence provides the textual and analytic building blocks that the courts use to assess whether an indictment pleads the “scheme to defraud” and the requisite “intent to defraud.” Three district and circuit decisions—United States v. Greenlaw, United States v. Evans, and United States v. Baker—illustrate how the Fifth Circuit treats materiality, deception, and the relationship between the defendant’s gain and the victim’s loss.

Greenlaw (84 F.4th 325) articulates the black‑letter formulation the panel relied upon in the pump‑and‑dump appeal: section 1348 “borrows key concepts” from the mail and wire fraud statutes, and a securities fraud charge requires (1) a “scheme to defraud” and (2) an “intent to defraud.” The “scheme” element turns on a material misrepresentation intended to deceive in order to obtain something of value—most commonly money or property—from the deceived party. Greenlaw’s reiteration of the two‑part framework provides the doctrinal baseline that other courts apply when assessing post‑Ciminelli claims.

Evans (892 F.3d 692) focuses on the first element—what counts as deception—and emphasizes that the government must plead a material misrepresentation or omission calculated to deceive. Evans also shows how courts analyze materiality in the corporate and securities contexts: the misrepresentation must be such that it would be likely to affect a reasonable investor’s decisionmaking. Evans thus supplies the material‑misstatement standard that anchors a fraudulent‑inducement theory: the government must identify the misstatements or deceptive omissions and tie them causally to the victim’s act of delivering money or property.

Baker (923 F.3d 390) is especially important because it addresses how courts treat the symmetry (or lack thereof) between a defendant’s gain and a victim’s loss. Baker explains that the fraud statutes do not require a “mirror image” loss—i.e., that the defendant’s gain be precisely equal to the victim’s loss. Instead, what matters is whether the defendant sought to obtain money or property through deceit; the victim’s ultimate net pecuniary position is not dispositive. That principle became critical after Kousisis clarified that a defendant may be guilty even if the defendant furnishes something of equal apparent value, so long as the victim was induced to transfer money or property pursuant to materially false pretenses.

Taken together, these Fifth Circuit precedents provide the analytical scaffolding adopted in the pump‑and‑dump opinion discussed earlier. They show that a properly pled indictment must (a) identify the material misrepresentations or deceptive omissions, (b) allege that the misstatements were intended to induce the transfer of money or property, and (c) allege the defendant’s intent to obtain that money or property—even when the victims later receive marketable securities or some other benefit.

## Seventh Circuit’s application of Kousisis (Smith) to inducement cases

The Seventh Circuit was quick to apply the Supreme Court’s Kousisis decision to cases involving social‑media‑driven inducement. In United States v. Smith (No. 23‑2840 et seq., 2025 WL 2406104), the court confronted facts similar to those in many modern pump‑and‑dump cases: social‑media influencers touted securities, followers purchased, and the influencers then sold positions. Smith applied Kousisis to validate criminal charges premised on fraudulent inducement even where victims may have received securities in exchange for their money and may not have suffered net pecuniary loss.

Smith demonstrates several practical points. First, it endorses the Kousisis distinction between the right‑to‑control theory (invalid under Ciminelli) and the fraudulent‑inducement theory (valid under Kousisis): where the government alleges that the defendant used misrepresentations to induce the purchase of a security, it is alleging the obtaining of money or property, not merely informational deprivation. Second, Smith affirms that a victim’s receipt of marketable securities does not automatically defeat a fraud indictment, provided the government pleads that the victim purchased those securities in reliance on a material misstatement and that the defendant intended to obtain money or property thereby. Third, Smith clarifies evidentiary contours: courts will expect prosecutors to show temporal and causal connections between misstatements and subsequent purchases, and to produce corroborating transactional records (brokerage statements, timing of trades, profit realizations).

Smith thereby functions as a model for other circuits that must decide how to apply Kousisis in contexts where communications are broadcast to a wide audience via social platforms. The Seventh Circuit’s reasoning makes clear that courts will look to the mechanics of the transaction—who bought, when they bought, what the influencer knew and failed to disclose, and whether the influencer reaped identifiable profits—instead of relying on amorphous theories that treat information itself as the victim’s property.

## Post‑Ciminelli charging trends and DOJ guidance themes

After Ciminelli and especially after Kousisis, the Justice Department adjusted charging practices and messaging. Several themes recur in post‑Ciminelli DOJ guidance and in charging decisions:

- Framing the theory: USAOs and Main Justice now focus on pleading fraudulent inducement when the facts support it. That means carefully framing allegations to show that the defendant used material misrepresentations to induce transfers of money or property (e.g., purchases of securities), rather than alleging an abstract interference with decisionmaking or the “right to control” assets.

- Particularization of misstatements and timing: Indictments increasingly specify (i) the exact misrepresentations; (ii) the platform and audience; (iii) the timing of purchases by followers; and (iv) the defendant’s secret sales or profit‑taking. Prosecutors recognize that Ciminelli invites scrutiny of generic allegations, so more precise factual pleading helps survive pretrial challenges.

- Reliance and causation: Charging memos and grand jury presentations emphasize evidence showing that the defendant’s communications were seen and relied upon by purchasers—screenshots, subscription lists, timestamps, brokerage records, and communications indicating that followers bought in response to posts.

- Mens rea evidence: Because criminal securities fraud requires intent to defraud, prosecutors focus on circumstantial proof of intent—immediate sales after promotional activity, lack of disclosure of positions, use of anonymous or coordinated accounts, concealment of compensation, and contemporaneous incriminating communications. Willful blindness and knowledge inferred from a pattern of conduct are often highlighted.

- Coordination with civil enforcement: DOJ increasingly coordinates with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) and state authorities, sharing evidence and aligning charges where possible. That coordination is useful for securing civil remedies, disgorgement, and preserving evidence, but DOJ retains prosecutorial independence and must adhere to criminal standards of proof and mens rea.

- Technology and investigative tools: DOJ guidance has acknowledged the need for specialized technological tools—data analytics to trace transaction patterns, subpoenas to platform operators, geolocation and IP logs—to support allegations that online statements caused particular financial movements.

In short, the Department’s charging posture evolved from earlier eras when prosecutorial theory might be more elastic, toward particularized, evidence‑heavy indictments that tie misrepresentations to actual transfers of money or property.

## Civil‑criminal cross‑pollination: lessons and limits

Civil securities enforcement and criminal prosecution often draw on the same factual matrix—misstatements to investors, trading records, and internal communications—but the doctrines, standards, and objectives differ. Understanding what each regime borrows from the other and where the lines must be drawn is crucial for practitioners on both sides.

Lessons that transfer from civil to criminal practice include materiality analysis, the importance of chronology, and the utility of transaction records and communications evidence. Civil securities suits inform the criminal understanding of what statements would be material to an investor and what circumstantial evidence supports an inference of scienter. For example, Rule 10b‑5 civil litigation has developed extensive standards on materiality and causation that criminal prosecutors look to when drafting indictments and presenting cases to grand juries.

But limits are significant. Criminal law requires a higher mens rea—intent to defraud—than many civil claims, which can be grounded on negligence or recklessness depending on the theory. Civil liability can be remedial and regulatory in nature; criminal law focuses on punishment and requires proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Importantly, the Ciminelli line demonstrates a doctrinal divergence: civil remedies under securities laws have been interpreted more expansively in some contexts, whereas criminal statutes must hew to the Supreme Court’s narrowing construction of what constitutes “property” for fraud purposes. Thus, theories viable in civil suits (e.g., claims based on misleading disclosures affecting the market or investor decisionmaking) may not be sustainable as criminal charges absent proof that the defendant sought to obtain money or property.

Cross‑agency cooperation is another area of cross‑pollination and tension. The SEC’s investigations frequently supply facts and leads to prosecutors, and the SEC will often bring parallel administrative or civil actions. Practitioners must recognize the strategic interaction: a civil settlement or administrative finding can be persuasive in a criminal case but cannot substitute for the government’s burden to prove criminal intent. Likewise, executives should not assume that success in an internal or administrative proceeding precludes later criminal exposure if prosecutors can marshal evidence of fraudulent inducement.

Finally, civil discovery regimes can materially aid criminal defense but are not identical; issues of privilege, Fifth Amendment protections, and timing differ. Criminal practitioners must navigate these institutional differences while drawing useful lessons where appropriate.

## Technology‑driven schemes: bots, group chats, and signal services

Technology has changed the mechanics of securities fraud and accordingly has pushed courts and prosecutors to adapt doctrinally and tactically. Common modern features include coordinated bot networks, encrypted or ephemeral messaging on platforms like Telegram and Discord, subscription‑based “signal” services promising trade tips, and algorithm‑driven amplification. Each raises particular legal and evidentiary challenges.

First, the multiplicity and anonymity of accounts complicate attribution. Proving that a named defendant controlled accounts that amplified promotions or that a particular post was seen by a particular cohort of investors requires combining platform metadata, IP address logs, device fingerprints, and corroborating witness testimony. Courts are increasingly receptive to metadata and behavioral signatures as evidence of control or coordination, but defense counsel frequently attack the chain of custody, the integrity of logs, or the sufficiency of platform responses to subpoenas.

Second, coordinated bot activity and synthetic engagement can be used to create the illusion of a market consensus or to inflate perceived demand. Prosecutors often seek to demonstrate that defendants steered or paid for such activity, and also to show a temporal linkage between the artificial promotion and follower purchases. The law treats use of bots as one form of deceptive conduct; when bots are used to influence trading and money changes hands, that behavior is squarely within the fraudulent‑inducement frame.

Third, subscription signal services and private chatrooms raise doctrinal questions about “in connection with” the purchase or sale of securities. Courts look for whether the defendant’s communications were reasonably calculated to reach investors who would act—whether through public broadcasts, targeted solicitations, or closed communities where members can be shown to have traded in response. A message in a closed paid chatroom that explicitly instructs members to buy, and where members’ trading records show purchases shortly thereafter, fits comfortably within the fraudulent‑inducement theory.

Fourth, encryption and ephemeral messaging create evidence collection problems. Investigators must use lawful surveillance, preservation requests, and cooperative subpoenas to obtain messages before deletion. Where platforms are uncooperative or data is stored overseas, prosecutors resort to mutual legal assistance treaties, civil discovery in parallel cases, or creative use of device forensics to recover deleted material.

Finally, the prevalence of algorithmic trading and high‑frequency trades raises the question whether certain trading patterns constitute manipulation rather than fraud by inducement. Market‑manipulation statutes and SEC rules address spoofing, layering, and wash trading, and prosecutors will sometimes pursue overlapping counts (fraud and market manipulation) when the technology was used to generate artificial price movement that translated into profits obtained by deception.

The upshot for practitioners is that technology both enlarges the opportunities for fraud and complicates proof: prosecutors must assemble multidisciplinary evidence to show that online activity caused people to part with money or property, and defense counsel have multiple avenues—metadata reliability, lack of causation, or legitimate market speech—to dispute those allegations.

## Venue, jurisdiction, and multi‑district coordination in online schemes

Online schemes spread across state and national borders, creating complex venue and jurisdictional issues. The traditional venue doctrine in fraud cases focuses on where an element of the offense occurred—where the scheme was planned, where the mails or wires were used, where the effects were felt, or where the victims resided. In the social‑media context, those lines blur.

Courts have applied an “effects” analysis: a federal prosecution can be venued where the defendant’s conduct had a foreseeable and substantial effect—where victims lost money or where significant events (sales, transfers, executions) occurred. In practice, prosecutors frequently charge in districts that combine investigative convenience (where evidence or witnesses are located), where significant losses were concentrated, or where the grand jury can most easily assemble corroborating testimony. Defense counsel routinely challenge venue, pointing to dispersed victims and to the fact that the defendant may never have physically been present in the forum. Those challenges succeed only when the court finds a lack of a sufficient connection between the defendant’s conduct and the forum.

Large‑scale online fraud often touches multiple districts; criminal cases may be coordinated through transfer motions under 28 U.S.C. § 1404 (change of venue in criminal cases is rarer but still possible in the interests of justice), or more commonly through investigative coordination by the U.S. Attorney’s Office and Main Justice. Unlike civil cases, criminal cases are not consolidated through MDL panels, but parallel prosecutions sometimes lead to coordination agreements among USAOs to avoid duplicative charges, to preserve scarce witness testimony, and to manage discovery efficiently. In complex frauds involving many defendants and many affected jurisdictions, prosecutors and defense counsel may litigate severance, consolidation for trial, and discovery logistics.

For practitioners, the key remedies to venue and jurisdictional problems include early venue challenges, motions for a bill of particulars (to force more precise allegations about where predicate acts occurred), and fact‑intensive discovery into the reach and effect of the defendant’s communications. Prosecutors should expect such litigation and should draft indictments with careful attention to jurisdictional predicates—identifying specific wires, transactions, or victims tied to the chosen forum.

## International dimensions: cross‑border promotion and extraterritoriality questions

Transnational promotion of securities—whether via foreign influencers, offshore brokers, or international chatrooms—raises extraterritoriality and evidence‑gathering issues. Two doctrinal axes structure the analysis: (1) whether U.S. criminal statutes reach foreign conduct; and (2) how investigators obtain evidence located abroad.

On the first axis, the touchstone is conduct that has a substantial and intended effect in the United States. Courts will apply a targeting analysis: did the defendant direct conduct toward U.S. investors or do acts that foreseeably caused injury in the United States? While Morrison v. National Australia Bank constrains extraterritorial application of SEC civil claims and emphasizes transactions in U.S. markets, criminal statutes can be applied extraterritorially when Congress clearly intends or where the statutes reach conduct that has domestic effects—particularly when communications were directed at U.S. platforms, U.S. brokerage accounts were used, or U.S. investors were harmed. Nevertheless, foreign defendants not present in the United States raise practical and legal hurdles, including extradition limits and comity concerns.

On the second axis, obtaining evidence abroad is often slow and uncertain. Mutual legal assistance treaties (MLATs), Letters Rogatory, and cooperative relationships with foreign regulators and law‑enforcement agencies are the primary mechanisms. Prosecutors commonly coordinate parallel investigations with foreign authorities; sometimes the result is simultaneous charges abroad and in the United States. Where platform data or exchange records are hosted overseas, prosecutors must weigh the feasibility of obtaining that evidence against alternative domestic proof—such as aggregated trading records, U.S. witnesses, or the defendant’s domestic contacts.

International enforcement can also raise questions about deference to foreign regulatory schemes and the possibility of double jeopardy when foreign prosecutions precede U.S. charges. Practitioners should anticipate diplomatic and evidentiary frictions, and tailor charging decisions to the strength of domestically obtainable evidence and the likelihood of effective international cooperation.

Practical defense considerations include early motion practice to contest jurisdictional reach, pressing for specific factual allegations tying defendant conduct to the United States, and challenging the sufficiency of foreign evidence. Prosecutors should document the nexus to the United States carefully in indictments and be prepared to show that the defendant’s conduct was aimed at or had foreseeable consequences in the United States.

Conclusion: Bringing the strands together

The post‑Ciminelli and post‑Kousisis jurisprudence establishes a disciplined framework for criminal securities fraud: courts and prosecutors must distinguish between protected property deprivations (money or property obtained through deceit) and non‑cognizable informational injuries, and prosecutors must plead and prove deceptive inducement of transfers of value with sufficient particularity. Circuit authorities—most prominently the Fifth Circuit’s Greenlaw, Evans, and Baker decisions and the Seventh Circuit’s application in Smith—illustrate how that framework operates in practice. DOJ charging adjustments reflect a posture that favors specificity, demonstrable causation, and robust mens rea proof, while technology, venue realities, and international reach demand novel evidence‑gathering strategies and careful choice of forum.

For students and practitioners, the practical takeaways are straightforward: when evaluating or crafting a criminal securities fraud case in the modern, digitally mediated marketplace, (1) frame the theory as fraudulent inducement when the evidence supports it; (2) particularize the misstatements, the audience, the timing of trades, and the defendant’s profits; (3) collect and preserve the digital trail (platform metadata, brokerage records, and device evidence); (4) anticipate venue and jurisdictional disputes and plan accordingly; and (5) recognize both the utility and the limits of civil enforcement parallels. Those steps will help ensure that complaints and indictments survive threshold scrutiny and that prosecutions focus on the classic criminal fraud target: obtaining money or property by deceit.
