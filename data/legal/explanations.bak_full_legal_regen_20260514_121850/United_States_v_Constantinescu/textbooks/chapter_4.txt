Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

Chapter 4: # Issue One: Scheme to Defraud and the Property Interest Requirement

A central question in this appeal is whether the superseding indictment alleges a “scheme to defraud” that targets a traditional property interest—money or property—or whether it instead alleges only the deprivation of valuable economic information (a non‑traditional interest that, under the Supreme Court’s decision in Ciminelli, cannot support a fraud conviction). The Fifth Circuit answers that the indictment here alleges a classic fraudulent‑inducement scheme that sought to obtain money (or its equivalent) from followers who were induced to buy securities, and therefore it pleads a deprivation of a protected property interest.

Defendants’ argument: mere deprivation of information
The defendants’ dismissal argument rests on characterizing the indictment as alleging only that they deprived their followers of valuable economic information—i.e., that by concealing their true trading positions and motivations the defendants kept followers from information necessary to make fully informed trading decisions. If the indictment were limited to that kind of “right to control” or information‑control theory, the defendants say, it would run headlong into Ciminelli: the Supreme Court held that federal fraud statutes protect traditional property rights, not a mere right to valuable economic information. Thus, the defendants urge, the indictment fails to allege the deprivation of money or property and must be dismissed.

Ciminelli and the invalidated right‑to‑control theory
Ciminelli is the touchstone for this part of the analysis. In that case, the government prosecuted a defendant under a theory that he had schemed to deprive a state agency of the right to control the disposition of public funds by using a lobbyist to steer contracts. The Supreme Court rejected that “right‑to‑control” theory because it framed the victim’s “property” as the right to valuable economic information and control, which the Court held is not a traditional, protected property interest under the federal fraud statutes. The upshot: an indictment that alleges only that a defendant deprived someone of information necessary to make discretionary economic decisions cannot sustain a fraud conviction under §§ 1341/1343/1348.

The fraudulent‑inducement theory after Kousisis
Kousisis clarifies the distinction between an invalid right‑to‑control charge and a valid fraudulent‑inducement charge. Under the fraudulent‑inducement theory, a defendant makes a material misrepresentation to trick a victim into a transaction that requires the victim to hand over money or property. The key is that the misrepresentation induces the transfer of money or property—what is protected is the money or property that the victim parts with, not a mere informational right. Kousisis also rejects the notion that fraud requires the defendant to have intended to leave the victim worse off; it is enough that the defendant sought to obtain the victim’s money or property by deception.

Applying the frameworks to the indictment’s allegations
Read against these precedents, the indictment here does not rest on a right‑to‑control theory. It alleges a pump‑and‑dump scheme in which defendants, having purchased shares, posted false and misleading social‑media statements designed to induce followers to purchase the targeted securities. Those induced purchases are alleged to be the mechanism by which the followers parted with money or transferred property (cash in exchange for shares), after which the defendants sold into the inflated market and profited—$114 million, the indictment claims. This is the paradigmatic fraudulent‑inducement allegation: misrepresentations caused followers to enter into securities purchases that required them to hand over money or property, and obtaining that money/property was the object of the scheme.

Why this is not Ciminelli in disguise
Two features separate the indictment from the Ciminelli‑type theory that the Supreme Court rejected. First, Ciminelli’s problem was that the government attempted to treat a victim’s informational control as the victim’s property; here, the indictment treats the followers’ purchases—their parting with money or transfer of property—as the target of the deception. Second, Kousisis expressly distinguishes fraudulent inducement from a right‑to‑control theory: fraudulent inducement protects the money or property the victim transfers as a result of the deception. Because the indictment alleges that the defendants’ misrepresentations induced purchases (and that defendants profited when they sold), it pleads deprivation of a traditional property interest rather than mere withholding of information.

Conclusion: the indictment adequately alleges a scheme to deprive victims of money/property
For pleading purposes the court must accept the indictment’s allegations as true and determine whether the indictment alleges each material element of the charged offense. Here the indictment alleges material misrepresentations, an intent to induce purchases, and the obtainment of money/property through those induced purchases. Those allegations fit the fraudulent‑inducement theory validated in Kousisis and avoid the non‑actionable right‑to‑control theory struck down in Ciminelli. Accordingly, the Fifth Circuit concluded the indictment sufficiently alleges a scheme to defraud that targets money or property and thereby survives the defendants’ motion to dismiss on this ground.
