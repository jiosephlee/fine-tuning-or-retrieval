Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

Chapter 6: # Disposition and Significance

## Holding: reversal and remand
The Fifth Circuit reversed the district court’s dismissal and remanded the case for further proceedings. The court held that the superseding indictment adequately alleged both a “scheme to defraud” and an “intent to defraud” under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. Key to that conclusion was the indictment’s allegation that defendants posted materially false and misleading statements on social media to induce their followers to purchase securities, and that defendants then sold those securities for profit. Because those allegations describe fraudulent inducement that caused victims to part with money or property, the indictment pleaded a protected property interest and satisfied the elements of securities fraud sufficiently to survive a facial challenge.

## What proceeds on remand
On remand the criminal prosecution resumes in the district court; the dismissal is vacated and the indictment stands. Practically, that means the case will proceed to normal pretrial criminal proceedings (e.g., arraignment if not already done, motions, discovery disputes, potential plea negotiations, and, if necessary, trial). The Fifth Circuit’s opinion resolves the legal sufficiency question at the pleading stage, not the factual merits; the defendants retain all other defenses and may still contest materiality, causation, mens rea, or proof at trial.

## Charging guidance: avoid a right‑to‑control framing; plead fraudulent inducement to obtain money/property
A central lesson from the opinion is the distinction between the right‑to‑control theory rejected in Ciminelli and the fraudulent‑inducement theory endorsed by Kousisis. Prosecutors should avoid framing allegations as depriving victims merely of “valuable economic information” or the nebulous “right to control” assets; Ciminelli bars convictions based solely on that theory because the right to information is not a traditional property interest. Instead, § 1348 allegations should be structured to show that the defendant used material misrepresentations to induce victims to part with money or property—for example, by alleging that victims were tricked into purchasing securities. Pleadings should clearly identify (1) the misrepresentations, (2) how those statements induced victims to transfer money or property (such as purchasing shares), and (3) the defendant’s objective of obtaining money or property (e.g., proceeds from secret sales).

## Practical import for social‑media pump‑and‑dump cases
The opinion makes clear that traditional securities‑fraud theories extend to schemes that operate through social media. When a social‑media influencer posts false claims about a stock and followers buy based on those claims, the subsequent transfer of money (payment for securities) can be the property interest protected by § 1348. Thus, allegations that influencers “pumped” a stock through false posts and then “dumped” their holdings can, if pleaded with sufficient specificity, state a federal securities‑fraud offense. In practice, prosecutors should focus on tying particular posts to particular purchases and on alleging the influencer’s deceptive intent and personal financial gain; defense counsel may respond by challenging the causal link, materiality, and proof of intent.

## Doctrinal takeaway: intent to cause net loss is not required post‑Kousisis
A doctrinally significant point reiterated in this opinion is that, after Kousisis, the fraud statutes do not require proof that the defendant intended to leave the victim economically worse off. The relevant intent is an intent to deceive and an intent that the deception result in the transfer of money or property to the defendant (or as an object of the scheme). Thus, a defendant who obtains money or property by inducing a purchase through materially false statements can be guilty of securities fraud even if the victim receives the promised good or service or is not left with a net pecuniary loss. This undermines arguments that a scheme cannot be fraud because the victims technically received market‑value securities or because the defendant’s aim was profit rather than injuring investors.

## Concluding note
United States v. Constantinescu clarifies how post‑Ciminelli and Kousisis doctrines apply in the social‑media era: prosecutors may charge social‑media driven “pump and dump” schemes under § 1348 by pleading fraudulent inducement to obtain money or property, and courts should reject attempts to recast such allegations as mere deprivation of information. The Fifth Circuit’s decision disposes only of the pleading sufficiency issue; factual and affirmative defenses remain for resolution at trial or on further motion practice.
