Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Constantinescu

Chapter 1: # Case Background and Charges

This chapter sets out who the defendants are, the government’s theory of the alleged social‑media “pump and dump,” the specific kinds of misrepresentations the indictment alleges, the scale of the alleged fraud, and the federal criminal counts the government brought. Understanding these factual and charging allegations is essential to following the court’s later analysis of whether the indictment states an offense under the federal securities‑fraud statutes.

## Defendants’ identities and large social‑media followings

The superseding indictment names eight defendants: Edward Constantinescu, Perry “PJ” Matlock, John Rybarczyk, Gary Deel, Stefan Hrvatin, Tom Cooperman, Mitchell Hennessey, and Daniel Knight. The opinion emphasizes that each defendant had a substantial social‑media audience across multiple platforms and regularly posted about trading activity. The indictment portrays them not as private, anonymous traders but as public influencers who cultivated credibility as skilled stock traders and used their online audiences to communicate about securities and trading decisions. That public presence is central to the government’s theory because it is the means by which the alleged scheme would reach (and persuade) investors.

## Alleged pump‑and‑dump mechanics: buy, post false/misleading statements to induce purchases, secretly sell

The government’s core factual theory is a classic pump‑and‑dump adapted to social media. According to the indictment, the defendants would first acquire shares of a targeted security. They would then “pump” its market price by posting false or misleading statements about the security to their social‑media followers, creating increased demand and driving the price upward. Once the price had been inflated by the buying activity their posts generated, the defendants would “dump” their positions by secretly selling the shares for a profit. The alleged scheme thus depends on a sequence of acquisition, deceptive publicity to induce other investors to buy, and secret liquidation to realize gains before the artificially inflated price collapsed.

## Nature of the misrepresentations: trading positions and potential price of securities

The indictment alleges the misrepresentations took the form of false or misleading public posts concerning the defendants’ own trading positions and the prospects or potential price movements of the targeted securities. In other words, the alleged statements misrepresented what the defendants held or how successful a trade would be (or would appear to be), and they conveyed misleading information about the likelihood that the security’s price would rise. The opinion treats these allegations as allegations of material misrepresentations intended to deceive followers into making purchasing decisions they otherwise might not have made.

## Alleged profits from the scheme (approximately $114 million)

The indictment quantifies the alleged proceeds: the defendants are accused of profiting roughly $114 million from the scheme. That figure appears in the opinion as evidence of the scale and commercial nature of the alleged fraud. The size of the alleged profits is relevant both to the government’s theory that the defendants intended to obtain money and to the court’s assessment of whether the indictment alleged a scheme to obtain money or property (as opposed to merely depriving followers of non‑property information).

## Counts charged: conspiracy to commit securities fraud (18 U.S.C. § 1349) and substantive securities fraud (18 U.S.C. §§ 1348 & 2)

The superseding indictment charges the defendants with (1) conspiracy to commit securities fraud, in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 1349, and (2) multiple substantive counts of securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348, with liability for aiding and abetting under 18 U.S.C. § 2. Section 1348 criminalizes schemes to defraud in connection with any security and schemes to obtain money or property by false or fraudulent pretenses, representations, or promises in connection with the purchase or sale of a security. Section 1349 makes it a crime to conspire to commit an offense under the securities fraud statute, and § 2 provides for liability for those who intentionally aid, abet, counsel, command, induce, or procure the commission of the charged offenses. The indictment therefore alleges both an agreement among defendants to carry out the scheme (the conspiracy count) and the underlying fraudulent acts—false and misleading statements aimed at inducing followers to purchase securities—constituting substantive securities fraud (and, for some defendants, aiding and abetting).

Procedurally, the opinion notes that Knight pleaded guilty, while the remaining defendants moved to dismiss the indictment. The district court dismissed the indictment on the ground that it failed to allege deprivation of a traditional property interest, prompting the government’s appeal. The Fifth Circuit’s later analysis turns on whether the indictment’s allegations—viewed as true at this stage—adequately plead a scheme and intent to defraud under the statutes cited.
