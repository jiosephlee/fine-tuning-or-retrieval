Title: Blog about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

# Damages on a House of Cards: $166 Million Lump Sum Vacated After JMOL Reversal

## Why this case matters

When a jury awarded Finesse Wireless a $166.3 million lump-sum royalty for two patents covering methods to mitigate passive intermodulation (PIM) in radios, the award looked like a big win for a patent-holding plaintiff asserting complex wireless-technology claims. The Federal Circuit’s September 24, 2025 opinion in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC, however, shows how quickly a damages verdict built on an undifferentiated, portfolio-style or feature-based lump sum can collapse when liability fails. The court reversed JMOL-denials of noninfringement for all asserted claims of both patents and vacated the life-of-patent lump-sum damages award. The opinion underscores two interrelated lessons: (1) damages awards that rest on a single, large-number lump-sum tied to multiple patents or product features are especially vulnerable if the evidence of infringement is weak on essential claim elements; and (2) careful, claim-by-claim, feature-by-feature linkage between the asserted claim elements and the damages model is indispensable.

## What the Federal Circuit held

The Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement for both the ’134 and ’775 patents and vacated the damages award. The court’s substantive rulings turned on the sufficiency of Finesse’s infringement evidence.

For the ’134 patent—which requires that a receiver “over-sampl[e] a passband of received signals” that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals”—the court concluded that Finesse’s expert testimony was internally inconsistent and, crucially, failed to show the accused Nokia radios actually sampled the claimed “interference generating signals.” Finesse’s expert repeatedly identified a “modeled PIM path” in Nokia documents as the interference-generating signal, but the evidence showed that the modeled PIM path was generated after the receiver’s ADC sampling stage. The expert’s shifting testimony—later attempting to treat the two inputs x1 and x2 as the claimed signals—was ambiguous and contradicted earlier mappings. The panel found that where the patentee bears the burden of proof, contradictory expert testimony cannot supply substantial evidence of infringement.

For the ’775 patent—which claims generating intermodulation cancellation signals by digitally multiplying “three signals S1, S2 and S3” in seven specified third-order combinations—the court concluded that the Nokia documents and the expert’s trial testimony established at most three distinct multiplications (and possibly ten listed in a separate Nokia document), but did not support the contention that the seven specific claimed multiplications were performed by the accused radios. The expert had not explained how the three (or ten) multiplications mapped onto the seven claimed products, and the documents described modulus and complex-conjugate operations that did not straightforwardly show the seven claimed multiplications. Thus, the court held the jury’s verdict of infringement was not supported by substantial evidence.

Because the court reversed noninfringement JMOL denials for all asserted claims, it vacated the damages award rather than remand on damages—there was no longer a valid infringement basis to sustain the life-of-patent lump sum.

## Why the court vacated rather than remanded

The panel did not simply remand the damages issue for recalculation; it vacated the award. That outcome follows directly from the appellate rulings: damages are remedial and depend on proven liability. If, as here, liability (infringement) has been reversed for all asserted claims, there is no legal basis on which to sustain an award of damages tied to those claims. The Federal Circuit therefore vacated the judgment as to damages and taxed costs to the defendants.

Put differently: remand on damages generally makes sense where some liability remains or where the appellate court alters only a portion of the liability findings. When the appellate court concludes that liability itself collapses across the asserted set of claims, nothing remains to support the jury’s damages calculation, so vacatur rather than recalculation is the predictable and doctrinally correct result.

## Doctrinal implications — proof, apportionment, and lump sums

Finesse reinforces several doctrinal points now familiar in modern patent litigation, and it sharpens certain practical boundaries.

First, the decision is a reminder that the Federal Circuit will demand coherent, substantive expert linkage between asserted claim elements and the accused product. Courts will not credit a damages or infringement story that depends on an expert’s shifting or internally inconsistent testimony. Johns Hopkins and other precedents make clear that where an expert’s opinion is the patentee’s proof, it must be consistent, well-founded, and tethered to the record.

Second, the case highlights the fragility of portfolio-wide lump-sum awards and feature-based lump sums when the plaintiff’s infringement proof does not survive careful scrutiny. A lump-sum, life-of-patent number can obscure the necessary mapping from individual claims (and specific claim limitations) to the value or value increment provided by the accused products or features. When that mapping fails because key claim elements are not proved, the whole structure can fall.

Third, the decision illustrates the continuing importance of apportionment. Even where infringement is established, a lump sum that aggregates across patents, features, or an undefined market must still satisfy basic apportionment principles: the royalty must be tied to the value that the patented invention contributes, not to the entire value of a multi-feature product. That principle cuts both ways—if an infringement verdict relies on only some claims or features, damages experts must show how the awarded sum corresponds to those claims/features, and cannot rely on an undifferentiated portfolio premium.

## Downstream effects for damages strategy

Practitioners—both plaintiffs and defendants—should absorb the practical lessons this opinion drives home.

For plaintiffs and their damages experts:
- Tie damages to proven claim elements. The damages model should include discrete pathways that connect specific asserted claim limitations to discrete product features and revenues. If a claim requires a particular internal signal path or a particular multiplication, the expert should show (with documents, code, or clear expert testing) that the accused product uses that exact path or operation and that the feature is economically meaningful.
- Prepare alternative damages scenarios. Present a primary model rooted in the strongest infringement theory, but also show fall-back models that apportion value by claim, feature, or market segment so that if some claims are lost, a robust residual model survives.
- Use clear, documentary mappings. Relying on a proprietary vendor document is fine, but experts must explain, in non-contradictory terms, how the document maps to claim language. If a document shows intermediate-model signals generated after sampling, the expert must reconcile that with patent requirements (or concede noninfringement for that theory).
- Avoid single, undifferentiated lump sums without a claim-by-claim and feature-by-feature bridge to revenue. Juries may award big numbers on the basis of persuasive narratives; the record must sustain those narratives on appeal.

For defendants:
- Challenge the linkage early and often. Motions in limine, Daubert challenges, and targeted cross-examination on the mapping between claimed elements and product operation can create the record for JMOL or JMOL alternatives if experts’ testimony is internally inconsistent.
- Attack apportionment and nexus directly. Even a sizable lump-sum award may be vulnerable if the plaintiff cannot show the patented technology drove the market or commanded a segregable portion of the product’s value.

For courts and juries:
- Be attentive to expert clarity. As the Federal Circuit said, when the patentee rests its case on expert testimony that is internally inconsistent, reasonable jurors cannot rely on it as substantial evidence. Trial judges should be willing to require clarity at the time of trial, both in testimony and in the underlying demonstratives and documents.

## How to structure damages evidence to survive a liability setback

If you represent a patentee who expects liability challenges, consider these concrete structural steps when building the damages case:

- Build modular damages models that can be recomputed at varying levels of liability. For example, produce damages spreadsheets keyed to (a) each asserted claim, (b) each accused product line, and (c) each accused radio feature (e.g., PIM cancellation on/off, inner vs. outer band). Make these independent so they survive whether the jury finds infringement of all, some, or none of the claims.

- Provide claim-feature charts that every damages witness can rely upon. Create explicit claim-to-feature charts that link each asserted limitation to a tangible product component, software module, or operational mode. Use the same exhibit across technical and damages experts to avoid inconsistent testimony.

- Quantify incremental value and nexus. Tie the patented feature to customer demand, willingness to pay, or cost savings. Use market evidence (marketing materials, product advisories, RFPs) and competitive comparisons to show the claimed feature had an observable effect on sales or pricing.

- Offer apportionment analyses grounded in economics and market reality. Avoid purely proportional or “because it’s a core function” arguments. Use discrete comparables, hypothetical-license baselines, or component-level profit/cost evidence where possible.

- Present alternate royalty structures that are defensible in the absence of full liability: running royalties by unit or per-feature, component-only lump sums for only those devices proved to infringe, or short-term lump sums for limited remaining life. Each alternative should be anchored in the Georgia-Pacific factors or equivalent negotiation-driven logic.

- Vet and harmonize expert testimony in advance. Ensure technical and damages experts use the same mappings and underlying documents. Cross-examine extensively in deposition to flush out any ambiguities before trial.

## Conclusion

Finesse Wireless is a cautionary tale for patentees who rely on an all-or-nothing lump-sum damages narrative without an ironclad, internally consistent evidentiary bridge from claim elements to product features and value. The Federal Circuit’s reversal and vacatur turned on failures of proof at the element level—confusing expert mappings and a lack of demonstrated performance of the claimed multiplications. The practical takeaway is straightforward: build damages models that are modular, tightly linked to proven claim elements, and capable of surviving partial or total reversals of liability. Lump sums can be persuasive to juries; they are perilous on appeal unless the record makes crystal clear why a single number equals the economic value of the patented contributions the jury has actually found.
