Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

Chapter 11: # Comparative Case Law: Substantial Evidence and Expert Testimony in the Federal Circuit

This chapter uses the Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC, and a sequence of companion decisions, to explore how the appellate court treats the sufficiency of trial evidence in patent cases where expert testimony is essential to map claim limitations onto accused products or processes. Finesse, a 2025 Federal Circuit decision, reversed the denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacated a damages award because the plaintiff’s case rested on equivocal, internally inconsistent expert testimony and on documentary materials that did not, as a matter of record, show the required element-by-element operations. That outcome is a useful vehicle for extracting doctrine about: (1) the standards of review applicable to JMOL and substantial-evidence challenges in patent trials; (2) the particular dangers posed by self-contradictory or speculative expert testimony; (3) how prosecution history amendments and statements can — but do not always — limit claim scope by clear and unmistakable disclaimer; and (4) the recurring appellate pattern of reversal when plaintiffs fail to prove each claim limitation with adequate precision at trial.

The remainder of the chapter situates Finesse alongside key authorities—Wi-LAN/Apple decisions, Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope, and Continental Circuits v. Intel—and draws practical and doctrinal lessons for litigators, experts, and judges.

## Apple v. Wi-LAN: substantial evidence review of infringement and damages

Two related sets of decisions supply the Federal Circuit’s approach to appellate review of factual findings in patent trials. When this court reviews a jury’s factual rulings—most prominently whether a jury could reasonably have found infringement or a particular damages amount—it asks whether those findings are supported by substantial evidence. Substantial evidence means such relevant evidence as a reasonable mind might accept as adequate to support the conclusion; put differently, whether a reasonable jury, viewing all the evidence presented at trial, could have returned the verdict reached.

In practical terms for patent cases, this standard carries three implications:

- The plaintiff must produce enough evidence at trial, and that evidence must be such that reasonable jurors could conclude each claim limitation is met. Documentary evidence, demonstratives, and expert testimony all count, but the evidence must be enough that a jury could reasonably connect the dots.
- The Federal Circuit will not weigh credibility anew on appeal; it will consider whether, given the evidence actually presented, a reasonable jury could have done what the jury did. But that standard is not toothless. Where the only testimony supporting infringement is speculative, internally inconsistent, or unmoored from the record, the finding may fail the substantial-evidence test.
- For damages, the same substantial-evidence rule applies to underlying factual findings (for example, that a particular licensing theory was adopted by the hypothetical negotiators), though legal conclusions (e.g., the proper measure of damages) are reviewed de novo.

Finesse illustrates these points: although a jury found infringement and awarded a large lump-sum damages figure for the life of two patents, the Federal Circuit reversed because the evidence—principally the plaintiff’s expert testimony and a set of Nokia technical documents—did not permit a reasonable jury to find the asserted claim limitations were met. The lesson is clear: substantial evidence review is forgiving of reasonable inferences, but it requires that the inferences be tethered to evidence actually introduced at trial.

## Wi-LAN v. Apple: regional-circuit JMOL standards and Federal Circuit deference

A distinct but related layer of review concerns JMOL (judgment as a matter of law, also called JNOV). The Federal Circuit applies the law of the regional circuit governing JMOL motions. In Finesse the Fifth Circuit’s standard controlled: when considering a motion for JMOL, the district court must view the evidence in the light most favorable to the non-moving party (the verdict winner), and the appellate court will reverse denial of JMOL only if the evidence points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that reasonable jurors could not have arrived at a contrary conclusion.

Two interacting points follow from this regional-circuit rule:

- The district court’s denial of JMOL is reviewed with the same deference embodied in the regional-circuit standard; the Federal Circuit asks whether, viewing the evidence as the district court was supposed to view it, the denial was legally defensible.
- That deference does not insulate fundamentally unsupported verdicts. If the record contains only equivocal, contradictory, or post-hoc explanations for how claim elements map onto the accused product, an appellate court can conclude that no reasonable jury could find for the patentee.

In practice, then, litigators must remember that the JMOL inquiry is not an invitation to reweigh credibility—the court must still ask whether the totality of the admissible evidence could have supported a reasonable jury verdict. Finesse demonstrates that, when the only plausible reading of the expert record is internally inconsistent (for example, the expert repeatedly says an accused signal is both generated before and after the receiver’s analog-to-digital converter), a reversal is appropriate because the evidence does not point in the way required by the standard.

## Johns Hopkins v. Datascope: effect of contradictory expert testimony

Johns Hopkins University v. Datascope Corp. (543 F.3d 1342 (Fed. Cir. 2008)) is one of the Federal Circuit’s most frequently cited authorities on the limits of expert testimony as a basis for a jury’s verdict. In Johns Hopkins the expert’s testimony was internally inconsistent about how an accused medical device contacted a blood vessel—testifying at different times that the device contacted the vessel in either three dimensions or in only two places. The court held that the expert’s contradictory testimony could not, as a matter of law, support a finding that the device literally met a claim limitation requiring three-dimensional contact.

Johns Hopkins establishes a practical rule: when an expert’s testimony is the centerpiece of a patentee’s mapping of claim elements onto an accused device, internal inconsistency in that testimony can defeat the sufficiency of the evidence. The Federal Circuit will not assume the jury resolved the inconsistency in favor of the patentee if the record shows the inconsistency is fundamental to whether a claim limitation is satisfied.

Finesse applies Johns Hopkins directly. There, the plaintiff’s expert (Dr. Wells) repeatedly identified particular signals in a Nokia schematic as (i) the “signals of interest” and (ii) the “interference generating signals.” On cross-examination, Appellants showed the modeled PIM path that the expert had identified as the interference-generating signal was generated downstream of the RF analog-to-digital converter (RF ADC)—i.e., after sampling—so it could not be something the RF ADC had sampled, as the claim required. The expert then equivocated and pointed to two signal labels (x1 and x2) as “the two signals” on the path, but never clearly mapped x1 and x2 onto the claimed terms. The Federal Circuit treated this change of footing as the type of contradictory and unclear evidence Johns Hopkins condemns: the patentee had the burden of proof and rested it on an expert whose testimony was internally inconsistent and insufficiently precise to support an element-by-element finding of infringement. As in Johns Hopkins, the appellate court reversed for lack of substantial evidence.

Doctrine distilled from Johns Hopkins and demonstrated in Finesse:

- An expert may testify to mapping claim limitations to accused structures, but the testimony must be consistent, comprehensible, and sufficiently precise that a reasonable jury could find for the patentee without resorting to speculation.
- If an expert’s core mapping is contradicted by other record evidence (for example, timing or signal-flow relationships shown in schematics or admitted at trial), and the expert cannot reconcile the contradiction clearly, the testimony will not sustain a verdict.
- Ambiguous references to diagrams or legends—without contemporaneous explanation linking labels on a diagram to the claim language—are fragile forms of proof.

## Continental Circuits v. Intel: disavowal/disclaimer standards and scope control

Continental Circuits LLC v. Intel Corp., 915 F.3d 788 (Fed. Cir. 2019), addresses how prosecution history can limit claim scope by disavowal. The Continental Circuits decision reiterates a familiar principle: for prosecution statements to produce a narrowing of claim scope (disclaimer), the patentee must make clear and unmistakable statements during prosecution that would lead a reasonable competitor to conclude that the patentee surrendered claim scope.

Continental Circuits sets a demanding standard for disavowal:

- An amendment or argument that narrows claim language for reasons of patentability does not automatically constitute a clear and unmistakable disavowal of broader claim scope. The examiner’s rejection and the patentee’s response must be read in context.
- The patentee’s statements must be sufficiently specific to demonstrate an intention to confine the claim to a particular meaning; general arguments that distinguish prior art on a particular ground—e.g., that a reference addresses a different problem such as active rather than passive interference—will not ordinarily produce a binding disclaimer of a claim feature unless phrased with the requisite clarity.

Finesse applied this doctrine in the following way: during prosecution the patentee amended claims to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3” and argued against a prior reference (Filipovic) that used two signals. Appellants argued Finesse had disclaimed two-signal embodiments; the Federal Circuit concluded the prosecution history did not contain the sort of clear and unmistakable surrender required by Continental Circuits. Instead, the amendment and accompanying arguments were reasonably read as addressing a patentability distinction (passive vs. active intermodulation) rather than as a clear disclaimer of any claim scope that might encompass two non-unique inputs that are separately identifiable in the claim sense.

Two practical takeaways from Continental Circuits and Finesse:

- Amendments that add limitations can invite an inference of narrowed scope, but courts will not infer forfeiture or disavowal lightly. The inquiry focuses on what the patentee actually said, and whether that communication shows an unmistakable intent to surrender particular subject matter.
- Conversely, patentees should be careful in prosecution statements. If a patentee intends to keep claim scope broad, it is safer to present distinguishing arguments in a way that does not suggest a categorical surrender of alternatives.

## Themes: the all-elements rule, evidentiary precision, and the dangers of post-hoc rationalization

Several themes thread these decisions together and inform litigation practice.

The all-elements rule and the burden of proof. The Federal Circuit repeatedly emphasizes that infringement requires proof that the accused product or process meets every claim limitation, whether literally or under the doctrine of equivalents. Because infringement is a question of fact when disputed, a jury may find infringement based on direct or circumstantial evidence, but the plaintiff bears the ultimate burden to prove each limitation. This “all-elements” requirement is not a formalistic checklist; it is the practical obligation to provide fact-based support—experimental data, contemporaneous documents, or consistent expert explanation—that a factfinder could accept without speculative leaps.

Evidentiary precision. Expert testimony often provides the crucial bridge between complex technical materials and claim language. But that bridge must be stable. Courts demand that experts do more than assert mappings; they must explain how and why particular components or operations correspond to the claim terms. When claimed elements involve temporal or causal relationships (as in Finesse, where the claim required sampling a passband that includes certain signals), experts must reconcile timing shown in accused-device schematics with the claim’s temporal language. Failure to do so—or testimony that reflects confusion about where signals are generated or sampled—will leave the record wanting.

The dangers of post-hoc rationalization. A recurring appellate fault line is reliance on theories developed or clarified only on appeal. Appellate courts will not credit a new mapping or explanation that was not presented to the jury. In Finesse the plaintiff attempted to shore up its case on appeal by offering a clearer mapping of the three multiplications required by the claim to multiplications listed in a Nokia document. Because that mapping was not presented to the jury, the appellate court would not accept it as support for the jury verdict. The basic rule is that appellate courts review the record as made at trial; strategies and explanations that did not exist then cannot be used to cure evidentiary insufficiency on appeal.

Concrete implications of these themes:

- Plaintiffs should ensure their experts present direct, non-contradictory mappings at trial, supported by demonstratives and, if possible, corroborating engineering documents or test data.
- Defendants should focus on exposing temporal or causal contradictions between claimed operations and the accused product’s design—differences in where something is generated vs. where something is sampled are classic, potent attacks.
- Trial courts should police expert testimony for clarity and consider precluding testimony that is speculative or that fails to provide an adequate factual basis to permit reasonable jurors to draw the asserted inferences.

## Patterns in reversals arising from missing or speculative element-by-element proof

Surveying the Federal Circuit’s decisions over the past two decades reveals predictable patterns where appellate reversal follows a jury verdict.

Pattern 1 — reliance on a single, equivocal expert. When the plaintiff’s case depends heavily on one expert and that expert’s testimony is inconsistent, conclusory, or flatly contradicted by contemporaneous documents, the appellate court often concludes that no reasonable jury could have found infringement. Johns Hopkins and Finesse exemplify this pattern.

Pattern 2 — failure to map each claim limitation to concrete operations or structures. A patentee may show that a device generally performs a function, but if it does not show how the device performs every claimed sub-step (especially when the claim enumerates discrete sub-steps), the evidence will be deemed insufficient. In Finesse the claims required that the radio sample a passband containing both signals of interest and interference-generating signals; the record did not permit a clear mapping of both labels to signals actually sampled by the RF ADC.

Pattern 3 — acceptance of ambiguous schematics without contemporaneous explanation. Technical diagrams are helpful, but legends and abbreviated notations can be misleading unless an expert clearly ties labels and signal flows in the diagram to the claimed elements. The Finesse expert’s reliance on a diagram legend that labeled two items together (“DL (TX) reference & modeled PIM path”) without a reliable explanation of how those labels corresponded to claimed, distinct signals was fatal.

Pattern 4 — post-hoc mappings on appeal. Appeals courts will not retrofit trial records with arguments made for the first time on appeal. Thus, litigants who rely on strategic obfuscation at trial cannot later rescue their positions by advanced mapping on appeal.

Pattern 5 — prosecution history pitfalls. Where a patentee’s prosecution statements plainly and unmistakably limit claim scope, courts will hold the patentee to those statements. But where the patentee’s arguments were contextualized to distinguish a reference on a different ground (e.g., active vs. passive IMPs), courts will not read a disavowal into the record without a clear and unmistakable statement. Finesse demonstrates that adding a limitation during prosecution (e.g., “three signals S1, S2 and S3”) does not necessarily mean the patentee intended to surrender all embodiments that could be read to involve only two unique physical signals.

Practical checklist to reduce appellate risk

- For each claim element, require the expert to prepare a demonstrative that (a) quotes the claim limitation, (b) shows the accused product’s component(s) or signal(s), and (c) explains, with citations to documents or test data, why the component or operation meets the limitation.
- If a claim requires temporal or causal order (e.g., sampling happens before processing), ensure the expert addresses timing expressly, with reference to architecture diagrams and signal flow.
- Anticipate cross-examination points that show the diagram’s labels are generated at different places/times and prepare reconciliations grounded in evidence (not supposition).
- Avoid relying only on legends or unexplained labels in technical documents; corroborate with source code, firmware, design documents, or test results where feasible.
- Preserve any necessary alternative mappings at trial; do not reserve the key explanation for appellate briefing.

## Conclusion

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T illustrates the boundaries of permissible proof of infringement in patent trials. The Federal Circuit’s reversal underscores that substantial-evidence review is meaningful: it protects defendants from jury verdicts grounded in speculative or self-contradictory expert testimony and from plaintiffs’ post-hoc theories advanced only on appeal. Johns Hopkins and Continental Circuits supply complementary guardrails—Johns Hopkins on the limits of inconsistent expert testimony, and Continental Circuits on when prosecution history narrows claim scope.

For practitioners, the message is practical and stark: win (and keep) infringement verdicts by presenting clear, contemporaneous, element-by-element evidence at trial. For judges and jurors, the decisions remind us that technical complexity cannot be used as a cover for ambiguity; the more complicated the subject matter, the more precise the evidentiary foundation must be.
