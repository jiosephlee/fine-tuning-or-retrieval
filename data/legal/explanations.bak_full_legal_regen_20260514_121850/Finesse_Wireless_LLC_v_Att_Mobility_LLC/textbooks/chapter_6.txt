Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC

Chapter 6: # Case Study Setup: Finesse Wireless v. AT&T & Nokia

This chapter situates the Federal Circuit’s decision in Finesse Wireless LLC v. AT&T Mobility LLC and Nokia of America Corp. within the litigation timeline and highlights the patents, the accused functionality, the trial outcome, and the post-trial and appellate issues. The opinion provides a useful vehicle to study how trial proof—particularly expert testimony tied to vendor documents—interacts with Rule 50 motions for judgment as a matter of law (JMOL), the appellate standards of review for JMOL denials, and related claim‑scope questions that were argued but not reached on appeal.

## The patents-in-suit: ’134 (sampling and cancellation pipeline) and ’775 (seven multiplications of three signals)

The litigation involved two patents that address passive intermodulation (PIM) interference in radio systems. The ’134 patent claims a signal-processing pipeline that begins by “over-sampling, at a desired frequency, a passband of received signals to create a bit stream” that includes both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals,” isolates those two categories with decimating filters, computes an estimate of the intermodulation products produced by the interference-generating signals, and cancels those estimated intermodulation products, including closed-loop phase and amplitude adjustments using sub-sample phase shifts. The technical problem is familiar: transmit signals can mix (when encountering passive defects or obstacles) to generate new frequencies—PIM—that may fall inband of desired receive signals; the claimed method samples a passband that contains both desired and interference‑generating energy, models the interfering products, and cancels them in the digital domain.

Claim 1 of the ’134 patent thus places at its front end a fact‑intensive limitation: the receiver must sample a passband that contains both “signals of interest” and “interference generating signals.” At trial Finesse’s infringement theory depended on identifying particular signals in Nokia technical documents and tying those signals to the claim terms. That mapping—and whether the accused receiver actually samples the claimed interference‑generating signals—proved pivotal on appeal.

The ’775 patent addresses cancellation of passive IMPs in a co‑located transmitter/receiver arrangement using a power‑series description of a nonlinear process. The representative claim requires “generating intermodulation product (IMP) cancellation signals (ICSs) . . . using copies of transmitter signals” and—critically—generating an n‑th order ICS by digitally multiplying three signals S1, S2, and S3 to produce seven distinct third‑order products (S1×S1×S2, S1×S2×S2, S1×S2×S3, etc.). During prosecution the claims were amended to recite “three signals S1, S2 and S3,” and the district court construed those three signals to be “separately identifiable” but not necessarily “unique.” The appellate dispute turned on whether the accused radios in fact use three separately identifiable signals and whether the system performs the seven claimed multiplications.

Both patents thus turned on technical mappings: (1) whether the receiver samples the claimed mix of signals in the ’134 pipeline, and (2) whether the accused implementation performs the seven third‑order multiplications from three inputs required by the ’775 claim language. These are typical issues in software/hardware patent cases: translating vendor documentation and expert testimony into concrete proof that accused products meet each claim limitation.

## Accused functionality: Nokia’s PIM‑C feature used in AT&T’s network

The accused functionality was a PIM cancellation (PIM‑C) feature implemented in certain Nokia radios deployed in AT&T’s network. The parties introduced internal Nokia technical documents showing signal flows and mathematical operations performed by the accused implementation. Key components of that documentation included a receiver analog‑to‑digital converter (RF ADC), downstream FPGA processing blocks (including what Nokia labeled a “modeled PIM path” and processing in a GROOT FPGA), and blocks performing modulus and complex‑conjugate operations and multiplications related to modeled cancellation signals.

Finesse relied on a Nokia diagram that placed a “DL (TX) reference” and a “modeled PIM path” on a diagrammed red path leading toward the RF ADC. Finesse’s expert, Dr. Wells, initially testified that the RF ADC samples both the “signals of interest” and the “interference generating signals.” Appellants pointed out—and repeatedly questioned at trial—that the Nokia documentation indicated the “modeled PIM path” is a digitally generated signal created downstream in FPGA logic after the RF ADC, so it could not be sampled by the RF ADC. That temporal/sequencing fact—whether a putative interference‑generating signal exists at the RF ADC input at sampling time—was decisive for the ’134 claim: the claim requires that the RF ADC sample a passband that contains the interference generating signals.

For the ’775 patent, the Nokia documents showed certain multiplications, some involving complex conjugates or moduli (e.g., x1⋅|x1|⋅|x1|, x1⋅x1⋅x2', x1⋅|x2|⋅|x2|), and other documentation listed a sequence of coefficients or polynomial terms. Finesse’s infringement theory equated the Nokia listed multiplications or polynomial coefficients with the seven third‑order products claimed by the ’775 patent. The defense emphasized that the Nokia documents disclosed only three distinct multiplications in the relevant portion, and that there was no evidence the asserted system actually executed the seven distinct multiplications required by the claim language.

In short, the accused functionality was not in dispute as deployed in AT&T’s network; the contested matter was how the internal signals in Nokia’s implementation corresponded, in time and kind, to the limitations recited in the patents.

## Jury verdict: liability and lump-sum damages award

After a bench and jury trial in the Eastern District of Texas (Judge J. Rodney Gilstrap presiding), the jury found all asserted claims of both patents valid and infringed. The asserted claims included claims 1–3 of the ’134 patent and a set of claims from the ’775 patent (claims 1, 4, 9, 16, 21, 29, and 36). The jury then awarded a lump‑sum, reasonable‑royalty damages award of $166,303,391 for the remaining life of the patents.

The damages phase was a single, lumped royalty calculation for the asserted patents rather than a running royalty or disgorgement. Finesse’s damages theory therefore depended on proving a reasonable royalty base and royalty rate and connecting the value of the patents to the Nokia PIM‑C feature’s contribution to the accused products’ value. The district court entered judgment on the jury verdict, and the defendants filed post‑trial motions seeking JMOL of noninfringement, JMOL on damages, and a new trial on damages.

The jury’s liability findings and substantial damages award are the touchstone for appellate review: when a patent plaintiff wins a jury verdict based largely on expert testimony and vendor documentation, appellate courts scrutinize whether the evidentiary foundation is legally sufficient to permit reasonable jurors to conclude infringement and to sustain the damages award.

## Post-trial motions: JMOLs and motion for new trial

Following the verdict, defendants filed Rule 50(b) motions for judgment as a matter of law and Rule 59 motions for a new trial. The defendants asked the district court to enter JMOL of noninfringement for both patents, arguing that Finesse had failed to meet its burden of proof on critical limitations: for the ’134 patent, that the receiver samples a passband including the interference‑generating signals; and for the ’775 patent, that the accused radios perform the seven required third‑order multiplications of three signals. Defendants also sought JMOL and/or a new trial on damages, challenging the sufficiency and admissibility of evidence supporting the jury’s lump‑sum award.

The district court denied the motions. On the ’134 patent the court concluded the jury had a factual basis to find that the RF ADC sampled the relevant signals (crediting inferences drawn from the expert testimony and Nokia’s documentation). For the ’775 patent the court accepted the district construction that S1, S2, and S3 need be “separately identifiable,” not necessarily unique, and concluded the record permitted a jury to find infringement by mapping Nokia’s listed operations to the claimed multiplications. The court also denied relief on damages.

These interlocutory denials preserved the defendants’ right to appeal because a Rule 50(b) JMOL motion is a condition precedent to raising JMOL grounds on appeal; the defendants renewed their JMOL arguments in this procedural posture and then pursued appeal to the Federal Circuit after final judgment. The defense also preserved challenges to the damages award via the district court motions and the appellate record.

## Appellate issues: noninfringement JMOL, damages/new trial, and constructions not reached

On appeal the parties preserved multiple issues, and the Federal Circuit narrowed its decision to those properly before it and supported by the appellate record. The main issues the court resolved were: (1) whether the district court erred in denying JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’134 patent; and (2) whether the district court erred in denying JMOL of noninfringement for the asserted claims of the ’775 patent. Because the Federal Circuit concluded infringement was not supported by substantial evidence for both patents, it reversed the district court’s denial of JMOL of noninfringement and vacated the damages award.

Doctrinally, the opinion is instructive on several points that students should note. First, the standard of review for a denial of JMOL is governed by the regional circuit’s law; here the Federal Circuit applied Fifth Circuit standards, reviewing the denial de novo but reversing only where, viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the verdict, the evidence “points so strongly and overwhelmingly in favor of one party that reasonable jurors could not arrive at any contrary conclusion.” The Federal Circuit also reviews factual findings for substantial evidence and claim constructions de novo (with underlying factual findings supporting constructions for clear error). These standards shaped how the court treated the trial record.

Second, the court emphasized the plaintiff’s burden to prove each claim limitation and focused on the sufficiency of the evidentiary support—particularly expert testimony tied to corporate documents. On the ’134 claim the court found the plaintiff’s expert testimony internally inconsistent and inconclusive as to whether the RF ADC actually samples the modeled PIM path. The expert repeatedly identified the “modeled PIM path” as the interference‑generating signals and simultaneously relied on a diagram legend that grouped the DL(TX) reference and modeled PIM path together; but the vendor documents and the expert placed the modeled PIM path as generated downstream of the RF ADC. The court concluded that when a patentee’s entire case rests on such self‑contradictory testimony, it may not constitute substantial evidence supporting the jury’s verdict; it cited precedent holding that internally inconsistent expert testimony may be insufficient as a matter of law to satisfy the plaintiff’s burden.

On the ’775 patent the court handled two discrete legal contentions. Defendants argued Finesse had surrendered claim scope over two‑signal implementations during prosecution and therefore could not now assert claims against an implementation that used only two signals. The Federal Circuit applied the familiar rule that prosecution disclaimer or surrender must be “clear and unmistakable” to narrow claim scope; it concluded that the prosecution history and amendment to recite “three signals” did not rise to such a clear and unambiguous disavowal and so did not bar Finesse’s theory that three “separately identifiable” signals might include copies of signals or signals that are not unique. However, even accepting the district court’s construction that the signals need only be separately identifiable, the court found no substantial evidence that the accused radios actually performed the seven claimed multiplications. The Nokia documents cited at trial listed only three distinct multiplications and involved modulus and complex‑conjugate operations; there was no evidence the particular seven third‑order terms claimed were performed or repeated in the manner required by the claims, and Finesse’s appellate clarifications were impermissible post‑hoc reconstructions of the trial record. As a result, the Federal Circuit reversed the denial of JMOL for the ’775 claims as well.

The Federal Circuit declined to reach other disputed constructions that had been briefed (for example, constructions of “receiver” or “co‑located”) because its reversal on noninfringement obviated the need to resolve those issues. That practice—deciding only what is necessary to resolve the appeal—is a reminder that appellate courts often dispose of cases on narrower record-supported grounds rather than reach all disputed claim‑construction or contention issues when they are not outcome‑determinative.

Finally, because the court reversed JMOL for noninfringement, it vacated the damages award as moot; without liability, there can be no damages. The court therefore did not resolve the separate damages/new‑trial arguments substantively.

## Jurisdictional path to the Federal Circuit

The procedural posture is straightforward and exemplifies the route by which patent appeals reach the Federal Circuit. The case was tried in the United States District Court for the Eastern District of Texas (Judge Gilstrap). After final judgment—entry of a judgment incorporating the jury’s verdict—the defendants timely appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit. The Federal Circuit’s jurisdiction over appeals from final decisions in patent cases is statutory, under 28 U.S.C. § 1295(a)(1).

Because JMOL was properly preserved by filing Rule 50 motions at the district court stage (Rule 50(a) at the close of evidence and Rule 50(b) after verdict), the defendants were able to press JMOL arguments on appeal. The Federal Circuit applied the regional (Fifth Circuit) standard for reviewing denials of JMOL but applied its own precedent for patent‑specific questions (substantial evidence for infringement findings, de novo review for claim construction with clear‑error review of underlying factual determinations). The opinion illustrates the interplay between regional procedural standards and Federal Circuit patent doctrine.

Conclusion

Finesse Wireless v. AT&T & Nokia is a paradigmatic example of how an infringement case that relies heavily on expert testimony tied to vendor documentation can founder if the mapping between documents, expert opinion, and claim language is internally inconsistent or insufficiently developed for a jury. The Federal Circuit’s reversal demonstrates the appellate court’s willingness to grant JMOL where, even accepting the jury verdict, the evidentiary record does not contain substantial evidence to support infringement of particular claim limitations. For students, the case is an instructive study of (1) how claim elements must be proved with coherent, non‑contradictory evidence; (2) how prosecution history affects claim scope (and the high bar for disavowal); and (3) how appellate standards of review apply when district courts deny JMOL and damage challenges.
