Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

Chapter 1: # The Legal Architecture of Premium-Financed Policy Cancellations in Louisiana

Louisiana’s statutory scheme for cancellations initiated by premium finance companies (also called premium finance agreements, finance companies, or premium financiers) crafts a precise, stepwise procedure. The controlling statute — La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) — authorizes a premium finance company that has advanced an insurer’s premium to exercise a power of attorney to request cancellation of the underlying insurance policy when the insured defaults on the finance agreement. Because the cancellation decision affects coverage (often for liability policies where third parties may be injured), Louisiana courts insist on strict adherence to the statute’s procedures: a procedural defect in the finance company’s process can render the cancellation ineffective and leave coverage in force. The Fifth Circuit’s recent decision in Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, 24-30646 (5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025), applies these principles to modern electronic transmission and helps define two recurring interpretive questions: (1) what does it mean for the finance company to “certify” the statutory elements, and (2) what constitutes the insurer’s “receipt” of the finance company’s notice so as to trigger permissible cancellation. This chapter explains the statutory architecture, the courts’ insistence on strict compliance, how Williams interpreted two ambiguities, and what consequence follows when the statutory sequence is not followed.

## Actors and Instruments: Who Does What, and How

Four actors and one instrument dominate this legal landscape.

- The insured: the policyholder who purchases insurance but uses a premium finance company to pay the insurer up front in exchange for installment payments to the finance company.
- The insurer: the insurance company that issued the policy and that will effectuate policy cancellation when proper statutory prerequisites are met.
- The premium finance company (finance company or APFC): the entity that pays the insurer the full premium and acquires a contractual right — typically by written finance agreement — to cancel the policy upon an insured’s default.
- Interested third parties and holders of security interests: entities (e.g., lenders, governmental agencies, or others identified in the finance agreement) who must be given notice of cancellation if the statute requires it.
- The instrument: the premium finance agreement, which normally contains (i) the finance terms, (ii) an express power of attorney authorizing the finance company to cancel the policy upon default, and (iii) the names and addresses of any parties who must be notified upon cancellation.

The finance agreement’s power-of-attorney clause is the crucial enabling document. Under § 9:3550(G), the finance company’s authority to effect cancellation derives from that power of attorney, but statutory mechanics — notice to the insured, certification to the insurer, and transmittal to interested parties — must be followed in the order and form the statute requires.

## The Statutory Framework: La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) and Related Provisions

La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) creates an exception to the general rule that insurers issue cancellations themselves: when an insured finances a premium, the finance company may request cancellation on the company’s own certification that specified conditions exist. Section 9:3550(G)(3)(a) (as applied in Williams) requires the finance company to send the insurer “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four discrete elements:

(i) the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as required by the subsection;  
(ii) the finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured;  
(iii) upon default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as required by the subsection, and that the notice specified the date of sending by the premium finance company to the insured; and  
(iv) copies of the notice were sent to all persons shown by the finance agreement to have an interest in any loss thereunder, specifying the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, security-holders, or third parties to whom notice was sent.

Once the insurer has “receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement,” § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) authorizes the insurer to consider that cancellation has been requested and to proceed to cancel the policy. The statute thus distributes duties among the parties: the finance company must effect the preliminary notices and certify compliance; the insurer may then cancel after it receives the certification.

These specialized provisions sit alongside Louisiana’s more general cancellation statutes (for example La. Rev. Stat. § 22:1266 and related provisions regulating cancellation of certain kinds of insurance), which impose independent notice and timing obligations on insurers. The premium-finance scheme interlocks with the general rules by assuring the insured receives a statutorily mandated cure period (often ten days) before cancellation becomes effective.

## The Statutory Sequence and the Timeline

The statutory process follows a sequential and time-sensitive path designed to (1) fix the date of cancellation with precision and (2) guarantee a minimal cure period during which the insured may avoid cancellation.

1. Default. The insured fails to make an installment payment due under the premium finance agreement. That uncured default is a precondition to exercise of the power of attorney to request cancellation.

2. Ten-day Notice to the Insured (the cure notice). The finance company must send — contemporaneously with the uncured default — a written notice to the insured warning that the policy will be cancelled unless payment is made within the statutorily specified cure period (commonly ten days). The statute requires the notice to specify the date of sending.

3. Notice to Interested Parties. The finance company must also send copies of that cancellation notice to all persons identified in the finance agreement as having an interest in the insured’s coverage (for example, lienholders, named agencies). The statute requires the finance company to identify the names and addresses of these recipients.

4. Certification to the Insurer. After the cure period has run without payment, the finance company must send the insurer a “copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the four elements set out above in § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv). The timing of this certification matters: it must reflect that the notice to the insured was sent and that the default was not timely cured.

5. Receipt by the Insurer; Cancellation. Once the insurer receives the finance company’s notice and certification, the insurer “shall consider that cancellation has been requested” and may proceed to cancel the policy. Receipt is the statutory trigger for insurer action.

The statute thus provides both a substantive right to cancel for the finance company (when the power of attorney and default exist) and a procedural protocol that places an unmistakable emphasis on documented transmission of notice and a fixed period for cure.

## The Four Certification Elements — What Must Be Communicated

Because the statute conditions the finance company’s power to effectuate cancellation on the content of its certification, attorneys and practitioners should think of § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) as a checklist that must be reflected in the certification delivered to the insurer:

1. Valid Power of Attorney. The certification must affirm that the finance agreement contains the statutory power-of-attorney language. That power enables the finance company to act on the insured’s behalf to request cancellation.

2. Uncured Default. The certification must state that the finance agreement is in default and that the default has not been timely cured within the statutorily provided cure period.

3. Ten-Day Notice to the Insured with Date of Sending. The certification must state that upon default, the finance company sent the insured the required notice of cancellation with the date of sending specified. This is essential to fix the start of the cure period and to avoid disputes about whether the insured had the statutory opportunity to cure.

4. Copies to Interested Parties. The certification must represent that copies of the ten-day notice were sent to persons identified in the finance agreement as having interests, and it should list their names and addresses (including any governmental agencies and holders of security interests).

Failure to communicate any of these four elements in the certification may be fatal; Louisiana courts have repeatedly emphasized that the statute’s text is mandatory and that any defect in the required process can invalidate the cancellation.

## Triggering Cancellation: What “Upon Receipt” Means

A recurring litigation point is the meaning of the statute’s phrase that the insurer may cancel “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement” sent by the finance company. Two interpretive issues flow from that phrase: (1) whether “receipt” requires physical possession by a human employee, and (2) whether the statutory “certifying” requires a signed, individualized signature.

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance addressed both questions.

- Receipt. The Fifth Circuit — guided by ordinary dictionary meanings — treated “receipt” as the act of receiving, particularly by taking physical possession, custody, or delivery, and concluded that modern, automated means of transmittal can satisfy the statute’s receipt requirement. In Williams, APAC’s system automatically e‑mailed a copy of the cancellation notice and certification to a GoAuto web service. GoAuto’s computer system, acting as a “listener” (a web service always “listening” for calls), received the e‑mail and automatically effected cancellation in the early morning hours. The Fifth Circuit held that the insurer’s computer taking custody of the transmitted notice is enough to constitute “receipt.” The court observed: statutes are enacted in a modern world in which companies use automated systems to perform human tasks; the statutory requirement of “receipt” should not be read to require a human signature or a human physically opening a mailed envelope. Electronic delivery and a demonstrable electronic record of delivery (e.g., e‑mail received by the insurer’s server or web-service logs showing the call and the date/time) will satisfy the receipt element so long as the insurer’s system actually takes possession or custody of the communication.

- Certifying and Signature. The plaintiffs in Williams argued that “certifying” necessarily imports an obligation that a named individual sign the certification before cancellation may be effected. The Fifth Circuit declined to graft a signature requirement onto the statute, explaining that the text requires only that the finance company — as an entity — certify the listed elements, and Louisiana jurisprudence had not mandated an individualized signature. While a few intermediate appellate opinions had criticized unsigned notices for lacking indicia of authoritativeness, no controlling Louisiana precedent required a signature. The Fifth Circuit therefore held that a finance company may “certify” by an unsigned statement on the company’s letterhead (or by an automated certification transmitted by email) so long as the content of the statutory certification is present. The court warned courts should not add requirements beyond the statute’s text; strict adherence means adhering to the procedures spelled out, not inventing additional formalities.

Two caveats follow from Williams. First, absence of a signature is not automatically harmless in every case — if an otherwise uncertified transmission lacks the four statutory elements or is ambiguous, the absence of a signature might be probative of infirmity. Benitez v. Elsayed (an intermediate appellate opinion) criticized a notice that included neither a named preparer nor a signature as failing to “purport to ‘certify’” the statements contained therein; but Benitez stopped short of holding the absence of a signature alone invalidated cancellation. Second, while automation is permitted, documentation of the chain of transmission is essential: the finance company and insurer should be able to produce electronic logs, timestamps, and copies to prove both the sending and the insurer’s receipt.

## The Doctrine of Strict Adherence

Louisiana courts routinely describe the § 9:3550(G) procedure as requiring “strict adherence.” See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993); Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990); Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). That doctrine serves two articulated functions:

1. Fixing the Date and Time of Cancellation. Strict adherence ensures there is an objective, provable date upon which cancellation is requested and, if carried out, becomes effective. This temporal certainty matters for questions such as whether a policy was in force at the time of an accident.

2. Guaranteeing the Cure Period. The statutory process gives the insured a minimum opportunity to cure the default. Strict compliance prevents premature cancellations that would deprive insureds of that legislatively mandated opportunity.

Consistent with these purposes, Louisiana courts have held that defects in the premium finance company’s procedure — for example, failing to send the ten-day notice to the insured, failing to include required dates or recipient lists, or otherwise omitting any of the required elements — will render a cancellation ineffective. Eaglin summarized the result plainly: “any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” 558 So. 2d at 287. Britten emphasized the cure-period protection aspect of the statute. Because the statute is mandatory and protective of an insured’s right to cure, courts will not tolerate procedural shortcuts.

Williams illustrates the constraints of the strict-adherence rule in two ways. First, the Fifth Circuit reiterated that courts should not use the strict-compliance doctrine as a pretext for adding requirements that the legislature did not. The insistence on strictness is backward-looking: it enforces the statute’s precise requirements rather than inventing additional form-based prerequisites (e.g., individualized signatures) absent textual support. Second, the doctrine preserves its protective teeth: if a certification genuinely lacks any of the four required elements, strict adherence will still demand invalidation. Williams did not relax the rule; it clarified its application in the context of electronic transmission and corporate certification.

## Downstream Consequences of an Ineffective Cancellation

When a cancellation effected under a finance company’s power of attorney is held ineffective, the consequences are practical and potentially severe:

- Coverage Continuity. The policy remains in force (subject to its own terms and the insurer’s obligations under law) for the period mistakenly thought cancelled. If an accident or loss occurs in that period, the insurer may be obligated to provide defense and indemnity even though the insurer would have cancelled had proper procedure been followed.

- Third-Party Claims Exposure. Insurers may face claims from injured third parties who reasonably expected the policy to respond. The insurer’s liability is not limited to the insured; it may have to pay judgments or settlements.

- Bad Faith and Tort Liability. Plaintiffs often assert tort claims — bad faith, negligent misrepresentation, or statutory claims for failure to communicate proper cancellation — alleging that the insurer or the finance company misrepresented coverage or failed to act in good faith. If the insurer’s cancellation was defective, plaintiffs may press these supplementary causes of action. Williams arose in the context of such claims; although the district court dismissed them after finding compliance, had the court found a statutory defect, the insurer might have faced attendant tort exposure.

- Coverage Disputes and Litigation Costs. Ineffective cancellations breed litigation over whether coverage existed at particular times, increasing defense costs and exposure for both the insurer and the finance company.

These consequences explain the careful judicial policing of the statutory steps and the need for precise documentation and auditable trails of notice and receipt.

## Applying the Doctrine — A Practical Checklist and Sample Problems

For practitioners and students, it is helpful to reduce the statutory and case law teachings to an application checklist that courts will use when assessing whether a cancellation by a premium finance company was effective:

1. Power of Attorney: Does the premium finance agreement contain the statutorily required power-of-attorney language authorizing cancellation? Is the clause in the finance agreement valid and enforceable?

2. Default and Cure: Was there an actual default under the finance agreement, and did the insured fail to cure within the statutorily required cure period (commonly ten days)? Are there proof of mailings or other evidence showing the date the cure notice was sent?

3. Notice to the Insured: Did the finance company send the ten-day cancellation notice to the insured and specify the date of sending in the certification? Are proof-of-mailing affidavits, tracking numbers, or email logs available?

4. Copies to Interested Parties: Were copies of the notice transmitted to all persons identified in the finance agreement? Did the certification identify their names and addresses?

5. Contents of the Certification: Does the certification sent to the insurer contain the four statutory elements verbatim or in substance?

6. Receipt by the Insurer: Did the insurer actually receive the notice and certification? Is there documentary proof of receipt (e.g., e-mail server logs, web-service transaction records, stamped received copies, or chain-of-custody items)? If electronic transmission was used, does the record show the insurer’s system accepted the transmission prior to cancellation?

7. Timing of Cancellation: Was cancellation effected only after insurer receipt? If an automated system canceled automatically upon receipt by the insurer’s server, is there an electronic record showing when that occurred and that the sequence complied with the statute?

Sample problem (application exercise): An insured defaults on a premium finance agreement. The finance company’s system emails a ten-day notice to the insured on January 1. No payment is received. On January 11 at 3:00 a.m., the finance company’s system automatically calls the insurer’s web service and transmits a certification but no individual signs the notice. The insurer’s web service registers the transaction and an automated process cancels the policy at 3:02 a.m. An individual at the finance company stamps a printed copy at 9:30 a.m. Is the cancellation effective under Louisiana law as interpreted in Williams?

Answer: Under Williams, the cancellation is likely effective if the email to the insured on January 1 is documented (establishing the start of the cure period), the certification transmitted on January 11 included the four statutory elements, and the insurer’s web service actually received the transmission (3:00 a.m.) before the automated cancellation occurred. The absence of an individualized signature does not, by itself, invalidate the certification. But if any of the required elements were missing (e.g., no proof the ten-day notice was actually sent to the insured, no list of interested parties, or failure to demonstrate insurer receipt), strict adherence requires invalidation.

## Operational and Litigation Advice

For finance companies and insurers who wish to minimize disputes, Williams reinforces several pragmatic best practices:

- Maintain contemporaneous, auditable records: email headers, server logs, web-service request/response logs, timestamps, and proof-of-mailing affidavits. Electronic metadata frequently proves both transmittal and receipt.

- Include the four statutory elements explicitly in the certification and, when feasible, attach copies of the notices and mailing proofs to the certification itself.

- Identify recipients: list names and addresses of interested parties and agencies to satisfy § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iv).

- Avoid unnecessary formalities that do not appear in the statute, but do ensure clarity and demonstrable corporate certification (company letterhead and a corporate attestation are useful).

- If relying on automation for transmittal and cancellation, create and preserve logs that show the insurer’s system took possession and the time at which that occurred.

For plaintiffs and defense counsel, litigation should focus on proving or disproving each checklist item. Where an insurer relies on automation, plaintiffs should probe the chain of custody: did the insurer’s system actually accept the transmission prior to cancellation, and is there electronic evidence? When a certification lacks one of the statutorily required elements, courts remain willing to invalidate cancellations.

## Certification of Questions to the State Supreme Court

Federal courts may certify unsettled questions of state law to the state’s highest court when helpful and appropriate. But the Fifth Circuit in Williams declined to certify the questions regarding the meaning of “certifying” and “receipt,” explaining that mere absence of a prior state-court definition is insufficient to warrant certification. See Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997) (federal courts should be chary about certification absent compelling reasons). Williams therefore resolved the interpretive issues for purposes of federal diversity litigation — at least unless the Louisiana Supreme Court later speaks differently.

## Conclusion

Louisiana’s statutory scheme for cancellations by premium finance companies is both precise and protective. The statute grants finance companies the power to request cancellation but requires a specified certification and documented notice sequence to preserve the insured’s cure opportunity and to fix the time of cancellation. The doctrine of strict adherence gives the statute teeth, invalidating cancellations that deviate from the prescribed steps. Williams v. GoAuto Insurance clarifies two contemporary issues: (1) a finance company’s corporate certification need not bear an individualized signature to be effective, and (2) electronic transmission that results in the insurer’s system taking possession satisfies the statutory “receipt” requirement. Williams does not weaken the strict-adherence rule; it merely adapts its application to modern business practices while preserving the statute’s core protective aims. Practitioners should therefore attend carefully to the statutory checklist, preserve contemporaneous records of transmission and receipt, and ensure that the four required certification elements are unmistakably present in any notice sent to the insurer.
