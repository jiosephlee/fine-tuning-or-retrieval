Title: Blog about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

# No Signature Required: The Fifth Circuit’s Textual Approach to “Certifying” Under La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a)

## Why this case matters

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is an important decision for insurers, premium finance companies, and litigants in Louisiana because it answers—at least for the Fifth Circuit—the seemingly small but practically significant question whether a premium finance company’s “certification” that certain prerequisites for cancellation are met must bear a handwritten or otherwise individualized signature before an insurer may treat a financed policy as cancelled. The court’s answer is: no. In short, the Fifth Circuit held that the statute’s use of the verb “certifying” does not, by itself, create a signature requirement; a company-level, unsigned certification communicated electronically can suffice so long as the insurer has “receipt” of the notice.

That holding matters beyond this specific dispute. Louisiana’s statute, La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b), conditions cancellation by a finance company’s exercise of a power of attorney on a “notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” a set of facts. Louisiana appellate courts have long enforced these requirements strictly, treating defects in the process as potentially fatal to cancellation. Against that background, the Fifth Circuit’s ruling—anchored in statutory text, informed by the modern realities of automated business-to-business communications, and cautious about adding requirements not present in the statute—signals a judicial willingness to accommodate automated, corporate attestations so long as the statutory predicates (notice, certification, and receipt) are satisfied. That has immediate operational consequences for how premium finance companies and insurers design cancellation workflows and preserve proof of compliance.

## What the court did and why

The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s cancellation process was defective because the “certification” from the premium finance company, APAC, was not signed until after GoAuto’s systems had already canceled the policy. The Fifth Circuit approached the problem by (1) reviewing the statutory text and Louisiana precedent emphasizing strict compliance, and (2) asking whether the text itself required a contemporaneous, individualized signature.

The court emphasized three points:

- The statute requires “a statement certifying” a set of facts—not expressly a signature. Louisiana decisions recount the requirement of strict adherence, but the existing cases do not uniformly treat a signature as a prerequisite. In particular, the court noted that Benitez v. Elsayed—an opinion that discussed an unsigned notice—did not establish that the absence of a signature alone invalidates cancellation when the statutory elements are otherwise present.

- The schematic reading of the statute places the duty to “certify” on “the insurance premium finance company” as a corporate actor. The court reasoned that a company can “certify” by means other than a named employee’s signature, and courts should not add requirements (like signature mandates) that the legislature did not write into the statute.

- Modern business practices matter. The court accepted that an automated e-mail from APAC’s system to a GoAuto web service constituted “receipt” by the insurer for purposes of La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i), and that a later-stamped, printed signature applied by an APAC employee did not retroactively create the certification required before cancellation. In short, the emailed company-level certification—coupled with the insurer’s receipt via its web service—satisfied the statute.

The court therefore affirmed summary judgment for GoAuto and declined to certify the interpretive question to the Louisiana Supreme Court.

## Analytical perspective: text, context, and institutional competence

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis is straightforward textualism tempered by pragmatic understanding of contemporary commercial operations. The court correctly notes that the statute uses “certifying” without defining the verb to require a signature. Given that omission, the court declines to judicially graft a signature rule onto a statute designed primarily to (a) fix a concrete time/date of cancellation and (b) ensure a minimum cure period for insureds. That is a defensible posture: courts should be cautious about creating formalistic traps not contemplated by the legislature, particularly when companies and insurers routinely rely on automated communications.

That said, the court’s refusal to require a signature does not eliminate the policy reasons recognized by Louisiana courts for strict compliance. Those cases view strict compliance as protective of insureds’ ability to cure defaults and as furnishing a clear evidentiary baseline for when coverage ceases. The Fifth Circuit tries to preserve those values by treating an automated transmission to an insurer’s web service as sufficient “receipt.” But that approach depends heavily on the practicalities and auditability of the communications: a baffling, untraceable corporate email would not carry the same force as a time-stamped, auditable electronic message with accompanying proof of mailing or automated delivery logs. The court implicitly recognizes this, by anchoring its conclusion in the record description of APAC’s systems and how they interact with GoAuto’s web service.

One might worry, however, about potential perverse incentives. If a signature is not required and “receipt” can occur via automated messages, finance companies and insurers might be tempted to rely on minimal or poorly preserved logs—inviting disputes over whether the certification was properly and timely sent, and whether copies and affidavits of mailing were really made available. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion preserves these evidentiary battles for another day, but it also signals judicial receptivity to automation so long as parties can show reliable systems and records.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Several doctrinal and practical consequences follow from Williams:

- Greater judicial acceptance of corporate-level attestations. Courts in the Fifth Circuit and perhaps elsewhere may be less inclined to require individualized signatures where the statute looks to a corporate actor’s certification. That aligns with broader trends recognizing corporate agents and automated communications as legally effective.

- Emphasis on demonstrable “receipt.” By adopting a functional definition of “receipt” (possession, custody, delivery) and crediting machine-to-machine transmissions, Williams highlights the importance of timestamped system logs, delivery receipts, and web-service records. Insurers and finance companies should be prepared to present technical evidence showing when messages were generated, transmitted, and processed.

- Potential narrowing of Benitez’s signal. Benitez noted deficiencies where a notice was unsigned and unidentified; Williams distinguishes Benitez and treats it as not dispositive. Future litigants citing Benitez to challenge cancellations based on unsigned notices will face a more skeptical audience if the record shows reliable electronic communication and company-level certification.

- Pressure on plaintiffs’ case strategies. Plaintiffs asserting ineffective cancellation must focus not only on the presence or absence of a signature but on the substantive proofs: that the finance company did not send the notices required under paragraph (2), that the certification statements are false, or that the insurer did not actually receive the certification. The court’s holding makes technical signature challenges less likely to succeed where modern automated systems demonstrably transmitted the necessary notices.

- Legislative and regulatory space for clarification. The decision may prompt lawmakers or regulators to revisit La. R.S. 9:3550(G) if they prefer a bright-line signature rule or specific formalities (for example, requiring an affidavit identifying a particular company agent). Absent such legislative action, courts will continue to adapt the statute to evolving business practices.

## Practical takeaways for industry and counsel

Although the Fifth Circuit dispenses with a categorical signature requirement, it places a premium on records and verifiable processes. Premium finance companies and insurers should treat Williams as an invitation to tighten and document procedures:

- Preserve electronic logs showing the generation, transmission, delivery, and processing of cancellation requests (including timestamps, message IDs, and web-service call traces).

- Retain proofs of mailing and affidavits (where applicable) and make them readily available; the GoAuto opinion repeatedly references such proofs as relevant to satisfying the statute.

- Where possible, identify an affiant or custodian of records who can authenticate the electronic systems and attest that the company’s automated messages operate as represented.

- Consider adding an explicit signature block (whether electronic or automated) on certifications to remove doubt in close cases, even if the law does not require it. A simple machine-generated “signed by [entity] at [timestamp]” statement tied to an auditable system log will reduce litigation risk.

- Insurers should ensure their systems are configured to capture and preserve the moment of “receipt” in a reliable, reconstructable form. If an insurer’s web service automatically cancels upon receiving a message, the insurer will want a clear audit trail showing the call and the content of the notice.

## Final assessment

Williams v. GoAuto is a pragmatic, text-grounded opinion that recognizes corporate certifications and automated communications as capable of satisfying Louisiana’s cancellation statute absent an express legislative requirement for individualized signatures. The Fifth Circuit manages a delicate balance: it resists judicially importing formalities not present in the statute while insisting on the kind of certifiable proof—delivery records, system logs, and mailing affidavits—that preserve the statute’s twin protective functions. The decision should calm some operational uncertainties for premium finance companies and insurers, but it also raises the stakes for rigorous record-keeping and authentication. Where automated processes replace human signatories, the quality and availability of system evidence will determine how robustly those processes withstand judicial scrutiny.
