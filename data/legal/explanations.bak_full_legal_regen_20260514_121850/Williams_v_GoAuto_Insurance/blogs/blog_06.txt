Title: Blog about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

## Compliance Playbook After Williams: What Insurers and Premium Finance Companies Should Do Now

Why Williams v. GoAuto Insurance matters
The Fifth Circuit’s October 2025 decision in Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is one of the first federal appellate opinions to squarely confront how modern, electronic workflows square with Louisiana’s strict statutory scheme for cancellations initiated by premium finance companies under La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3). For practitioners who insulate risk by automating cancellations and who defend insurers and finance companies against class actions alleging ineffective cancellation or bad-faith claims, Williams offers a practical roadmap — and a warning. The court reaffirmed that Louisiana requires strict adherence to the statute, but it also made clear that “strict” does not mean “antiquated”: an electronic transmission that puts the insurer in possession (here, an API/web-service call that the insurer’s system receives and that automatically triggers cancellation) satisfies the statute’s “receipt” requirement, and the statute does not itself impose a signature requirement on the financing company’s certification.

That combination — continued fidelity to the statutory elements, alongside recognition that machine-driven receipt can be receipt — will be critical for defending automated cancellation programs and for tightening operational controls to avoid litigation vulnerabilities.

What the court held and why it’s important
The Fifth Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment for GoAuto on the narrow legal question before it: GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3). The court’s reasoning turns on two points:

1. Certification: The statute requires that the premium finance company “certif[y]” certain facts in a notice (the existence of a valid power of attorney, uncured default, that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured with the date, and that copies were sent to interested third parties). The Fifth Circuit declined to read an extra-statutory requirement of an employee signature into “certifying.” Although Louisiana courts historically demand strict compliance with the statute, the Fifth Circuit concluded that a company-level certification in the form sent by APAC — even without an identified, handwritten signature — satisfied the statutory “certifying” requirement.

2. Receipt: The statute conditions cancellation on the insurer’s “receipt” of the cancellation notice and certification. The court adopted ordinary dictionary meanings of “receipt” and held that electronic delivery that causes the insurer’s system to take possession — here, an emailed/API-triggered request that a GoAuto web service receives and that automatically cancels the policy — qualifies as “receipt.” The court emphasized that modern statutes must be read in the context of modern business practices: company computers do what humans once did.

The court also declined to certify unsettled state-law questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court and refused to adopt the district court’s alternative theory (that the APAC employee delivering paper copies qualified as a “private carrier” under the statute).

Operational takeaways — a practical compliance playbook
Williams gives insurers and premium finance companies concrete benchmarks for minimizing litigation risk. If you run, implement, or defend an automated cancellation process in Louisiana, consider these actions now:

1. Make the certification mirror the statute verbatim
- Ensure every cancellation request contains clear, unambiguous language covering each statutory element in La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv): (i) existence of a valid power of attorney, (ii) default and failure to cure, (iii) that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured with the date of sending, and (iv) that copies were sent to all persons shown in the agreement to have an interest (with names/addresses).
- Where feasible, include a reference to the specific statutory subsection to avoid ambiguity about what the certification is intended to do.

2. Preserve reliable electronic transmission and receipt logs
- Treat the API call, email, or web-service request as the operative “sending” and the insurer’s logged receipt as operative “receipt.” Maintain immutable logs (e.g., write-once logs, secure timestamps, or hashed records) that show transmission, delivery, and the insurer system’s processing of the request.
- Save full headers, payloads, and server-side acknowledgements (HTTP response codes, transaction IDs) for each cancellation request. Those logs form the primary defense evidence that the insurer “received” the notice in the statutory sense.

3. Align timestamps and define the effective cancellation time
- Draft internal policies that state whether the policy is cancelled at the moment the insurer’s system receives the cancellation request, when the system processes the request, or at a specific time on the cancellation date. Williams treats electronic receipt leading to automatic cancellation as sufficient; but careful records showing when the web service “heard” the call and when the policy state changed will prevent disputes about whether the insured still had coverage at the moment of an accident.
- Synchronize clocks across systems (NTP) and keep time-zone handling consistent in logs so that timestamps admitted in evidence are reliable and comprehensible.

4. Document sending of the ten‑day notice to the insured
- Williams leaves open numerous avenues for plaintiffs to challenge cancellation effectiveness; chief among them is attacking the ten‑day notice itself. Continue to create and preserve proof of mailing/service for the ten‑day notice to the insured: certified mail receipts, USPS tracking, affidavits of mailing, or electronic delivery receipts if the parties agreed to electronic notice.
- Where notices are mailed, keep the underlying affidavits of mailing or third‑party evidence proximate in the record to the subsequent cancellation request.

5. Document notices to interested third parties
- The statute requires copies of the ten‑day notice be sent to persons “shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest.” That is a frequent litigation focal point. Maintain clear records documenting the names and addresses in the finance agreement and proof that notices were sent to those addresses (and retain those proofs).
- If your system constructs the list of interested parties programmatically, retain the output of that routine at the time of notice generation (so plaintiffs cannot later argue the list was incomplete).

6. Preserve the power-of-attorney documentation
- Keep an auditable copy of the premium finance agreement and the power of attorney the agreement purports to grant. Disputes occasionally focus on whether the power of attorney is valid, expired, or was properly executed.

7. Retain paper/digital artifacts and a chain-of-custody
- Even where cancellations are automated, retain a binder or electronic folder that aggregates (a) the ten‑day notice proof-of-mailing, (b) the finance agreement with POA, (c) the transmitted certification, and (d) the insurer’s receipt logs. Make chain-of-custody and retention policies clear and defensible to avoid spoliation claims.

8. Update contracts, SOPs, and vendor SLAs
- Ensure third-party vendors (including premium finance companies) contractually commit to producing the required statutory content, to retaining and producing logs on short notice, and to indemnifying for failures that create liability.
- Train personnel to understand the statutory elements that must be present in the certification and the operational importance of preserving evidence.

What Williams means for litigation strategy
Williams is an important defensive precedent. If a cancellation is otherwise supported by a certification that tracks the statute and by reliable transmission/receipt logs, class-action claims that hinge on “ineffective” cancellation or on misrepresentation and bad-faith theories are less likely to succeed. The Fifth Circuit’s disposition shows courts will look to the substance of statutory compliance, not demand an extra-statutory signature formality.

But the decision also highlights where plaintiffs will continue to press: procedural defects that the appellate court didn’t find here — and which remain classic weak spots — remain viable pathways for relief.

Likely plaintiff attack vectors that survived Williams
- Ten‑day notice defects: plaintiffs can still challenge whether the ten‑day notice required by La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iii) was actually sent in compliance with the statute (timing, content, or inadequate proof of mailing). Proof-of-mailing affidavits that are inconsistent, missing, or backdated remain potent weapons.
- Incomplete notification of interested third parties: failure to send copies of the ten‑day notice to all persons “shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest” can render a cancellation ineffective. Disputes about the contractually identified parties, or about addresses used, often surface here.
- Power of attorney validity: arguments that the finance agreement does not create the required POA, that it was invalidly executed, or that the POA expired can undermine cancellation authority.
- Timing and causation disputes: even where electronic receipt is admitted, plaintiffs will litigate whether cancellation was effective before an accident (critical in first‑party/UM/UIM contexts) — hence the importance of synchronized, auditable timestamps.
- Human‑process gaps: where a later manual signature stamp occurs after cancellation (as in Williams), plaintiffs may try to argue the sequence evidences a post hoc certification. Williams rejects that theory where the statutory elements and receipt are present, but sloppy or inconsistent records can reopen the issue.

Doctrinal and regulatory implications
Williams navigates between two doctrinal poles. On one hand, Louisiana’s case law demands “strict adherence” to the cancellation statute — an anchor that protects insureds’ opportunity to cure defaults and fixes cancellation timing. On the other hand, Williams resists judicial aggrandizement of the statute into requirements not contained in text (e.g., requiring an identifiable employee signature), reflecting a broader trend of courts accommodating genuine modernization of business processes where statutory goals are preserved.

Practically, the decision signals that state legislatures (and regulators) that care about consumer notice protections should clarify statutory language where they meant to require a human signature, express receipt by a particular person, or specify methods for proof of delivery in the electronic age. Absent that change, courts may continue to treat well‑documented, automated processes as compliant so long as they effect the statute’s core protections: meaningful notice and an opportunity to cure.

Downstream litigation and appellate considerations
Williams is precedential in the Fifth Circuit and persuasive elsewhere — especially in other jurisdictions that read statutory “receipt” and “certify” language in light of electronic transmissions. Defense counsel should cite Williams when defending automated cancellation systems. Plaintiffs’ counsel, conversely, will intensify discovery on the ten‑day notice proof, the content and timing of the certification, and logs showing the insurer’s system state at the precise times of the alleged loss and cancellation.

Finally, Williams illustrates a procedural lesson: the Fifth Circuit refused to certify open state-law questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. So long as federal courts are willing to resolve these issues on the statutory text and ordinary definitions (as the Fifth Circuit did), expect fewer certified questions and more federal appellate precedents shaping the interplay between automation and state statutory protections.

Bottom line for practitioners
Williams affirms that modern, automated cancellation workflows can satisfy La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3) — but only if they are backed by careful adherence to the statute’s four certification elements and by rigorous, contemporaneous documentation of notices, transmissions, and receipt. For insurers and premium finance companies, the safest course is operational and evidentiary discipline: make the certification language unambiguously statutory, log everything immutably, synchronize timestamps, preserve proof-of-mailing for ten‑day notices, and document notices to all listed interested parties. Do that, and Williams becomes a solid defensive precedent rather than a litigation trap.
