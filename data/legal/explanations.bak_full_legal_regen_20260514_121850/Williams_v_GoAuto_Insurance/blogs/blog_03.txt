Title: Blog about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

# Strict Adherence, Modern Methods: Reconciling Eaglin, Britten, and Automated Cancellations

Why Williams v. GoAuto matters is obvious once you step back from the facts: the case asks how a long-settled Louisiana rule of “strict adherence” to statutory cancellation procedures for financed insurance policies fits with the mechanized, near-instant communications that govern insurance operations today. For decades Louisiana courts have treated any defect in a premium finance company’s cancellation process as fatal — a rule designed to fix precise cancellation timing and to protect the insured’s limited cure period. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in Williams v. GoAuto, No. 24-30646 (5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025), affirms a district court judgment for the insurer, but it does so by refusing to import ritualistic formalities (a signature, human receipt) that the statute itself does not require. That balance — preserving strict adherence as a doctrinal backstop while reading the statute in light of modern, automated workflows — is the decision’s most consequential contribution.

What the court held
At the heart of Williams are two disputes that arise out of a common commercial setup: insureds finance premiums through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (APAC), which was authorized by power of attorney to cancel the policies after a default; APAC’s systems generated an automatic e-mail to GoAuto requesting cancellation; GoAuto’s web service, on receiving that electronic request, immediately cancelled the policies; later that morning APAC printed the notice and affixed a signature stamp. The plaintiffs asserted Louisiana’s cancellation statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b)(i), requires both (1) a signed certification from the finance company before cancellation and (2) that the insurer actually receive possession of the written request before action is taken.

The Fifth Circuit rejected both contentions. First, the court concluded that although Louisiana courts require “strict adherence” to the statute, the statute’s command that the finance company “certif[y]” certain facts does not, on its face, impose a signature requirement. The court reviewed relevant Louisiana appellate decisions, found no clear precedent converting “certifying” into a pre-cancellation signing ritual, and cautioned against judicially adding formalities that the legislature did not write. Second, the court adopted a common-sense definition of “receipt”: under ordinary legal and dictionary usage, “receipt” means taking possession, and a computer system that receives an e-mailed cancellation request and triggers an automated cancellation suffices as the insurer’s receipt. Having found GoAuto’s procedures consistent with the statute, the court affirmed summary judgment for GoAuto and declined the plaintiffs’ request to certify questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court.

How the opinion navigates strict adherence
The Williams opinion threads a careful path between two poles. On the one hand, it explicitly acknowledges Louisiana’s long line of cases (Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., Britten v. Reavis, Delatte v. Lemotte) that treat any procedural defect as rendering a cancellation ineffective. Those authorities justify a demanding posture: precision matters because the whole protective purpose of the statute is to fix the cancellation time and preserve a cure window.

On the other hand, the Fifth Circuit resists transforming “strict adherence” into a demand for hyper-formalistic rituals untethered to the statutory text. The court noted that the statute places the duty to “certify” on the finance company, not on a particular employee, and that the statute does not explicitly require a signature or a human-hand-delivery model. Reading the statute with deference to its text, the court accepted electronic transmission and machine-triggered receipt as satisfying both the certification and receipt requirements.

Analytical perspective: formalism, technology, and statutory fidelity
Williams does not repudiate Louisiana’s strict-adherence doctrine; it narrows the universe of what counts as defect. Critically, it substitutes a text-centered formalism for a ritual-centered one. Rather than enforcing formalities that no text requires (e.g., a handwritten or manually affixed signature before the cancellation effective date), the court enforces the statute’s substantive prerequisites — that the finance company certify the listed facts and that the insurer have “receipt” of such a notice.

That approach is defensible: statutory regimes that anticipate commercial actors should be interpreted in ways that accommodate contemporary business practices unless the legislature signals otherwise. The court’s reliance on ordinary dictionary meanings to define “receipt,” and its deference to corporate information systems as agents of receipt, makes practical sense and avoids perverse incentives (e.g., race-to-the-desk stampings) that a signature requirement could create.

At the same time, the decision leaves meaningful teeth in the strict-adherence rule. The court repeatedly emphasizes that it will not “add to” the statutory procedures. That caveat preserves appellate and trial courts’ ability to invalidate cancellations when the finance company fails to provide the statutorily required certification contents, to prove timely mailing to the insured, or to show notice was sent to third parties with interests. The opinion therefore reads strict adherence as demanding compliance with the statute’s substantive, textually specified safeguards, not pro forma rituals.

Where strictness will remain most acute
Practically speaking, Williams points to three doctrinal fault lines where courts will continue to enforce the statute sternly.

First is proof of mailing to the insured. The statutory scheme requires a ten-day notice to the insured and an accompanying certification that such notice was sent, specifying the date. Louisiana precedent treats proof of timely mailing as central to the protective function; absent reliable proof, a cancellation will be vulnerable.

Second is notice to third-party interests — holders of security interests, governmental agencies, and other named parties under the finance agreement. The statute requires the finance company to list these recipients and to show copies were sent. Failures here are classic bases for invalidating cancellations.

Third is the substance of the finance company’s certification. The statute mandates particular facets of the certification (existence of a valid power of attorney, uncured default, sending of ten-day notice, and who else was notified). The Fifth Circuit’s rejection of a signature requirement does not make the contents of the certification fungible; if those recitals are untrue, incomplete, or otherwise deficient, courts applying Eaglin and Britten will remain ready to declare the cancellation ineffective.

Downstream effects and litigation dynamics
Williams will likely produce a couple of predictable responses. Insurers and finance companies will be encouraged to rely more heavily on automated interfaces and web services but will also take greater care to preserve robust, contemporaneous electronic records showing the time-stamped transmission, system receipt, and the certified contents. Plaintiffs’ lawyers will shift tactics toward attacking the evidentiary sufficiency of the certification (mailing affidavits, lists of third-party recipients, accuracy of the power-of-attorney representation) rather than demanding analog signatures.

Two unresolved tensions may continue to spur litigation. First, although the Fifth Circuit declined to endorse the district court’s “private carrier” theory, future litigants may press different theories about what constitutes meaningful receipt when multiple corporate actors share access to shared filing systems. Second, the Fifth Circuit’s refusal to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court means that an authoritative state ruling on signature and receipt issues remains possible; a state high-court decision could recalibrate this federal-court reading.

Conclusion
Williams v. GoAuto preserves the protective core of Louisiana’s cancellation statute while updating the means by which compliance may be shown. The case illustrates an accommodating form of textual formalism: courts will enforce the letter of statutorily prescribed safeguards, but they will not demand extra-textual ceremonies. For practitioners, the lesson is straightforward: automation is acceptable so long as it produces clear, auditable compliance with the statute’s substantive requirements — proof of timely notice to the insured, proper certification content, and notice to affected third parties. Those remain the battlegrounds where strict adherence will live or die.
