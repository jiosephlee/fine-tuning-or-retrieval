Title: Blog about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

# The District Court’s “Private Carrier” Theory That Wasn’t: A Curious Detour the Fifth Circuit Declined to Take

## Why this case matters

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is a reminder of two parallel developments in modern litigation about insured financing and statutory compliance. First, state statutes that allow premium finance companies to cancel insurance policies on an insured’s default are strictly construed; courts have long warned that any defect in the statutory process can render a cancellation ineffective. Second, those statutes were enacted in a pre-digital age, and litigants and judges increasingly must decide whether traditional statutory terms like “mail” and “receipt” encompass contemporary electronic workflows.

The Fifth Circuit’s October 3, 2025, decision in Williams v. GoAuto, No. 24-30646, settled one central question in this case: GoAuto’s automated, system-to-system e-mail/web-service cancellation process satisfied Louisiana’s statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3). But the opinion is also notable for what it declined to do. The district court had sustained GoAuto’s position with a novel theory: the APAC employee who physically handled the paper copy of the cancellation notice qualified as a “private carrier,” so delivery requirements were met. The Fifth Circuit affirmed the judgment on different grounds and expressly declined to adopt or evaluate the district court’s “private carrier” analogies drawn from railroad law. That restraint matters for two reasons. It preserves a preferable textual approach to statutory interpretation in the digital era, and it signals to trial courts and practitioners to avoid overreaching analogies when the statute already speaks directly to electronic transmission.

## How the courts approached the statute

The statute permits a premium finance company to effect cancellation of an insurance policy after an uncured default by “sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” certain enumerated facts. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv). Louisiana decisions insist on “strict adherence” to these requirements because the statutory process fixes the precise moment of cancellation and protects the insured’s right to cure. See Delatte v. Lemotte; Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.; Britten v. Reavis — decisions cited by the Fifth Circuit.

GoAuto’s operational practice was straightforwardly modern: APAC’s system sent an e-mail with a copy of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation and a certification; GoAuto’s web service (a listener) accepted that request and automatically canceled the policy in the early morning hours; later that morning an APAC employee affixed a signature stamp to printed documents and placed them in a shared filing cabinet, and—after 2017—an APAC employee had a GoAuto employee stamp the document as “received.” The plaintiffs argued two defects: (1) the certification was unsigned before the automatic cancellation, and (2) GoAuto never “received” the cancellation request before canceling because human, physical receipt came later.

The Fifth Circuit rejected both objections. On the signature point, the panel noted that the statute requires the premium finance company itself to “certify” certain matters, but the statutory text does not mention a personal signature. Louisiana appellate decisions the court reviewed were inconclusive about whether a signature is a necessary component of a valid certification; one case cited the lack of a signature as a shortcoming but did not hold that signature absence by itself voided cancellation. The Fifth Circuit wrote the sensible—if unremarkable—proposition that courts should not add requirements the statute does not impose: “Strict adherence” does not license judicial supplementation to require a named, hand-signed certification when the text contemplates certification by the finance company.

On “receipt,” the court turned to ordinary meaning. Citing Black’s definitions, the panel construed “receipt” to mean taking “possession, delivery, or custody” and held that the insurer’s automated acceptance of APAC’s electronic cancellation request (via the web service) satisfied that requirement. The GoAuto system’s “listener” received a system-to-system call and performed the cancellation; in the court’s view, modern computers do what people once did, so receipt via electronic transmission was consistent with the statute’s list of permissible methods and with ordinary usage.

## The district court’s private-carrier detour — and why the Fifth Circuit’s avoidance was salutary

The district court had tried a different route. Because the statute lists “depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail” as alternative means of sending the notice, the district court analogized APAC’s later physical handling of the paper documents to delivery “with a private carrier.” To support that conclusion it relied on a loose analogy to a panel opinion about railroads, concluding that the APAC employee who placed the documents in a shared filing area could be treated as a private carrier delivering the notice to GoAuto.

The Fifth Circuit expressly declined to reach that theory. The panel noted the district court’s “imaginative and unconventional” definition of “private carrier” and, sensibly, avoided endorsing an interpretive leap that would have extended the category beyond any obvious or textually supported scope. Instead, the court rested its affirmance on the simpler and more straightforward ground that the e-mail/web-service interaction itself constituted “receipt.”

This refusal to bless the district court’s analogy was the right move. There are two basic reasons. First, the statute enumerates three alternatives — mail, private carrier, and electronic mail — and treats electronic mail as an independent, recognized method. That textual arrangement suggests the legislature intended to cover electronic transmissions directly, not only indirectly via analogies to physical carriers. If a statute expressly contemplates e-mail, there is little reason to import the law of “private carriers” (or railroads) to justify the same result. Second, the district court’s private-carrier theory risked producing arbitrary and fact-bound outcomes: might a hand-delivery by a temporary contractor constitute a private carrier in some cases but not others? Courts should avoid creating ad hoc doctrines when the statutory text supplies a straightforward answer.

The Fifth Circuit’s choice reflects sound interpretive priorities: follow the text first; treat statutory alternatives as meaningful; and be cautious about analogical extensions that could complicate an already strict statutory compliance regime.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Williams v. GoAuto clarifies a few practical and doctrinal points worth flagging for practitioners and judges in Louisiana and beyond.

First, electronic transmissions and automated system-to-system communications can constitute “sending” and the insurer’s “receipt” under statutes that expressly recognize electronic means. The Fifth Circuit’s treatment endorses a plain-meaning, modernized reading that aligns statutory grammar with contemporary commerce. That should reduce friction for insurers and premium finance companies that rely on automated workflows—provided they can document transmission and receipt.

Second, the opinion reiterates that courts will resist judicially augmenting statutory requirements like signature formalities where the text does not demand them. Plaintiffs will be hard-pressed to argue that an unsigned (but company-generated) certification is ineffective absent state precedent imposing such a requirement.

Third, the panel’s refusal to bless the district court’s private-carrier analogy is instructive. Trial courts should be wary of analogizing to doctrinal categories (railroads, common carriers, etc.) when the statute contains an explicit list of means of delivery; such analogies invite complexity and inconsistent results. Likewise, litigants should not expect courts to expand “private carrier” doctrines to accommodate contemporary digital practices when the statute already contemplates e-mail.

Finally, the decision leaves a residual uncertainty that plaintiffs in similar cases may attempt to exploit: how much evidence of electronic transmission and automated receipt is enough to establish “receipt” in fact? The Fifth Circuit accepted GoAuto’s showing about the web service listener and the timing of automatic cancellation, but future cases may turn on the completeness of the record: system logs, time stamps, automated acknowledgement messages, or other metadata will become the evidentiary backbone of these disputes.

## Practical guidance for trial courts and practitioners

For defense counsel representing insurers or finance companies: preserve system logs and metadata showing that an electronic cancellation request was transmitted and accepted, and that it triggered an automated cancellation action. Produce documentation of the certification language that accompanies the electronic notice and evidence tying that certification to the finance company’s corporate action (letterhead, automated headers, affidavit of the systems administrator). Avoid relying on post hoc paper handling or contested “private carrier” theories to explain delivery.

For plaintiffs’ counsel: if you intend to challenge an automated process, develop a record that probes the timing, whether the certification existed in final form at the moment of transmission, whether the automated web service truly “received” the request or simply queued it, and whether any acknowledgement was generated. Where possible, show gaps in logs, nonstandard workflows, or ambiguity about who had custody at critical times. If statutory text is ambiguous in your jurisdiction, consider seeking certification to the state supreme court—but be aware the Fifth Circuit in Williams declined certification as unwarranted here.

For judges: start with the statutory text. If the statute lists electronic mail as an independent method of sending notice, ask whether the legislature’s inclusion was meant to cover automated system-to-system communications. Avoid creating or extending analogues (e.g., defining a contractor as a “private carrier”) when the text provides a discrete, modern mechanism. And, finally, demand a clear evidentiary record about the timing of transmissions and the mechanics of automated systems before deciding whether “receipt” occurred.

## Conclusion

Williams v. GoAuto is notable not for doctrinal fireworks but for judicial modesty. The Fifth Circuit resolved the case by bringing statutory language into the 21st century: electronic transmissions and automated receptions can satisfy statutory “sending” and “receipt.” Equally important, the court declined to bless an elaborate but unnecessary analogical theory advanced by the district court. That restraint preserves textual clarity and gives practitioners a clear roadmap: document your electronic workflows, and don’t rely on creative analogies when the statute plainly contemplates the technology at issue.
