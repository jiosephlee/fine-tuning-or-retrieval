Title: Blog about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

# No Signature Required: The Fifth Circuit’s Textual Approach to “Certifying” Under La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a)

## Why this case matters

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is an important decision for insurers, premium finance companies, and litigants in Louisiana because it answers—at least for the Fifth Circuit—the seemingly small but practically significant question whether a premium finance company’s “certification” that certain prerequisites for cancellation are met must bear a handwritten or otherwise individualized signature before an insurer may treat a financed policy as cancelled. The court’s answer is: no. In short, the Fifth Circuit held that the statute’s use of the verb “certifying” does not, by itself, create a signature requirement; a company-level, unsigned certification communicated electronically can suffice so long as the insurer has “receipt” of the notice.

That holding matters beyond this specific dispute. Louisiana’s statute, La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b), conditions cancellation by a finance company’s exercise of a power of attorney on a “notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” a set of facts. Louisiana appellate courts have long enforced these requirements strictly, treating defects in the process as potentially fatal to cancellation. Against that background, the Fifth Circuit’s ruling—anchored in statutory text, informed by the modern realities of automated business-to-business communications, and cautious about adding requirements not present in the statute—signals a judicial willingness to accommodate automated, corporate attestations so long as the statutory predicates (notice, certification, and receipt) are satisfied. That has immediate operational consequences for how premium finance companies and insurers design cancellation workflows and preserve proof of compliance.

## What the court did and why

The plaintiffs argued that GoAuto’s cancellation process was defective because the “certification” from the premium finance company, APAC, was not signed until after GoAuto’s systems had already canceled the policy. The Fifth Circuit approached the problem by (1) reviewing the statutory text and Louisiana precedent emphasizing strict compliance, and (2) asking whether the text itself required a contemporaneous, individualized signature.

The court emphasized three points:

- The statute requires “a statement certifying” a set of facts—not expressly a signature. Louisiana decisions recount the requirement of strict adherence, but the existing cases do not uniformly treat a signature as a prerequisite. In particular, the court noted that Benitez v. Elsayed—an opinion that discussed an unsigned notice—did not establish that the absence of a signature alone invalidates cancellation when the statutory elements are otherwise present.

- The schematic reading of the statute places the duty to “certify” on “the insurance premium finance company” as a corporate actor. The court reasoned that a company can “certify” by means other than a named employee’s signature, and courts should not add requirements (like signature mandates) that the legislature did not write into the statute.

- Modern business practices matter. The court accepted that an automated e-mail from APAC’s system to a GoAuto web service constituted “receipt” by the insurer for purposes of La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i), and that a later-stamped, printed signature applied by an APAC employee did not retroactively create the certification required before cancellation. In short, the emailed company-level certification—coupled with the insurer’s receipt via its web service—satisfied the statute.

The court therefore affirmed summary judgment for GoAuto and declined to certify the interpretive question to the Louisiana Supreme Court.

## Analytical perspective: text, context, and institutional competence

The Fifth Circuit’s analysis is straightforward textualism tempered by pragmatic understanding of contemporary commercial operations. The court correctly notes that the statute uses “certifying” without defining the verb to require a signature. Given that omission, the court declines to judicially graft a signature rule onto a statute designed primarily to (a) fix a concrete time/date of cancellation and (b) ensure a minimum cure period for insureds. That is a defensible posture: courts should be cautious about creating formalistic traps not contemplated by the legislature, particularly when companies and insurers routinely rely on automated communications.

That said, the court’s refusal to require a signature does not eliminate the policy reasons recognized by Louisiana courts for strict compliance. Those cases view strict compliance as protective of insureds’ ability to cure defaults and as furnishing a clear evidentiary baseline for when coverage ceases. The Fifth Circuit tries to preserve those values by treating an automated transmission to an insurer’s web service as sufficient “receipt.” But that approach depends heavily on the practicalities and auditability of the communications: a baffling, untraceable corporate email would not carry the same force as a time-stamped, auditable electronic message with accompanying proof of mailing or automated delivery logs. The court implicitly recognizes this, by anchoring its conclusion in the record description of APAC’s systems and how they interact with GoAuto’s web service.

One might worry, however, about potential perverse incentives. If a signature is not required and “receipt” can occur via automated messages, finance companies and insurers might be tempted to rely on minimal or poorly preserved logs—inviting disputes over whether the certification was properly and timely sent, and whether copies and affidavits of mailing were really made available. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion preserves these evidentiary battles for another day, but it also signals judicial receptivity to automation so long as parties can show reliable systems and records.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Several doctrinal and practical consequences follow from Williams:

- Greater judicial acceptance of corporate-level attestations. Courts in the Fifth Circuit and perhaps elsewhere may be less inclined to require individualized signatures where the statute looks to a corporate actor’s certification. That aligns with broader trends recognizing corporate agents and automated communications as legally effective.

- Emphasis on demonstrable “receipt.” By adopting a functional definition of “receipt” (possession, custody, delivery) and crediting machine-to-machine transmissions, Williams highlights the importance of timestamped system logs, delivery receipts, and web-service records. Insurers and finance companies should be prepared to present technical evidence showing when messages were generated, transmitted, and processed.

- Potential narrowing of Benitez’s signal. Benitez noted deficiencies where a notice was unsigned and unidentified; Williams distinguishes Benitez and treats it as not dispositive. Future litigants citing Benitez to challenge cancellations based on unsigned notices will face a more skeptical audience if the record shows reliable electronic communication and company-level certification.

- Pressure on plaintiffs’ case strategies. Plaintiffs asserting ineffective cancellation must focus not only on the presence or absence of a signature but on the substantive proofs: that the finance company did not send the notices required under paragraph (2), that the certification statements are false, or that the insurer did not actually receive the certification. The court’s holding makes technical signature challenges less likely to succeed where modern automated systems demonstrably transmitted the necessary notices.

- Legislative and regulatory space for clarification. The decision may prompt lawmakers or regulators to revisit La. R.S. 9:3550(G) if they prefer a bright-line signature rule or specific formalities (for example, requiring an affidavit identifying a particular company agent). Absent such legislative action, courts will continue to adapt the statute to evolving business practices.

## Practical takeaways for industry and counsel

Although the Fifth Circuit dispenses with a categorical signature requirement, it places a premium on records and verifiable processes. Premium finance companies and insurers should treat Williams as an invitation to tighten and document procedures:

- Preserve electronic logs showing the generation, transmission, delivery, and processing of cancellation requests (including timestamps, message IDs, and web-service call traces).

- Retain proofs of mailing and affidavits (where applicable) and make them readily available; the GoAuto opinion repeatedly references such proofs as relevant to satisfying the statute.

- Where possible, identify an affiant or custodian of records who can authenticate the electronic systems and attest that the company’s automated messages operate as represented.

- Consider adding an explicit signature block (whether electronic or automated) on certifications to remove doubt in close cases, even if the law does not require it. A simple machine-generated “signed by [entity] at [timestamp]” statement tied to an auditable system log will reduce litigation risk.

- Insurers should ensure their systems are configured to capture and preserve the moment of “receipt” in a reliable, reconstructable form. If an insurer’s web service automatically cancels upon receiving a message, the insurer will want a clear audit trail showing the call and the content of the notice.

## Final assessment

Williams v. GoAuto is a pragmatic, text-grounded opinion that recognizes corporate certifications and automated communications as capable of satisfying Louisiana’s cancellation statute absent an express legislative requirement for individualized signatures. The Fifth Circuit manages a delicate balance: it resists judicially importing formalities not present in the statute while insisting on the kind of certifiable proof—delivery records, system logs, and mailing affidavits—that preserve the statute’s twin protective functions. The decision should calm some operational uncertainties for premium finance companies and insurers, but it also raises the stakes for rigorous record-keeping and authentication. Where automated processes replace human signatories, the quality and availability of system evidence will determine how robustly those processes withstand judicial scrutiny.



# When Is Notice “Received” in the Digital Age? Machine Receipt and Automatic Cancellation Under Louisiana Premium Finance Law

## Why this case matters

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is an instructive, practical decision about how old statutory language adapts to modern communications. At issue was a narrowly focused § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) question: when may an insurer be said to have “receipt” of a premium-finance company’s cancellation request? The Fifth Circuit held that receipt occurs when an insurer’s automated systems receive an electronic cancellation request — here, an APAC process calling a GoAuto web service and triggering an automatic cancellation — even if no human at the insurer has physically handled a signed paper at that moment. Because many premium finance arrangements, insurers, and third-party servicers now rely on automated exchanges and API calls to process cancellations, this decision has immediate operational and litigation implications for insurers, finance companies, and policyholders in Louisiana and potentially elsewhere.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

The panel began with the statutory scheme: a finance company may exercise a power of attorney to cancel a policy by “sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” certain facts. When the finance company sends such a notice, “[u]pon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement . . . the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested” and “may proceed to cancel” the policy.

Plaintiffs argued the statute required a later, physical receipt — indeed, they emphasized the absence of a signature on the certification until after the automatic cancellation occurred. The Fifth Circuit rejected that argument on two interconnected grounds.

First, the court refused to read a signature requirement into the word “certifying” where the statute does not expressly impose one; Louisiana authorities cited by the court support strict statutory adherence, but not the addition of requirements the statute does not contain. The panel concluded a financing company can “certify” by sending a statutorily sufficient statement even if no individual signs it by name.

Second, and dispositive, the court adopted common-law dictionary definitions of “receipt” (Black’s Law Dictionary) — the act of receiving or taking physical possession — and translated that concept into the electronic age: receipt includes taking “possession, delivery, or custody” of a cancellation request by the insurer’s computer systems. The undisputed operational facts — APAC’s system called a GoAuto web service, the web service accepted the request, and GoAuto’s system automatically cancelled the policy — satisfied the statute’s receipt requirement. The court emphasized that the statute is “a modern statute set in current times” and that companies’ automated systems can do what humans once did. Because the e-mailed/requested cancellation was received by GoAuto’s system prior to human review or stamped signatures, the statutory trigger occurred at that machine-receipt point.

The court also declined to certify the statutory question to the Louisiana Supreme Court and found no need to address the district court’s alternative theory about “private carrier” status.

## An analytical perspective: persuasive, practical, but not unproblematic

The Fifth Circuit’s approach makes practical sense. Statutes that contemplate “sending” or “receipt” were drafted in a world of mail and courier; interpreting “receipt” to exclude an insurer’s automated acceptance of a properly formatted electronic cancellation request risks producing anomalous results that frustrate legislative purpose: to fix a clear time and date for cancellation and give the insured a minimum cure window. If an insurer’s system consistently and predictably processes cancellation requests sent by a finance company's systems, it is sensible to treat the insurer as having “received” the request when its system actually takes custody of the message.

The court’s reliance on Black’s Law Dictionary and on the operational interaction (APAC calling a GoAuto web service) anchors the holding in familiar interpretive techniques — textual definition plus facts showing how the statute operates in practice. The opinion is also careful to avoid expanding the statute by manufacture: it denies a signature requirement not found in the text, even while protecting the statute’s strict-compliance purpose.

That pragmatism, however, raises some delicate doctrinal and evidentiary issues. Louisiana law has long required strict compliance with § 9:3550’s mechanics because any defect can deprive an insured of notice and of the opportunity to cure. Translating “receipt” into the machine context makes the adequacy of cancellation procedures hinge on technical facts that are often opaque to plaintiffs and courts: API logs, message formats, timestamps, server time zones, retries, queueing behavior, and system downtimes. The Fifth Circuit’s ruling is defensible, but it shifts the focus of “strict adherence” from the textual content of the notice and the finance company’s procedural steps to the operational integrity and proof of system-to-system communications.

## Practical consequences and how parties will litigate this going forward

Operationally, the opinion puts a premium on demonstrable machine-level evidence. If receipt occurs when a web service accepts a call, then proving receipt in litigation will typically require production of:

- API call logs showing the incoming request, the request payload (the notice), timestamps, and the recipient service’s response (200 OK or other status).
- Server logs or application logs demonstrating that the insurer’s system parsed and processed the request and that an automatic cancellation action was initiated at a specific timestamp.
- Email headers and message-IDs if cancellation requests are transmitted via email, showing delivery to the insurer’s mailserver and any auto-processing that consumes those messages.
- Audit trails from both the finance company and insurer showing correlation between the request and the cancellation action (e.g., transaction IDs).
- Metadata and time-synchronization information (time zone, NTP accuracy) to preclude disputes about sequencing.

For plaintiffs, the upshot is twofold. First, plaintiffs will likely challenge the sufficiency and authenticity of electronic logs, raising issues about chain-of-custody, system backups, and whether logs were altered. Second, plaintiffs will press the strict-compliance line by demonstrating gaps: missing log entries, delayed processing, system errors, or absence of the statutorily required content in the machine payload (e.g., an explicit “certification” text). Benitez and prior Louisiana precedents remain useful tools for plaintiffs to argue that the presence of every statutory element and a clear human signatory promote certainty for insureds — even if those cases did not categorically require a signature.

For insurers and finance companies, the decision sends a clear compliance roadmap: if you rely on system-to-system cancellations, maintain robust, tamper-evident logs; design your APIs and message contents to mirror statutory requirements (including an explicit “certification” field); ensure redundancy and alerts for failed transmissions; and preserve contemporaneous audit artifacts. An insurer should be able to produce an unbroken chain of electronic evidence showing the receipt and processing of a cancellation request.

Three operational risk areas deserve special attention:

1. Auditability and retention. Logs must be durable, time-stamped, and backed up. Retention policies should match the statute of limitations and expected litigation windows. Immutable logging (WORM storage, digital signatures) will minimize credibility attacks.

2. System downtime and error handling. If an API call is made while an insurer’s web service is down (or returns a transient error), is the policy canceled? The court’s analysis suggests receipt is a condition precedent — not mere sending by the finance company. Parties should implement explicit handshake/acknowledgment patterns: a 2-step exchange where the insurer’s service returns an acknowledged receipt message and only then triggers cancellation, or automatic retry plus a human review if acknowledgments fail.

3. Security and authentication. Who is calling the web service matters. Proper authentication (mutual TLS, API keys), message signing, and unique identifiers reduce disputes about whether the finance company (not a third party) made the cancellation request and whether the request came from the finance company’s valid power of attorney.

## Doctrinal ripple effects and legislative questions

The Fifth Circuit’s decision is likely to be influential in other contexts where statutes use “send” and “receipt” language but predate widespread API-driven processes. Courts elsewhere may adopt a similar machine-receipt approach so long as the statutory text is agnostic about medium. But some courts may resist, emphasizing the protective purpose of strict compliance when individuals’ rights — here, the insured’s right to a cure period — are at stake.

Legislatively, states that want greater certainty could amend premium-finance statutes to explicitly address electronic transmissions: define “receipt” for automated systems, require an acknowledgment protocol, require embedded certification language or electronic signature standards, or set safe-harbor evidentiary rules (e.g., presuming receipt where an insurer’s authenticated web service returns an acknowledgement code or where the insurer’s logs show acceptance within X minutes). Absent statutory clarification, litigation will remain the primary forum for sorting disputes about the sufficiency of machine-to-machine processes.

## Practical recommendations

For insurers and finance companies:
- Adopt explicit, auditable protocols for cancellation requests: include a machine-readable “certification” field matching the statutory text, a unique transaction identifier, and an authenticated request/acknowledgement pattern.
- Preserve and be able to produce API logs, message payloads, server responses, and audit trails with secure retention and tamper-evident storage.
- Build into operations an escalation path for failed acknowledgments or transient errors: retries, human review, and fixed SLAs to avoid unintended cancellations (or failures to cancel).
- Consider contract and policy-language updates to clarify acceptable electronic channels and to allocate responsibility for proving receipt.

For plaintiffs and defense counsel:
- Plaintiffs should target gaps in logs, inconsistencies in timestamps, missing payload fields, and any evidence of system downtime or failed deliveries to undermine a claim of machine receipt.
- Defendants should produce a clear chain of custody for electronic evidence and demonstrate that automatic cancellation was the predictable result of the system-to-system exchange.

## Conclusion

Williams v. GoAuto reflects judicial adaptation of traditional statutory concepts to contemporary automated commerce. The Fifth Circuit’s holding that an insurer’s system can “receive” a cancellation request when it accepts an electronic call is sensible and operationally realistic, but it also spotlights how “strict adherence” to protective statutes now depends on IT architectures and evidentiary practices. For litigants, compliance officers, and regulators, the decision should be a call to harden technical proof and to consider statutory clarification so that “receipt” in the digital age is not only conceptually sound but also provably reliable.



# Strict Adherence, Modern Methods: Reconciling Eaglin, Britten, and Automated Cancellations

Why Williams v. GoAuto matters is obvious once you step back from the facts: the case asks how a long-settled Louisiana rule of “strict adherence” to statutory cancellation procedures for financed insurance policies fits with the mechanized, near-instant communications that govern insurance operations today. For decades Louisiana courts have treated any defect in a premium finance company’s cancellation process as fatal — a rule designed to fix precise cancellation timing and to protect the insured’s limited cure period. The Fifth Circuit’s decision in Williams v. GoAuto, No. 24-30646 (5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025), affirms a district court judgment for the insurer, but it does so by refusing to import ritualistic formalities (a signature, human receipt) that the statute itself does not require. That balance — preserving strict adherence as a doctrinal backstop while reading the statute in light of modern, automated workflows — is the decision’s most consequential contribution.

What the court held
At the heart of Williams are two disputes that arise out of a common commercial setup: insureds finance premiums through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (APAC), which was authorized by power of attorney to cancel the policies after a default; APAC’s systems generated an automatic e-mail to GoAuto requesting cancellation; GoAuto’s web service, on receiving that electronic request, immediately cancelled the policies; later that morning APAC printed the notice and affixed a signature stamp. The plaintiffs asserted Louisiana’s cancellation statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b)(i), requires both (1) a signed certification from the finance company before cancellation and (2) that the insurer actually receive possession of the written request before action is taken.

The Fifth Circuit rejected both contentions. First, the court concluded that although Louisiana courts require “strict adherence” to the statute, the statute’s command that the finance company “certif[y]” certain facts does not, on its face, impose a signature requirement. The court reviewed relevant Louisiana appellate decisions, found no clear precedent converting “certifying” into a pre-cancellation signing ritual, and cautioned against judicially adding formalities that the legislature did not write. Second, the court adopted a common-sense definition of “receipt”: under ordinary legal and dictionary usage, “receipt” means taking possession, and a computer system that receives an e-mailed cancellation request and triggers an automated cancellation suffices as the insurer’s receipt. Having found GoAuto’s procedures consistent with the statute, the court affirmed summary judgment for GoAuto and declined the plaintiffs’ request to certify questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court.

How the opinion navigates strict adherence
The Williams opinion threads a careful path between two poles. On the one hand, it explicitly acknowledges Louisiana’s long line of cases (Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., Britten v. Reavis, Delatte v. Lemotte) that treat any procedural defect as rendering a cancellation ineffective. Those authorities justify a demanding posture: precision matters because the whole protective purpose of the statute is to fix the cancellation time and preserve a cure window.

On the other hand, the Fifth Circuit resists transforming “strict adherence” into a demand for hyper-formalistic rituals untethered to the statutory text. The court noted that the statute places the duty to “certify” on the finance company, not on a particular employee, and that the statute does not explicitly require a signature or a human-hand-delivery model. Reading the statute with deference to its text, the court accepted electronic transmission and machine-triggered receipt as satisfying both the certification and receipt requirements.

Analytical perspective: formalism, technology, and statutory fidelity
Williams does not repudiate Louisiana’s strict-adherence doctrine; it narrows the universe of what counts as defect. Critically, it substitutes a text-centered formalism for a ritual-centered one. Rather than enforcing formalities that no text requires (e.g., a handwritten or manually affixed signature before the cancellation effective date), the court enforces the statute’s substantive prerequisites — that the finance company certify the listed facts and that the insurer have “receipt” of such a notice.

That approach is defensible: statutory regimes that anticipate commercial actors should be interpreted in ways that accommodate contemporary business practices unless the legislature signals otherwise. The court’s reliance on ordinary dictionary meanings to define “receipt,” and its deference to corporate information systems as agents of receipt, makes practical sense and avoids perverse incentives (e.g., race-to-the-desk stampings) that a signature requirement could create.

At the same time, the decision leaves meaningful teeth in the strict-adherence rule. The court repeatedly emphasizes that it will not “add to” the statutory procedures. That caveat preserves appellate and trial courts’ ability to invalidate cancellations when the finance company fails to provide the statutorily required certification contents, to prove timely mailing to the insured, or to show notice was sent to third parties with interests. The opinion therefore reads strict adherence as demanding compliance with the statute’s substantive, textually specified safeguards, not pro forma rituals.

Where strictness will remain most acute
Practically speaking, Williams points to three doctrinal fault lines where courts will continue to enforce the statute sternly.

First is proof of mailing to the insured. The statutory scheme requires a ten-day notice to the insured and an accompanying certification that such notice was sent, specifying the date. Louisiana precedent treats proof of timely mailing as central to the protective function; absent reliable proof, a cancellation will be vulnerable.

Second is notice to third-party interests — holders of security interests, governmental agencies, and other named parties under the finance agreement. The statute requires the finance company to list these recipients and to show copies were sent. Failures here are classic bases for invalidating cancellations.

Third is the substance of the finance company’s certification. The statute mandates particular facets of the certification (existence of a valid power of attorney, uncured default, sending of ten-day notice, and who else was notified). The Fifth Circuit’s rejection of a signature requirement does not make the contents of the certification fungible; if those recitals are untrue, incomplete, or otherwise deficient, courts applying Eaglin and Britten will remain ready to declare the cancellation ineffective.

Downstream effects and litigation dynamics
Williams will likely produce a couple of predictable responses. Insurers and finance companies will be encouraged to rely more heavily on automated interfaces and web services but will also take greater care to preserve robust, contemporaneous electronic records showing the time-stamped transmission, system receipt, and the certified contents. Plaintiffs’ lawyers will shift tactics toward attacking the evidentiary sufficiency of the certification (mailing affidavits, lists of third-party recipients, accuracy of the power-of-attorney representation) rather than demanding analog signatures.

Two unresolved tensions may continue to spur litigation. First, although the Fifth Circuit declined to endorse the district court’s “private carrier” theory, future litigants may press different theories about what constitutes meaningful receipt when multiple corporate actors share access to shared filing systems. Second, the Fifth Circuit’s refusal to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court means that an authoritative state ruling on signature and receipt issues remains possible; a state high-court decision could recalibrate this federal-court reading.

Conclusion
Williams v. GoAuto preserves the protective core of Louisiana’s cancellation statute while updating the means by which compliance may be shown. The case illustrates an accommodating form of textual formalism: courts will enforce the letter of statutorily prescribed safeguards, but they will not demand extra-textual ceremonies. For practitioners, the lesson is straightforward: automation is acceptable so long as it produces clear, auditable compliance with the statute’s substantive requirements — proof of timely notice to the insured, proper certification content, and notice to affected third parties. Those remain the battlegrounds where strict adherence will live or die.



# The District Court’s “Private Carrier” Theory That Wasn’t: A Curious Detour the Fifth Circuit Declined to Take

## Why this case matters

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is a reminder of two parallel developments in modern litigation about insured financing and statutory compliance. First, state statutes that allow premium finance companies to cancel insurance policies on an insured’s default are strictly construed; courts have long warned that any defect in the statutory process can render a cancellation ineffective. Second, those statutes were enacted in a pre-digital age, and litigants and judges increasingly must decide whether traditional statutory terms like “mail” and “receipt” encompass contemporary electronic workflows.

The Fifth Circuit’s October 3, 2025, decision in Williams v. GoAuto, No. 24-30646, settled one central question in this case: GoAuto’s automated, system-to-system e-mail/web-service cancellation process satisfied Louisiana’s statute, La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3). But the opinion is also notable for what it declined to do. The district court had sustained GoAuto’s position with a novel theory: the APAC employee who physically handled the paper copy of the cancellation notice qualified as a “private carrier,” so delivery requirements were met. The Fifth Circuit affirmed the judgment on different grounds and expressly declined to adopt or evaluate the district court’s “private carrier” analogies drawn from railroad law. That restraint matters for two reasons. It preserves a preferable textual approach to statutory interpretation in the digital era, and it signals to trial courts and practitioners to avoid overreaching analogies when the statute already speaks directly to electronic transmission.

## How the courts approached the statute

The statute permits a premium finance company to effect cancellation of an insurance policy after an uncured default by “sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” certain enumerated facts. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv). Louisiana decisions insist on “strict adherence” to these requirements because the statutory process fixes the precise moment of cancellation and protects the insured’s right to cure. See Delatte v. Lemotte; Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co.; Britten v. Reavis — decisions cited by the Fifth Circuit.

GoAuto’s operational practice was straightforwardly modern: APAC’s system sent an e-mail with a copy of the Ten Day Notice of Cancellation and a certification; GoAuto’s web service (a listener) accepted that request and automatically canceled the policy in the early morning hours; later that morning an APAC employee affixed a signature stamp to printed documents and placed them in a shared filing cabinet, and—after 2017—an APAC employee had a GoAuto employee stamp the document as “received.” The plaintiffs argued two defects: (1) the certification was unsigned before the automatic cancellation, and (2) GoAuto never “received” the cancellation request before canceling because human, physical receipt came later.

The Fifth Circuit rejected both objections. On the signature point, the panel noted that the statute requires the premium finance company itself to “certify” certain matters, but the statutory text does not mention a personal signature. Louisiana appellate decisions the court reviewed were inconclusive about whether a signature is a necessary component of a valid certification; one case cited the lack of a signature as a shortcoming but did not hold that signature absence by itself voided cancellation. The Fifth Circuit wrote the sensible—if unremarkable—proposition that courts should not add requirements the statute does not impose: “Strict adherence” does not license judicial supplementation to require a named, hand-signed certification when the text contemplates certification by the finance company.

On “receipt,” the court turned to ordinary meaning. Citing Black’s definitions, the panel construed “receipt” to mean taking “possession, delivery, or custody” and held that the insurer’s automated acceptance of APAC’s electronic cancellation request (via the web service) satisfied that requirement. The GoAuto system’s “listener” received a system-to-system call and performed the cancellation; in the court’s view, modern computers do what people once did, so receipt via electronic transmission was consistent with the statute’s list of permissible methods and with ordinary usage.

## The district court’s private-carrier detour — and why the Fifth Circuit’s avoidance was salutary

The district court had tried a different route. Because the statute lists “depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail” as alternative means of sending the notice, the district court analogized APAC’s later physical handling of the paper documents to delivery “with a private carrier.” To support that conclusion it relied on a loose analogy to a panel opinion about railroads, concluding that the APAC employee who placed the documents in a shared filing area could be treated as a private carrier delivering the notice to GoAuto.

The Fifth Circuit expressly declined to reach that theory. The panel noted the district court’s “imaginative and unconventional” definition of “private carrier” and, sensibly, avoided endorsing an interpretive leap that would have extended the category beyond any obvious or textually supported scope. Instead, the court rested its affirmance on the simpler and more straightforward ground that the e-mail/web-service interaction itself constituted “receipt.”

This refusal to bless the district court’s analogy was the right move. There are two basic reasons. First, the statute enumerates three alternatives — mail, private carrier, and electronic mail — and treats electronic mail as an independent, recognized method. That textual arrangement suggests the legislature intended to cover electronic transmissions directly, not only indirectly via analogies to physical carriers. If a statute expressly contemplates e-mail, there is little reason to import the law of “private carriers” (or railroads) to justify the same result. Second, the district court’s private-carrier theory risked producing arbitrary and fact-bound outcomes: might a hand-delivery by a temporary contractor constitute a private carrier in some cases but not others? Courts should avoid creating ad hoc doctrines when the statutory text supplies a straightforward answer.

The Fifth Circuit’s choice reflects sound interpretive priorities: follow the text first; treat statutory alternatives as meaningful; and be cautious about analogical extensions that could complicate an already strict statutory compliance regime.

## Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

Williams v. GoAuto clarifies a few practical and doctrinal points worth flagging for practitioners and judges in Louisiana and beyond.

First, electronic transmissions and automated system-to-system communications can constitute “sending” and the insurer’s “receipt” under statutes that expressly recognize electronic means. The Fifth Circuit’s treatment endorses a plain-meaning, modernized reading that aligns statutory grammar with contemporary commerce. That should reduce friction for insurers and premium finance companies that rely on automated workflows—provided they can document transmission and receipt.

Second, the opinion reiterates that courts will resist judicially augmenting statutory requirements like signature formalities where the text does not demand them. Plaintiffs will be hard-pressed to argue that an unsigned (but company-generated) certification is ineffective absent state precedent imposing such a requirement.

Third, the panel’s refusal to bless the district court’s private-carrier analogy is instructive. Trial courts should be wary of analogizing to doctrinal categories (railroads, common carriers, etc.) when the statute contains an explicit list of means of delivery; such analogies invite complexity and inconsistent results. Likewise, litigants should not expect courts to expand “private carrier” doctrines to accommodate contemporary digital practices when the statute already contemplates e-mail.

Finally, the decision leaves a residual uncertainty that plaintiffs in similar cases may attempt to exploit: how much evidence of electronic transmission and automated receipt is enough to establish “receipt” in fact? The Fifth Circuit accepted GoAuto’s showing about the web service listener and the timing of automatic cancellation, but future cases may turn on the completeness of the record: system logs, time stamps, automated acknowledgement messages, or other metadata will become the evidentiary backbone of these disputes.

## Practical guidance for trial courts and practitioners

For defense counsel representing insurers or finance companies: preserve system logs and metadata showing that an electronic cancellation request was transmitted and accepted, and that it triggered an automated cancellation action. Produce documentation of the certification language that accompanies the electronic notice and evidence tying that certification to the finance company’s corporate action (letterhead, automated headers, affidavit of the systems administrator). Avoid relying on post hoc paper handling or contested “private carrier” theories to explain delivery.

For plaintiffs’ counsel: if you intend to challenge an automated process, develop a record that probes the timing, whether the certification existed in final form at the moment of transmission, whether the automated web service truly “received” the request or simply queued it, and whether any acknowledgement was generated. Where possible, show gaps in logs, nonstandard workflows, or ambiguity about who had custody at critical times. If statutory text is ambiguous in your jurisdiction, consider seeking certification to the state supreme court—but be aware the Fifth Circuit in Williams declined certification as unwarranted here.

For judges: start with the statutory text. If the statute lists electronic mail as an independent method of sending notice, ask whether the legislature’s inclusion was meant to cover automated system-to-system communications. Avoid creating or extending analogues (e.g., defining a contractor as a “private carrier”) when the text provides a discrete, modern mechanism. And, finally, demand a clear evidentiary record about the timing of transmissions and the mechanics of automated systems before deciding whether “receipt” occurred.

## Conclusion

Williams v. GoAuto is notable not for doctrinal fireworks but for judicial modesty. The Fifth Circuit resolved the case by bringing statutory language into the 21st century: electronic transmissions and automated receptions can satisfy statutory “sending” and “receipt.” Equally important, the court declined to bless an elaborate but unnecessary analogical theory advanced by the district court. That restraint preserves textual clarity and gives practitioners a clear roadmap: document your electronic workflows, and don’t rely on creative analogies when the statute plainly contemplates the technology at issue.



# Declining to Certify: Federal-State Judicial Dialogue and the Fifth Circuit’s Erie Prediction on Insurance Cancellations

Why this matters: The Fifth Circuit’s decision in Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is more than a routine summary-judgment affirmance in an insurance dispute. It is a compact lesson in federal courts’ posture toward unsettled state-law questions — particularly in heavily regulated areas like insurance — and in the risks and tradeoffs inherent in making an Erie prediction rather than certifying to a state supreme court. By refusing to certify and construing Louisiana’s premium-finance cancellation statute itself, the Fifth Circuit has embraced a construction that favors modern, technology-driven cancellation workflows (email/web-service triggers) and rejects a presumption that a handwritten or human signature is required. That outcome preserves one set of practices for market actors — at least in the Fifth Circuit — but it also leaves open the possibility of future divergence if the Louisiana courts reach a different conclusion.

## The court’s approach: why no certification and what it decided

At the heart of Williams was the interpretation of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3): when a premium-finance company “certifies” compliance and the insurer “receives” that certification, may the insurer treat the policy as effectively cancelled? The plaintiffs advanced two main statutory-contention points: (1) that the statute required a signature on the finance company’s certification before cancellation took effect; and (2) that “receipt” by the insurer means physical custody by a human employee of the insurer (as opposed to an automated electronic intake).

The Fifth Circuit rejected both arguments. Looking first at “certify,” the court emphasized that the statute commands the insurance-premium finance company itself to certify certain facts; it does not explicitly mandate a signature by an identified employee. The court surveyed sparse Louisiana authority and concluded there is no clear state-law requirement that a human signature precede cancellation. It cautioned against judicial supplementation of the statutory text under the rubric of Louisiana’s strict-adherence line of cases; absent clearer state-law authority, courts should not add a signature requirement the legislature did not write.

On “receipt,” the Fifth Circuit adopted a practical and technologically attuned reading. It looked to ordinary dictionary definitions of “receipt” and accepted that “receipt” may occur when a company’s computer system takes delivery of an emailed notice — here, an APAC-generated email/web-service call that automatically triggers GoAuto’s cancellation process. In other words, machine-to-machine transmission and automated action can, in the court’s view, constitute the insurer’s “receipt” sufficient to authorize cancellation.

Crucially, the plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify these questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. The Fifth Circuit declined, invoking its longstanding reluctance to certify absent a compelling reason, and relying on Jefferson v. Lead Industries for the principle that mere absence of state-court precedent is not a sufficient basis to certify. Having made its Erie prediction, the court affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment for GoAuto.

## Assessing the Fifth Circuit’s decision to make an Erie prediction

The court’s refusal to certify rests on two related themes: stewardship of a scarce procedural tool, and confidence that the federal court can reliably predict how the Louisiana Supreme Court would resolve the issue. There are good reasons for both instincts. Certification is not a roulette wheel to be spun whenever a federal panel confronts a difficult state-law question; frequent certification could overwhelm state courts and impair the federal court’s role in resolving disputes. And when a question can be decided on the statute’s plain text and is not a matter of particularly novel or uniquely local doctrine, an Erie prediction can be appropriate.

But the choice carries tradeoffs. Insurance law is a regulated, policy-sensitive domain in which state courts frequently develop doctrines informed by local regulatory practices, insurer-regulator interaction, and precedents that emphasize consumer protection. The Louisiana jurisprudential tradition the Fifth Circuit cites — strict adherence to cancellation procedures to protect insureds and preserve cure periods — is precisely the kind of background that might counsel deference to the state supreme court’s interpretive instincts. The Fifth Circuit acknowledged that Louisiana courts require “strict adherence” but also warned against judicial addition of requirements not in the statute. A state supreme court might tilt the balance differently, emphasizing remedial or protective policies that justify reading “certify” to include an identifiable human signature or construe “receipt” narrowly to require human possession (at least where automated systems can produce gaps or timing uncertainties). Such an approach would reflect precaution in a space where insureds lose coverage and potential liability consequences are significant.

An Erie prediction thus presents the risk of future divergence. If the Louisiana Supreme Court later interprets the statute differently, the Williams panel’s decision will be distinguished or limited. That could create an uneven landscape in the interim: insurers and finance companies operating inside the Fifth Circuit (or before federal courts applying Louisiana law) will rely on the Fifth Circuit’s reading, while plaintiffs or state courts might receive different answers elsewhere in Louisiana. That uncertainty raises two practical concerns: first, insurers who relied on automated cancellation workflows risk future exposure and second, plaintiffs may strategically choose state-court fora to press claims where a more protective state-court reading is plausible.

## Doctrinal and practical implications

Doctrinally, Williams is a modest but meaningful extension of a textualist, modernizing approach to statutory interpretation in state-regulated fields. The Fifth Circuit’s reliance on dictionaries and the plain statutory actor — “the insurance premium finance company” — underscores an interpretive posture that privileges legislative text and contemporaneous commercial practices (i.e., digital communications and web services). The opinion also reinforces a conservative certification approach; federal courts will avoid certification where a clear-enough textual case exists and where certification would not be compelled by precedent or a significant unsettled issue unique to state law.

Practically, Williams leaves the insurance industry with a usable precedent validating certain machine-driven cancellation mechanics — at least within the Fifth Circuit’s reach. Insurers and finance companies who have implemented email- and web-service-triggered cancellations can point to Williams as supporting the lawfulness of those systems when they produce the documentary “certification” required by § 9:3550. But the decision also signals a need for operational caution: firms should maintain robust audit trails, ensure their notices include clear statutorily mandated content, and consider operational steps (like timely stamping or logging by insurer personnel) to guard against claims that “receipt” was lacking.

For plaintiffs and defense counsel, the decision sharpens forum strategy. Plaintiffs who see Williams as unfavorable may prefer to litigate in Louisiana state court, where the statutory interpretation question might be presented to and shaped by the state appellate system — and where the state supreme court could adopt a different rule emphasizing insured protection. Defendants, conversely, may favor federal removal where an Erie prediction is likely to yield a more insurer-friendly reading under Fifth Circuit precedent.

Finally, Williams underscores the value — and limits — of certification. The Jefferson line remains a formidable hurdle. Parties who genuinely seek state supreme court guidance must present a compelling case that a legal question is determinative, unsettled in a way that cannot be responsibly resolved by federal prediction, and of statewide importance that warrants use of the certification channel.

## Conclusion

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is a case about more than signatures and emails; it is a snapshot of federal-state judicial dialogue in action. The Fifth Circuit made an Erie prediction favoring contemporary commercial practices over a textual importation of a signature requirement, and it declined to press the certiorari-style pause-button that certification would have provided. That choice advances predictability for some stakeholders now, but it also preserves the possibility of future doctrinal friction if Louisiana’s courts take a different path. For litigants and regulated entities operating in Louisiana’s insurance ecosystem, the case should prompt careful attention to operational compliance, recordkeeping, and strategic decisions about forum selection — and it is a reminder that even “plain-text” statutory disputes can raise significant policy stakes that sometimes are best settled by the state courts charged with supervising the industry.



## Compliance Playbook After Williams: What Insurers and Premium Finance Companies Should Do Now

Why Williams v. GoAuto Insurance matters
The Fifth Circuit’s October 2025 decision in Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is one of the first federal appellate opinions to squarely confront how modern, electronic workflows square with Louisiana’s strict statutory scheme for cancellations initiated by premium finance companies under La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3). For practitioners who insulate risk by automating cancellations and who defend insurers and finance companies against class actions alleging ineffective cancellation or bad-faith claims, Williams offers a practical roadmap — and a warning. The court reaffirmed that Louisiana requires strict adherence to the statute, but it also made clear that “strict” does not mean “antiquated”: an electronic transmission that puts the insurer in possession (here, an API/web-service call that the insurer’s system receives and that automatically triggers cancellation) satisfies the statute’s “receipt” requirement, and the statute does not itself impose a signature requirement on the financing company’s certification.

That combination — continued fidelity to the statutory elements, alongside recognition that machine-driven receipt can be receipt — will be critical for defending automated cancellation programs and for tightening operational controls to avoid litigation vulnerabilities.

What the court held and why it’s important
The Fifth Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment for GoAuto on the narrow legal question before it: GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3). The court’s reasoning turns on two points:

1. Certification: The statute requires that the premium finance company “certif[y]” certain facts in a notice (the existence of a valid power of attorney, uncured default, that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured with the date, and that copies were sent to interested third parties). The Fifth Circuit declined to read an extra-statutory requirement of an employee signature into “certifying.” Although Louisiana courts historically demand strict compliance with the statute, the Fifth Circuit concluded that a company-level certification in the form sent by APAC — even without an identified, handwritten signature — satisfied the statutory “certifying” requirement.

2. Receipt: The statute conditions cancellation on the insurer’s “receipt” of the cancellation notice and certification. The court adopted ordinary dictionary meanings of “receipt” and held that electronic delivery that causes the insurer’s system to take possession — here, an emailed/API-triggered request that a GoAuto web service receives and that automatically cancels the policy — qualifies as “receipt.” The court emphasized that modern statutes must be read in the context of modern business practices: company computers do what humans once did.

The court also declined to certify unsettled state-law questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court and refused to adopt the district court’s alternative theory (that the APAC employee delivering paper copies qualified as a “private carrier” under the statute).

Operational takeaways — a practical compliance playbook
Williams gives insurers and premium finance companies concrete benchmarks for minimizing litigation risk. If you run, implement, or defend an automated cancellation process in Louisiana, consider these actions now:

1. Make the certification mirror the statute verbatim
- Ensure every cancellation request contains clear, unambiguous language covering each statutory element in La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv): (i) existence of a valid power of attorney, (ii) default and failure to cure, (iii) that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured with the date of sending, and (iv) that copies were sent to all persons shown in the agreement to have an interest (with names/addresses).
- Where feasible, include a reference to the specific statutory subsection to avoid ambiguity about what the certification is intended to do.

2. Preserve reliable electronic transmission and receipt logs
- Treat the API call, email, or web-service request as the operative “sending” and the insurer’s logged receipt as operative “receipt.” Maintain immutable logs (e.g., write-once logs, secure timestamps, or hashed records) that show transmission, delivery, and the insurer system’s processing of the request.
- Save full headers, payloads, and server-side acknowledgements (HTTP response codes, transaction IDs) for each cancellation request. Those logs form the primary defense evidence that the insurer “received” the notice in the statutory sense.

3. Align timestamps and define the effective cancellation time
- Draft internal policies that state whether the policy is cancelled at the moment the insurer’s system receives the cancellation request, when the system processes the request, or at a specific time on the cancellation date. Williams treats electronic receipt leading to automatic cancellation as sufficient; but careful records showing when the web service “heard” the call and when the policy state changed will prevent disputes about whether the insured still had coverage at the moment of an accident.
- Synchronize clocks across systems (NTP) and keep time-zone handling consistent in logs so that timestamps admitted in evidence are reliable and comprehensible.

4. Document sending of the ten‑day notice to the insured
- Williams leaves open numerous avenues for plaintiffs to challenge cancellation effectiveness; chief among them is attacking the ten‑day notice itself. Continue to create and preserve proof of mailing/service for the ten‑day notice to the insured: certified mail receipts, USPS tracking, affidavits of mailing, or electronic delivery receipts if the parties agreed to electronic notice.
- Where notices are mailed, keep the underlying affidavits of mailing or third‑party evidence proximate in the record to the subsequent cancellation request.

5. Document notices to interested third parties
- The statute requires copies of the ten‑day notice be sent to persons “shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest.” That is a frequent litigation focal point. Maintain clear records documenting the names and addresses in the finance agreement and proof that notices were sent to those addresses (and retain those proofs).
- If your system constructs the list of interested parties programmatically, retain the output of that routine at the time of notice generation (so plaintiffs cannot later argue the list was incomplete).

6. Preserve the power-of-attorney documentation
- Keep an auditable copy of the premium finance agreement and the power of attorney the agreement purports to grant. Disputes occasionally focus on whether the power of attorney is valid, expired, or was properly executed.

7. Retain paper/digital artifacts and a chain-of-custody
- Even where cancellations are automated, retain a binder or electronic folder that aggregates (a) the ten‑day notice proof-of-mailing, (b) the finance agreement with POA, (c) the transmitted certification, and (d) the insurer’s receipt logs. Make chain-of-custody and retention policies clear and defensible to avoid spoliation claims.

8. Update contracts, SOPs, and vendor SLAs
- Ensure third-party vendors (including premium finance companies) contractually commit to producing the required statutory content, to retaining and producing logs on short notice, and to indemnifying for failures that create liability.
- Train personnel to understand the statutory elements that must be present in the certification and the operational importance of preserving evidence.

What Williams means for litigation strategy
Williams is an important defensive precedent. If a cancellation is otherwise supported by a certification that tracks the statute and by reliable transmission/receipt logs, class-action claims that hinge on “ineffective” cancellation or on misrepresentation and bad-faith theories are less likely to succeed. The Fifth Circuit’s disposition shows courts will look to the substance of statutory compliance, not demand an extra-statutory signature formality.

But the decision also highlights where plaintiffs will continue to press: procedural defects that the appellate court didn’t find here — and which remain classic weak spots — remain viable pathways for relief.

Likely plaintiff attack vectors that survived Williams
- Ten‑day notice defects: plaintiffs can still challenge whether the ten‑day notice required by La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iii) was actually sent in compliance with the statute (timing, content, or inadequate proof of mailing). Proof-of-mailing affidavits that are inconsistent, missing, or backdated remain potent weapons.
- Incomplete notification of interested third parties: failure to send copies of the ten‑day notice to all persons “shown by the premium finance agreement to have an interest” can render a cancellation ineffective. Disputes about the contractually identified parties, or about addresses used, often surface here.
- Power of attorney validity: arguments that the finance agreement does not create the required POA, that it was invalidly executed, or that the POA expired can undermine cancellation authority.
- Timing and causation disputes: even where electronic receipt is admitted, plaintiffs will litigate whether cancellation was effective before an accident (critical in first‑party/UM/UIM contexts) — hence the importance of synchronized, auditable timestamps.
- Human‑process gaps: where a later manual signature stamp occurs after cancellation (as in Williams), plaintiffs may try to argue the sequence evidences a post hoc certification. Williams rejects that theory where the statutory elements and receipt are present, but sloppy or inconsistent records can reopen the issue.

Doctrinal and regulatory implications
Williams navigates between two doctrinal poles. On one hand, Louisiana’s case law demands “strict adherence” to the cancellation statute — an anchor that protects insureds’ opportunity to cure defaults and fixes cancellation timing. On the other hand, Williams resists judicial aggrandizement of the statute into requirements not contained in text (e.g., requiring an identifiable employee signature), reflecting a broader trend of courts accommodating genuine modernization of business processes where statutory goals are preserved.

Practically, the decision signals that state legislatures (and regulators) that care about consumer notice protections should clarify statutory language where they meant to require a human signature, express receipt by a particular person, or specify methods for proof of delivery in the electronic age. Absent that change, courts may continue to treat well‑documented, automated processes as compliant so long as they effect the statute’s core protections: meaningful notice and an opportunity to cure.

Downstream litigation and appellate considerations
Williams is precedential in the Fifth Circuit and persuasive elsewhere — especially in other jurisdictions that read statutory “receipt” and “certify” language in light of electronic transmissions. Defense counsel should cite Williams when defending automated cancellation systems. Plaintiffs’ counsel, conversely, will intensify discovery on the ten‑day notice proof, the content and timing of the certification, and logs showing the insurer’s system state at the precise times of the alleged loss and cancellation.

Finally, Williams illustrates a procedural lesson: the Fifth Circuit refused to certify open state-law questions to the Louisiana Supreme Court. So long as federal courts are willing to resolve these issues on the statutory text and ordinary definitions (as the Fifth Circuit did), expect fewer certified questions and more federal appellate precedents shaping the interplay between automation and state statutory protections.

Bottom line for practitioners
Williams affirms that modern, automated cancellation workflows can satisfy La. R.S. 9:3550(G)(3) — but only if they are backed by careful adherence to the statute’s four certification elements and by rigorous, contemporaneous documentation of notices, transmissions, and receipt. For insurers and premium finance companies, the safest course is operational and evidentiary discipline: make the certification language unambiguously statutory, log everything immutably, synchronize timestamps, preserve proof-of-mailing for ten‑day notices, and document notices to all listed interested parties. Do that, and Williams becomes a solid defensive precedent rather than a litigation trap.
