Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

### Under Erie, why did the Fifth Circuit rely on Louisiana intermediate appellate cases and Black’s Law Dictionary to predict Louisiana law?
Question:
The court says there’s no Louisiana Supreme Court authority, cites several Louisiana appellate decisions, and then adopts Black’s definitions for “receipt.” Is this the standard methodology for making an Erie guess in Louisiana—give weight to intermediate courts absent contrary signals—and use general legal dictionaries when the Civil Code/statute doesn’t define a term? Should the court also have discussed Louisiana Civil Code canons for interpreting legislation (e.g., generally prevailing meaning)?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the Fifth Circuit followed the ordinary Erie prediction method: it gave controlling weight to available Louisiana appellate precedent where it existed, and where the Louisiana Supreme Court hadn’t spoken and the statute was silent it resorted to a well‑recognized legal dictionary definition. The court expressly relied on intermediate appellate decisions to show the absence of controlling state‑supreme‑court authority and on Black’s for the ordinary meaning of “receipt.” It was not required to run through every Louisiana Civil Code interpretive canon once a clear, ordinary meaning (supported by the authorities the court cited) resolved the question.

Why that is the correct methodology (and how the opinion applies it)

- Erie/Guaranty trust principle: federal courts sitting in diversity must predict how the state’s highest court would decide. When the state supreme court hasn’t spoken, federal courts give weight to intermediate appellate decisions unless there are persuasive reasons those decisions are wrong or inapplicable. The opinion follows that path: it surveys Louisiana appellate authority about § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)–(b) (citing Delatte, Eaglin, Britten, KMJ Servs., Hodges, Lewis, Benitez) and notes that the Louisiana Supreme Court has not defined the key terms. The court uses those intermediate decisions to show what Louisiana law has required (e.g., “strict adherence” to the statute) and to demonstrate that prior appellate opinions are inconclusive on the signature/receipt questions. See discussion at pp. 5–8 (collecting Delatte, Eaglin, Britten and the appellate cases that are “similarly unhelpful”).

- Use of Black’s Law Dictionary when the statute is silent: the statute uses “receipt” without a statutory definition. The Fifth Circuit explicitly acknowledges the absence of Louisiana Supreme Court authority and a statutory definition, then adopts Black’s ordinary‑usage definitions of “receipt” and “receive” to resolve whether GoAuto had “receipt” of the cancellation request. The opinion states: “A leading law dictionary defines ‘receipt’ as ‘[t]he act of receiving something, esp[ecially] by taking physical possession,’ and ‘receive’ as ‘[t]o take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of or get from some outside source.’ Receipt, Black’s Law Dictionary (12th ed. 2024) . . . We accept these definitions and conclude that GoAuto may cancel a policy once it takes possession, delivery, or custody of a request for cancellation.” (Opinion pp. 9–10).

- Why this is acceptable under Erie: when a statute is ambiguous or silent on a term and the state’s highest court has not spoken, federal courts may look to (1) intermediate appellate rulings, (2) the plain or ordinary meaning of words, and (3) persuasive secondary authorities (including dictionaries) to predict state law. The opinion showed a reasonable chain of reasoning: intermediate courts did not fix the issue, the statute has no definition, and Black’s gives the ordinary meaning that sensibly fits the statute’s purpose (facilitating clear timing of cancellation). The court also relied on the statutory text itself (the insurer cancels “upon receipt”) and the statute’s purpose (setting exact time and date of cancellation; see Britten) to support adopting the ordinary meaning.

Should the court have analyzed Louisiana Civil Code canons (e.g., “generally prevailing meaning”)?

- It could have — but it was not necessary to decide the case. Louisiana’s interpretive canons (e.g., that words are to be given their generally prevailing meaning, see La. Civ. Code art. 12) provide tools for statutory interpretation, and invoking them can strengthen an Erie prediction. The Fifth Circuit implicitly applied that approach by (a) looking for Louisiana authority, (b) treating the statute as modern and to be read in light of modern practices (computerized communications), and (c) adopting the ordinary dictionary meaning of “receipt.” The court explicitly framed the statute as a “modern statute set in current times, and company computers do much that individuals once did,” which explains why an electronic delivery that triggers a cancellation satisfies “receipt.” (Opinion p. 10).

- In practice, citing a Civil Code canon would have been an elaboration supporting the same result: the court’s adoption of Black’s ordinary meaning is functionally equivalent to applying the “generally prevailing meaning” canon. Because the intermediate decisions were unhelpful and the statutory text/purpose plus ordinary meaning resolved the issue, the panel reasonably declined to add a separate canon analysis. The court also declined to certify the question to the Louisiana Supreme Court (Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n standard), indicating it did not view the interpretive gap as large enough to require certification.

Bottom line

The Fifth Circuit followed standard Erie practice: it surveyed Louisiana appellate authority, found no definitive state‑supreme‑court answer, and reasonably relied on the ordinary meaning of “receipt” (as given in Black’s) to predict how the Louisiana Supreme Court would construe the statute. A formal invocation of Louisiana Civil Code canons might have been helpful explanatory color, but it wasn’t necessary here because the combination of statutory text, appellate precedents, and the ordinary dictionary meaning produced a clear result.
