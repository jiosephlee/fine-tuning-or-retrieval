Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

### What evidentiary showing at summary judgment established that GoAuto actually ‘received’ the cancellation emails before auto-cancelling?
Question:
The opinion references deposition testimony describing APAC’s call to a GoAuto web service that triggers cancellation. Was that testimony alone sufficient under Rule 56, or were there logs/emails or business records in the summary judgment record? How do courts handle authentication and hearsay issues for computer-system interactions when determining undisputed facts about timing and ‘receipt’?
Answer:
Short answer
The Fifth Circuit relied on GoAuto’s summary‑judgment record — principally the testimony explaining APAC’s automated call/e‑mail into a GoAuto web service plus the undisputed description of what the GoAuto system does when it “receives” that call/e‑mail — to conclude GoAuto took “receipt” of the cancellation before the later, human stamp. The opinion does not describe separate system logs or business‑record printouts being necessary; the deposition explaining how the systems interact and the sequence of (1) APAC’s electronic request, (2) GoAuto’s computer‑system cancellation, and (3) later human stamping was the evidentiary foundation the court accepted.

Why that was enough in the opinion
- The opinion quotes a GoAuto deponent who described the APAC process “mak[ing] a call to a GoAuto web service to request cancellation” and explained what a web service does (“it’s always listening … when it’s called … it performs the activity”). The court treats that testimony as establishing that the APAC transmission reached and was acted on by GoAuto’s automated system.
- The panel also relied on other undisputed record facts about timing: the e‑mailed notice was “received in early morning hours by GoAuto’s computer system” and that system “cancels the policy” at that time, with human involvement occurring only later in the morning when an APAC employee affixes a signature stamp and a GoAuto employee stamps “received.” Those facts, as recited in the opinion, are the basis for holding that GoAuto had “receipt” under the statute before the later manual handling.

How courts treat this type of evidence at summary judgment (as reflected in the opinion)
- Testimony is competent summary‑judgment evidence. The opinion treats the deponent’s explanation of system interaction as admissible evidence establishing what happened (i.e., that GoAuto’s system received and acted on APAC’s request). In other words, admissible deposition testimony describing automated system behavior can create an undisputed fact about receipt/timing.
- The opinion does not say the court needed e‑mail headers, server logs, or certified business records to authenticate the interaction. That does not mean such records would be inadmissible or unnecessary in other cases, only that they were not required here given the testimony and other record facts.
- Practically, if a party wanted additional or stronger evidence of the precise timing or that a transmission was actually received, courts typically look for (and admit, when authenticated) system logs, e‑mail headers, or business‑record printouts. Those items are ordinarily authenticated by witness testimony about the systems (custodian or IT witness) — exactly the kind of testimony the Fifth Circuit credited here. The opinion thus shows the common path: a witness explains how the system works and ties the log/message to the automatic consequence; that explanation can both authenticate system records and stand alone as admissible evidence for Rule 56 purposes.

A short procedural/legal point
- Under Rule 56, a party may rely on admissible deposition testimony to show undisputed facts. Hearsay objections do not bar the use of deposition testimony if the testimony itself is offered by an opposing party (or otherwise admissible). And business records or system logs, when offered, are typically authenticated through witness testimony (the same kind of testimony the court accepted in this opinion).

Bottom line
In Williams v. GoAuto the Fifth Circuit accepted the deposition testimony describing APAC’s call to GoAuto’s web service and the undisputed sequence of automated cancellation followed by later human stamping as sufficient summary‑judgment evidence that GoAuto “received” the cancellation before auto‑cancelling. The opinion does not identify separate e‑mail/server log exhibits or certified business records as part of the crucial showing, but it also illustrates the usual evidentiary route: a witness who can explain and connect the system behavior can authenticate computer interactions and make those interactions admissible and dispositive at summary judgment.
