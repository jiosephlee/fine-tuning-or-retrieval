Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

# The Legal Architecture of Premium-Financed Policy Cancellations in Louisiana

Louisiana’s statutory scheme for cancellations initiated by premium finance companies (also called premium finance agreements, finance companies, or premium financiers) crafts a precise, stepwise procedure. The controlling statute — La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) — authorizes a premium finance company that has advanced an insurer’s premium to exercise a power of attorney to request cancellation of the underlying insurance policy when the insured defaults on the finance agreement. Because the cancellation decision affects coverage (often for liability policies where third parties may be injured), Louisiana courts insist on strict adherence to the statute’s procedures: a procedural defect in the finance company’s process can render the cancellation ineffective and leave coverage in force. The Fifth Circuit’s recent decision in Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, 24-30646 (5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025), applies these principles to modern electronic transmission and helps define two recurring interpretive questions: (1) what does it mean for the finance company to “certify” the statutory elements, and (2) what constitutes the insurer’s “receipt” of the finance company’s notice so as to trigger permissible cancellation. This chapter explains the statutory architecture, the courts’ insistence on strict compliance, how Williams interpreted two ambiguities, and what consequence follows when the statutory sequence is not followed.

## Actors and Instruments: Who Does What, and How

Four actors and one instrument dominate this legal landscape.

- The insured: the policyholder who purchases insurance but uses a premium finance company to pay the insurer up front in exchange for installment payments to the finance company.
- The insurer: the insurance company that issued the policy and that will effectuate policy cancellation when proper statutory prerequisites are met.
- The premium finance company (finance company or APFC): the entity that pays the insurer the full premium and acquires a contractual right — typically by written finance agreement — to cancel the policy upon an insured’s default.
- Interested third parties and holders of security interests: entities (e.g., lenders, governmental agencies, or others identified in the finance agreement) who must be given notice of cancellation if the statute requires it.
- The instrument: the premium finance agreement, which normally contains (i) the finance terms, (ii) an express power of attorney authorizing the finance company to cancel the policy upon default, and (iii) the names and addresses of any parties who must be notified upon cancellation.

The finance agreement’s power-of-attorney clause is the crucial enabling document. Under § 9:3550(G), the finance company’s authority to effect cancellation derives from that power of attorney, but statutory mechanics — notice to the insured, certification to the insurer, and transmittal to interested parties — must be followed in the order and form the statute requires.

## The Statutory Framework: La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) and Related Provisions

La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G) creates an exception to the general rule that insurers issue cancellations themselves: when an insured finances a premium, the finance company may request cancellation on the company’s own certification that specified conditions exist. Section 9:3550(G)(3)(a) (as applied in Williams) requires the finance company to send the insurer “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four discrete elements:

(i) the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as required by the subsection;  
(ii) the finance agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured;  
(iii) upon default, a notice of cancellation was sent to the insured as required by the subsection, and that the notice specified the date of sending by the premium finance company to the insured; and  
(iv) copies of the notice were sent to all persons shown by the finance agreement to have an interest in any loss thereunder, specifying the names and addresses of any governmental agencies, security-holders, or third parties to whom notice was sent.

Once the insurer has “receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement,” § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i) authorizes the insurer to consider that cancellation has been requested and to proceed to cancel the policy. The statute thus distributes duties among the parties: the finance company must effect the preliminary notices and certify compliance; the insurer may then cancel after it receives the certification.

These specialized provisions sit alongside Louisiana’s more general cancellation statutes (for example La. Rev. Stat. § 22:1266 and related provisions regulating cancellation of certain kinds of insurance), which impose independent notice and timing obligations on insurers. The premium-finance scheme interlocks with the general rules by assuring the insured receives a statutorily mandated cure period (often ten days) before cancellation becomes effective.

## The Statutory Sequence and the Timeline

The statutory process follows a sequential and time-sensitive path designed to (1) fix the date of cancellation with precision and (2) guarantee a minimal cure period during which the insured may avoid cancellation.

1. Default. The insured fails to make an installment payment due under the premium finance agreement. That uncured default is a precondition to exercise of the power of attorney to request cancellation.

2. Ten-day Notice to the Insured (the cure notice). The finance company must send — contemporaneously with the uncured default — a written notice to the insured warning that the policy will be cancelled unless payment is made within the statutorily specified cure period (commonly ten days). The statute requires the notice to specify the date of sending.

3. Notice to Interested Parties. The finance company must also send copies of that cancellation notice to all persons identified in the finance agreement as having an interest in the insured’s coverage (for example, lienholders, named agencies). The statute requires the finance company to identify the names and addresses of these recipients.

4. Certification to the Insurer. After the cure period has run without payment, the finance company must send the insurer a “copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the four elements set out above in § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv). The timing of this certification matters: it must reflect that the notice to the insured was sent and that the default was not timely cured.

5. Receipt by the Insurer; Cancellation. Once the insurer receives the finance company’s notice and certification, the insurer “shall consider that cancellation has been requested” and may proceed to cancel the policy. Receipt is the statutory trigger for insurer action.

The statute thus provides both a substantive right to cancel for the finance company (when the power of attorney and default exist) and a procedural protocol that places an unmistakable emphasis on documented transmission of notice and a fixed period for cure.

## The Four Certification Elements — What Must Be Communicated

Because the statute conditions the finance company’s power to effectuate cancellation on the content of its certification, attorneys and practitioners should think of § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) as a checklist that must be reflected in the certification delivered to the insurer:

1. Valid Power of Attorney. The certification must affirm that the finance agreement contains the statutory power-of-attorney language. That power enables the finance company to act on the insured’s behalf to request cancellation.

2. Uncured Default. The certification must state that the finance agreement is in default and that the default has not been timely cured within the statutorily provided cure period.

3. Ten-Day Notice to the Insured with Date of Sending. The certification must state that upon default, the finance company sent the insured the required notice of cancellation with the date of sending specified. This is essential to fix the start of the cure period and to avoid disputes about whether the insured had the statutory opportunity to cure.

4. Copies to Interested Parties. The certification must represent that copies of the ten-day notice were sent to persons identified in the finance agreement as having interests, and it should list their names and addresses (including any governmental agencies and holders of security interests).

Failure to communicate any of these four elements in the certification may be fatal; Louisiana courts have repeatedly emphasized that the statute’s text is mandatory and that any defect in the required process can invalidate the cancellation.

## Triggering Cancellation: What “Upon Receipt” Means

A recurring litigation point is the meaning of the statute’s phrase that the insurer may cancel “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement” sent by the finance company. Two interpretive issues flow from that phrase: (1) whether “receipt” requires physical possession by a human employee, and (2) whether the statutory “certifying” requires a signed, individualized signature.

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance addressed both questions.

- Receipt. The Fifth Circuit — guided by ordinary dictionary meanings — treated “receipt” as the act of receiving, particularly by taking physical possession, custody, or delivery, and concluded that modern, automated means of transmittal can satisfy the statute’s receipt requirement. In Williams, APAC’s system automatically e‑mailed a copy of the cancellation notice and certification to a GoAuto web service. GoAuto’s computer system, acting as a “listener” (a web service always “listening” for calls), received the e‑mail and automatically effected cancellation in the early morning hours. The Fifth Circuit held that the insurer’s computer taking custody of the transmitted notice is enough to constitute “receipt.” The court observed: statutes are enacted in a modern world in which companies use automated systems to perform human tasks; the statutory requirement of “receipt” should not be read to require a human signature or a human physically opening a mailed envelope. Electronic delivery and a demonstrable electronic record of delivery (e.g., e‑mail received by the insurer’s server or web-service logs showing the call and the date/time) will satisfy the receipt element so long as the insurer’s system actually takes possession or custody of the communication.

- Certifying and Signature. The plaintiffs in Williams argued that “certifying” necessarily imports an obligation that a named individual sign the certification before cancellation may be effected. The Fifth Circuit declined to graft a signature requirement onto the statute, explaining that the text requires only that the finance company — as an entity — certify the listed elements, and Louisiana jurisprudence had not mandated an individualized signature. While a few intermediate appellate opinions had criticized unsigned notices for lacking indicia of authoritativeness, no controlling Louisiana precedent required a signature. The Fifth Circuit therefore held that a finance company may “certify” by an unsigned statement on the company’s letterhead (or by an automated certification transmitted by email) so long as the content of the statutory certification is present. The court warned courts should not add requirements beyond the statute’s text; strict adherence means adhering to the procedures spelled out, not inventing additional formalities.

Two caveats follow from Williams. First, absence of a signature is not automatically harmless in every case — if an otherwise uncertified transmission lacks the four statutory elements or is ambiguous, the absence of a signature might be probative of infirmity. Benitez v. Elsayed (an intermediate appellate opinion) criticized a notice that included neither a named preparer nor a signature as failing to “purport to ‘certify’” the statements contained therein; but Benitez stopped short of holding the absence of a signature alone invalidated cancellation. Second, while automation is permitted, documentation of the chain of transmission is essential: the finance company and insurer should be able to produce electronic logs, timestamps, and copies to prove both the sending and the insurer’s receipt.

## The Doctrine of Strict Adherence

Louisiana courts routinely describe the § 9:3550(G) procedure as requiring “strict adherence.” See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993); Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990); Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). That doctrine serves two articulated functions:

1. Fixing the Date and Time of Cancellation. Strict adherence ensures there is an objective, provable date upon which cancellation is requested and, if carried out, becomes effective. This temporal certainty matters for questions such as whether a policy was in force at the time of an accident.

2. Guaranteeing the Cure Period. The statutory process gives the insured a minimum opportunity to cure the default. Strict compliance prevents premature cancellations that would deprive insureds of that legislatively mandated opportunity.

Consistent with these purposes, Louisiana courts have held that defects in the premium finance company’s procedure — for example, failing to send the ten-day notice to the insured, failing to include required dates or recipient lists, or otherwise omitting any of the required elements — will render a cancellation ineffective. Eaglin summarized the result plainly: “any defect in this process results in an ineffective cancellation of the policy.” 558 So. 2d at 287. Britten emphasized the cure-period protection aspect of the statute. Because the statute is mandatory and protective of an insured’s right to cure, courts will not tolerate procedural shortcuts.

Williams illustrates the constraints of the strict-adherence rule in two ways. First, the Fifth Circuit reiterated that courts should not use the strict-compliance doctrine as a pretext for adding requirements that the legislature did not. The insistence on strictness is backward-looking: it enforces the statute’s precise requirements rather than inventing additional form-based prerequisites (e.g., individualized signatures) absent textual support. Second, the doctrine preserves its protective teeth: if a certification genuinely lacks any of the four required elements, strict adherence will still demand invalidation. Williams did not relax the rule; it clarified its application in the context of electronic transmission and corporate certification.

## Downstream Consequences of an Ineffective Cancellation

When a cancellation effected under a finance company’s power of attorney is held ineffective, the consequences are practical and potentially severe:

- Coverage Continuity. The policy remains in force (subject to its own terms and the insurer’s obligations under law) for the period mistakenly thought cancelled. If an accident or loss occurs in that period, the insurer may be obligated to provide defense and indemnity even though the insurer would have cancelled had proper procedure been followed.

- Third-Party Claims Exposure. Insurers may face claims from injured third parties who reasonably expected the policy to respond. The insurer’s liability is not limited to the insured; it may have to pay judgments or settlements.

- Bad Faith and Tort Liability. Plaintiffs often assert tort claims — bad faith, negligent misrepresentation, or statutory claims for failure to communicate proper cancellation — alleging that the insurer or the finance company misrepresented coverage or failed to act in good faith. If the insurer’s cancellation was defective, plaintiffs may press these supplementary causes of action. Williams arose in the context of such claims; although the district court dismissed them after finding compliance, had the court found a statutory defect, the insurer might have faced attendant tort exposure.

- Coverage Disputes and Litigation Costs. Ineffective cancellations breed litigation over whether coverage existed at particular times, increasing defense costs and exposure for both the insurer and the finance company.

These consequences explain the careful judicial policing of the statutory steps and the need for precise documentation and auditable trails of notice and receipt.

## Applying the Doctrine — A Practical Checklist and Sample Problems

For practitioners and students, it is helpful to reduce the statutory and case law teachings to an application checklist that courts will use when assessing whether a cancellation by a premium finance company was effective:

1. Power of Attorney: Does the premium finance agreement contain the statutorily required power-of-attorney language authorizing cancellation? Is the clause in the finance agreement valid and enforceable?

2. Default and Cure: Was there an actual default under the finance agreement, and did the insured fail to cure within the statutorily required cure period (commonly ten days)? Are there proof of mailings or other evidence showing the date the cure notice was sent?

3. Notice to the Insured: Did the finance company send the ten-day cancellation notice to the insured and specify the date of sending in the certification? Are proof-of-mailing affidavits, tracking numbers, or email logs available?

4. Copies to Interested Parties: Were copies of the notice transmitted to all persons identified in the finance agreement? Did the certification identify their names and addresses?

5. Contents of the Certification: Does the certification sent to the insurer contain the four statutory elements verbatim or in substance?

6. Receipt by the Insurer: Did the insurer actually receive the notice and certification? Is there documentary proof of receipt (e.g., e-mail server logs, web-service transaction records, stamped received copies, or chain-of-custody items)? If electronic transmission was used, does the record show the insurer’s system accepted the transmission prior to cancellation?

7. Timing of Cancellation: Was cancellation effected only after insurer receipt? If an automated system canceled automatically upon receipt by the insurer’s server, is there an electronic record showing when that occurred and that the sequence complied with the statute?

Sample problem (application exercise): An insured defaults on a premium finance agreement. The finance company’s system emails a ten-day notice to the insured on January 1. No payment is received. On January 11 at 3:00 a.m., the finance company’s system automatically calls the insurer’s web service and transmits a certification but no individual signs the notice. The insurer’s web service registers the transaction and an automated process cancels the policy at 3:02 a.m. An individual at the finance company stamps a printed copy at 9:30 a.m. Is the cancellation effective under Louisiana law as interpreted in Williams?

Answer: Under Williams, the cancellation is likely effective if the email to the insured on January 1 is documented (establishing the start of the cure period), the certification transmitted on January 11 included the four statutory elements, and the insurer’s web service actually received the transmission (3:00 a.m.) before the automated cancellation occurred. The absence of an individualized signature does not, by itself, invalidate the certification. But if any of the required elements were missing (e.g., no proof the ten-day notice was actually sent to the insured, no list of interested parties, or failure to demonstrate insurer receipt), strict adherence requires invalidation.

## Operational and Litigation Advice

For finance companies and insurers who wish to minimize disputes, Williams reinforces several pragmatic best practices:

- Maintain contemporaneous, auditable records: email headers, server logs, web-service request/response logs, timestamps, and proof-of-mailing affidavits. Electronic metadata frequently proves both transmittal and receipt.

- Include the four statutory elements explicitly in the certification and, when feasible, attach copies of the notices and mailing proofs to the certification itself.

- Identify recipients: list names and addresses of interested parties and agencies to satisfy § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(iv).

- Avoid unnecessary formalities that do not appear in the statute, but do ensure clarity and demonstrable corporate certification (company letterhead and a corporate attestation are useful).

- If relying on automation for transmittal and cancellation, create and preserve logs that show the insurer’s system took possession and the time at which that occurred.

For plaintiffs and defense counsel, litigation should focus on proving or disproving each checklist item. Where an insurer relies on automation, plaintiffs should probe the chain of custody: did the insurer’s system actually accept the transmission prior to cancellation, and is there electronic evidence? When a certification lacks one of the statutorily required elements, courts remain willing to invalidate cancellations.

## Certification of Questions to the State Supreme Court

Federal courts may certify unsettled questions of state law to the state’s highest court when helpful and appropriate. But the Fifth Circuit in Williams declined to certify the questions regarding the meaning of “certifying” and “receipt,” explaining that mere absence of a prior state-court definition is insufficient to warrant certification. See Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997) (federal courts should be chary about certification absent compelling reasons). Williams therefore resolved the interpretive issues for purposes of federal diversity litigation — at least unless the Louisiana Supreme Court later speaks differently.

## Conclusion

Louisiana’s statutory scheme for cancellations by premium finance companies is both precise and protective. The statute grants finance companies the power to request cancellation but requires a specified certification and documented notice sequence to preserve the insured’s cure opportunity and to fix the time of cancellation. The doctrine of strict adherence gives the statute teeth, invalidating cancellations that deviate from the prescribed steps. Williams v. GoAuto Insurance clarifies two contemporary issues: (1) a finance company’s corporate certification need not bear an individualized signature to be effective, and (2) electronic transmission that results in the insurer’s system taking possession satisfies the statutory “receipt” requirement. Williams does not weaken the strict-adherence rule; it merely adapts its application to modern business practices while preserving the statute’s core protective aims. Practitioners should therefore attend carefully to the statutory checklist, preserve contemporaneous records of transmission and receipt, and ensure that the four required certification elements are unmistakably present in any notice sent to the insurer.

# Text, Technology, and Tests: Interpreting “Certifying,” “Receipt,” and Methods of Delivery

This chapter examines how a modern statutory cancellation scheme accommodates contemporary communications and automated workflows. Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, a Fifth Circuit decision construing Louisiana’s cancellation statute, provides the immediate vehicle for analysis. That opinion confronts three interrelated interpretive problems that recur whenever statutory text drafted in a paper era meets automated business processes: (1) what does it mean for a premium finance company to “certify” the statutorily specified facts; (2) when has an insurer “received” a cancellation request; and (3) which modes of delivery—mail, private carrier, or electronic mail—satisfy the statute. The chapter integrates the statute, Louisiana appellate precedent, and principles from electronic-transactions law to propose workable doctrinal tests and practical safeguards that litigants and regulators can apply to new fact patterns.

Louisiana’s statutory baseline appears in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3). That provision permits a premium finance company to effect cancellation of an insured’s policy upon default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” a set of discrete, enumerated facts. The statute then provides that “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested . . . and the insurer may proceed to cancel such contract.” Interpreting those phrases in the age of servers, web services, and automated email dispatch is the task at hand.

## What “certifying” requires: text, context, and the signature question

The most immediate dispute in Williams was whether “certifying” requires a signature or the identification of a particular employee before a cancellation can be effective. A leading interpretive principle in Louisiana caselaw is “strict adherence” to the statutory scheme: courts will not treat a cancellation as effective if the finance company fails to follow the statutory steps. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993); Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990). The function of strict adherence, the appellate courts have explained, is to fix the exact time and afford the insured an opportunity to cure. See Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). Those objectives are legitimate and limit judicial tolerance for procedural shortcuts.

But strict adherence is not the same as judicial amendment of clear statutory language. The statute requires that the finance company send “a statement certifying” the listed facts; it does not say that the statement must be signed by a named individual or that it must bear a wet ink signature. Under orthodox textualism, a court should not add a requirement that the legislature omitted. Williams adopts that approach. The Fifth Circuit observed that Louisiana appellate opinions have described the statutory duty as requiring “a statement certifying compliance” (KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood) or have allowed cancellation “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement” (Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.). Only one appellate opinion, Benitez v. Elsayed, noted the absence of a signature as a shortcoming in a cancellation notice, but that court did not hold the absence of a signature alone would invalidate cancellation when other statutory elements were satisfied. Williams therefore concluded the statute does not require a signature; a corporate-level certification that the four statutory elements are satisfied will do, so long as the statement in fact communicates each statutory element.

From these materials the following doctrinal principles emerge. First, “certifying” means communicating, in a statement sent to the insurer, that the statutorily enumerated facts are true. The word imports an assertion by the finance company that the requisites have been met; it does not carry a gloss that the assertion must be accompanied by the name or signature of a particular employee. Second, the absence of a signature will be salient only to the extent the notice fails to perform its core functions: fixing the time and giving the insured (and other interested persons) the opportunity to cure. If the purported certification is otherwise deficient—if it does not identify the date the notice was sent to the insured, does not confirm that an actual ten-day notice went to the insured, or does not identify interest holders as the statute requires—the absence of a signature will be one factor supporting invalidation. If, however, the document clearly states the four statutory items and the finance company can show reliable evidence of transmission, courts should not add a signature requirement.

In short: certification must be substantive and complete; form alone (a missing signature) should not defeat an otherwise adequate statement. That is the limiting rule Williams adopts, and it reconciles the “strict adherence” requirement with the realities of corporate practice and automation.

## Survey of Louisiana appellate treatment of certification and content sufficiency

A survey of the relevant Louisiana cases shows two consistent themes: courts insist upon strict compliance with the statutory elements, and appellate decisions vary in how literal they are in policing form.

- Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins. (546 So. 2d 898) and KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood are often cited for the proposition that cancellation is effective “upon receipt” of a notice and a statement from the finance company. Those authorities emphasize that a statement of the necessary facts must accompany a notice of cancellation.

- Delatte, Eaglin, and Britten articulate the strict-adherence rule and explain why it matters—date certainty and cure opportunity—so courts will scrutinize whether the four statutorily required facts are present and demonstrably true.

- Benitez v. Elsayed is the most explicit appellate decision to comment adversely on the lack of a signature or named preparer; the Benitez court pointed to the absence of a named employee and signature as a shortcoming. But Benitez did not hold an unsigned certification is per se invalid; rather, the court treated the absence of an identifiable certifier as one of the deficiencies in a notice that failed on other grounds.

- Lewis v. Julien and other more recent panels parse content sufficiency and procedural compliance with the statute, but they do not mandate a signature.

Read together, the cases establish that courts will invalidate a cancellation when the finance company’s paperwork fails to show the legislatively required facts—especially the date the insured was notified and the identity (or absence) of third parties receiving notice. They do not, however, uniformly require an individual’s signature on the statement of certification.

Williams wraps these strands into a modern synthesis: certification can be a corporate act communicated electronically; but courts will enforce the statutory elements and the statute’s purposes.

## Modes of delivery: mail, “private carrier,” and “electronic mail”

The statute expressly authorizes three modes of delivery: depositing in the mail, delivery by a private carrier, or delivery “via electronic mail.” Historically, the first two covered the ways notice could physically move from sender to recipient; the addition of “via electronic mail” makes clear the legislature contemplated non-paper communications.

What does the statute mean by each mode? “Mail” remains what it always was; an affidavit of mailing and proof of postage will be strong evidence a notice was sent. “Private carrier” is less clear. The district court in Williams explored an imaginative reading of “private carrier,” but the Fifth Circuit declined to rely on that rationale and the Louisiana appellate courts have not provided a settled definition in this statutory context. Until a state high court defines the phrase, litigants should treat “private carrier” as covering recognized courier services that actually deliver paper to the insurer’s premises or an agent with authority to accept physical delivery.

“Electronic mail” plainly includes email transmissions and, under Williams, embraces server-to-server or web-service communications that function as the modern equivalent of sending a written document. That is, a finance company’s system that transmits an email or a programmatic request to an insurer’s designated web service may satisfy the statutory delivery requirement so long as the insurer’s system, not just the sender’s, receives the communication in a way that permits the insurer to fix the time and date of receipt.

These modal distinctions matter because the statute conditions cancellation on both sending (in the authorized mode) and the insurer’s receipt. Courts will therefore ask both whether the notice was sent by one of the authorized means and whether the insurer actually received it.

## Electronic transactions background: Louisiana UETA and relevant concepts

Interpretation of “electronic” modes and “receipt” is aided by the Louisiana Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA), codified at La. Rev. Stat. §§ 9:2601 et seq. UETA defines an “electronic record” (information created, sent, received, or stored in digital form) and an “electronic signature” (a process or symbol attached to an electronic record with the intent to sign). More importantly for this statute, UETA sets out principles for when an electronic record is “received.” Under UETA-style rules, electronic information is received when it enters the recipient’s designated information processing system in a form that the recipient can access and use.

Although Williams does not rely directly on UETA language, that framework provides useful interpretive tools. If the finance company’s transmission causes the notice to enter the insurer’s system—whether by email delivery to a monitored inbox or by a web-service call that the insurer’s servers accept and process—UETA principles support treating the insurer as having received the communication. Conversely, an undelivered email that bounces, or a transmission to an address the insurer did not designate or monitor, will not count as receipt.

UETA also helps with “electronic signature” issues. A finance company can adopt an electronic signature or an automated attestation in its systems to mark that a statement has been “certified.” A corporate attestation embedded in a transmitted electronic document—if it is intended by the sender to authenticate the communication and is reliably attributable to the finance company—can function as a valid electronic signature even though no human hand applied ink.

Thus, UETA supplies technological and doctrinal scaffolding: it supplies means to talk about server receipt, retrievability, and electronic attestation, all of which are relevant to how a court will evaluate whether a sent notice truly “certifies” and has been “received.”

## Defining “receipt” in practice: a functional test for insurer systems

Williams endorses a common-sense dictionary definition of “receipt” (“the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession”) and adapts it to electronic realities: receipt occurs when the insurer takes “possession, delivery, or custody” of a request for cancellation. Translating this into a durable test that a court can apply to diverse technologies yields the following practical three-part Receipt Test:

1. Acceptance into the insurer’s designated system: The notice must enter an insurer-controlled or insurer-designated information system (an email inbox the insurer monitors, a web service the insurer operates, or a database the insurer authorizes for receipt). Server-level delivery that triggers an insurer-controlled process satisfies this prong.

2. Retrievability and processing capacity: The communication must be stored in a form the insurer can access, retrieve, and process without reliance on the sender’s continued intervention. A message that has been received but is corrupted or cannot be retrieved by the insurer does not satisfy this prong.

3. Temporal certainty and traceability: The system must record a time-and-date stamp or other reliable audit trail showing when the communication entered the insurer’s system so the cancellation’s effective time can be fixed. If an insurer cancels before any such record exists, the insurer bears the burden of showing reliable contemporaneous evidence of receipt.

Applied to Williams, the insurer’s web-service listener and automated cancellation process satisfied the Receipt Test: APAC’s system sent an electronic cancellation request that triggered GoAuto’s web service; the web service “heard” the call and performed the cancellation; the insurer’s system therefore accepted and processed the communication. That server-level reception was sufficient even though no human signer had applied a signature stamp until later in the morning.

Important negative examples illustrate the borderline. If an email is sent to an insurer’s old or inactive address and bounces, there is no receipt; if a transmission is accepted by an intermediary but never forwarded to the insurer’s systems, courts will likely find no receipt by the insurer. If an insurer’s system receives the message but fails to retain or time-stamp it and later cannot show when it arrived, the insurer risks a finding that the cancellation cannot be reliably timed and therefore may be ineffective.

## The unresolved “private carrier” question and the risks of relying on non-email delivery

Williams did not reach a definitive definition of “private carrier,” and the Fifth Circuit expressly declined to adopt the district court’s expansive view. Why does that matter? Two reasons. First, where a finance company relies on physical delivery by a person (for example, an employee of APAC handing paper to a GoAuto receptionist), the legal characterization of the deliverer as the insurer’s agent, a private carrier, or neither will affect whether the insurer can be deemed to have “received” the notice. Second, absent clear case law, a plaintiff may challenge cancellations that rest upon ambiguous hand-delivery chains of custody.

Because Louisiana courts have not settled the boundaries of “private carrier” in this statutory setting, parties and practitioners should be cautious about relying on hand-delivery to marginal or informal recipients (for example, leaving documents in a shared filing room without a reliable registry or acceptance by an authorized insurer employee). Those practices invite litigation over whether the insurer actually received the notice and when. If a delivery relies on a recognized courier service with reliable proof-of-delivery records, litigation risk is lower. If it relies on informal hand-offs between employees of separate but affiliated companies, risk is higher.

The safest practice is to use delivery modes that (a) are expressly authorized by statute and (b) produce system-level evidence of receipt—email to monitored addresses, programmatic web-service calls, or certified mail with proof of mailing and proof of receipt—rather than ambiguous physical drops or shared filing cabinets without a documented chain of custody.

## Best-practice content and recordkeeping for the finance company’s certification

Williams emphasizes that content matters: “certifying” is not a formality; the certification must convey the statutorily required facts. Practitioners should therefore ensure that every cancellation communication includes, at minimum, the following elements in explicitly captioned form:

- A clear corporate attestation that the finance company is exercising the power of attorney and certifying compliance with the statutory prerequisites.

- An itemized recital of each of the four statutorily required facts: (i) that the premium finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney as required; (ii) that the agreement is in default and the default is uncured; (iii) that a ten-day (or applicable) notice of cancellation was sent to the insured on a specified date (include the date); and (iv) that copies of the notice were sent to each person shown in the premium finance agreement to have an interest, with names and addresses listed.

- An explicit statement identifying the date on which the finance company sent notice to the insured and the method(s) used to transmit those insured notices (e.g., first-class mail, certified mail, email, or personal delivery), and, where used, an affirmation that proof-of-mailing or affidavit evidence is available.

- If sending electronically, an embedded electronic attestation that functions as an electronic signature (a unique process or identifier showing intent to sign), plus an audit trail that links the electronic attestation to the transmission (time stamp, sender address or system account, message ID).

- Contact information and, where feasible, a unique transmission identifier or policy number that ties the sent notice to a particular insured and policy.

Recordkeeping should be robust. Maintain transmission logs, server logs showing delivery and receipt timestamps, copies of the notices sent to the insured (and proof of mailing where applicable), and any affidavit proofs. Where a web service is used, preserve the web-service logs showing the request and the insurer’s response. These records serve both to establish that the statutorily required statements were made and to fix the time of receipt for the insurer’s cancellation.

## Applying the tests to new fact patterns: practical examples

Consider three short hypotheticals that demonstrate how the doctrinal rules operate.

1. Automated web-service cancellation, no human signature: A finance company’s system makes a web-service call to the insurer’s listener that acknowledges receipt and triggers an automated cancellation at 1:02 a.m. The insurer’s system time-stamps the request and stores the request data. Later at 8:00 a.m., an employee applies a stamped signature to printed copies. Under the Receipt Test and Williams, the cancellation is effective because the insurer’s system accepted the request and recorded the time; the absence of an earlier human signature does not vitiate the certification so long as the transmitted statement contained the statutory attestations.

2. Email sent to an unmonitored address that bounces: A finance company emails a cancellation to cancellation@insurer-inc.com, but that address has been deactivated and the message bounces back. The finance company claims it “sent” the notice. Because the insurer did not receive the message, no cancellation can be effective. The sender’s mere transmission is insufficient; receipt into the insurer’s system is the critical event.

3. Paper notice left in a shared unlocked file cabinet that both companies access: APAC places a printed certification in a shared filing cabinet used by personnel of both companies, with no log or timestamp. The insurer cancels the policy that night. Later the insured sues and challenges whether the insurer “received” the cancellation. This is the sort of informal physical delivery that Williams warns is risky: absent proof that the insurer took possession or an authorized employee accepted the document, a court may find cancellation defective under the strict-adherence rule.

These examples show the importance of system-level evidence of receipt and complete content in the certification.

## Litigation posture and judicial deference: where courts will push back

Although courts will not invent a signature requirement, they will insist that the statutory elements be present and demonstrably true. Williams illustrates judicial restraint: the court refused to add a signature requirement where the certification otherwise complied, and it accepted server-level receipt as sufficient. But courts will invalidate cancellations when the certification is substantively incomplete (e.g., it fails to state the date the insured was given the ten-day notice), or when the sender cannot prove that the insurer received the statement.

Two admonitions follow. First, insurers should not assume automation is a panacea; they must ensure their systems generate reliable, time-stamped records and preserve them. Second, finance companies should not rely on ad hoc paper handoffs; where possible they should use delivery modes that produce neutral, auditable evidence of both transmission and receipt.

## Conclusion: balancing statutory fidelity with technological reality

Williams v. GoAuto harmonizes two truths: Louisiana’s requirement of strict adherence to cancellation procedures is rooted in protecting the insured’s cure opportunity and fixing cancellation time, and modern business processes increasingly accomplish those statutory aims through electronic means. Courts should therefore evaluate cancellations by substance rather than form. “Certifying” means asserting the statutory facts in a communicative act attributable to the finance company; a named human signature is not invariably necessary. “Receipt” occurs when the insurer’s designated systems take custody of the notice in a retrievable, time-stamped form. Parties who adhere to these principles—by including explicit statutory attestations, using reliable electronic delivery methods, and preserving contemporaneous logs and proofs—both minimize litigation risk and satisfy the statute’s protective purposes.

Practitioners and judges confronting new fact patterns should apply the Receipt Test and the content sufficiency principles described above: ask whether the insurer’s system actually accepted a certification that states each statutory element, whether the communication was retrievable and time-stamped, and whether the documentation permits the exact time of cancellation to be fixed. Where those elements exist, modern, automated cancellations should be upheld; where they do not, the traditional strict-adherence rule will remain an appropriate tool to protect insureds’ cure rights.

# Williams v. GoAuto Insurance: Applying the Statute to Automated Cancellations

This chapter uses Williams v. GoAuto Insurance, 5th Cir. Oct. 3, 2025, as the vehicle for examining how Louisiana’s premium-finance cancellation statute is applied to modern, automated cancellation systems. The opinion confronts three recurring questions in insurance-finance litigation: (1) what does it mean for a premium-finance company to “certify” compliance with statutory requirements—must a human sign the certification; (2) what counts as the insurer’s “receipt” of a cancellation request in an era of e‑mail and automated web services; and (3) when should a federal court certify a question of state law to the state supreme court? Williams is important not because it radically reshapes Louisiana law, but because it shows how courts reconcile the statute’s strict-adherence doctrine with contemporary business practices and how they avoid grafting new formalities onto clear statutory text.

## Factual and procedural setting

The operative facts are straightforward and typical of premium-finance disputes. Several GoAuto insureds financed their premiums through American Premium Assistance Company, LLC (APAC). Under each financing agreement APAC advanced the policy premium to GoAuto and, in exchange, received monthly installment payments from the insured. The financing agreements included a power of attorney authorizing APAC to cancel the policy upon an uncured default. When an insured missed a payment, APAC mailed or otherwise sent the insured a ten-day cancellation notice; if payment did not arrive by midnight on the tenth day APAC’s computer system automatically sent an e-mail to GoAuto containing (1) a copy of the ten‑day notice, and (2) a written request on APAC letterhead that “certif[ied]” the statutory facts (existence of the power of attorney, uncured default, mailing of the ten‑day notice, and copies sent to interested third parties). GoAuto’s systems were configured to cancel the policy upon receipt of that e-mail/web-service call. Later that same morning an APAC employee affixed a signature stamp to the printed cancellation request and a GoAuto employee stamped it “received” (or the documents were placed in a shared filing cabinet, depending on the period). Plaintiffs sued, but the sole issue decided at summary judgment was whether GoAuto’s cancellation procedures complied with Louisiana law. The district court concluded they did; the Fifth Circuit affirmed.

This posture—cross-motions on a pure question of statutory compliance—permits a close doctrinal analysis: if the statutory prerequisites are met, cancellation is effective and any derivative torts premised on an ineffective cancellation fail.

## The statutory framework and the rule of “strict adherence”

The relevant Louisiana statute allows a premium-finance company to request cancellation upon default “by sending to the insurer, by depositing in the mail or with a private carrier, or via electronic mail . . . a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” four enumerated items: (i) the financing agreement contains a valid power of attorney; (ii) the agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured; (iii) the ten‑day notice of cancellation was sent to the insured, specifying the date of sending; and (iv) copies of the ten‑day notice were sent to all persons shown by the agreement to have an interest in any loss under the policy, with identities and addresses specified. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv). The statute then says that “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company, the insurer shall consider that cancellation . . . has been requested” and may cancel the policy. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i).

Louisiana courts have long required “strict adherence” to these procedures when a finance company exercises its power of attorney to cancel. The cases emphasize that any defect in the statutory process can render a cancellation ineffective because the statute performs two key functions: (1) it fixes the precise time and date of cancellation, and (2) it guarantees the insured a minimum cure period before the policy lapses. See Delatte v. Lemotte, 633 So. 2d 686, 689 (La. App. Cir. 1993); Eaglin v. Champion Ins. Co., 558 So. 2d 284, 287 (La. App. Cir. 1990); Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). That background explains why litigation often turns on fine-grained questions of timing, content, and delivery.

But “strict adherence” is not the same as judicial lawmaking. Williams underscores an important interpretive boundary: courts must enforce the statute’s textual requirements stringently, but they may not read into the statute additional formalities that the legislature did not command.

## The first issue: does “certifying” require a signature?

The plaintiffs argued that the word “certifying” in the statute implies a signed statement—i.e., a human signature must appear on the certification before cancellation becomes effective. The Fifth Circuit rejected that contention.

Analysis and reasoning. The opinion first surveyed Louisiana appellate jurisprudence and found no controlling decision holding that “certifying” requires a signature. Some courts have paraphrased the statute as requiring “a statement certifying compliance” (KMJ Servs., Inc. v. Hood) or allowed cancellation “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement” (Hodges v. Colonial Lloyd’s Ins.). One decision criticized a notice that “is not signed by any individual” as part of a broader list of deficiencies (Benitez v. Elsayed), but it did not hold that absence of a signature alone invalidates cancellation. The Fifth Circuit therefore concluded there was no clear state-law rule imposing a signature requirement.

Turning to the statute’s text, the court observed that the statute speaks of the “insurance premium finance company” certifying the enumerated matters; it does not expressly require that an identifiable employee sign the statement. The court reasoned that the act of certifying can be accomplished by a corporate statement and that modern corporate processes often do not mirror the old practice of a named employee physically signing a paper. To add a signature requirement, the court said, would be to graft a non-textual formalism onto the statute in tension with the legislature’s phrasing. The Fifth Circuit therefore held that a notice that on its face “certifies” the required facts satisfies the “certifying” element even if no named individual physically signed at the time the insurer’s systems received the notice.

Doctrinal lesson. The Williams court applied a textualist, pragmatic approach: require compliance with the statute’s specified elements, but do not impose additional ceremony that the text does not demand. Strict adherence remains the rule, but courts will not presume the legislature meant to require a specific form of authentication (a handwritten or printed name and signature) in the absence of textual language to that effect.

Application to other fact patterns. The holding does not mean that signatures will never matter. Consider several hypotheticals:

- If the “statement certifying” omitted one of the statutory items (for example, failed to state that copies were sent to interested third parties), the cancellation would likely be ineffective under the strict-adherence doctrine. The statute specifies four items; each is material.

- If the certified statement is demonstrably false (e.g., it asserts that a ten‑day notice was mailed when it was not), the certification may be disregarded or the cancellation invalidated; falsity raises additional contract and tort issues (fraud, bad faith).

- If a financing company’s system produces a boilerplate email that says “certifies” but the company cannot produce evidence that the ten‑day notice was in fact sent, the absence of corroborating proof (mailing affidavit, server logs, etc.) may doom the cancellation.

- Conversely, if a company produces contemporaneous, reliable electronic records showing the required acts (e.g., mailing logs, e‑mail traces, server timestamps), courts are likely to treat the corporate certification as sufficient even without a person’s signature.

Practical takeaways. Although Williams permits unsigned corporate certifications, prudent practice suggests including clear corporate attestations and retaining objective, contemporaneous proof (affidavits of mailing, mail logs, e‑mail headers, server logs, and stamped “received” artifacts). For litigation risk management, a finance company should be able to show the date and time of the notice and the transmission to the insurer; a signature is useful but not indispensable under Williams.

## The second issue: what constitutes “receipt” by the insurer?

The statute makes cancellation effective only “upon receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” The plaintiffs argued that “receipt” requires the insurer or its agent to take physical possession of the cancellation request before the insurer may cancel the policy. Because GoAuto’s systems automatically canceled the policy when an APAC e-mail or web-service call was received—before any human inspected or affixed a signature to the printed documents—the plaintiffs contended the insurer did not actually “receive” the request before cancellation.

Analysis and reasoning. The Fifth Circuit began with basic dictionary definitions of “receipt” and “receive”: the act of taking something, especially physical possession, and to come into possession of something from an outside source. The court accepted that those ordinary meanings are consistent with a requirement that the insurer take possession, delivery, or custody of the cancellation request.

But the court then applied those meanings to contemporary business operations. It found that GoAuto’s computer systems were equipped to accept and process an incoming e-mail or web-service call and that the web-service listener “took” or “accepted” the request when the APAC system called it. The web service’s receipt triggered the automated cancellation. For the court, the insurer’s server functions as a “listener” that is always ready to be called; when it receives the request it has taken possession of the communication in a manner functionally equivalent to physical delivery of a letter to a clerk. The court observed that this is a modern statute applied in a modern business context and concluded that a server-level receipt and automated processing satisfy the statutory receipt requirement.

The court also noted, but did not rely on, the district court’s alternative rationale—that the employee who physically delivered or stamped the notice fit the statute’s phrase “private carrier.” The appellate court declined to decide whether the delivery person was a private carrier because it disposed of the appeal on the basis that the electronic delivery constituted receipt.

Doctrinal lesson. Williams modernizes the concept of “receipt.” The statutory function—establishing a fixed time/date for cancellation and assuring the insured a cure period—can be fulfilled by electronic delivery so long as the insurer, through its systems, actually comes into possession or custody of the communication. Courts will look to whether the insurer’s systems took the communication in a reliable, traceable way that can be used to establish timing and content.

Application to other fact patterns. Several hypothetical scenarios illustrate the contours of the court’s ruling:

- E-mail accepted by insurer’s server but auto-responded with “mailbox full”: If the server accepted the message (i.e., did not bounce it), that should satisfy receipt even if a human later never opens it. If the server bounced the message, however, there is no receipt.

- Web-service call that returns an error code: If the call is properly received and acknowledged by the insurer’s web service (e.g., responds with a success code and logs the request), receipt is established. If the call times out or returns an error and the insurer’s system never persists the request, there is no receipt.

- Transmission to a shared third-party mailbox not controlled by the insurer: The facts matter. If the shared mailbox is under the insurer’s custody or part of an integrated system the insurer uses to process cancellations (even if shared with the finance company), courts will scrutinize whether the insurer actually controlled or possessed the mailbox contents at the relevant time. Williams indicates courts will focus on whether the insurer’s systems were the place of delivery and whether the insurer had the ability to accept and act on the communication.

- Delayed human review before cancellation: Where an insurer’s system does not auto-cancel and waits for human review, “receipt” will be the moment the insurer—or its agent designated to receive—actually takes possession.

Evidence and proof issues. Because the statute’s goal is to fix timing, proof of receipt will frequently hinge on electronic records: server logs, timestamps, delivery confirmations, exchange/SMTP logs, web-service call traces, and audit trails. These records should be preserved and produced in litigation; they are the modern equivalent of a stamped “received” letter.

Practical takeaways. Insurers and finance companies should ensure that electronic delivery pathways are robust, logged, and documented. Finance companies should require delivery acknowledgments or acceptance codes when using web services. Insurers should configure systems to retain immutable audit logs establishing the time and content of incoming cancellation requests, because those logs will often be dispositive.

## The district court’s alternative “private carrier” rationale

The district court offered an alternative ground—that the person who physically carried or stamped the documents fit within the statute’s “private carrier” delivery option. The Fifth Circuit acknowledged this rationale but did not address its validity because the court disposed of the appeal on the basis of electronic receipt. The appellate court flagged the district court’s approach as “imaginative and unconventional” and declined to adopt or endorse its expansive view of “private carrier.” The careful reader should note two points: first, alternative rationales that are unnecessary to the judgment are not binding; second, the classification of particular delivery methods as “private carrier” is a fact-bound question that can generate its own litigation.

## Certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court: when and why federal courts send questions home

Plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify the disputed interpretive issues to the Louisiana Supreme Court. Certification allows a federal court to ask the state’s highest court to resolve unsettled state-law questions. The Fifth Circuit declined, applying its own precedent: certification is reserved for exceptional circumstances; mere absence of state-court precedent on a term is not enough. See Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245, 1247 (5th Cir. 1997). The appellate court emphasized a conservative approach to certification—one that preserves the tool for genuinely novel or potentially state-defining questions where federal resolution risks deviating from how the state’s courts would decide.

Doctrinal lesson. A federal court asked to certify should show that (1) the question is determinative of the case and (2) there is no controlling state precedent, and (3) resolution by federal courts would be uncertain or likely to differ from the state supreme court’s approach in a way that justifies certification. Mere novelty or the plaintiffs’ interest in state clarification is insufficient.

Practice note. Parties seeking certification should explain why the question is outcome-determinative, why existing state-law materials (cases, statutes, legislative history) do not furnish an answer, and why the question would have statewide significance that merits the Louisiana Supreme Court’s attention.

## Effect on derivative claims: misrepresentation and bad-faith theories

Because the district court and the Fifth Circuit concluded GoAuto’s cancellations complied with the statute, the plaintiffs’ derivative claims—misrepresentation and breach of good faith premised on an ineffective cancellation—failed. An important procedural lesson follows: when statutory compliance resolves the threshold issue of legal cancellation, ensuing tort claims that rely on the cancellation’s invalidity typically cannot survive summary judgment.

This dynamic highlights litigation strategy: plaintiffs who challenge a cancellation should assemble evidence that undermines statutory compliance (timing, content, receipt, or truthfulness of the certification). Defendants should show contemporaneous records establishing each statutory element.

## Practical compliance checklist and litigation-proofing

Williams suggests a practical set of best practices for finance companies and insurers operating in Louisiana:

- Ensure the financing agreement expressly contains the power-of-attorney language contemplated by La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i).

- When sending the insured’s ten‑day notice, retain contemporaneous proof of mailing or delivery (certified mail returns, affidavits of mailing, certified e-mail receipts).

- When notifying the insurer, include an explicit, prominent corporate “statement certifying” the four statutory elements. Although a human signature is not required by Williams, including an identifiable signer (name, title, date) reduces the chance of litigation over authenticity.

- Use delivery systems that produce durable, tamper-evident logs: SMTP headers, message‑ID and delivery logs, web-service request and response records, timestamps, and return codes. Configure systems to persist these logs without modification.

- Maintain an audit trail showing the moment the insurer’s systems accepted the communication (server receipt) and the moment the system processed the cancellation.

- If humans remain involved (printing, stamping, storing), ensure chain-of-custody procedures are clear and that the insurer’s access or control over the storage is well documented.

- When possible, obtain delivery acknowledgments from the insurer’s system (success codes, receipts), not merely “sent” logs on the finance company side.

## Concluding doctrinal observations

Williams v. GoAuto is a useful example of how courts apply old statutory principles—here, Louisiana’s strict-adherence rule—to modern technology. The opinion stresses two complementary points. First, courts will enforce the statute’s enumerated requirements strictly; failure to satisfy the statutory items can render a cancellation ineffective. Second, courts will not invent textual requirements—like a human signature—unless the statute demands them. The decision modernizes “receipt,” treating server-level acceptance and web‑service calls as functional equivalents of physical delivery so long as the insurer’s systems have taken possession in a way that fixes timing and content.

For students and practitioners, Williams is a reminder that statutory interpretation must balance fidelity to text with the realities of contemporary business practices. Effective compliance turns on documentary proof: whether signatures are required may be debatable, but the presence of immutable, contemporaneous records showing the statutorily required acts will determine outcomes in litigation.

# Practice, Proof, and Future Fights: Compliance Programs and Litigation After Williams

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is a short opinion with outsized practical consequences. The Fifth Circuit’s decision confronts a recurring issue in Louisiana insurance law—the point at which a premium-finance company’s act of “certifying” a cancellation has completed the statutory prerequisites and the insurer may treat the policy as cancelled. The court resolved that (1) the statute’s requirement that a premium finance company “certify” certain facts in a notice does not, as a matter of Louisiana law, require a handwritten signature or the identification of a particular employee; and (2) an insurer’s automated receipt of an electronic notice—here, an e-mail or a web-service call that triggers cancellation—constitutes “receipt” within the meaning of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3). The district court’s judgment for the insurer was therefore affirmed.

This chapter translates Williams into operational rules and litigation strategy. It explains what companies should do now to reduce litigation risk, what insurers should do to make cancellations defensible, which categories of evidence best carry a party over the summary-judgment bar, and which questions Williams leaves open for future appellate or state-court resolution.

## Operational compliance for finance companies

Williams underscores that strict adherence to La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) remains the controlling standard. For premium finance companies, the safe compliance posture is to design processes that make it mechanically impossible to omit the statutory elements and that generate durable, auditable records.

At a minimum, a compliant protocol should do the following consistently and contemporaneously when a policyholder defaults:

- Generate and retain a Ten-Day Notice to the insured that plainly states the date sent and the deadline for cure, with a contemporaneous record of the issuance method (e.g., certificate of mailing, tracking number, or electronic delivery confirmation).
- Prepare a notice to the insurer that explicitly “certifies” each statutory element—use the statute’s language (or a close paraphrase) and include each subsection (i)–(iv) in discrete, numbered statements so the certification is unmistakable.
- Include with the insurer notice copies of the Ten‑Day Notice sent to the insured and retain supporting proof of mailing/sending for that notice (e.g., USPS Certificate of Mailing, commercial carrier tracking, or an electronic delivery receipt).
- Identify, enumerate, and document all persons shown on the finance agreement to have an interest in potential losses (governmental agencies, lienholders, third‑party beneficiaries) and effect service or forwarding of copies of the Ten‑Day Notice to those entities with contemporaneous proof of transmission.
- Preserve audit metadata showing who, when, and how the notice was generated and transmitted; where practical, use digital signatures (or documented corporate-process certifications) to support attribution even when a handwritten signature is not required.

Those practices are not merely bureaucratic luxuries. Williams accepts corporate certification in place of employee-signed statements, but it also emphasizes fidelity to the statute’s text. That means courts will expect the notices to contain the precise factual assertions the statute requires, accompanied by proof that those assertions were in fact true at the time of transmission.

## Operational compliance for insurers

Williams validates that an insurer may rely on automated mechanisms to cancel coverage after it has “received” the finance company’s certification via electronic means. That convenience carries responsibilities: insurers must treat system events as legal acts and therefore must design systems that create reliable, contemporaneous evidence of receipt and triggering.

Insurer compliance programs should include:

- Designation in policy documentation and internal procedures of the systems and endpoints through which notices from premium finance companies will be received (for example, a monitored e‑mail address or a web service/API endpoint), with specification of acceptable formats and message content.
- Robust logging and time‑stamping of inbound notices, including preserved copies of raw inbound messages (complete email headers, MIME parts), web-service call logs (request and response bodies), and system-level artifacts showing the date/time an inbound event was accepted.
- An auditable linkage between receipt and action: the automated cancellation event should be tied to the inbound record by unique identifiers (policy number, request ID), and the system should record both the moment the request was received and the moment the cancellation was executed.
- Retention, indexing, and discoverability of the electronic artifacts in the normal course of business so the insurer can produce them in litigation without reconstruction.
- Defined roles and procedures for manual review where necessary (for example, when a receipt is disputed) and for reconciling any discrepancies between automated events and later human processing.

In short, if an insurer’s systems will cancel upon electronic notice, those systems must create the documentary footprint that courts will expect to see when the validity of a cancellation is litigated.

## Proof for strict compliance at summary judgment

Williams makes clear that courts evaluating summary judgment will look beyond high-level assertions to technical and documentary evidence that the statutory steps were performed. A party seeking summary judgment on the question of statutory compliance should marshal proof that is both technical and fact-specific; generalities will not substitute for contemporary logs and affidavits.

Effective evidence packages typically include the following categories:

- System architecture and process descriptions: concise declarations from IT or system architects explaining the automated flow—how the finance company’s system transmits a notice (e‑mail or API), how the insurer’s server receives and processes it, and how the cancellation trigger is executed. These declarations should describe the systems’ design, retention policies, and normal operating behaviors.
- Server and application logs: raw e-mail headers and bodies, SMTP/IMAP/Exchange logs, web-service request and response logs, and application event logs that show a receipt timestamp and an action timestamp. Exported logs should include timezones and be explained by a witness to ensure court comprehension.
- Affidavits or declarations from knowledgeable witnesses: sworn statements from finance-company officers verifying the content and timing of the Ten‑Day Notice mailings, and from insurer personnel explaining receipt and processing. These declarations should identify the declarant’s role and the basis of their knowledge (e.g., “I oversaw APAC’s notice system and have access to production logs”).
- Copies of the notices themselves: the Ten‑Day Notice to the insured, the certification notice to the insurer, and any attachments; where the statute requires copies sent to third parties, include the receipts or proof of those transmissions.
- Proof of mailing/transmission: USPS certificates of mailing, return receipts, commercial-carrier tracking records, or electronic delivery receipts; where a finance company used mass-mailing vendors, include vendor logs and affidavits describing the vendor’s process.
- Chain-of-custody and retention documentation: records showing how electronic artifacts are retained, where backups reside, and why the copies produced are authentic and unaltered.
- Reproduction of the cancellation transaction in a controlled forensic export: a time‑stamped, read-only audit trail exported by a neutral agent or system admin, accompanied by a hash or similar integrity check where feasible.

When these elements are assembled and explained in admissible sworn form (or with witness deposition testimony corroborating them), a court will have the factual record to determine whether the statute’s procedural prerequisites were met.

## Avoiding Benitez‑type pitfalls

Benitez v. Elsayed (a Louisiana appellate decision cited in Williams) illustrates common documentary pitfalls that can doom a cancellation: a notice that lacks express certification language, fails to identify recipients or dates, or is unsigned and otherwise ambiguous as to the asserting party. Williams does not make a signature mandatory, but it does show that omissions of statutory particulars remain perilous.

To avoid a Benitez-style failure, a premium finance company should ensure that every insurance-statement addressed to an insurer contains all of the following, in clear and unambiguous terms:

- An express statement that the sender is a premium finance company and is “certifying” the listed facts.
- A discrete, numbered listing mirroring § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv) so a reader can see each statutory element separately addressed (e.g., a parenthetical identifying the valid power of attorney; a statement of default; the date the Ten‑Day Notice was sent; the names and addresses of interested third parties).
- Specific dates (not relative terms like “recently” or “on or about”) for the Ten‑Day Notice transmission and, where applicable, for the attempted service to interest holders.
- Attachments or links to the actual Ten‑Day Notice and the proof of its dispatch.
- Corporate attribution—e.g., a line such as “American Premium Assistance Company, LLC, by its authorized representative” or a corporate seal—rather than relying on the absence of a signature to suggest corporate authorship.

The key lesson from Benitez and Williams is that the statute demands content, not ceremony. A document that fails to say what the statute requires invites an opponent to argue that the statute’s strictness has not been satisfied.

## Integrating UETA principles into recordkeeping and process design

Williams is attentive to the realities of modern, electronic commerce. Where cancellation notices are sent and received electronically, the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA) and related federal principles (e.g., E‑SIGN) provide persuasive rules for treating electronic transmissions and records as legally operative and admissible.

Practically, parties should do the following to align their processes with UETA principles:

- Obtain or document a party’s consent to transact electronically where practicable. Although UETA validates electronic records in many contexts without express contract language, explicit agreement eliminates ambiguity. This may be done in policy paperwork or finance agreements (e.g., “We agree APAC may send notices to insurer electronically at the e‑mail address on file.”).
- Designate addresses and endpoints for receipt. For example, identify a specific e‑mail address or API endpoint in the finance agreement or in an operational addendum so that both parties understand where legally operative notices may be sent.
- Ensure records are retrievable in the ordinary course of business. UETA requires that electronic records are able to be reproduced for subsequent reference. This means log retention policies, backup systems, and discovery readiness are practical compliance tools.
- Use technologies that support attribution and integrity. Digital signatures, transport-layer security (TLS), signed e-mail standards (DKIM/SPF), API tokens, or mutual TLS for web services make it easier to establish who sent a notice and when it was received.
- Maintain contemporaneous evidence of the parties’ deployment of electronic systems (e.g., SOPs, onboarding checklists, and configuration records), which will help prove assent to electronic transactions if litigated.

UETA alignment does not replace the need to satisfy the statute’s substantive prerequisites, but it helps make electronic notices legally durable and admissible.

## Litigation strategies: framing “receipt,” timing, and authenticity

Williams provides both offensive and defensive playbooks. Plaintiffs challenging cancellations will attack timing, authenticity, and substantive compliance; defendants will want to use technical records to neutralize those attacks.

Tactics for defendants (finance companies and insurers):

- Foreclose the signature issue early. Use a corporate certification statement and an affidavit from a senior official explaining the company’s process and corporate liability for the notice. Williams signals courts will accept corporate attestation where the statutory language points to the company as actor.
- Produce native-format electronic records with full metadata rather than mere PDFs. Courts and opposing counsel quibble less about authenticity when they can see headers, timestamps, and original message IDs.
- Offer expert IT or electronic-records testimony to explain logs, time zones, idempotency of web‑service calls, and how the system treats duplicate or late-inbound messages.
- Cross-index cancellation triggers with independent records (e.g., the insurer’s cancellation event audit trail, customer-account history, and collections logs) to show the cancellation was the result of an identifiable inbound event rather than ad hoc action.

Tactics for plaintiffs:

- Challenge chain of custody and authenticity where logs are thin. Ask for raw server logs, message queue dumps, and backup retention records. If a defendant produces only summary prints, argue that the absence of original logs raises an issue of material fact.
- Focus on meaningfully missing statutory elements. For example, if the defendant’s notice says copies of Ten‑Day Notices are “available upon request,” press whether those copies were in fact sent and whether the insurer knew the date of sending—the statute requires the certification to specify the date.
- Exploit any gap between automatic cancellation and human corroboration. Williams accepts automated receipt, but an argument can be made when automated cancellation is triggered before the finance company can have physically completed its mailing obligations or before proof-of-mailing is available.
- Use e‑discovery to obtain vendor records. Many finance companies outsource mailings or e‑mailing; where vendor records are not produced, request depositions or subpoena records to test the company’s proof-of-sending assertions.

Across both lines, technical testimony is the core battlefield. A well‑prepared system-declaration that explains logs, time synchronization (NTP), and retention policies is often decisive on summary judgment.

## Open questions and likely future fights

Williams resolved two central issues in the Fifth Circuit, but it leaves a number of doctrinal and practical questions open. Attorneys and compliance officers should expect further litigation on these fronts:

- The meaning and scope of “private carrier.” The district court had attempted an analogy that Williams explicitly declined to adopt. Lower courts and litigants will press for a clearer definition: is a physical person delivering paper to a shared filing room a “private carrier”? Does an electronic intermediary count? Until the Louisiana Supreme Court or the legislature clarifies, this will be an ambiguous area.
- Whether courts may require identification of a responsible corporate officer or employee in any case. Williams permits corporate certification without a named signatory, but other courts may reach different conclusions depending on the case-specific record or on policy arguments about accountability and fraud prevention.
- The sufficiency of “available on request” proof-of-mailing language. Williams notes that APAC’s statements said that affidavits of mailing were “available on request.” Whether that practice will survive closer scrutiny when a plaintiff shows the insurer cancelled before proof of mailing was actually created is an unresolved evidentiary line.
- The applicability of UETA to disputes of this kind. Williams leans toward treating electronic receipts as “receipt,” but future litigation may turn on whether parties expressly agreed to transact electronically and how statutes of frauds or other specific statutory schemes interact with UETA.
- Possible administrative or legislative guidance. Given the potential for recurring disputes and the importance of clear commercial practices, the Louisiana legislature or state insurance regulators could adopt implementing regulations—particularly to define “receipt,” “private carrier,” or the level of identification required on certification notices.

These open questions frame the contours of future appellate review and possible state-law fixes. Practitioners should monitor Louisiana appellate dockets and administrative guidance for developments.

## Significance of Williams within the statutory and technological evolution of cancellation law

Williams is significant because it operationalizes a principle courts have expressed for decades: Louisiana demands “strict adherence” to the statutory cancellation process, but courts should not import additional formalities that the statute does not require. Williams applies that principle in an electronic age. Its principal takeaways are threefold.

First, the opinion modernizes “receipt.” Where a finance company’s electronic transmission can be authenticated and is received by an insurer’s system, that receipt suffices to trigger cancellation. This is a predictable result that aligns statutory interpretation with common business practice: systems that communicate via e‑mail or APIs can carry legal force.

Second, Williams rejects the judicial imposition of a signature requirement in the absence of textual support. Although signature and identification remain safe practices, the court recognized that the statute speaks of the finance company’s certification as the operative act and that corporate attestation can satisfy that command.

Third, the decision emphasizes that “strict adherence” means fidelity to the statute’s content requirements rather than adding ceremonial requirements. Courts will expect the required facts to be certified explicitly and proven; they will not, however, do what the legislature did not ask for by demanding a signature or a named employee by rote.

Practically, the decision should reduce coverage litigation where companies have robust electronic logs and process discipline. It also puts a premium on the kinds of contemporaneous evidence that courts will prize: native-format transmissions, server logs, time-stamped cancellations, and clear proof-of-mailing to insureds and interest holders. For those who lack such systems, the risk of litigation remains acute.

Conclusion

Williams v. GoAuto is a guidepost for companies, insurers, and litigators operating at the intersection of statute-driven formalities and automated processes. It reiterates the requirement of strict adherence to statutory elements, validates corporate certification and electronic receipt, and dissuades courts from inventing signature formalities not found in the statute. The practical tasks are now clear: premium finance companies must produce crisp, content-complete certification notices and contemporaneous proof of Ten‑Day Notice mailings; insurers must build auditable pipelines for receiving and retaining electronic notices; and litigants must marshal technical, contemporaneous evidence to win summary judgment. Where Williams leaves gaps—private carrier, the sufficiency of “available on request” language, the desirability of an identified human certifier—future cases and perhaps legislative clarification will supply answers. Until then, adherence to the statute’s words and the creation of durable electronic records will best protect both coverage certainty and litigable fairness.
