Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Williams v. GoAuto Insurance

Chapter 3: # Williams v. GoAuto Insurance: Electronic Receipt and Corporate Certification

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance is a contemporary decision about how a Louisiana statute governing cancellations of financed insurance policies operates in an age of automated communications and corporate information systems. The Fifth Circuit’s opinion provides a concise, practical application of two contested statutory terms—“certifying” and “receipt”—in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3). It illustrates how courts balance the doctrinal command of “strict adherence” to statutory cancellation procedures against the limit that courts may not add extra-textual requirements (such as a handwritten signature) when the statute itself is silent. The decision is therefore useful both for understanding Louisiana’s premium-finance cancellation rules and for working through how state statutory language interacts with modern, automated business processes.

This chapter explains the facts and workflow at issue, extracts the legal rules the court applied, situates the opinion among Louisiana precedent, and sets out practical guidance for applying § 9:3550(G)(3) to new fact patterns.

## Factual workflow: APAC’s ten‑day notice, the automated e‑mail, and GoAuto’s web service

The operative factual sequence in Williams is straightforward and emblematic of automated corporate processes:

- APAC (the premium finance company) finances an insured’s premium and has a power of attorney authorizing cancellation if the insured defaults.
- When an insured misses a payment, APAC sends the insured a ten‑day cancellation notice warning that the policy will be cancelled if payment is not received within ten days.
- If the insured does not cure the default by midnight of the tenth day, APAC’s computer system automatically sends an e‑mail to GoAuto. That e‑mail contains (a) a copy of the ten‑day notice and (b) a four‑part certification statement that mirrors the requirements of La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv): (1) the finance agreement contains a valid power of attorney; (2) the agreement is in default and the default has not been timely cured; (3) a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured specifying the date; and (4) copies of the ten‑day notice were sent to other interested parties as required.
- GoAuto’s information system is configured to treat receipt of that e‑mail (the web service call) as a cancellation request and to cancel the policy automatically when the e‑mail arrives.
- Later that morning a human employee prints the documents and affixes a signature stamp to the printout; the physical documents are placed in a shared filing cabinet (or otherwise marked “received” in later practice).

The plaintiffs challenged the legal sufficiency of those procedures on two principal grounds: (1) the statutory requirement that the finance company “certif[y]” the items in § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) implicitly requires a human signature on the certification before cancellation occurs, and (2) the insurer must actually obtain physical possession of the written cancellation request before cancellation—mere electronic submission to a web service that cancels automatically does not qualify as “receipt” by the insurer.

The Fifth Circuit rejected both arguments.

## Issue 1 — Does “certifying” require a human signature before cancellation?

The statute requires the premium finance company to send the insurer “a copy of the notice of cancellation together with a statement certifying” the four enumerated matters. La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a)(i)–(iv). Plaintiffs argued that “certifying” implies a signature: because GoAuto’s policy was cancelled automatically by its web service before any human signed the printed certification, the certification element was not satisfied at the moment of cancellation.

Textual approach and silence. The Fifth Circuit began (appropriately) with the statutory text. The statute requires a company to send “a statement certifying” specified facts; it does not mention a signature or identify any particular manner of attestation. The court stressed that the text names the insurance premium finance company—not an individual employee—as the actor that “certif[ies].” Where a statute is silent about formalities such as a signature, courts should not add requirements the legislature did not enact. The court put it plainly: “Strict adherence” to a statute does not allow courts to judicially import formalities—like a handwritten signature—absent textual support.

Corporate certification and who may certify. The court treated the finance company’s written statement—on APAC letterhead and expressly stating the four certification items—as an act of corporate-level certification. A corporate statement can satisfy a requirement that the company “certif[y]” facts so long as the statement is sufficiently clear and unambiguous to constitute the company’s attestation to the enumerated items. The Fifth Circuit contrasted Williams with the Benitez opinion (a Louisiana appellate decision) that had observed the absence of an individual’s name or signature as one shortcoming among others. Benitez did not rule that lack of signature alone rendered a cancellation ineffective; it merely noted the lack of an individual name and signature in rejecting the adequacy of notice in that case. Williams therefore stands for a narrower proposition: an unsigned corporate statement that unmistakably certifies the required matters can satisfy the statute.

Function of “strict adherence.” Louisiana courts require “strict adherence” to the statutory mechanics for finance-company cancellations because the statutory procedure serves two articulated functions: (1) to fix the exact time and date of cancellation, and (2) to grant a minimum period during which the default may be cured. See Britten v. Reavis, 503 So. 2d 1149, 1154 (La. App. Cir. 1987). The Fifth Circuit emphasized that strict adherence is meant to police the statutory steps, not to add extra-tech formalities. If the required items—power of attorney, uncured default, statement that the ten‑day notice was sent specifying the date, and notice to interested parties—are all present in the company’s transmitted statement, the statutory certification requirement is fulfilled even if no human hand signed at the moment the insurer’s automated system effectuated cancellation.

How to analyze “certifying” in new fact patterns. When confronted with a dispute about whether a finance company “certified” as required, do the following:

- Identify whether the transmitted statement contains the four required elements with sufficient specificity (POA, uncured default, date of the ten‑day notice, and notice to interested parties).
- Ask whether the statement is presented as the finance company’s attestation (company letterhead, explicit “APAC certifies that …,” or equivalent language).
- Consider whether any additional defects exist—e.g., the statement is ambiguous, materially false, or not actually transmitted.
- Be cautious about assuming a signature requirement where the statute is silent; instead, focus on whether the content and form of the statement establish corporate attestation.

Thus Williams instructs that “certify” is satisfied by a clear, corporately-originated written attestation that conveys the required facts, even if that attestation is unsigned by an individual.

## Issue 2 — What counts as “receipt” by the insurer?

The statute conditions the insurer’s power to cancel upon “receipt of such notice of cancellation and statement from the premium finance company.” La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(b)(i). The plaintiffs argued that “receipt” requires physical possession by an actual GoAuto employee and that the insurer may not cancel until such a person has obtained and reviewed the written request.

Ordinary meaning and modern systems. The Fifth Circuit turned to ordinary legal definitions—Black’s Law Dictionary—to determine the meaning of “receipt” and “receive.” Those definitions include “the act of receiving something, especially by taking physical possession” and “to take (something offered, given, sent, etc.); to come into possession of.” The court accepted that “receipt” involves coming into possession or custody, but it also recognized that modern companies use automated systems to receive and process communications. A server or web service that accepts an incoming request and triggers a cancellation performs the functional equivalent of taking possession for a corporate recipient.

Application to GoAuto’s web service. GoAuto’s system architecture was critical: APAC’s automated process called a GoAuto web service and the web service was “always listening.” On receipt of a properly formed request, the web service immediately performed the cancellation. The Fifth Circuit concluded that an insurer may claim “receipt” when its information system receives the cancellation request and takes custody or control—here, when GoAuto’s server accepted the e‑mail/web service call and the system cancelled the policy.

Timing and consequences. Because cancellation was triggered at the moment the web service accepted the request, the court treated the insurer as having timely “received” the notice at that point. The subsequent human actions—printing, stamping, or signing—were procedural housekeeping that did not negate the earlier electronic receipt. In other words, when a system designed and controlled by the insurer accepts a transmitted cancellation request, the insurer has received the notice even though no human had yet physically reviewed or signed the printed document. The statutory purpose—to fix the time of cancellation and give a cure period—was served by the timestamped electronic transaction.

How to analyze “receipt” in new fact patterns. To determine whether an insurer has “received” a cancellation request under § 9:3550(G)(3):

- Identify the method of transmission and the recipient’s configured acceptance point (mailroom, courier delivery, e‑mail inbox, server/web service, private carrier).
- Determine whether the insurer’s system actually took possession or control—e.g., server logs show connection and acceptance, or an employee physically received a package.
- Evaluate whether the system that “received” the notice is under the insurer’s procedural control and is the mechanism by which the insurer processes such requests.
- If the insurer’s systems automatically act on incoming notices (cancel, record, update), electronic acceptance is functionally equivalent to receipt.

Thus, Williams recognizes electronic receipt by an insurer’s systems as sufficient to satisfy the statute’s “receipt” requirement.

## District court’s alternative “private carrier” rationale and the Fifth Circuit’s choice to decide on electronic receipt grounds

At summary judgment, the district court offered an alternative theory: APAC’s employee who physically delivered printed notices in the shared filing area fit within the statute’s phrase “depositing in the mail or with a private carrier,” so cancellation could be justified on the private‑carrier theory. The Fifth Circuit declined to adopt or evaluate this novel “private carrier” theory because it found the statute satisfied on the electronic receipt ground. The appellate court thought the district court’s private‑carrier analysis rested on unconventional analogies and unnecessary interpretive leaps, and therefore did not need to reach that route.

This posture reflects a common appellate approach: resolve the case on the narrowest, defensible legal ground available—here, the acceptance of electronic receipt—rather than adopt a broader or novel theory unnecessary to the holding.

## Certification to the Louisiana Supreme Court declined: standards and Erie methodology

Plaintiffs asked the Fifth Circuit to certify questions of Louisiana law to the Louisiana Supreme Court. Federal appellate courts may certify unsettled questions of state law when state law is ambiguous and certification would be helpful. But the Fifth Circuit declined, citing its guarded approach to certification and the standard articulated in Jefferson v. Lead Indus. Ass’n, 106 F.3d 1245 (5th Cir. 1997): the availability of certification is a valuable but narrow tool; mere absence of state‑supreme‑court precedent does not automatically warrant certification.

In the federal courts’ Erie-era practice, a federal court interpreting state law should attempt to predict how the state’s highest court would rule. Certification is appropriate when the state court would likely welcome guidance or where the question is novel and of substantial importance and certification would materially reduce the risk of an erroneous prediction. Here, the Fifth Circuit believed it could responsibly resolve the questions from existing statutes and intermediate appellate decisions without certification. The court therefore applied ordinary canons of construction, state intermediate appellate decisions, and the statutory text to reach conclusions it deemed faithful to Louisiana law.

## Holding and doctrinal takeaways

The Fifth Circuit affirmed summary judgment for GoAuto. Its controlling doctrinal conclusions are:

- A written corporate statement that plainly “certif[ies]” the facts enumerated in La. Rev. Stat. § 9:3550(G)(3)(a) satisfies the statutory certification requirement even if it bears no individual’s handwritten signature at the precise moment the insurer’s systems effect cancellation. The statute’s silence on signatures means courts should not judicially add such a formal requirement.
- “Receipt” by the insurer occurs when the insurer (or its system) takes possession, control, or custody of the cancellation notice; modern electronic acceptance by a web service or server constitutes “receipt” if the system is the insurer’s mechanism for accepting such requests and acts upon them.
- The doctrine of strict adherence to the statutory cancellation procedure is a constraint on judicial permissiveness about missed steps, but it is not a license to impose extra‑textual formalisms.

These principles reconcile the statute’s protective purposes—the fixing of the cancellation time and the guarantee of a cure period—with the realities of automated corporate operations.

## Practical implications and compliance checklist

Williams has immediate practical value for premium finance companies, insurers, and counsel. To minimize litigation risk under § 9:3550(G)(3), a finance company and an insurer should ensure the following practices:

- The transmitted cancellation notice must be a clear company attestation that addresses each statutory element: (i) existence of a valid power of attorney, (ii) point of default and that it remains uncured, (iii) that a ten‑day notice was sent to the insured and the date of mailing or sending, and (iv) names and addresses of any interested third parties that were sent copies.
- Use unambiguous language of corporate certification (e.g., “APAC certifies that…”), and place the statement on company letterhead or equivalent to show corporate origin.
- Retain contemporaneous evidence of sending the notice to the insured (affidavit of mailing or server log entries) and preserve copies of the ten‑day notice actually sent to the insured and to any interested parties.
- If cancellations are automated, ensure the insurer’s system creates reliable machine‑readable logs: timestamps, headers, web service call records, and error logs. These artifacts establish the moment of “receipt.”
- Maintain chain‑of‑custody evidence for physical documents if paper copies are printed and stored (including who printed and the time printed), but do not rely exclusively on later signature stamps to validate earlier electronic transmissions.
- Train personnel to follow the company’s standard operating procedures for initiating cancellations and recording mailings, and document those procedures.

Failure to include a required statutory element in the transmitted certification or to produce evidence that the insurer actually received the notice (whether electronically or physically) will remain a chief vulnerability in litigation. Williams clarifies what qualifies as adequate compliance, but it does not obviate the need for clear proof.

## Applying Williams to hypothetical fact patterns

- If a finance company’s automated e‑mail to an insurer contains the four statutory items in plain terms and the insurer’s server accepts the e‑mail and cancels the policy, the cancellation is likely effective under Williams—even if no individual signed the document until later.
- If the transmitted statement omitted the date of the ten‑day notice or failed to state that notices were sent to interested parties, the cancellation would likely be ineffective under the “strict adherence” principle because an essential statutory element is missing.
- If the insurer’s web service did not accept the incoming message (e.g., it timed out, generated an error, or the message bounced) and no human took possession, the insurer did not “receive” the notice and cannot claim a valid cancellation.
- If the finance company sent a generic e‑mail that did not state the four certification elements or failed to demonstrate it had sent a ten‑day notice to the insured, courts following Williams would probably find the cancellation defective.

## Conclusion

Williams v. GoAuto Insurance resolves two common questions that arise when statutory cancellation procedures encounter automated corporate processes. The Fifth Circuit held that (1) a corporate statement that plainly “certif[ies]” the statutory items is sufficient—no separate, individual signature is required by the statute—and (2) modern, automated receipt by an insurer’s information system is actual “receipt” so long as the system takes custody or control of the transmitted notice and uses that input as the mechanism for cancellation. The opinion is a pragmatic synthesis of statutory text, Louisiana precedent emphasizing strict adherence, and recognition of contemporary business practices. It instructs litigants and practitioners to focus on substantive compliance with the statutory elements and on preserving reliable, contemporaneous evidence of transmission and receipt.
