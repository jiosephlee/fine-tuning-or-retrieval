Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

Chapter 12: # Standing, Justiciability, and the Zone of Interests

This chapter uses Pacito v. Trump (Ninth Circuit stay order, Sept. 12, 2025) to illuminate how courts decide who may sue and what claims they may bring in litigation that challenges refugee admissions policies and related programmatic funding decisions. Pacito is useful because the panel confronted several overlapping justiciability issues in expedited posture: the limits of judicial review of entry restrictions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the distinct legal character of claims that target post‑entry services and funding under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, organizational standing when cooperative agreements are terminated, the scope of the APA’s zone‑of‑interests inquiry, the narrow reach of consular nonreviewability in programmatic suits, and mootness questions that arise when agencies change course mid‑litigation.

The lesson of Pacito is a familiar one: not all plaintiffs and not all claims are created equal. A plaintiff’s institutional position (admitted refugee, applicant abroad, NGO) and the legal theory it advances (challenge to an entry suspension, demand for statutorily authorized services, contract/cooperative‑agreement relief) determine whether the federal courts can — and will — redress the alleged wrongs.

## Refugee plaintiffs: injuries to admitted refugees vs. applicants abroad

Standing doctrine begins with the Article III requirements of injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability. Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife supplies the familiar baseline: a plaintiff must show a concrete and particularized injury that is actual or imminent, fairly traceable to the challenged action, and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. Those principles take particular shape in immigration and refugee contexts.

Admitted refugees (those already physically in the United States) typically present the clearest standing. In Pacito, the Ninth Circuit recognized that refugees who had already been admitted or who had been conditionally approved and were imminently to enter have concrete, imminent interests: access to resettlement services, cash and medical assistance, or simply the right to remain in (or enter) the country under existing approvals. Those are injuries readily understood as “personal and individual” and as likely redressable by court orders restoring services or enforcing statutory duties.

By contrast, applicants abroad who lack any concrete government approval or imminence of entry face steeper obstacles. Consular refusals and discretionary entry decisions are often treated as nonjusticiable or at least as presumptively immune from intrusive review; and for applicants without a concrete, government‑recognized entitlement, the injury is frequently speculative. The Pacito stay reflects these differences: the panel found that the government was likely to prevail on challenges to the President’s power to suspend entry under § 1182(f), but it preserved (carved out from its stay) relief for certain non‑citizens who had been conditionally approved for resettlement before the order’s effective date. That carveout shows the court’s willingness to treat conditional approvals as creating a sufficiently concrete, imminent interest to justify judicial relief — a middle ground between admitted refugees and remote applicants abroad.

Practical point for litigators: if you represent refugees abroad, obtain and document any formal government approvals, conditional admissions, or other agency actions that make entry imminent. Those formal markers materially affect redressability and the injury‑in‑fact analysis.

## Organizational standing: diversion of resources and loss of cooperative agreements

Organizations bring two common models of standing: (1) representational/associational standing to sue on behalf of members, and (2) organizational standing premised on a direct injury to the organization itself. The Supreme Court’s decisions in Havens Realty and Friends of the Earth are the textbooks here: an organization may show standing by demonstrating that the defendant’s conduct frustrated its mission and forced it to divert resources to counteract that conduct (Havens), or that the organization has concrete environmental or programmatic interests harmed by the defendant’s actions (Friends of the Earth).

In Pacito, the organizational plaintiffs were resettlement agencies that previously held cooperative agreements with the State Department. When the government terminated those agreements and suspended funding, the organizations alleged immediate operational injuries: loss of contract revenue, the need to divert staff time to mitigate the loss and to assist clients, and damage to their relationships and infrastructure for overseas processing and domestic reception. Those assertions fit comfortably within established organizational‑standing doctrine. Economic injury from lost contracts is classical standing; diversion of resources to protect concrete programs or to fill statutory gaps also demonstrates the type of organizational injury courts recognize.

Two points are worth emphasizing. First, the required showing is fact‑specific: generalized mission frustration is insufficient if the organization cannot show a concrete diversion or a specific lost programmatic opportunity. Second, timing and documentation matter: contemporaneous evidence—board minutes authorizing staff reassignments, accounting showing lost revenue, communications documenting cancelled programs—bolsters the causal connection between the government action and the organizational injury.

## Zone‑of‑interests test under the APA for § 1522 claims

When plaintiffs invoke the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) to challenge agency action (or inaction), courts apply the “zone‑of‑interests” test to determine whether the plaintiff is a proper plaintiff to bring the statutory claim. The test asks whether the plaintiff’s injury falls within the interests sought to be protected by the statute whose violation is alleged. The test is designed to be “modest,” not a demanding merits‑based inquiry, but it is a threshold limitation on APA review.

Pacito turned this inquiry into a central battleground over 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the statutory provision authorizing and outlining federal programs for refugees’ domestic resettlement and assistance. The majority concluded that § 1522, read in context, imposes meaningful duties — not merely discretionary authorizations — with respect to reception and placement services, cash and medical assistance, and certain responsibilities for unaccompanied minors. Because § 1522 speaks directly to the welfare and resettlement of refugees, the court reasoned, refugees and resettlement organizations plainly fall within the law’s protective zone; hence APA review of alleged unlawful defunding or termination of cooperative agreements was warranted.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent highlights the other side of the interpretive divide: the statute’s title (“Authorization for programs”) and repeated language that assistance is “to the extent of available appropriations” can be read as conferring permissive authority rather than imposing mandatory duties. If § 1522 is purely permissive, then plaintiffs may lack an enforceable statutory interest that the APA can vindicate; under that view the zone‑of‑interests test would be fatal to the suit.

The doctrinal point for students and practitioners is twofold. First, zone‑of‑interests analysis is often resolved by close statutory interpretation: where the statute uses “shall” in operational provisions, courts may find enforceable expectations; where the statute is framed as authorization, courts may be hesitant to treat it as imposing mandatory duties. Second, the APA inquiry does not require a plaintiff to show that it is the quintessential beneficiary of the program; it only requires that the interest claimed be among those Congress plausibly sought to protect. In refugee‑services litigation, the statutory text, structure, and historical practice (e.g., longstanding cooperative agreements) inform that plausibility determination.

## Limits of consular nonreviewability in programmatic challenges

The doctrine of consular nonreviewability protects many individualized visa and consular decisions from judicial review. But that doctrine is narrow: it does not license wholesale nonreviewability of programmatic or statutory claims that do not seek to review an individual consular officer’s discretionary visa denial.

Pacito demonstrates the distinction. The panel emphasized deference to the President’s authority under § 1182(f) to suspend entry — invoking Trump v. Hawaii for the proposition that courts should not conduct a searching inquiry into the President’s foreign‑policy judgments about who may enter. Yet the court did not treat this deference as a blanket bar to review of statutory claims about domestic resettlement obligations. The government’s authority to restrict entry is one thing; its statutory obligations to provide post‑entry reception and placement services under § 1522 are another. Thus, a plaintiff’s programmatic challenge to the termination of cooperative agreements or to the cessation of domestic services is not automatically foreclosed by consular nonreviewability or by deference to executive branch entry policies.

In practical terms: when the relief sought aims at restoring statutory benefits or contractual relationships domestically, courts will be more receptive than when plaintiffs try to litigate the merits of an individual visa denial, which is squarely within consular latitude and often insulated from judicial second‑guessing.

## Mootness and voluntary cessation when agencies pivot mid‑litigation

Agency actions in politically charged contexts often shift mid‑litigation. When an agency terminates a program or reverses a policy after suit is filed, defendants frequently argue that the case is moot because the complained‑of conduct ceased. The Supreme Court warns, however, that voluntary cessation moots a case only in “exceptional circumstances” — it must be “absolutely clear” that the wrongful behavior will not recur. Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw and other decisions set a high bar.

Pacito highlights those concerns: after the district court issued an initial preliminary injunction, the government terminated cooperative agreements and later represented it was preparing requests for proposals for new resettlement agencies. The Ninth Circuit was skeptical that the government’s ad hoc representations would render the controversy moot. Accordingly, the panel required the government either to reinstate cooperative agreements (at least for refugees already admitted) or to face continued judicial supervision, because unilateral termination in the face of a pending claim does not, by itself, erase the controversy.

Two related doctrines often rescue plaintiffs from technical mootness. First, the “capable of repetition, yet evading review” exception applies to disputes that are by nature too short in duration to be litigated fully before cessation but are likely to recur. Second, injunctive relief addressing systemic policies is less likely to be moot than requests for narrowly tailored, time‑limited relief. Courts also assess whether the defendant’s cessation was prompted by litigation (suggesting a strategic, temporary move) and whether structural guarantees or commitments exist to prevent recurrence.

Litigators should therefore preserve records showing the defendant’s intent and capacity to resume the challenged conduct: internal memos, budgetary plans, and any evidence that the change was temporary or litigiously motivated. Plaintiffs should also frame relief to address ongoing or recurring harms (e.g., requirements to follow statutory procedures or to maintain minimum services) to reduce mootness risk.

## Conclusion: practical rules of thumb

- Distinguish carefully between challenges to entry suspensions (a realm where § 1182(f) and political‑branch deference loom large) and challenges to domestic programmatic actions (where statutory duties, contractual relationships, and APA review can provide a basis for judicial relief). The former may demand heightened deference; the latter is often amenable to ordinary statutory and administrative review.
- For refugee applicants abroad, secure and document any formal approvals or conditional admissions before framing claims as redressable injuries; such approvals materially strengthen standing.
- Organizations that lose cooperative agreements typically can show concrete injury: lost revenues and the need to divert resources. Document the operational impacts.
- In APA suits, focus early on the zone‑of‑interests question as a threshold issue: show that the statutory text and structure reflect an intent to protect the very interests you assert.
- Do not assume consular nonreviewability immunizes all facets of refugee litigation — it protects individualized visa decisions but does not bar review of domestic programmatic obligations.
- Anticipate and counter mootness arguments by preserving evidence of ongoing impacts and by seeking durable relief that addresses systemic deficiencies rather than ephemeral practices.

Pacito teaches what many doctrinal lines already suggest: standing and justiciability are flexible tools that reflect the character of the relief sought and the nature of the plaintiff. In refugee‑program litigation, understanding those doctrinal contours is the sine qua non of framing a viable claim.
