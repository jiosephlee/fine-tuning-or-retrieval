Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

# Presidential Power, Refugee Protection, and Judicial Review: An Overview

This chapter examines how the President’s authority over the admission of noncitizens, the statutory architecture of the United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), and the courts’ reviewing posture interact when the executive takes emergency or broad policy action affecting refugees. The recent Ninth Circuit interim order in Pacito v. Trump, Nos. 25-1313, 25-1939 (9th Cir. Sept. 12, 2025) (stay pending appeal), provides a compact case study: the court accepted that the President may suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) yet identified a distinct avenue of judicial enforcement for statutorily mandated resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. From that starting point we trace four strands law students must master: (1) the plenary-power doctrine and its evolution; (2) the difference between inherent executive power and powers delegated by statute; (3) the statutory design and operation of USRAP; and (4) the institutional rules and standards courts use to review executive decisions in immigration and foreign affairs.

## The plenary power doctrine and its evolution in immigration law

The plenary power doctrine—often summarized as the political branches’ broad authority to control entry and removal of noncitizens—rests on a long line of decisions dating to the late nineteenth century. The Supreme Court’s early pronouncements in cases such as Chae Chan Ping v. United States (the Chinese Exclusion Case) and Fong Yue Ting v. United States established that control of the border and immigration is “a political question” largely entrusted to the political branches. Those early rulings recognized that border control implicates national sovereignty and foreign relations, and thus courts should exercise restraint.

Over time, the Court refined the practical consequences of plenary power: it does not mean that the Constitution and statutes are irrelevant, but it does mean that courts typically defer to the political branches when they exercise authority over admission and exclusion. This deference has several dimensions. First, when the President acts under statute to regulate entry, courts often interpret the statute in light of a strong presumption of executive latitude, especially where national security or foreign affairs are invoked. Second, where the President acts pursuant to what he asserts are inherent powers (those tied to the constitutional role in foreign affairs and national security), courts treat the action as presumptively within executive competence, subject to only a limited judicial inquiry.

A modern articulation of this deference appears in Trump v. Hawaii (2018). The Court there upheld presidential travel restrictions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and reasoning that review should not involve “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications” when the statutory grant confers broad authority in a field touching national security and foreign relations. The Ninth Circuit in Pacito relied on that language, describing § 1182(f)’s text as one that “exudes deference” and concluding the President had “undoubtedly” made the required finding that entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Pacito thus exemplifies the contemporary rule: when the President acts under § 1182(f) to suspend entry of a class of aliens, courts will generally treat the President’s factual finding and policy judgment as within the core of executive discretion.

But plenary power is not absolute. The courts retain the power to interpret Congress’s statutes and to enforce statutory rights where the political branches’ actions collide with clear statutory mandates. Plenary power reduces—but does not obliterate—judicial review. The boundary between judicial modesty in immigration and the obligation to enforce statutory and constitutional limits is a central doctrinal tension illustrated in Pacito, where the panel granted a stay as to admissions but declined to stay the district court’s order directed to resettlement services payable to refugees already admitted.

## Delegated statutory authority vs. inherent executive power

Distinguishing between authority that is inherent to the President and authority delegated by Congress is crucial. Inherent executive power derives from the Constitution’s allocation of foreign affairs and commander-in-chief functions to the President. Delegated authority arises when Congress enacts a statute that assigns certain powers or procedures to the executive branch.

8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) is a paradigmatic example of a statutory delegation that is interpreted against the backdrop of the executive’s foreign-affairs responsibilities. It provides that “[w]henever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States, he may … suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens … or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate.” The statute’s language—particularly the “may” and the requirement of a presidential “find[ing]”—has been interpreted to grant the President very broad discretion to restrict entry. Courts have historically treated such delegations as entitling the President to substantial deference, especially where national security and foreign relations are implicated.

But delegated authority has limits: Congress can impose substantive obligations and procedural constraints on how the executive acts. Where a statute uses mandatory language (“shall”), establishes substantive duties, conditions relief, or ties obligations to appropriations and reporting, courts will consider whether the executive action is consistent with the statute’s mandatory terms. In Pacito the Ninth Circuit applied this bifurcated approach. It concluded that the President’s use of § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions fit comfortably within the broad delegation of power recognized in Trump v. Hawaii. By contrast, the court treated certain provisions of 8 U.S.C. § 1522—statutory provisions addressing reception and placement services—as potentially imposing enforceable duties on the executive that could be violated by unilateral defunding and termination of cooperative agreements.

Two doctrinal tools frame this analysis. First, statutory interpretation: when Congress has clearly required the executive to take specific actions (for example, “shall” combined with unambiguous directives and mandatory reporting requirements), courts are likelier to find non-discretionary duties. Second, the Administrative Procedure Act and related nondelegation/reviewability doctrines govern whether the courts can review agency or executive action that allegedly violates statutory commands. The APA provides a general cause of action—except where agency action is “committed to agency discretion by law,” 5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2)—and authorizes review for actions that are “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law,” 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). The key question becomes whether the statute either committed the matter to executive discretion or conversely provided sufficient legal standards for the courts to apply.

This divide is precisely the locus of disagreement in Pacito. The majority read § 1522 as creating enforceable obligations to provide specified reception and placement services “to the extent of available appropriations,” and therefore subject to judicial enforcement under the APA. Judge Lee’s partial dissent argued § 1522’s structure, headings, and frequent use of the verb “authorize” support a permissive reading: authorization to act, not a judicially enforceable command to operate programs. The dispute illustrates how textual cues—titles, verbs, cross-references, and appropriations language—shape courts’ conclusions about whether a statutory provision constitutes an enforceable mandate or a grant of discretionary authority.

## The U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP): purposes and architecture

The Refugee Act of 1980 substantially reorganized federal refugee policy and created the contemporary architecture of USRAP. Negusie v. Holder (2009) described the Refugee Act as establishing “a general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons.” USRAP is a multi‑agency enterprise with distinct roles:

- The Department of Homeland Security (through U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services, USCIS) adjudicates refugee claims and determines eligibility for resettlement.
- The Department of State manages overseas processing and works with international partners and non‑governmental organizations to identify and refer cases to the United States.
- The Department of Health and Human Services (HHS), primarily through the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR), is statutorily charged with reception and placement—or “resettlement”—services for refugees after arrival.

Congress authorized and funded refugee programs through provisions now codified at, among other places, 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and § 1523. Section 1522 contemplates a suite of services: employment training and placement; English language instruction; cash and medical assistance projects; and particular protections and responsibilities regarding unaccompanied refugee children. The statute authorizes the Secretary (and the Director of ORR) to provide these services “to the extent of available appropriations,” and authorizes the use of contracts, grants, and cooperative agreements with nonprofit agencies to deliver services.

In practice, resettlement in the United States has been delivered through cooperative agreements and grants with a network of national and local non‑profits. These organizations perform intake, provide initial housing and cash assistance, enroll refugees in social services, place refugees with employment and language programs, and coordinate with local communities. Because of the logistical complexity and the importance of local knowledge, the federal government has long relied on cooperative agreements with nonprofits for reception and placement.

Pacito puts the practical operation into relief. There, an executive order suspending admissions was accompanied by a separate suspension or termination of funding for USRAP cooperative agreements. The district court enjoined significant parts of the executive action; on appeal the Ninth Circuit recognized that the President’s statutory authority to suspend entry under § 1182(f) likely supported suspension of admissions, but it also confronted the statutory text of § 1522 and the machine of resettlement. The court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have already been admitted, based on the conclusion that the government might be unable to show it could meet § 1522’s mandates. Thus, USRAP’s multi-agency structure and reliance on cooperative agreements create discrete legal claims: one about the executive’s authority to stop future admissions, and another about statutory duties to care for those already admitted.

## Courts’ institutional role and standards of deference in immigration and foreign affairs

When courts review executive action affecting immigration and foreign affairs, they balance three institutional considerations: the need to enforce legal limits imposed by Congress and the Constitution, the need for judicially manageable standards, and respect for the political branches’ prerogatives in national security and external affairs. The results are a series of doctrinally distinct—but overlapping—standards.

1. Deference under statutory delegations in the national‑security/foreign‑affairs context. As the Supreme Court explained in Trump v. Hawaii, courts should accord substantial deference where Congress has delegated broad authority and where action touches foreign affairs or national security. Under this approach judicial review is limited: courts accept the President’s “finding” unless Congress has provided clear text to the contrary, and they decline to conduct “searching” scrutiny of the President’s policy justifications.

2. Consular nonreviewability and the Kleindienst framework. When the consular officer or the political branches deny a visa, Kleindienst v. Mandel instructs that federal courts will not substitute their own judgment where the executive provides a “facially legitimate and bona fide” reason for denial. That doctrine narrows judicial inquiry into the motives or merits of exclusion or refusal of entry in many individual visa cases, but it is not an absolute bar to all review—especially when procedural or statutory requirements have been violated.

3. The APA and the scope of judicial review. Where executive action is taken pursuant to statute, the APA supplies the baseline framework for judicial review—if the action is reviewable. Courts ask whether statutes leave room for judicial review (5 U.S.C. § 701) and whether the action was “arbitrary or capricious” in light of the statute’s commands (5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A)). In Pacito the Ninth Circuit applied APA principles to § 1522 claims, concluding that the government might not satisfy the statutory duties and that the APA therefore provided a vehicle for judicial enforcement. The court’s order directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements exemplifies how courts can fashion relief to vindicate statutory rights even while staying other injunctions that affect foreign affairs.

4. Political‑question, state‑secrets, and national‑security limits. Some executive acts raise institutional concerns that lead courts to find matters nonjusticiable or to apply highly constrained review. Political‑question doctrine or tailpiece doctrines like state‑secrets privilege may foreclose judicial relief where no judicially manageable standard exists or where litigation would compromise national security.

5. Equitable remedies and the stay standard. When injunctions or stays are sought pending appeal, courts use the Nken v. Holder four‑factor framework (likelihood of success on the merits; irreparable injury absent a stay; injury to other parties; and the public interest), with the first two factors “most critical.” Pacito applied Nken: finding strong deference to the President under § 1182(f) on admissions but concluding plaintiffs were likely to prevail on at least some APA claims tied to resettlement services, and crafting relief accordingly.

Practical consequences follow from these standards. Courts will ordinarily resist detailed second‑guessing of executive judgments about whom to admit when the action rests on broad statutory authority linked to foreign affairs. But courts are more willing to enforce clear statutory mandates governing domestic programs and benefits—even when the beneficiaries are noncitizens already lawfully admitted—because those statutory duties fit readily within judicial competence and the APA’s remedial framework.

Conclusion: applying the lessons

Pacito encapsulates the core lesson: immigration law divides into domains where judicial deference is heavy and domains where ordinary statutory enforcement applies. Students must be able to identify which regime governs a given dispute. Ask these questions when you analyze a new fact pattern: (1) Is the executive action premised on an expression of inherent power or on a statutory delegation? (2) If statutory, does the statute grant broad, discretionary authority or impose clear, mandatory duties? (3) Is the action intertwined with foreign affairs or national security such that the judiciary should exercise heightened restraint? (4) Is there a manageable legal standard (statutory text, APA) that courts can apply without intruding on politically charged judgments?

Mastering those questions will enable you to predict when courts will enforce statutory protections for refugees and when courts will defer to presidential decisions about who may enter the United States. Pacito shows both outcomes can occur in the same dispute: suspension of admissions found likely within presidential power; statutory resettlement duties found judicially enforceable.

# The Statutory Framework: INA § 1182(f), § 1185(a), and the Refugee Act of 1980

This chapter explains the statutory architecture that governs presidential power to restrict entry, the statutory regime that governs refugee admissions and post‑arrival resettlement, and the ways appropriations language conditions executive obligations. Pacito v. Trump (9th Cir. 2025) provides the immediate doctrinal hook: the majority emphasized the breadth of the President’s § 1182(f) authority to suspend entry while simultaneously concluding that statutory duties created by the Refugee Act—chiefly 8 U.S.C. § 1522—may impose judicially enforceable obligations to provide reception and placement services once refugees are admitted. Judge Lee’s partial concurrence and dissent in Pacito illustrates the contrasting interpretive paths courts take when statutory text uses both “shall” and permissive language such as “authorized” and “to the extent of available appropriations.” The remainder of this chapter unpacks the statutory text, history, and interpretive tools that a student must use to analyze actions at the intersection of immigration, refugee law, and appropriations law.

## INA § 1182(f): text, history, and reach of the President’s suspension authority

Text and mechanism. 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) (often cited historically as § 212(f) of the INA) provides in its operative form that whenever the President “finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” he “may by proclamation, and for such period as he shall deem necessary, suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens as immigrants or nonimmigrants, or impose on the entry of aliens any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate.” Two features of the text deserve emphasis: (1) the presidential finding is the statutory trigger, and (2) Congress used permissive verbs—“may,” “suspend,” and “impose … restrictions”—conveying broad discretion.

Historical context. § 1182(f) was enacted as part of the Immigration and Nationality Act of 1952. Its historic purpose was to provide an executive mechanism to respond rapidly to risks presented by foreign nationals—broadly conceived as threats to national interests—without requiring new legislation. Over time courts came to treat § 1182(f) as one of the key textual bases for the executive’s plenary control of the border and admission decisions. That posture matured in a series of modern decisions that emphasize deference to the President’s foreign‑policy and national‑security judgments when he acts pursuant to § 1182(f).

Judicial reach and limits. The principal line of Supreme Court authority that recent courts cite for extreme deference is Trump v. Hawaii (585 U.S. ___ (2018)), where the Court accepted that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and supplies “ample power” to impose entry restrictions. From that premise courts often conclude that a court’s review of a § 1182(f) proclamation must be restrained: the statute requires only that the President “find” detriment to U.S. interests, and courts should not conduct a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Yet that deference is not limitless. Two kinds of constraints remain relevant.

First, the statute is a creature of text; it does not allow actions that plainly exceed textual limits or that violate other statutory commands. If a separate statutory provision unambiguously prohibits the President’s chosen measure, a court will not be constrained by § 1182(f). Second, constitutional limits still bind—equal protection principles, the Establishment Clause, Due Process, and the First Amendment are not erased by § 1182(f). Courts therefore frame their review as constrained: examinable doxastic or rational basis inquiries into whether the President made the requisite “finding,” while generally declining to substitute their assessment of foreign‑policy judgment for that of the political branches.

Operational consequences. When a President invokes § 1182(f), the analysis begins with whether the invocation satisfies the textual trigger: did the President make the requisite finding and issue the proclamation? If so, courts typically treat the action as presumptively within the President’s authority under § 1182(f) unless another statute or the Constitution provides a clear limit. As Pacito illustrates, a court may nevertheless find other statutory duties (for example, duties to provide services after admission) that the executive cannot disclaim simply by suspending future admissions.

## INA § 1185(a): regulation of entry and departure; interaction with § 1182(f)

Text and function. 8 U.S.C. § 1185(a) grants the executive power to regulate the entry and departure of aliens by promulgating “such rules and regulations” and taking such steps “as the President may prescribe” for the inspection and control of arrivals. Historically, § 1185(a) has been read as a regulatory complement to § 1182(f): where § 1182(f) authorizes suspension or restrictions on entry by proclamation, § 1185(a) authorizes rulemaking and administrative management of entry, exit, and ports of arrival.

Complementary scope. The two provisions operate at different levels. § 1182(f) is a blunt instrument that authorizes suspension or restriction of classes of entry as a matter of declarative presidential policy. § 1185(a) is a delegated rulemaking provision enabling the executive to set procedural and regulatory requirements for inspection, boarding, departure, and conditions at ports. In practice, a President may rely on § 1182(f) to effect a broad policy pause in admissions and on § 1185(a) to craft implementing rules that give operational form to that pause—procedures at airports, visa rescissions, and parole rules, for example.

Interaction in judicial review. Courts often cite both provisions when evaluating executive action that affects entry. When a broad prohibition looks like a policy decision grounded in national‑security judgment, judges point to § 1182(f) as the operative authority and invoke the high level of deference recognized in cases like Trump v. Hawaii. When litigants challenge the method of enforcement or the administrative rules the executive uses to implement an admission restriction, courts review the § 1185(a) rulemaking authority to determine whether the regulatory action stays within statutory bounds and administrative procedure norms. The distinction matters practically: § 1182(f) signals the President’s policy choice and carries deference; § 1185(a) often demands that implementing rules be within the bounds of delegated authority and, where they are “rulemaking” in the administrative‑law sense, that they conform to statutory or constitutional procedural requirements.

Boundaries and congressional intent. Neither § 1182(f) nor § 1185(a) is absolute. If Congress has elsewhere enacted a statute that creates a particular right or process, such as the Refugee Act’s requirements for consultation and admissions ceilings (discussed below), courts must harmonize the provisions. That harmonization often requires attention to statutory timing and operative words: does congressional language evince intent to limit executive discretion, or merely to authorize particular programs? The necessary textual work is a standard task in statutory construction.

## The Refugee Act of 1980: §§ 1157 (admissions ceilings, consultation) and 1522 (domestic resettlement and assistance)

Structure and purpose. The Refugee Act of 1980 reformed U.S. refugee law by embedding a statutory process for refugee admissions, establishing an annual admission ceiling subject to presidential determination and congressional consultation, and creating mechanisms to deliver post‑arrival services and funding. The Act reflects a dual design: to preserve executive flexibility in setting admission numbers and vetting individuals while establishing statutory responsibilities for resettlement so that refugees can integrate effectively after admission.

8 U.S.C. § 1157 — admissions ceilings and consultation. Section 1157 sets the framework for annual refugee admissions. It empowers the President to set—the “Presidential determination”—a worldwide ceiling on refugee admissions for a fiscal year and to consult with Congress on that determination. The statute thus places the decision over the annual ceiling squarely in the political branches: the President proposes and consults; Congress participates through appropriations and oversight. The Refugee Act’s procedures are not an absolute constraint on the President’s § 1182(f) power, but they do supply a regime that governs planned admissions. When a President exercises § 1182(f) to suspend a program that would otherwise admit refugees, courts must read that suspension against the background statutory framework Congress established in § 1157.

8 U.S.C. § 1522 — domestic resettlement and assistance. Section 1522 addresses what happens to refugees after they enter the United States. Its provisions include authorizations for a range of services: employment training and placement, English language instruction, cash and medical assistance for newly arrived refugees, and particular protections and responsibilities for unaccompanied minors. The statute authorizes the use of contracts, grants, or cooperative agreements with public and private nonprofit agencies to provide reception and placement services; it provides reporting and oversight requirements; and it includes language—“to the extent of available appropriations”—that links the substantive duties to the availability of funding.

Interpretive tensions illustrated by Pacito. In Pacito the Ninth Circuit majority concluded that the government’s termination of cooperative agreements to provide reception and placement services likely contravened § 1522: even if the President may suspend future admissions under § 1182(f), the government may not simply withdraw statutory reception and placement services from refugees already admitted. The majority therefore directed the reinstatement of cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” The opinion grounds that remedy in the statutory language that imposes obligations—frequently phrased with “shall”—on the Director and on the Secretary to see that services are provided “in such a manner” as to promote self‑sufficiency, to assume legal responsibility for unaccompanied minors when necessary, and to “develop and implement” certain projects for medical and cash assistance.

Judge Lee’s concurrence and dissent in Pacito demonstrates the countervailing interpretive path: § 1522’s title—“Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees”—and the repeated use of the word “authorized” suggest to her that Congress intended § 1522 primarily as permissive funding authority rather than a mandate to operate programs. Her view foregrounds the statute’s structure and the reality that many of § 1522’s operative provisions are expressly conditioned on appropriations. That interpretive divide—mandatory duty versus permissive authorization—governs plaintiffs’ ability to obtain judicial relief. If § 1522 creates a nondiscretionary duty, then courts can compel the executive to fulfill it (or at least to proceed consistent with the statute); if § 1522 is permissive, courts lack a basis to command the executive to run programs.

Practical doctrinal takeaway. When confronting conflicts between a presidential suspension of admissions and statutory resettlement obligations, a court must (1) determine the text’s operative force—whether Congress intended to create a mandatory duty or merely an authorization; (2) evaluate whether Congress has appropriated funds to satisfy the duty; and (3) reconcile the resulting obligation with the President’s national‑security or foreign‑policy prerogatives under § 1182(f). Pacito shows that these steps can lead a court to require the executive to provide statutorily mandated services to those already admitted while allowing the President great deference with respect to future admissions.

## Appropriations riders and qualifiers: “to the extent of available appropriations” and their legal effect

What the phrase does. “To the extent of available appropriations” is a frequently used statutory qualifier that conditions a statutory duty on Congress’s provision of funds. It has two primary effects. First, it recognizes the constitutional fact that Congress controls the purse: an executive agency cannot lawfully spend funds that Congress has not appropriated. Second, it signals that the specified statutory program or obligation is subject to funding limits and may not be enforceable if Congress declines to provide money for it.

Interpretive consequences. The presence of the qualifier creates a threshold question for courts asked to enforce a statutory duty: have appropriations been made and are they still available? If Congress has appropriated funds for the program and has not expressly rescinded them, then the agency must decide whether to expend money consistent with statutory conditions. If the statute otherwise creates a nondiscretionary duty (for example, by using “shall” in ways that impose an obligation), then a court may have authority to compel the agency to act within the appropriation. But if Congress has not appropriated funds—or has appropriated and then rescinded or conditioned them—the government can plausibly contend that the duty cannot be executed because the financial means are absent.

Limits on judicial compulsion. Courts are careful in using equitable power to compel executive spending because of separation‑of‑powers concerns. When an agency alleges that no appropriations are available, a court should demand clear proof before enjoining or ordering spending. If funds are available but the executive claims policy reasons for withholding them, courts will scrutinize whether the statute imposes a non‑discretionary duty and whether judicial relief would unconstitutionally commandeer executive functions. Thus, the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” creates a practical barrier to judicial enforcement in many cases, but it is not an absolute bar: appropriation has both legal and evidentiary dimensions.

Appropriations riders and policy. In the Pacito record Congress had appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance for FY 2025 but had enacted later rescissions that reduced the totals. The Ninth Circuit pointed to those appropriations and to statutory reporting and accountability duties to explain why it could order reinstatement of cooperative agreements for those already admitted, reasoning that the government had not demonstrated its ability to provide the statutorily mandated services without the cooperative agreements. That approach highlights how courts often treat appropriations questions as fact‑intensive: availability must be shown; mere assertions of funding shortfall are insufficient to prevent enforcement of statutory duties when funds are in play.

A student applying these principles should proceed in two steps: (1) identify whether the statutory duty is expressly conditioned on appropriations, and (2) inquire—on the record—whether appropriations exist or are available. If appropriations exist and the statute imposes a non‑discretionary duty, judicial relief may be appropriate; if appropriations do not exist, the duty cannot be performed and courts generally will not (and often cannot) force the executive to expend funds it says are not available.

## Entry vs. admission post‑IIRIRA and how courts reconcile § 1182(f)’s “entry” language

Definitional distinction. The INA draws a formal distinction between “entry” and “admission.” “Admission” is defined in the statute (8 U.S.C. § 1101(a)(13)) as “the lawful entry of the alien into the United States after inspection and authorization by an immigration officer.” “Entry” is not as precisely defined in the INA’s text, and in common usage “entry” can mean either the physical act of crossing a border or the legal status that follows. Courts therefore have had to interpret what “entry” means in different statutory contexts.

Post‑IIRIRA landscape. The Illegal Immigration Reform and Immigrant Responsibility Act of 1996 (IIRIRA) revised many removal and entry provisions and affected the conceptual architecture around “entry” and “admission.” In particular, IIRIRA sharpened the legal consequences of being “admitted” and broadened removal consequences for those without proper admission or those reentering unlawfully. The post‑IIRIRA statutory environment therefore encourages careful attention to whether a person is an “admitted” alien (i.e., had lawful entry) versus someone who is merely physically present or in transit and who never secured admission.

Application to refugees and suspensions. How do courts reconcile § 1182(f)’s power to suspend “entry” with the Refugee Act’s protections for those already admitted? The general answer courts adopt is pragmatic: § 1182(f) targets future inbound movement—suspension of the ability to enter or be admitted—while statutory protections that attach after admission remain operative for persons already inside the United States. Thus, a refugee who has completed the admission process and is physically within the United States is not typically within the class that § 1182(f) is designed to control; § 1182(f) suspends the President’s willingness to allow new entries or admissions, not to strip legal status from those here already.

The Ninth Circuit’s handling of “conditionally approved” refugees in Pacito illustrates the doctrinal complications. The court carved out from the stay individuals who were “conditionally approved for refugee status by USCIS before Jan 20, 2025,” implicitly recognizing that certain pre‑entry approvals carry reliance‑based or statutory expectations that make a categorical suspension harder to apply. That approach reflects two points. First, pre‑admission administrative steps (such as USCIS approval) may place an alien in a liminal position that invites narrower judicial protection than someone already present but still gives greater protection than a simple visa applicant abroad. Second, courts will often focus on whether an individual has a vested right or a completed statutory status that the executive cannot extinguish by fiat.

Consequential distinctions for relief. For students analyzing fact patterns, the key doctrinal choice is whether the person at issue has been “admitted” (and so enjoys statutory and constitutional protections that the executive cannot abrogate wholesale) or is still abroad and seeking “entry.” If the person is abroad and has not obtained admission, the executive’s § 1182(f) suspension will generally be given much greater deference; if the person has been admitted, statutory duties like § 1522’s reception and placement obligations and constitutional protections likely govern the dispute.

Synthesis and doctrinal checklist

When a legal problem involves a presidential suspension of refugee admissions, statutes governing immigration entry, and post‑arrival resettlement responsibilities, apply the following worksheet:

- Identify the authoritative statutory texts. Ask whether the President invoked § 1182(f) and whether § 1185(a) or any implementing regulations are implicated. Determine which Refugee Act provisions (especially §§ 1157 and 1522) are potentially inconsistent with or limiting of the presidential action.

- Resolve the temporal status of the persons at issue. Are they already admitted to the United States? Are they conditionally approved for admission? Courts treat these categories differently; the former typically invokes statutory and constitutional protections that cannot be undone by an order suspending future entries.

- Read the statute for mandatory versus permissive language. Look for “shall” in combination with other textual signals: titles (“Authorization”), repeated use of “authorized,” and appropriations qualifiers. If a duty is framed as mandatory and funds are available, it may be judicially enforceable; if it is discretionary or expressly conditioned on appropriations, the executive has more latitude.

- Investigate appropriations. Determine whether Congress has appropriated funds for the program and whether the funds remain available. The presence or absence of appropriations will often decide whether a statutory duty can realistically be performed or judicially compelled.

- Account for constitutional constraints. Even when the text seems to give the President broad authority, ask whether constitutional protections (equal protection, due process, Establishment Clause) place limits on the executive action.

- Consider practical remedies and separation‑of‑powers risks. Courts are more willing to enjoin withholding of statutory duties when funds are appropriated and the duty is nondiscretionary; they are more reluctant to mandate specific budgetary allocations or micro‑manage executive decisionmaking in areas where the statute leaves substantial discretion.

Conclusion

The intersection of § 1182(f), § 1185(a), and the Refugee Act of 1980 is a site where statutory text, historical practice, and constitutional values collide. Pacito displays how a court can simultaneously give broad deference to a presidential finding that entry should be suspended while recognizing that Congress enunciated post‑arrival statutory duties that persist for those already admitted. Students should approach such cases with a layered statutory‑textual analysis that carefully distinguishes entry from admission, reads mandatory words against appropriations qualifiers, and recognizes that judicial relief turns on both legal interpretation and factual showings about appropriations and administrative capability.

# Judicial Review and Remedies: APA, Deference, and Emergency Relief

This chapter examines how courts review executive immigration actions and agency funding decisions, the current posture of judicial deference in statutory interpretation (post–Loper Bright), and the standards for obtaining emergency relief: preliminary injunctions and stays pending appeal. The Pacito v. Trump opinion (Ninth Circuit, Sept. 12, 2025) offers a compact vehicle for illustrating how these doctrines intersect in practice. Pacito presents three interlaced issues: (1) the President’s use of 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions; (2) challenges under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) to the termination of cooperative agreements and defunding of resettlement services required by 8 U.S.C. § 1522; and (3) interlocutory relief — district-court preliminary injunctions and an appellate stay pending appeal. The court’s treatment of these issues, and Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent, illuminate the limits of deference, the boundary between nonreviewable foreign-affairs power and reviewable statutory duties, and the practical contours of equitable relief.

## APA reviewability: § 701(a)(2), final agency action, and the “law to apply” inquiry

A litigant’s starting point is whether the APA permits judicial review at all. Two doctrinal thresholds control: (1) the APA’s exclusion of review for agency action “committed to agency discretion by law” (5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2)), and (2) whether the challenged conduct constitutes final agency action subject to review (5 U.S.C. § 704). Pacito is useful because the court treated these thresholds differently for two separate problems.

Section 701(a)(2) and “law to apply.” Section 701(a)(2) denies review when “agency action is committed to agency discretion by law,” a phrase courts have interpreted to mean there is no judicially manageable standard — no “law to apply” — that would allow meaningful review. The Supreme Court’s immigration precedents (most notably Trump v. Hawaii) show how § 701(a)(2) can operate in the foreign-affairs and entry context: when Congress confers extraordinarily broad discretion or when the statutory command is framed as a single, nonjusticiable determination (for example, “if the President finds…entry would be detrimental”), courts have often treated the matter as committed to the political branches and thus effectively insulated from searching judicial review.

In Pacito, the Ninth Circuit relied on Trump v. Hawaii to explain why the President’s exercise of § 1182(f) receives exceptional deference. The court observed that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” because its “sole prerequisite” is the President’s finding that the entry of certain aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” When a statute conditions executive action on a named official’s explicit “finding,” courts often conclude there is little room for judicial second-guessing — the statutory text leaves no manageable standard beyond the executive’s own determination. That is the classic § 701(a)(2) profile: a politically assigned determination with no judicially administrable standard.

But § 701(a)(2) is not a blanket exclusion. Courts must ask whether the statute and governing law furnish a standard by which to judge the agency’s action. If there is “law to apply” — i.e., statutory or constitutional guideposts that would permit meaningful judicial assessment — then the action is reviewable. Pacito illustrates this principle by bifurcating the litigation: the court concluded the President was likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the suspension of admissions under § 1182(f), because that statutory provision presents the kind of discretionary presidential determination courts historically treat as nonjusticiable or at least deserving of extreme deference. At the same time, the court concluded that plaintiffs were likely to succeed on their APA claim regarding the defunding and termination of cooperative agreements under § 1522. Why? Because § 1522 itself contains statutory duties and reporting requirements (and a web of implementing regulations) that supply law to apply and thus permit judicial review of whether the government is meeting those statutory obligations.

Final agency action. Even when § 701(a)(2) does not bar review, the APA’s review provisions require that the challenged action be “final.” Bennett v. Spear provides the modern test for finality: (1) the action must mark the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process (it cannot be tentative or interlocutory), and (2) the action must have direct and appreciable legal consequences — it must alter the party’s legal position or rights. In Pacito, the State Department’s termination of cooperative agreements and the Department of State/HHS’s suspension of funding were concrete, consummated decisions producing immediate legal and practical effects on resettlement providers and refugees — the sort of action that passes Bennett’s finality test. The plaintiffs’ challenge thus presented a paradigmatic case of final agency action susceptible to APA review.

Applying these tests in practice: When confronting an executive immigration or funding decision, a lawyer should first map the statutory text that purportedly authorizes the action. If the statute (like § 1182(f)) centers the determination on an executive “finding,” courts may treat it as committed to executive discretion; if the statute (like § 1522) sets forth specific duties, conditions, or reporting requirements, it is more likely to furnish law to apply and to permit review. Next, ask whether the challenged action is a completed decision with tangible legal consequences — an agency’s policy statement may be too tentative, but termination of a funding agreement, the revocation of an application approval, or the denial of benefits that alters legal relations will likely be final.

## Standards of review: arbitrary and capricious, and statutory interpretation post–Loper Bright

Once reviewability and finality are established, the court determines the appropriate standard of review. Two distinct axes exist: (1) the standard for reviewing an agency’s factual or policy-based decisions — typically the “arbitrary and capricious” standard of 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A); and (2) the standard for reviewing agency statutory interpretation.

Arbitrary and capricious review. The APA directs courts to set aside agency actions that are “arbitrary, capricious, an abuse of discretion, or otherwise not in accordance with law.” Under the Supreme Court’s precedents, arbitrary-and-capricious review requires that an agency have examined relevant data, articulated a satisfactory explanation for its action (including a rational connection between facts and choice), considered important aspects of the problem, and responded to significant countervailing evidence or arguments. Typical defects include failure to consider relevant factors, reliance on plainly wrong facts, unexplained departures from prior policy, and failure to assess alternatives or to justify reliance on new evidence.

But the intensity of review varies with context. Courts give more searching review when the agency failed to address important reliance interests, reversed course without explanation, or acted on a record that suggests pretext. Conversely, in domains touching core executive responsibilities — foreign affairs, national security, and matters requiring confidentiality or expertise — courts ordinarily exercise a more deferential form of arbitrary-and-capricious review. The Ninth Circuit in Pacito exemplified this modulation: it declined to engage in “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications” when addressing § 1182(f), invoking prior hands-off treatment of national-security and entry decisions, but it scrutinized the government’s termination of resettlement funding more carefully because § 1522 created statutory responsibilities that the agencies had to satisfy.

Statutory interpretation post–Loper Bright: Chevron’s decline and Skidmore persuasion. The second axis — reviewing agency statutory interpretation — has undergone a fundamental shift in recent years. Historically, Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council (1984) instructed courts to apply a two-step inquiry: if Congress’s intent is unambiguous, follow the statute; if ambiguous, defer to an agency’s reasonable interpretation. Pacito leans on deference doctrines in the national-security/immigration context through Trump v. Hawaii, but statutory interpretation in many other contexts is now governed by the post–Loper Bright regime.

Following Loper Bright, courts across the federal judiciary have treated Chevron deference as no longer the controlling approach; agencies’ statutory interpretations are to be evaluated under a framework resembling Skidmore v. Swift. Under Skidmore persuasion, the weight accorded an agency’s interpretation depends on its persuasiveness — the thoroughness of its reasoning, consistency with earlier positions, expertise, and the overall fit with statutory text and structure. That means courts undertake independent statutory interpretation, but they may give the agency’s view considerable persuasive weight when it is well-reasoned and grounded in expertise.

Practical consequences. Under Skidmore-style review, litigants cannot rely on automatic, near-conclusive deference to an agency reading. Courts will closely analyze statutory text, structure, and the presence or absence of clear Congressional direction. In Pacito, the Ninth Circuit did not apply any species of Chevron deference to § 1522; rather, it examined the statutory language setting out specific duties and funding mechanisms and found there was “law to apply” that permitted judicial enforcement. Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent further illustrates statutory interpretive techniques: she emphasized textual signals — titles such as “Authorization,” the repeated use of “is authorized,” and the qualifying phrase “to the extent of available appropriations” — to conclude § 1522 is permissive, not mandatory. That contest between the panel’s majority and the concurrence demonstrates how post–Loper Bright review requires courts to parse statutory grammar and structure, apply canons of construction, and weigh the persuasiveness of agency interpretations rather than simply accepting them under a deferential Chevron step-two.

## Foreign affairs and national security considerations in APA cases: scope but not elimination of review

Foreign-affairs and national-security contexts complicate administrative review but do not make it impossible. Courts recognize constitutional and prudential reasons for judicial caution when reviewing executive actions implicating national security, diplomatic relations, or immigration. The executive’s institutional competence and constitutional prerogatives support substantial deference. But deference is not a moratorium on review: where statutory obligations exist, or where executive action effects private rights in ways that are judicially manageable, courts retain authority to examine and, when appropriate, to enjoin agency conduct.

Pacito’s dual result vividly demonstrates the balance. The court emphasized that § 1182(f) confers broad presidential authority to suspend entry, and that prior precedent counsels reluctance to probe the persuasiveness of the President’s stated national-security justifications. Accordingly, the panel found the government likely to succeed on the merits of the challenge to the moratorium on admissions — a conclusion rooted in statutory text and national-security deference. Yet the panel also concluded that national-security power did not extinguish statutory obligations the executive owes to refugees who have already been admitted. The statutory duties in § 1522 — which address reception and placement services, accountability, and reporting — are not swallowed by the President’s § 1182(f) power. The court therefore concluded that the APA challenge to defunding and contract termination could proceed and that, at least preliminarily, judicial relief ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees was appropriate.

Key takeaways. When national-security or foreign-affairs considerations are asserted to shield agency action from review, courts ask (1) whether Congress has assigned the particular determination to the political branches in a way that leaves no judicially manageable standard and (2) whether the case involves legal interests (statutory or constitutional rights) that courts are equipped to protect. If a statute creates specific duties (for example, funding and programmatic duties for resettlement services), courts may review whether those duties are being satisfied even if the government’s broader policy decisions (for example, suspending admissions) remain highly protected.

## Winter v. NRDC and Nken v. Holder: preliminary injunctions and stays pending appeal

Emergency equitable relief — preliminary injunctions and stays pending appeal — is governed by well-established factors, and courts tailor their analysis to the interplay between likelihood-of-success questions and the balance of harms.

Preliminary injunctions: Winter’s framework. The Supreme Court in Winter v. NRDC clarified the preliminary-injunction standard. To obtain a preliminary injunction, the movant must show: (1) a likelihood of success on the merits; (2) that the movant is likely to suffer irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary relief; (3) that the balance of equities tips in the movant’s favor; and (4) that an injunction is in the public interest. Winter rejected a formulation that permitted courts to grant injunctions where there were “serious questions” going to the merits as long as the balance of harms tipped sharply in the movant’s favor; while some circuits still use a sliding-scale variant, Winter requires courts to consider likelihood of success as a critical threshold. The irreparable-harm requirement is prominent: monetary losses are generally not enough; the harm must be non-compensable and imminent.

Stays pending appeal: Nken’s four-factor test. When a party seeks a stay pending appeal, the Supreme Court’s Nken v. Holder decision supplies parallel considerations, often stated as four factors: (1) whether the stay applicant has made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuing the stay will substantially injure other parties; and (4) where the public interest lies. Nken clarifies that the first two factors—likelihood of success and irreparable injury—are the most critical, but that the balance-of-interests and public-interest factors also play an important role.

Pacito as illustration. The Ninth Circuit’s stay pending appeal in Pacito applied the Nken factors. The panel concluded the government made a strong showing of likelihood of success on the plaintiffs’ challenge to the suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f), citing both the statute’s broad presidential discretion and Supreme Court precedent that counsels deference in entry-suspension contexts. The panel also concluded that the remaining Nken factors—irreparable injury to the government (interpreted to include foreign and domestic policy implications), injury to third parties, and public interest—tilted in favor of a stay. At the same time, the panel held that the government was not likely to prevail on the APA challenge to termination of cooperative agreements under § 1522, and therefore the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services for refugees already admitted — a narrow remedial carveout. This outcome shows how courts can grant broad deference in one dimension (admission suspensions) while preserving relief where concrete statutory duties and imminent harms are at stake.

Practical advice for litigators. When seeking a preliminary injunction or opposing a stay, counsel should calibrate argument to the four-factor framework. Emphasize the strength of the merits, but do not neglect irreparable harm: produce concrete evidence (testimony, affidavits, operational details) showing imminent, non-compensable injury. Identify the balance of equities, including harms to third parties, and make persuasive public-interest arguments. In cases implicating national security or foreign affairs, anticipate the court’s sensitivity to diplomatic and safety considerations and tailor evidentiary submissions accordingly.

## Consular nonreviewability and its limits; review of generally applicable policies vs. individual visa denials

The consular nonreviewability doctrine bars courts from second-guessing a consular officer’s decision to grant or deny visas to particular applicants. Kleindienst v. Mandel established the core rule: when the Executive head of the political department (usually the Secretary of State) provides a “facially legitimate and bona fide” reason for denying a visa, courts will not probe the merits. Subsequent cases (for example, Kerry v. Din, Trump v. Hawaii) have fleshed out and complicated this doctrine, but its central thrust is that individual visa denials and certain admission decisions are not amenable to searching judicial review because they implicate the political branches’ authority over entry and foreign relations.

Nonetheless, consular nonreviewability is not absolute. Several important limits should be noted and were implicitly engaged by the issues in Pacito:

- Individual vs. programmatic or generally applicable policies. Courts are more likely to abstain from reviewing specific denial determinations than to decline review of generally applicable policies or statutory implementation. A facial challenge to an agency policy that applies broadly and affects statutory rights can be reviewable even if individual visa decisions are not. Pacito illustrates this difference: the court was unwilling to entertain deep scrutiny of the President’s statutory power to suspend entry under § 1182(f), but it did entertain APA challenges to the executive’s funding decisions that affected refugees already admitted — a programmatic, statutory-duty context rather than a classic consular visa denial.

- Constitutional claims and clear statutory entitlements. Consular nonreviewability does not bar all judicial remedies for constitutional violations, nor does it extinguish review where Congress has crafted a statutory scheme that creates enforceable rights and duties. For example, if an individual’s constitutional rights are implicated by an exclusion, or if statute creates particular entitlements to a benefit or procedure, courts retain the ability to review. The doctrine also does not immunize arbitrary administration of programs once aliens are within the United States or where domestic statutory protections apply.

- The “facially legitimate and bona fide” test has limits. Where the Executive's stated rationale for denial is pretextual, contradicted by the record, or not bona fide, courts have been willing to probe further. This is rare and entails high evidentiary thresholds, but it signals that the political-branch insulation is not absolute.

In short, consular nonreviewability gives the Executive special protection in individual entry and visa determinations, but programmatic policies that implicate statutory duties or that affect aliens already admitted fall outside the doctrine’s protective shield.

## Applying the doctrines in Pacito: the remedy the court ordered and the split over statutory interpretation

Pacito’s outcome was a nuanced application of these principles. The Ninth Circuit stayed the district court’s preliminary injunctions against the suspension of refugee admissions pending appeal — concluding substantial deference to the President under § 1182(f) and relying on Trump v. Hawaii. But the panel also held that the plaintiff refugees and resettlement organizations were likely to succeed on an APA claim because the government may have violated statutory duties set out in § 1522 to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. The remedy was correspondingly narrow and pragmatic: the panel directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services for refugees who had been admitted to the United States, and to provide “the usual and customary services” afforded under the prior agreements.

Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent exposes the statutory-interpretation crux that many cases will present post–Loper Bright. She argued that § 1522 is better read as permissive, not mandatory, because its heading uses the word “Authorization,” several specific program provisions “authorize” the Director to provide services, and the text repeatedly qualifies duties by the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations.” From her perspective, reading § 1522 as mandating the provision of services would disregard textual signals and improperly constrain executive discretion — a point that underscores how post–Loper Bright review requires courts to parse statutory text and structure and to evaluate the persuasiveness of competing interpretations rather than simply defer to agency characterizations.

For practitioners, Pacito supplies a model for crafting remedies in cases that interlock foreign-affairs discretion and statutory protections. When a court finds limited review is appropriate, it will tailor relief narrowly: it may preserve executive latitude in the nationally sensitive domain (suspending admissions) while enforcing statutory duties that protect concrete domestic interests (resettlement services for already admitted refugees). Remedial orders are likely to be field-specific and limited to what the statute requires and the evidence shows.

## Practical roadmap for litigating and deciding cases like Pacito

When you confront a challenge to an immigration or agency funding decision, proceed through the following steps (each of which Pacito exemplifies):

1. Identify the challenged action and the actor. Is the action presidential, agency (regulation, policy), or an operational decision (termination of cooperative agreements, funding rescission, consular denial)? Is the actor the President, a cabinet secretary, or a subordinate agency official?

2. Determine reviewability under the APA.
   - Ask whether § 701(a)(2) applies: does the statute commit the particular decision to agency or executive discretion with no judicially manageable standard? If the statutory command centers on an official’s unreviewable “finding,” courts may treat it as not subject to meaningful review.
   - If § 701(a)(2) does not bar review, determine whether the challenged action is final under Bennett.

3. Define the “law to apply.” If statute, regulation, or constitutional text provides guideposts (duty, procedure, reporting requirement), then there is law to apply and courts can review the agency’s compliance. If not, reviewability is jeopardized.

4. Choose the standard of review.
   - For factual and policy choices, apply arbitrary-and-capricious review; examine whether the agency considered relevant factors, explained itself, and did not act arbitrarily or inconsistently.
   - For statutory questions, apply post–Loper Bright methodology: independently interpret the statute while assessing the persuasiveness of the agency’s interpretation (Skidmore-type weight), giving more deference to well-reasoned, expertise-grounded agency interpretations but not automatic Chevron step-two deference.

5. Account for foreign-affairs and national-security contexts. Recognize that courts will be hesitant to engage in searching review of presidential or high-level foreign-affairs determinations, but that statutory mandates and domestic interests can still be enforceable.

6. If seeking emergency relief, frame your arguments around Winter (preliminary injunction) or Nken (stay pending appeal) factors. Emphasize evidence of irreparable harm and the practical harms to third parties and the public interest. Expect courts to balance national-security or diplomatic concerns against statutory protections and imminent harms.

7. Craft remedies carefully. Courts will construe injunctions to avoid trenching on core political-branch authority while protecting statutory rights and preventing irreparable injury. Narrow, tailored relief that preserves executive discretion in sensitive domains while enforcing concrete statutory obligations stands a greater chance of surviving appeal.

## Conclusion

Pacito provides a concise case study in modern administrative and immigration law. It shows that courts will often apply a context-sensitive approach: deferring to the political branches in matters of entry and national security while retaining the capacity to enforce statutory duties and protect concrete rights, especially once aliens are lawfully within the United States. Post–Loper Bright, courts will not routinely accord Chevron-style deference to agencies’ statutory interpretations; instead, agencies’ interpretations must persuade. Finally, the equitable standards of Winter and Nken require litigants to develop solid evidence of likelihood of success and irreparable harm, and courts will tailor relief to respect both national-security prerogatives and enforceable statutory obligations.

# Doctrinal Baselines from Supreme Court Precedent

This chapter surveys the Supreme Court authorities that anchor the modern legal landscape of presidential authority over immigration, the Court’s willingness to defer to the political branches on entry and foreign‑affairs decisions, and the limits on that deference when statutory text or the Refugee Act rights are implicated. Those precedents form the doctrinal backdrop to the Ninth Circuit’s Pacito decision: on one hand, a strong current of judicial deference to Presidential judgments about “entry” and national security; on the other hand, statutory obligations and administrative‑law doctrines that can check executive action where Congress has commanded or conditioned a program. We examine five doctrinal loci: the mid‑20th century entry‑control cases (Knauff and Mezei), the extraterritorial/refugee context of Sale v. Haitian Centers Council, the modern statement of deference in Trump v. Hawaii, the Refugee Act framework and its application in Negusie v. Holder, and the post‑Loper Bright environment for agency statutory interpretation. Each section explains the holding, the reasoning, the applicable standards of review or interpretive tests, and how the precedent matters when courts evaluate executive action that intersects immigration and statutory duties.

## Knauff and Mezei: historical roots of plenary power and entry control

Knauff v. Shaughnessy (1950) and Landon v. Plasencia/United States ex rel. Mezei (1953) are the classical authorities that established and exemplified the doctrine that the political branches possess plenary and predominantly unreviewable authority to exclude noncitizens at the border or to control their entry. Those cases are the starting point for any inquiry into judicial review of Presidential or executive decisions categorically suspending or limiting admission.

Knauff arose out of an administrative decision to exclude a noncitizen spouse of a United States citizen during an era of heightened national‑security concern. The Supreme Court upheld the exclusion even though the excluded alien had been married to a U.S. citizen and despite procedural limitations on the opportunity to contest the ground for exclusion. The Court famously stated that “the power to exclude or expel aliens” is “a fundamental sovereign attribute exercised by the Government’s political departments largely immune from judicial inquiry or control.” The opinion justified narrow procedural protections for arriving aliens but refused to substitute the courts’ judgment for the political branches’ national‑security determinations.

Mezei extended those principles to an even starker factual pattern. Mezei had been a lawful long‑term resident who left the United States and later sought readmission; the Attorney General denied entry and the alien was detained on Ellis Island for years. The Court held that the exclusion of a noncitizen who had not obtained a right of reentry could be indefinite and did not create the kind of “presence” that would trigger full constitutional protections. The key doctrinal points from Mezei are (1) a sharp distinction between “entry” (the point at which the sovereign may control who comes into its territory) and mere presence or residency; and (2) a very limited role for the judiciary in second‑guessing policy judgments about exclusion. In Mezei the Court accepted absence of judicial inquiry into the merits of the admission decision when the political branches say exclusion is required.

What these cases teach about standards of review is essential. Knauff and Mezei do not articulate a multi‑factor test the way later cases will; instead they establish a doctrine of very great deference to the executive and legislative judgments on entry. Judicial review—when permitted at all—looked to process and to whether the exclusion was authorized by competent authority rather than to the substantive reasonableness of the exclusion decision. Those decisions therefore underpin modern holdings that courts will often refrain from “searching” review when a national‑security or foreign‑policy justification for excluding classes of noncitizens is proffered.

Limitations and subsequent developments must be noted. Knauff and Mezei were decided in a different era, and later cases have given more procedural protections where presence or residency is at stake, or where deprivation of fundamental rights follows from prolonged detention or the withdrawal of a long‑standing status. Nonetheless, for present purposes their enduring doctrinal contribution is clear: when the question is admission or exclusion at the border, the political branches possess dominant authority and courts defer—often intensely—to those branches’ judgments.

## Sale v. Haitian Centers Council: extraterritorial application and statutory interpretation in the refugee context

Sale v. Haitian Centers Council, 509 U.S. 155 (1993), addresses a distinct but related question: how statutory protections for refugees operate when actions take place outside the sovereign territory of the United States. The case involved U.S. interdiction of vessels on the high seas to prevent Haitian migrants from reaching the United States; the migrants challenged that practice on statutory and treaty grounds, notably under the Refugee Act and the Convention Against Torture (CAT).

The core holding of Sale was that the protections of the United States’ refugee statutes (as interpreted in light of the Refugee Act) do not automatically reach aliens intercepted and turned away on the high seas. The Court parsed Congress’s statutory language and applied the presumption against extraterritoriality: because the Refugee Act and related provisions spoke to rights and processes “in the United States,” the Court declined to read an extraterritorial application into the statute absent an unmistakably clear statement from Congress. The Court also emphasized that when immigration controls are executed as part of foreign‑affairs and interdiction practices, the political branches’ policy judgments deserve deference.

Sale is instructive for two doctrinal reasons. First, it reminds students that statutory text matters when determining whether statutory constraints limit executive power: the presence or absence of an explicit territorial application can be dispositive. Courts will typically invoke the presumption against extraterritorial application and will not assume that Congress intended to apply domestic statutory regimes to conduct abroad unless it clearly said so. Second, Sale exemplifies a repeated theme: where border control policy intersects international relations, the judiciary is reluctant to substitute its own judgment for the political branches’ assessments of how best to pursue those policies.

Sale also shows the limits of international law arguments in domestic statutory interpretation. The Court acknowledged CAT and other international obligations but refused to import those norms into domestic law absent clear Congressional instruction. For students, Sale is a cautionary lesson on how statutory methodology (text, structure, presumption against extraterritoriality) and institutional comity to the political branches combine to constrain a court’s willingness to impose domestic remedies that would regulate executive action outside national territory.

## Trump v. Hawaii: breadth of § 1182(f), deference to presidential findings, and limits on searching inquiry

Trump v. Hawaii, 585 U.S. ___ (2018), is the modern anchor for judicial deference to Presidential proclamations made under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The case involved a Presidential proclamation restricting entry by nationals of several countries; plaintiffs brought constitutional and statutory challenges. The Supreme Court upheld the proclamation and articulated a mode of review that is both deferential and doctrinally specific to this statutory context.

Three principles from Trump v. Hawaii are most significant. First, the Court reaffirmed that § 1182(f) grants the President broad authority to suspend the entry of any “class of aliens” whenever the President “finds” their entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The statute’s plain language and historical pedigree, the Court said, justify substantial deference to Presidential findings that underpin an entry restriction.

Second, the Court described the permissible scope of judicial inquiry into those findings. Importing the language of rational‑basis review in an immigration context, the Court declared that courts should not conduct “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justification.” Put differently, as long as the proclamation is “facially legitimate and bona fide,” and the President has set forth some stated reason that could plausibly support the determination that entry should be restricted, courts must be circumspect. The Court reasoned that the combination of the statutory grant to the President and the foreign‑policy character of the decision counsels against intensive evidentiary review by the judiciary.

Third, the Court carefully delineated limits: § 1182(f) is not unbounded. The President's proclamation must fit within the statutory text (there must be a finding that entry would be detrimental), and constitutional constraints remain operative. The plurality also intimated that overtly discriminatory motives that are evident on the face of a proclamation could produce different results; the Court’s approach emphasized a focus on the facial validity and bona fides of the Presidential declaration.

How should students apply Trump v. Hawaii? When an executive action suspending or restricting entry is challenged, a court assessing a claim about § 1182(f) ought first to determine whether the President has complied with the textual predicate of the statute (i.e., has “found” detriment) and whether the measure is facially legitimate and bona fide. If so, the judicial role is narrow: courts will not substitute their own appraisal of the wisdom of the policy and will accept reasonable inferences from the executive’s stated justification, subject to the usual protections for constitutional rights. Trump thus strengthens the executive’s hand in defending categorical suspensions of entry while leaving some space for judicial protection against categorical races to violate obvious textual or constitutional limits.

Pacito’s Ninth Circuit relied directly on Trump to conclude that the government is likely to prevail on the merits of the claim that Executive Order No. 14163 suspending refugee admissions falls within the President’s § 1182(f) authority. The Ninth Circuit quoted Trump’s instruction that courts should not undertake a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness” of the President’s stated reasons and that § 1182(f)’s “sole prerequisite” is the President’s “finding.” Students should understand that Trump reduces the inquiry a court will perform when an admission suspension is framed as a Presidential finding under § 1182(f), but it does not make the statute a blank check.

## Negusie v. Holder and the Refugee Act as a general framework for refugee treatment

The Refugee Act of 1980 incorporated the U.N. Refugee Convention’s definition of “refugee” into U.S. law and created statutory mechanisms for asylum and for refugee resettlement and assistance. Understanding that statutory framework is essential when statutory claims — for example, under the Administrative Procedure Act — are brought to constrain executive action affecting refugees.

Negusie v. Holder, 555 U.S. 511 (2009), illustrates an application of the Refugee Act’s substantive statutes to a delicate fact pattern concerning the persecutor bar. The question was whether an alien who had participated in persecutory acts while acting under duress (compelled by government actors) nevertheless could be held to have “assisted” persecutors, thus triggering the bar to asylum or withholding of removal. The Supreme Court held that where the administrative decision implicitly relied on an incorrect legal standard — for instance, by failing to consider whether assistance was compelled rather than voluntary — review and remand were required. The case highlights two points.

First, Negusie characterizes the Refugee Act as providing a “general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees and displaced persons.” The Act defines who qualifies for refugee status, the processes for adjudicating claims, and important protections that apply to refugees and certain classes of applicants — rules that operate independently from, and sometimes in tension with, the political branches’ authority over admissions. In other words, even as the political branches control entry, Congress has established substantive and procedural rights that apply when persons are found to be refugees or when refugees are in the United States.

Second, Negusie emphasizes that administrative adjudications under the Refugee Act remain subject to ordinary judicial review for legal error. When an agency or board misstates the legal standard — as the Board of Immigration Appeals did by failing properly to account for duress — courts must ensure that statutory standards are correctly applied. The case is therefore a reminder that an individual asylum or withholding claim is not beyond judicial correction where legal mistakes affect the outcome.

This doctrinal balance — broad executive authority over admission, but statutory rights and agency adjudication subject to judicial correction — explains why a court like the Ninth Circuit in Pacito could simultaneously find strong deference to the President under § 1182(f) and yet conclude that statutory obligations created by Congress (for example, the reception and placement duties of § 1522) may be enforceable against the executive under the APA. Negusie helps students see that the Refugee Act supplies substantive expectations about treatment of refugees that do not evaporate simply because the executive announces a change in admissions policy.

## Post–Loper Bright environment: agency interpretations after Chevron’s demise

For over thirty years, Chevron U.S.A., Inc. v. Natural Resources Defense Council, Inc., 467 U.S. 837 (1984), shaped judicial review of agency statutory interpretations. Under the Chevron two‑step, courts asked (1) whether Congress’s statutory text was unambiguous on the particular question; if ambiguous, then (2) whether the agency’s interpretation was reasonable. Auer deference extended similar deference to agencies’ good‑faith interpretations of their own regulations. Those doctrines gave agencies significant interpretive primacy.

Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (2024) changed that landscape. The Supreme Court in Loper Bright curtailed the Chevron framework, returning to a model in which courts apply traditional tools of statutory interpretation and give agency interpretations weight only to the extent persuasive under Skidmore v. Swift & Co., 323 U.S. 134 (1944), or under whatever mode of analysis the Court prescribed. The result is twofold.

First, courts must now resolve statutory ambiguities without the presumption that agency interpretations are entitled to controlling deference merely because Congress left a gap. Courts instead conduct an independent textual and contextual analysis and may adopt the agency’s view if it is persuasive under ordinary canons and if it coheres with the statute’s structure and purpose. Second, agencies cannot rely on a presumption of deference to immunize their statutory constructions from searching judicial analysis; they must persuade courts that their reading is correct.

What are the implications for immigration law and for cases like Pacito? Two points matter.

- Agency statutory interpretations that previously might have survived scrutiny by invoking Chevron must now survive a more searching review under ordinary interpretive tools. Thus, an agency that claims that § 1522 authorizes or mandates particular resettlement services will no longer be able to depend on Chevron to shield its interpretation from a court’s independent textual analysis. In Pacito, this dynamic helps explain why the Ninth Circuit felt authorized to scrutinize the government’s statutory obligations under § 1522 and to conclude that reinstating cooperative agreements might be required.

- Chevron’s reduction does not erase other doctrines of judicial deference that are rooted in the Constitution’s allocation of powers to the political branches, especially in foreign affairs and national security. Trump v. Hawaii’s instruction that courts should not conduct a “searching inquiry” into Presidential findings under § 1182(f) rests less on agency‑interpretation deference and more on separation‑of‑powers principles and international‑relations comity. Loper Bright does not automatically displace that mode of deference; it changes how courts treat agencies’ claims about what Congress meant when Congress alone wrote the statute.

Students should therefore appreciate the post‑Loper Bright legal environment as one in which statutory disputes with agencies are resolved more by independent judicial interpretation of the statutory text, but where the judiciary still may give substantial, functionally different deference to Presidential or executive determinations when Congress has conferred special, textually grounded authorities (as with § 1182(f)) and when foreign affairs or national‑security considerations are implicated. In short, Loper Bright narrows the shield Congress historically gave agencies under Chevron, but it does not eliminate the separate, often robust, reason for judicial deference to political‑branch judgments on immigration admissions and foreign‑affairs policy.

## Bringing the precedents together: how the doctrinal baselines govern cases like Pacito

Taken together, these precedents establish two complementary lessons that inform Pacito and future litigation about refugee suspensions, resettlement funding, and APA challenges.

First, when the executive claims authority to suspend or limit entry under a statute like § 1182(f), courts will typically be highly deferential to the President’s finding that entry would be “detrimental,” and they will refrain from conducting a searching inquiry into the merits of the national‑security or foreign‑policy rationale. Knauff and Mezei supply the historical foundation for that deference; Trump v. Hawaii furnishes the modern formulation: judicial review is narrow when the action is framed as an exercise of the President’s admission authority.

Second, statutory duties that Congress imposes on the executive — including obligations to provide reception and placement services for refugees under the Refugee Act (e.g., 8 U.S.C. § 1522) — remain judicially enforceable. Sale emphasizes the territorial boundaries of statutory protection and the role of text in determining coverage; Negusie emphasizes that the Refugee Act frames substantive rights and fair adjudicatory practices to which courts may hold the executive. After Loper Bright, courts will resolve statutory ambiguities without automatic deference to agencies and will more readily subject agency interpretations to independent textual analysis. Thus, if Congress’s statutory commands are clear, the executive cannot evade them merely by invoking policy judgments about admission; and even where ambiguity exists, the agency must persuasively justify its reading to a court that now performs a more searching textual inquiry.

Applying these lessons in practice requires careful sequencing. A court faced with a case like Pacito must first identify the legal question and the source of authority or obligation. If the question concerns the President’s authority to suspend entry under § 1182(f), Trump v. Hawaii guides the analysis and calls for deference to the Presidential finding provided that the proclamation is facially legitimate and bona fide. If the question concerns whether the executive has complied with statutory obligations (for example, duties to provide reception and placement services to persons already admitted), the court will examine the statutory text, structure, and purposes, and—post‑Loper Bright—will give the agency’s interpretation no special presumption of correctness. Where Congress has spoken clearly, the executive must comply; where Congress has not, the agency must persuade the court that its interpretation is the correct reading.

That bifurcation explains the Ninth Circuit’s result in Pacito: a likelihood of success for the government’s claim under § 1182(f) (drawing on Trump and the plenary power tradition) and, simultaneously, a ruling that the government may have violated the statutory resettlement obligations in § 1522 (where independent statutory interpretation and enforcement via the APA remain appropriate). Students should therefore be alert to the differing doctrinal pathways courts use—one deferring to Presidential factual and policy judgments at the border; the other enforcing statutory duties that Congress imposed on the executive even as the outer contours of executive authority over admissions remain broad.

# The USRAP Ecosystem: Who Does What and How

The United States Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) is not a single agency or program; it is an interlocking ecosystem of federal offices, statutory duties, cooperative and contractual relationships with nongovernmental partners, and annual appropriations. The recent Ninth Circuit expedited proceedings in Pacito v. Trump (Sept. 12, 2025) provide a useful lens for understanding how this ecosystem operates in practice and how disputes about roles, instruments, and funding turn into litigation. This chapter explains the operational division of labor among the Department of State (and its Bureau of Population, Refugees, and Migration, PRM), the Department of Health and Human Services (via the Office of Refugee Resettlement, ORR), and the private nonprofit resettlement organizations (the “VOLAGs” and their local affiliates). It also walks through the legal instruments—cooperative agreements, grants, and contracts—used to deliver services, the distinct legal consequences of those instruments for administrative-law review, and how funding flows and appropriation constraints shape what actually can be done on the ground.

## State Department (PRM) and ‘Reception and Placement’ (R&P) cooperative agreements

At the overseas and initial U.S.-entry level, responsibility largely lies with the State Department’s Bureau of Population, Refugees, and Migration (PRM). PRM coordinates overseas processing of refugee claims and manages key cooperative agreements with international and domestic nongovernmental organizations to facilitate both overseas processing and the immediate reception of refugees once admitted. The Ninth Circuit’s opinion in Pacito frames this role: the State Department had entered into cooperative agreements with resettlement organizations to provide reception and placement (R&P) services and then terminated those agreements in February 2025, precipitating the litigation.

Reception and placement (R&P) services refer to the immediate, short-term services that newly admitted refugees receive—transportation from port of entry, initial reception at airport or arrival point, short-term housing, an initial package of cash support, health checks/referrals, case management, and rapid employment services or referrals. These services exist to bridge the initial gap after admission and to carry out the statutory purpose of facilitating resettlement and self-sufficiency.

Legally, PRM most often uses “cooperative agreements” with public or private nonprofit organizations (the VOLAGs and their local affiliates) to deliver R&P services. Cooperative agreements are one of the statutory mechanisms contemplated by Congress in the Refugee Act and related statutory schemes for funding and partnering with NGOs; they permit PRM to be more programmatically involved than a simple grant relationship. The Ninth Circuit’s order recognized the historic use of cooperative agreements for these functions and tied their termination to the government’s responsibility under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. In practice, PRM’s cooperative agreements typically contain programmatic obligations, reporting requirements, and conditions about performance, but they remain assistance instruments that depend on appropriations.

Because PRM manages the overseas flow and the handoff to domestic resettlement partners, decisions at PRM about terminating cooperative agreements have immediate, operational consequences: if PRM ends cooperative agreements without a viable alternative, there may be no organized mechanism to receive and place admitted refugees in U.S. communities. That factual reality informed the Ninth Circuit’s interim remedy directing reinstatement of necessary cooperative agreements pending fuller adjudication.

## HHS Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) grants for longer-term integration services under § 1522

On the domestic side, longer‑term services and integration efforts—such as cash and medical assistance for up to thirty-six months, employment training and placement, English language services, and special protections for unaccompanied refugee children—are statutorily tied to the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) within HHS, principally under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. Section 1522 and implementing regulations (e.g., 45 C.F.R. §§ 400.200 et seq.) create the statutory framework for the federal government’s provision of resettlement assistance and authorize (and, as litigated, arguably constrain) the types of programs ORR should support.

ORR typically distributes funds through grants and cooperative agreements to state and local agencies and to nonprofit resettlement organizations. These instruments fund the medium-term services that enable economic self-sufficiency: employment services, English-learning programs, case management, and targeted cash/medical supports. ORR also exercises a statutory responsibility with respect to unaccompanied children, including assuming certain responsibilities “during any interim period while such a child is in the United States” (8 U.S.C. § 1522(d)(2)(B)), which can entail immediate financial and legal responsibility in limited circumstances.

The Ninth Circuit’s order emphasized § 1522’s statutory regime when evaluating whether the termination of cooperative agreements and suspension of funding could violate the Administrative Procedure Act. The court distinguished the President’s broad immigration entry authority (8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)) from statutory obligations to provide services to refugees already admitted. In short: PRM and ORR have different but complementary roles—PRM handles overseas processing and arrival logistics; ORR funds and oversees domestic integration services—but both operate within statutory and appropriations constraints.

## Public and private nonprofit partners: the role of voluntary agencies (VOLAGs) and affiliates

Private nonprofit partners—often called “VOLAGs” (voluntary agencies)—are the operational backbone of USRAP inside the United States, and many also provide overseas liaison or processing support. Historically prominent VOLAGs include Church World Service, HIAS, Lutheran Immigration and Refugee Service, U.S. Committee for Refugees and Immigrants, Episcopal Migration Ministries, and others. These national VOLAGs manage national intake, coordinate local affiliate networks, and receive federal funding (via grants, cooperative agreements, or contracts) to deliver R&P and longer-term integration services. Local affiliates and subcontractors carry out on-the-ground tasks—housing placements, initial casework, employment services, and local community integration.

The legal relationship between VOLAGs and the federal government is shaped by the instrument used (cooperative agreement vs grant vs contract), by the terms of individual agreements (performance metrics, reporting, and termination clauses), and by applicable federal assistance rules (e.g., 2 C.F.R. Part 200 and agency-specific regulations). VOLAGs act as both implementers of public policy and as private parties that may have statutory or contractual expectations. As Pacito demonstrates, when the government abruptly terminates cooperative agreements, VOLAGs may seek judicial relief—either on statutory or APA grounds—arguing that the termination deprives refugees of congressionally required services or rests on arbitrary or unlawful agency action.

VOLAGs’ enforcement options vary. Where an agreement creates enforceable rights or is a “final agency action” for APA purposes, a recipient may challenge its termination in federal court under the APA. If a termination implicates breach-of-contract theories, sovereign immunity can limit remedies unless an express waiver exists in the contract. The structure and wording of funding instruments therefore matter greatly to downstream legal remedies.

## Cooperative agreements vs. grants vs. contracts: legal distinctions and implications for APA review

Practically and legally, it matters whether the federal government uses a cooperative agreement, a grant, or a procurement contract. Each instrument carries different expectations about federal involvement, statutory oversight, procurement law, and the potential for judicial review.

- Cooperative agreements: These are assistance instruments used when the federal agency expects substantial programmatic involvement with the recipient. The government plays an ongoing role in program design, monitoring, and technical collaboration. Cooperative agreements are governed by the agency’s grant regulations and by the Uniform Guidance (2 C.F.R. Part 200) for federal awards. Their collaborative nature does not automatically make them federal procurement contracts, but they do reflect a close operational relationship. When terminated, cooperative agreements often present claims under the APA (if the termination is an “agency action”), and under the statutory framework applicable to federal assistance. Courts may treat termination of cooperative agreements as reviewable final agency action where the termination has legal consequences (cf. Bennett v. Spear) or where statutes create mandatory duties that funding decisions frustrate.

- Grants: Grants are assistance instruments where the federal government generally provides funds with more limited programmatic involvement. Grants remain subject to uniform grant rules and typically contemplate greater recipient discretion in program execution. Like cooperative agreements, grants depend on congressional appropriations and are governed by grant regulations. Challenges to grant decisions—award, denial, or termination—are sometimes treated as nonreviewable if they amount to policy choices or resource allocation, but they can be reviewable where the agency’s action is final, has legal consequences, or violates statutory duties.

- Contracts: Procurement contracts are governed by procurement law and the Federal Acquisition Regulation (FAR). They are exchange agreements in which the government procures specific goods or services for its own use. Contract law claims typically proceed in the Court of Federal Claims or under procurement dispute mechanisms; sovereign immunity is often waived under the Contract Disputes Act for bilateral remediation, but different remedies and doctrines apply than for grants/cooperative agreements.

For APA review, the central question is whether the agency action being challenged is “final” and not committed to agency discretion by law. Bennett v. Spear supplies the two-part test for final agency action: (1) the action must mark the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process (i.e., not be tentative or interlocutory); and (2) it must be one by which rights or obligations have been determined or from which legal consequences will flow. The Ninth Circuit’s order in Pacito found that termination of cooperative agreements and suspension of USRAP funding could produce such legal consequences—implicating statutory duties under § 1522—and thus may be reviewable under the APA. This contrasts with pure programmatic, resource-allocation decisions that the Supreme Court has often treated as nonreviewable (Heckler v. Chaney) unless statutory duties produce a different calculus.

One practical implication: agencies that want to preserve discretion should draft assistance instruments and termination decisions carefully to emphasize discretionary language and to avoid creating enforceable legal entitlements—though statutory frameworks (like § 1522) may limit that effort.

## Funding flows and appropriation constraints; rescissions and apportionment

The legal reality of USRAP is shaped by the concentric rings of statutory authorization, congressional appropriation, and executive apportionment. § 1522 and related statutory provisions authorize programs and specify statutory expectations. But authorization alone does not create an appropriation: Congress must appropriate funds in annual or supplemental appropriation acts for agencies to obligate and expend federal money. The Pacito opinion underscores this duality: the panel recited § 1522’s duties while also acknowledging that many of those duties are qualified “to the extent of available appropriations.” Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent focused on this point—reading § 1522 as an authorization framework that contemplates but does not independently create funding obligations in the absence of appropriations.

Appropriations law also encompasses rescissions and apportionment. Congress may enact rescissions that reduce previously appropriated sums (the Pacito opinion cited the FY2025 appropriations figure of $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance and a subsequent $800 million rescission). Once Congress rescinds or reduces appropriations, agencies must operate within those reduced limits and OMB must apportion the remaining funds during the fiscal year. Antideficiency Act constraints prohibit agencies from obligating funds beyond the appropriated and apportioned amounts, so executive decisions—like suspending program funding—must be grounded in lawful appropriation management.

The Ninth Circuit observed these fiscal facts when balancing the stay factors: the court recognized statutory duties but also the reality that funding depends on congressional appropriations and that the executive exercises discretion when apportioning or suspending obligations. Where the agency claims it cannot fulfill statutory functions because funds have been rescinded or not apportioned, courts will weigh the administration’s representation against evidentiary showings. In Pacito, the government told the court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals for a new resettlement agency” yet could not identify any progress; the court treated that uncertainty as part of the justification for directing interim reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to meet statutory obligations.

The practical lesson is that statutory “shall” language must be read in context of appropriations clauses and the separation of powers. Courts will confront tensions between congressional mandates and appropriations cuts and may order relief where the record shows the executive cannot lawfully or practically fulfill statutory duties without reinstating existing arrangements.

## Practical consequences and doctrinal takeaways

Pacito highlights several recurring doctrinal themes a practitioner or student should internalize:

- Allocation of authority matters. The President’s broad statutory authority under § 1182(f) to suspend admission of classes of aliens is distinct from statutory duties to provide services to aliens already admitted. Courts will afford deference on entry controls but scrutinize executive actions that frustrate specific statutory programs.

- The choice of instrument matters. Cooperative agreements create programmatic entanglement and often produce concrete legal consequences when terminated—consequences that may be reviewable under the APA. Grants provide more recipient discretion but are still tied to appropriations and regulatory strings. Contracts live in procurement law’s separate world.

- Statutory language matters. “Shall” and “authorized” create different expectations. “To the extent of available appropriations” is a limiting clause that frequently controls outcome, but it does not automatically nullify statutory expectations; courts will examine whether appropriations cuts or executive rescissions render the agency incapable of meeting mandatory duties.

- Funding is political and legal. Appropriations and rescissions are congressional choices that constrain agencies; OMB apportionment and the Antideficiency Act constrain administrative action. An agency that terminates cooperative agreements because of budgetary choices must show a lawful and reasoned basis that accords with statutes and regulations.

- Remedies will be tailored to operational realities. Where termination of agreements produces immediate harm and the government cannot show an alternative to achieve statutory aims, courts may order reinstatement of agreements or other equitable relief pending fuller review—what the Ninth Circuit directed here.

For students, litigators, and policymakers, Pacito is a reminder that USRAP is a layered system of statutory directives, appropriations, federal implementing instruments, and private partners. Understanding who does what—PRM overseas and for R&P cooperative agreements; ORR for longer-term integration under § 1522; and VOLAGs as the implementers—is necessary to predict legal exposure and to design instruments and litigation strategies. When disputes arise, the legal classification of an instrument, the statutory text, and the fiscal record will often be dispositive both for reviewability and for the form of judicial relief.

# Pacito v. Trump: Facts, Procedure, and the Interim Stay Order

This chapter uses the Ninth Circuit’s expedited interim order in Pacito v. Trump (Sept. 12, 2025) as a vehicle to study three intertwined themes that commonly arise in litigation over executive immigration action: (1) the scope of presidential authority to exclude classes of aliens under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and related immigration statutes; (2) the limits imposed by federal statutes that create programmatic duties or authorize resettlement services for refugees; and (3) the procedure and standard for stays pending appeal of preliminary injunctions (the Nken factors), including how courts reconcile deference to the President on immigration policy with statutory duties that fall on other agencies. The opinion is unusual because the panel simultaneously concluded that the President likely had authority to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f) while also concluding the government was unlikely to prevail on at least one Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenge tied to statutory obligations under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The concurrence/dissent by Judge Lee provides a contrasting interpretive account of § 1522 that sharpens the doctrinal issue: is § 1522 a source of mandatory statutory duties that the courts may enforce, or merely an authorization that leaves the matter to executive discretion?

Below we set out the relevant executive actions and statutes, then walk through the district court’s injunctions, the Ninth Circuit’s stay analysis, and Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent. At each step we explain the doctrinal rules and how to apply them to new fact patterns.

## Executive Order 14163: Suspension of Refugee Entry under § 1182(f) and § 1185(a)

Executive Order No. 14163, titled “Realigning the United States Refugee Admissions Program,” directed a suspension of refugee admissions to the United States. The Order invoked two statutory authorities: 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) and § 1185(a). Section 1182(f) provides that “whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States, he may by proclamation . . . suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens.” Section 1185(a) gives the President power to “make rules and regulations for the conduct of [immigration-related] functions” and to protect border security and the administration of immigration laws. Together, these provisions have been repeatedly cited by Presidents as the source of authority to impose entry restrictions on foreign nationals.

Legal doctrine. Pacito shows the continuing force of two related principles. First, courts traditionally afford substantial deference to the President in the immigration and national security contexts, particularly when Congress has granted broad, textually explicit authority. The Supreme Court’s decision in Trump v. Hawaii (2018) is the key precedent: it characterized § 1182(f) as a grant of broad, plenary authority that “exudes deference” to the President and limited courts’ inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications. Under that line of cases, when the President “finds” that entry would be detrimental, courts generally treat that finding as the prerequisite that Congress required, and judicial review of the substantive policy decision is highly constrained.

Second, however, deference under § 1182(f) is not absolute. The statute’s plain text, as interpreted in the context of statutory structure, limits — at least in theory — the President’s authority to what Congress authorized. If another statute imposes duties or protects particular classes of people, the interplay between those statutory directives and § 1182(f) may be a matter for judicial resolution. The Pacito panel recognized this tension: it emphasized the high barrier to overturning the President’s suspension of admissions, while preserving the possibility that other statutory obligations (for example, duties to provide reception and placement services) could constrain the executive’s implementation of a suspension.

Applying the doctrine. Where an executive action cites § 1182(f), a court considering a challenge must ask (1) whether the President has complied with § 1182(f)’s textual prerequisite (i.e., that he “finds” entry of a class would be detrimental), and (2) whether the action exceeds textual or structural limits imposed by statute or constitutional constraints. Under Trump v. Hawaii, the first question will usually be resolved in the President’s favor; the second question requires careful attention to whether other statutes create mandatory rights or duties that the President’s action would nullify.

Practice tip. When litigating or analyzing a § 1182(f) action, separate the core question of authority under § 1182(f) (where deference is strong) from other statutory or constitutional claims (e.g., claims that the action violates a separate statutory duty). Demonstrating that § 1182(f) language is present is necessary but often not sufficient to win on every related claim.

## Executive Order 14169 and the Suspension of State Department Funding for USRAP

Shortly after EO 14163, the State Department suspended all funding for the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP), citing a separate executive order concerning foreign aid (EO 14169). The funding suspension was broad: it included overseas processing support and domestic resettlement (“reception and placement”) funding for refugees already admitted to the United States.

Statutory context. The Refugee Act of 1980 and the Immigration and Nationality Act create both authorization and appropriations pathways for refugee assistance and resettlement services. Relevant for Pacito is 8 U.S.C. § 1522, which is titled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” Section 1522 contains several subsections: (a) “Conditions and consideration” (with some mandatory phrasing), (b) and (c) through (e) describing programmatic authorities (often phrased as “is authorized”), (d) addressing unaccompanied refugee children, and (e) addressing cash and medical assistance (including § 1522(e)(7)(A) requiring the Secretary to “develop and implement” projects “under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed”). Section 1523 requires reporting and accountability to Congress. All of these provisions operate in the shadow of the appropriations power: some duties appear “to the extent of available appropriations” and many programmatic provisions presuppose congressional funding.

Legal doctrine. Two separate, but related, statutory doctrines are in play. One is the classic distinction between authorization statutes (which permit or authorize spending or program creation) and appropriations statutes (which provide the money). Courts insist that an authorization standing alone does not obligate an agency to spend; only an appropriation obliges money to be spent. The second doctrinal axis is statutory interpretation: whether language in § 1522 is mandatory (creating a judicially enforceable duty) or permissive (a delegation of discretion to the executive). The presence of words like “shall” weighs toward a mandatory reading; words like “is authorized” point towards discretion. Context and structure matter: provisions that state conditions “in providing assistance under this section” may mean that the “shall” language creates conditions that apply only if the government chooses to provide assistance.

Applying the doctrine. In Pacito the government used EO 14169 as the means to cut off the financial infrastructure that supported refugee resettlement. Whether that cutoff was lawful depends on whether § 1522 imposes enforceable duties that continue even when the President issues an order suspending admissions or when the State Department cuts funding. If § 1522 creates a mandatory statutory duty to provide certain reception and placement services for refugees already admitted, then suspending funding for those services may violate statute and the APA; if § 1522 is merely an authorization, the executive’s funding decisions remain within the political branches’ discretion.

Practice tip. When arguing whether a statute is mandatory or permissive, examine (a) the statutory text (presence of “shall,” “is authorized,” or “to the extent of available appropriations”), (b) the structure of the statutory scheme (does the “shall” qualify an authorization, or is it freestanding?), (c) legislative history sparingly where necessary, and (d) the practical possibility of enforcing the provision (e.g., whether an injunction ordering an agency to spend money is consistent with appropriations law).

## The District Court’s Preliminary Injunctions and Their Scope

After the two executive orders and the State Department’s terminations of cooperative agreements, a group of plaintiffs — refugees already admitted, refugees approved for resettlement but outside the country, and three resettlement organizations — sued in the Western District of Washington. The complaint asserted that EO 14163’s suspension violated the Refugee Act and that the defunding of USRAP violated the APA and other statutory duties. The district court issued preliminary injunctive relief in two stages.

Scope of the injunctions. On February 25, 2025, the district court orally enjoined the enforcement or implementation of §§ 3(a), (b), (c), and 4 of EO 14163 — i.e., the operative provisions suspending refugee admissions and setting terms for the program. The court supplemented that order in a written February 28 order that explicitly prohibited suspension of USRAP funding. When the State Department terminated cooperative agreements in late February, the district court, on March 24, issued a second preliminary injunction ordering the State Department to reinstate all cooperative agreements terminated after the February 25 ruling. In short, the district court enjoined (1) the President’s suspension of refugee admissions, (2) the State Department’s suspension of funding for USRAP, and (3) the terminations of cooperative agreements to provide reception and placement services.

Legal doctrine. Preliminary injunctions are extraordinary equitable remedies. To obtain preliminary relief, a plaintiff ordinarily must show (1) a likelihood of success on the merits (or at least serious questions going to the merits and a balance of hardships that tips sharply in the plaintiff’s favor), (2) a likelihood of irreparable harm in the absence of preliminary relief, (3) that the balance of equities favors the plaintiff, and (4) that an injunction is in the public interest. Here the district court concluded that plaintiffs had satisfied those requirements as to the suspensions and terminations.

Two features of this particular set of injunctions are important for doctrine and strategy. First, injunctive relief against executive action that implicates national security or foreign policy normally triggers a heightened concern for judicial restraint; courts will not lightly order executive agencies to conduct particular policy choices in those realms. Second, where the relief ordered requires agencies to spend money — for example, to reinstate cooperative agreements or resume funding for services — courts must take care not to exceed their authority under the Appropriations Clause or to issue orders that effectively compel the Executive to spend money without an appropriation. The district court in Pacito remedied what it viewed as statutory violations affecting refugees already admitted and the organizations that provide reception and placement services; the tension with executive authority and appropriations law is precisely why the case was immediately appealed and moved rapidly through the Ninth Circuit.

Practice tip. When seeking preliminary relief against a federal agency, frame the relief to avoid requiring the court to order spending in the absence of appropriations, or be prepared to justify such relief by reference to statutory mandatory duties (i.e., show a clear statutory command that controls agency discretion).

## The Ninth Circuit’s Stay Analysis under Nken: Carveouts, Vacatur of Prior Stays, and Current Directives

Procedure and the stay question. The government sought a stay of the district court’s February 28 and March 24 injunctions pending appeal. The Ninth Circuit applied the Supreme Court’s four-factor test for a stay pending appeal articulated in Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418 (2009): (1) whether the stay applicant is likely to succeed on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will be irreparably injured absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties interested in the proceeding; and (4) where the public interest lies. Nken directs courts to give particularly close attention to the first two factors.

How the panel applied Nken. The Ninth Circuit, sitting in an expedited motions panel and then en banc-ishly as a merits panel issuing an interim order, concluded that the government was likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the validity of EO 14163’s suspension of refugee admissions. The court relied on Trump v. Hawaii for the proposition that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” and the President’s “finding” that entry would be detrimental is the sole textual prerequisite the statute requires. Because the President had made that finding, the court reasoned, the Executive Order was within the President’s statutory authority, and courts should not engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s policy justifications in that context. This finding supported a grant of a stay as to the district court’s injunctions that paused the suspension of admissions.

But the panel did not stop there. It also considered the plaintiffs’ challenges that were not directed at the President’s authority under § 1182(f) but instead argued that other statutory duties — specifically, the reception-and-placement obligations in 8 U.S.C. § 1522 — created enforceable duties that the government had failed to meet when it terminated cooperative agreements and suspended funding. The Ninth Circuit concluded that the government was not likely to prevail on at least one of these APA-based challenges. In particular, the court concluded that § 1522 imposes statutory obligations, and that the government’s actions created uncertainty about the availability of reception-and-placement services for refugees already admitted. For that reason the court directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide those services to refugees who have already been admitted to the United States. At the same time, the court stayed the district court’s orders in their entirety except for this narrow directive.

Carveouts, vacatur, and the interim posture. The procedural posture of Pacito is messy but instructive. Earlier motions-panel orders had partially granted stays and partially denied them — the panel had carved out from a stay individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status prior to January 20, 2025. The court subsequently vacated prior administrative stays and other stay orders and issued the present interim stay pending final resolution on appeal, but it carved out the continued enforcement of statutory reception-and-placement duties for refugees already admitted. The practical result: the government could proceed, for the time being, with the suspension of refugee admissions and with some funding suspensions, but the Ninth Circuit required the government to reinstate cooperative agreements and services for persons who were already inside the United States and who depended on those services.

Legal doctrine. Pacito demonstrates how Nken plays out where national security or immigration deference is pitted against statutory duties that implicate non-immigration agencies. The Nken factors can pull in different directions because the courts must weigh (a) deference to executive findings under § 1182(f), which makes “likelihood of success” on a challenge to the suspension of admissions difficult to show, against (b) the likelihood of success on statutory claims under § 1522 that may impose programmatic duties on agencies such as HHS and the State Department. When a court thinks that the government is likely to prevail on the core immigration authority claim but not on a separate statutory duty, the court may issue a limited stay: enjoin enforcement of the district court’s injunction generally but leave intact the district court’s orders that protect a narrow class of persons covered by a non-delegable statutory duty.

Applying the doctrine. When applying Nken to an appeal from a preliminary injunction that targets executive immigration action, separate the challenged action’s sources: is the challenge primarily to the President’s statutory authority under § 1182(f) (where deference is strongest), or is it to the executive branch’s compliance with other statutory obligations that may impose ministerial or mandatory duties? If the former, a stay pending appeal is more likely to be granted; if the latter, plaintiffs may be able to demonstrate a substantial likelihood of success on the merits and the court may preserve the injunction with limited carveouts.

Practice tip. In seeking a stay, the government should (a) emphasize Supreme Court precedents that constrain judicial review of the President’s finding under § 1182(f) and (b) separate any action that may be challenged as violating other statutory duties. Plaintiffs seeking to resist a stay should frame discrete classes of persons for whom statutory duties create a heightened need for injunctive protection (e.g., refugees already admitted who will suffer immediate and irreparable harm if reception-and-placement services disappear).

## Judge Lee’s Partial Concurrence and Partial Dissent: Authorization versus Mandate under § 1522

Judge Lee’s opinion, concurring in part and dissenting in part, crystallizes the statutory-interpretation question at the heart of Pacito: does § 1522 create mandatory statutory duties that a court may enforce, or is § 1522 a set of authorizations that leaves the funding and programmatic decisions to executive discretion?

Judge Lee’s position. Judge Lee agreed with the majority that the President has broad authority under § 1182(f) to suspend refugee admissions and therefore would have stayed the district court’s injunctions as to that suspension. He parted company, however, with the majority’s conclusion that § 1522 imposes enforceable obligations requiring the government to provide reception-and-placement services to refugees already admitted. His textual argument runs along familiar lines:

- Section 1522’s title is “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees.” The use of the word “authorization” signals permissiveness rather than compulsion.
- Many subsections of § 1522 use the phrase “is authorized” or otherwise grant programmatic authority to the Director or Secretary; such phrasing indicates discretion.
- Where § 1522 uses “shall,” that language is best read as setting conditions “in providing assistance under this section” — that is, mandatory conditions that apply only if the Executive chooses to exercise the discretionary authority and provide such assistance.
- Several provisions that might be read as more commanding (for example, subsection (d)(2)(B) on unaccompanied refugee children and subsection (e)(7)(A) on developing interim projects) are qualified by words such as “if necessary” or “as needed,” further suggesting a discretionary, contingent duty rather than an unconditional statutory command.

From these premises Judge Lee concluded that the district court’s order requiring the Executive to reinstate cooperative agreements is an overreach: courts are not in a position to compel the Executive to operate programs that Congress has only authorized but not mandated or funded.

Doctrinal analysis. Judge Lee’s opinion presents a classic statutory-interpretation contest between a textualist reading that privileges labels and bandwidth words like “authorized,” and a more functional reading that examines the interplay of “shall” and appropriations language together with the statutory purpose. The majority read § 1522 differently: it treated at least some of § 1522’s duties as mandatory or at least judicially enforceable in the face of abrupt executive terminations that left refugees and resettlement organizations in immediate peril. The majority’s approach rests on the practical and textual observation that some parts of § 1522, read together, suggest that when Congress contemplates the temporary care of refugees (including unaccompanied minors and those within their first thirty-six months in the U.S.), the statute places affirmative responsibilities on the relevant agencies that cannot be lightly evaded through an across-the-board funding suspension.

Strengths and weaknesses. Judge Lee’s approach has virtues: it respects the text’s explicit use of “authorization,” hews closely to the ordinary meaning of “is authorized” vs. “shall,” and avoids judicial ordering of inter-branch budgetary choices. But it also has weaknesses. If a statute’s “authorization” is only ornamental and Congress clearly intended to establish a baseline program of reception-and-placement services essential to the functioning of the refugee resettlement framework, then a court may find that certain statutory duties are intended to be substantive and not easily suspended. The majority was concerned with precisely that problem: the governmental terminations left refugees already inside the United States unable to access statutorily contemplated supports, and the Ninth Circuit concluded that the district court’s narrow remedy — reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services for admitted refugees — was a proportionate response.

Applying the doctrine. Students should see Judge Lee’s opinion as a case study in statutory interpretation methods. When deciding whether a statutory provision is mandatory, ask:

- What does the title and structure of the statute suggest (authorization vs. requirement)?
- Are there clear “shall” commands, and if so, do they apply unconditionally or only “in providing assistance”?
- Does the statutory scheme presuppose appropriations, and if so, does Congress elsewhere provide funding or require reporting and oversight that implies an expectation of program continuity?
- Are there practical consequences if the provision is read as permissive rather than mandatory (for example, immediate harms to vulnerable populations)?

Practice tip. If you represent plaintiffs seeking to enforce a statutory program against the Executive, emphasize textual elements that indicate mandatory treatment (unqualified “shall,” ministerial duties, reporting requirements) and practical consequences of non-enforcement. If you represent the government, emphasize the appropriations context and the permissive language (e.g., “is authorized,” “to the extent of available appropriations”), and argue that courts should avoid intruding into budgeting and programmatic discretion absent a clear congressional directive.

## Synthesis: How Pacito Teaches to Divide Issues and Predict Outcomes

Pacito is useful for learning how to break complex litigation down into analytically separable claims that attract different standards and degrees of deference. The case shows that:

- Claims that target the President’s statutory authority to exclude aliens under § 1182(f) face a steep hill: courts applying Trump v. Hawaii will usually treat the President’s finding as satisfying § 1182(f)’s textual prerequisite and will not subject the decision to searching substantive review.
- Claims that do not directly challenge the President’s authority to suspend admissions but instead assert that other statutes impose ministerial or mandatory duties on agencies are more likely to survive under normal APA analysis, because deference to presidential immigration authority does not automatically displace other statutes’ programmatic commands.
- In the stay context (Nken), these separate tracks can produce mixed results: a court may stay a district court injunction as to the President’s suspension of admissions while refusing to stay relief that protects persons who are within the U.S. and covered by separate statutory obligations.

For law students, Pacito provides a roadmap for litigating complex cross-cutting disputes: identify each statutory or constitutional source of authority or duty, determine the standard of review applicable to each (e.g., de novo statutory interpretation, arbitrary-and-capricious review under the APA, or heightened deference for national-security/immigration decisions), and then map how each claim should fare under the Nken factors where a stay is requested.

Concluding practice pointers

- When drafting complaints or motions in this area, organize claims so that challenges to presidential authority under § 1182(f) are separated from claims that seek enforcement of statutory obligations on other agencies. That separation maximizes the chance that at least some relief will survive if the court is inclined to defer to the President on admissions.
- When opposing a stay, focus on irreparable harms to individuals within the United States and on statutory language that creates immediate responsibilities (e.g., care of unaccompanied minors, time-limited cash and medical supports). Emphasize the practical immediacy of harm to tip the Nken balance.
- On appeal, remember that the first Nken factor — likelihood of success on the merits — is a compound issue. A single appeal may present multiple merits questions governed by different legal rules and standards; show how each one should be evaluated on the stay record.

Pacito v. Trump therefore stands as an instructive example of how federal courts handle high-stakes, multi-dimensional challenges to executive immigration policy: strong deference to presidential authority on exclusions, concurrent enforceability concerns under separate statutory programs, and the use of the Nken stay framework to craft provisional remedies that reflect the court’s best assessment of competing statutory and equitable claims.

# Presidential Entry Suspensions and Judicial Deference

This chapter examines how the Ninth Circuit applied the Supreme Court’s decision in Trump v. Hawaii (2018) to an executive suspension of refugee admissions in Pacito v. Trump (Sept. 12, 2025) and what Pacito means for the limits of judicial review over presidential findings under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). Pacito arose from an executive order that suspended the U.S. Refugee Admissions Program (USRAP) and simultaneous defunding of programs that provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted. The Ninth Circuit, in a stay opinion issued while the case was expedited, applied the deferential framework of Hawaii to sustain the President’s authority to suspend refugee admissions under § 1182(f) but identified a separate, non-§1182(f) route by which plaintiffs could likely prevail—an Administrative Procedure Act (APA) challenge to the termination of statutory resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The panel thus stayed the district court injunctions enjoining the moratorium on admissions but ordered the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already in the United States. The opinion — and Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent — give students a useful vantage for understanding what courts will and will not scrutinize when the President invokes § 1182(f).

The discussion that follows unpacks Pacito’s application of Hawaii’s deference doctrine, analyzes what counts as a “finding” of detriment, describes the permissible scope and duration of entry suspensions under § 1182(f), explains how § 1182(f) interacts with refugee-specific statutory regimes (notably the admissions ceiling and the Refugee Act’s resettlement obligations), and draws out practical implications for litigants who seek to challenge or defend executive entry suspensions in the post-Hawaii landscape.

## Application of Hawaii’s deferential framework to § 1182(f) suspensions

Trump v. Hawaii is the starting point for modern judicial treatment of presidential entry restrictions. In Hawaii, the Supreme Court held that when the President acts pursuant to § 1182(f), courts should give substantial deference to the executive’s findings and national-security judgments. The Court characterized § 1182(f) as a textual grant of broad power to the President to “suspend the entry” of classes of aliens “for such period as he shall deem necessary,” and it rejected searching judicial inquiry into whether the President’s national-security rationales were persuasive. The Hawaii opinion instructs courts to treat the President’s “finding” as presumptively legitimate, leaving review narrow in scope.

Pacito follows that path. Faced with an executive order suspending refugee admissions, the Ninth Circuit applied the stay factors articulated in Nken v. Holder but treated the likelihood-of-success-on-the-merits inquiry through the lens of Hawaii’s deference. The panel concluded the government had “made a strong showing that it is likely to prevail” on the question whether the President lawfully suspended refugee admissions under § 1182(f). That conclusion rested on two propositions taken from Hawaii: (1) § 1182(f) requires only that the President “find” that admissions “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” and (2) courts may not engage in a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications when that statutory predicate is met.

What does this mean doctrinally? First, courts will treat presidential proclamations and executive orders that express a finding of detriment as satisfying the statutory prerequisite unless the proclamation is a legal nullity or clearly outside § 1182(f)’s textual scope. Second, the standard of inquiry is not normal administrative review (e.g., substantial evidence or arbitrary-and-capricious review applied to agency factfinding); rather, courts decide whether the President made a formal “finding” and whether the suspension falls within § 1182(f)’s textual grant. If so, the President’s policy judgment is insulated from in-depth factual attack.

Pacito illustrates how this framework operates on a preliminary-relief posture. Because Nken places heavy weight on the merits and irreparable-injury factors, the Ninth Circuit found that Hawaii’s deference made it difficult for plaintiffs to show they were likely to prevail on their facial statutory challenge to the moratorium’s validity. Thus, where a challenge is fundamentally an attack on the President’s § 1182(f) finding and the scope of authority under that section, courts will be especially reluctant to enjoin the President at the preliminary stage.

Yet Pacito also demonstrates the limits of Hawaii’s protection. The panel did not allow the executive’s invocation of § 1182(f) to immunize related government actions that are statutory in origin and distinct in nature—here, the termination of cooperative agreements to provide services to refugees already admitted. The court distinguished a suspension of the “entry” of aliens from agency decisions affecting duties owed to people already within the United States under other statutory schemes. Where statutory duties exist apart from the entry power, courts retain the capacity to review agency compliance with those statutes under the APA. This is why Pacito sustained the stay as to the admissions moratorium but ordered reinstatement of cooperative agreements to preserve resettlement services: judicial deference under § 1182(f) does not automatically shield unrelated agency actions from statutory review.

## What counts as a ‘finding’ of detriment and how specific it must be

A central, recurring question under § 1182(f) is what the statute requires for the President to “find” that entry of certain aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” The threshold is low. Hawaii makes clear that the statutory predicate is satisfied when the President articulates a formal finding in a proclamation or executive order; courts need not adjudicate the empirical merits of that finding. Pacito follows Hawaii’s reasoning and states flatly that the President “has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” In short, explicit language in an executive order stating that continued refugee admissions would be “detrimental” suffices as a statutory finding.

Does the finding need to be concrete, detailed, or supported by a particularized factual record made public? Pacito and Hawaii suggest the answer is no: a formal yes/no finding formally expressed by the President will meet the statutory requirement. The Court in Hawaii declined to require the President to assemble a public evidentiary record or meet a judicially crafted standard of specificity for national-security findings. The Ninth Circuit echoed that approach in Pacito, refusing to subject the President’s judgment to “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” The operative lesson is that the “finding” is principally a political-branch judgment, not a judicially enforceable factual adjudication.

That said, litigants and courts should not assume the finding is entirely immune to any form of judicial consideration. There are limited scenarios in which the “finding” might be treated as insufficient. For example: if the document labeled a finding actually contains only conclusory or inconsistent language that has no plausible claim to be a presidential finding; if the alleged “finding” plainly concerns persons or conduct that the text of § 1182(f) does not reach; or if the finding were a pretext for action that violates other explicit statutory limits or constitutional protections (e.g., if the recommendation rested on race-based animus that implicates constitutional equal-protection principles). None of these borderline categories was present in Pacito; the court accepted the executive order’s formal finding as satisfying § 1182(f).

Practical consequence: challengers who want to attack a § 1182(f) suspension should not, as a first step, attempt to litigate the factual sufficiency of the President’s finding. That route is unlikely to succeed post-Hawaii. Instead, they should pursue arguments that the presidential action falls outside § 1182(f)’s textual scope or that other statutes grant concrete, mandatory duties that cannot be overridden by an § 1182(f) proclamation. Alternatively, constitutional challenges that do not rest on reweighing foreign-relations judgments may also remain viable where they do not require courts to second-guess national-security findings.

## Scope of permissible entry restrictions and duration under § 1182(f)

The text of § 1182(f) is capacious. It authorizes the President, “whenever [he] finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens into the United States would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” to “suspend the entry of all aliens or any class of aliens” and to impose “any restrictions he may deem to be appropriate,” “for such period as he shall deem necessary.” The statutory grammar conveys three principal features: (1) coverage of any class of aliens (broad reach); (2) discretion to limit entry in several ways (broad mode-of-action); and (3) authority to set duration as the President thinks fit (broad temporal reach).

Pacito, reading Hawaii, gave full effect to that breadth in the entry context: the court indicated that a moratorium on refugee admissions is within the core textual grant of § 1182(f) and that courts should not substitute their judgment about whether the duration is necessary. The Ninth Circuit’s stay reasoning therefore treats duration and scope — including a complete moratorium on a program admitting refugees — as political judgments that fall within Executive Branch competence absent some other statutory or constitutional constraint.

But breadth in the statute does not mean unlimited power in practice. A few important textual and structural limits remain salient:

- The statute speaks about “aliens” and “entry.” It is not a license to regulate post-entry conduct of persons lawfully within the United States apart from matters plainly tied to the entry decision. Pacito stressed this distinction: the government could suspend future refugee admissions, but that did not free it to ignore statutory duties to refugees already admitted.

- The President’s exercise of § 1182(f) must be a genuine exercise of the power conferred by the statute, not a pretext for overriding other specific statutory mandates. If a specific provision elsewhere in the INA or another statute sets forth mandatory duties or limits that conflict with the President’s action, courts will need to reconcile those texts. In Pacito, the court found that statutory duties under § 1522 could give plaintiffs a likely APA claim even as the moratorium itself was protected by § 1182(f).

- Constitutional constraints remain in the background. Hawaii reserved to the judiciary the role of enforcing the Constitution; the Court emphasized deference, not abdication. There may yet be constitutional limits to an executive suspension depending on the particular facts — for example, if executive action were motivated by hostile discrimination against a protected class and the legal challenge could be resolved without second-guessing national-security judgments. Those issues did not carry the day in Pacito, but students should not read Hawaii or Pacito as extinguishing all constitutional review of entry restrictions.

In sum, § 1182(f) grants broad authority to suspend entry, including the duration of that suspension, but scope is not limitless: statutory text, other statutory duties, and the Constitution can constrain the exercise of that power.

## Interaction with § 1157 admissions ceilings and consultation duties

The Refugee Act and implementing statutes create both planning and reporting mechanisms to manage refugee admissions. Section 1157, among other provisions, governs admissions of refugees and provides mechanisms for setting admissions ceilings for annual refugee admissions. The administrative apparatus created by the Refugee Act anticipates a continuing program of admissions accompanied by consultation, appropriation, and reporting duties. How does the President’s invocation of § 1182(f) interact with these other statutory elements?

Pacito did not fully litigate every statutory cross-reference between § 1182(f) and the refugee-admissions framework, but its reasoning points to the general approach courts will take. If the President lawfully suspends entry under § 1182(f), that suspension can operate to override the practical implementation of any admissions ceiling for the period of suspension: a suspension saying “no entries” will prevent the filling of the ceiling. The statutory ceiling is a maximum number the government may admit in a fiscal year; it is not an individual right that would necessarily bind the President’s § 1182(f) power. Pacito, following Hawaii’s deference, treated the President’s moratorium as within § 1182(f) even though § 1157 contemplates admissions ceilings.

That said, the interaction is not entirely free from statutory constraints. Congress controls appropriations and the substantive obligations connected with the refugee program. The Refugee Act imposes affirmative duties on agencies (e.g., resettlement services in § 1522 and reporting duties in § 1523) that cannot simply be nullified by an executive proclamation if the statute requires action for persons already admitted or if Congress has appropriated funds conditioned on certain uses. Pacito’s holding about reinstating cooperative agreements grew out of precisely this tension: while suspending future admissions falls squarely within § 1182(f), cutting funding and aborting the statutory resettlement infrastructure required by the statute for refugees already admitted could violate other statutory provisions and thus be reviewable under the APA.

Moreover, some statutory provisions require consultation or reporting to Congress. If the statute conditions the President’s ability to take certain actions on consultation or notice requirements, an executive action in disregard of those conditions might be vulnerable. In practice, however, courts will be reluctant to find such conditions decisive when the action is a textually authorized suspension under § 1182(f), unless Congress’s text unmistakably constrains the President’s suspension authority. The lesson is that the most plausible statutory challenges will target collateral actions—funding decisions, programmatic terminations, or procedural failings—in the refugee regime rather than a direct attack on the decision to suspend admissions under § 1182(f).

## Implications for litigants: evidentiary showings and litigation posture post-Hawaii

Pacito shows that after Hawaii, litigants face a changed landscape when attacking presidential entry suspensions. The litigation strategy that once focused on compiling factual proof of the President’s error or lack of evidence is no longer promising. Courts will treat the President’s formal finding under § 1182(f) as a political judgment and will not entertain a searching factual inquiry into its wisdom. The consequence is twofold: plaintiffs must pick their targets, and defendants should shape the administrative record to the extent possible.

For plaintiffs seeking injunctive relief, the Nken stay framework remains controlling at the preliminary-relief stage. Because courts weigh likelihood of success and irreparable harm most heavily, a plaintiff who bases the merits entirely on contesting the President’s finding will likely fail to show a likelihood of success in light of Hawaii-style deference. Pacito illustrates this point: the Ninth Circuit found the government likely to succeed on the merits of the § 1182(f) suspension and therefore granted a stay of the district court’s injunctions as to the moratorium.

What should plaintiffs do instead? Consider these practical approaches illuminated by Pacito:

- Focus on statutory obligations that operate independently of the entry power. Pacito’s recognition that the Department’s statutory duties under § 1522 may not be displaced by an § 1182(f) proclamation shows that APA challenges to ancillary agency actions (e.g., termination of cooperative agreements that deliver statutorily mandated services) can succeed where the government cannot show that the statutory obligation has been lawfully altered.

- Emphasize concrete, imminent harms to protected statutory beneficiaries that are not contingent on future admissions. Injunctive relief is much more plausible when plaintiffs can show direct harm flowing from agency noncompliance with statutory obligations (for example, loss of benefits, services, or statutory entitlements already enjoyed by admitted refugees).

- Preserve record evidence of pretext where possible without relying solely on the merits of the President’s foreign-relations judgment. If plaintiffs can show that an agency’s stated reason for terminating services bears no relationship to the agency’s established statutory mission or that agency decisionmaking was procedurally defective (e.g., arbitrary-and-capricious action without reasoned explanation), those claims are adjudicable under the APA and do not require second-guessing the President’s national-security findings.

For defendants defending a § 1182(f) suspension, Pacito suggests several defensive steps:

- Ensure the formal “finding” is articulated and documented. While courts do not require an evidentiary record in every case, a clearly expressed formal finding in the President’s proclamation or supporting documentation strengthens the government’s position under Hawaii and Pacito.

- Separate entry-related actions from unrelated statutory obligations. If policy requires suspending admissions, preserve statutory programs that serve persons already in the United States unless there is a clear statutory basis to pause those programs. Pacito’s decision to require reinstatement of cooperative agreements reflects judicial willingness to enforce statutes directed at in-country beneficiaries even while deferring on entry restrictions.

- Anticipate collateral litigation under the APA and spend administrative effort to show reasoned decisionmaking for related agency actions (e.g., termination of contracts or cooperative agreements). A thorough administrative record may avoid an APA reversal even if the decision follows from a presidential suspension.

Finally, for courts and litigants alike, Pacito underscores the normative balance courts are trying to strike: respect for the President’s foreign-affairs and national-security decisionmaking combined with enforcement of statutory and constitutional constraints that protect rights and entitlements for persons already within the United States. The practical upshot is that successful constitutional or statutory challenges in this domain are more likely to be routed through non-§1182(f) avenues—procedural-regularity claims, statutory-mandate claims, funding and appropriations law, contractual obligations, and distinct statutory duties to persons already admitted—rather than direct factual attack on the President’s finding under § 1182(f).

## Conclusion — doctrinal synthesis and future contours

Pacito applies Hawaii’s deferential framework to a refugee moratorium, reasserting the principle that courts should not engage in searching fact-finding about presidential judgments made under § 1182(f). The Ninth Circuit’s stay opinion thus confirms that the formal “finding” requirement in § 1182(f) is a low hurdle and that courts will generally defer to presidential determinations about the detriment of admitting classes of aliens.

Yet Pacito also clarifies an important limitation: § 1182(f) is not a universal voucher that permits the Executive to extinguish other statutory obligations. Where Congress has conferred mandatory duties with respect to persons already in the United States (as in the reception-and-placement obligations of § 1522), courts retain the power to require agency compliance under the APA. Judge Lee’s partial dissent in Pacito illuminates the textual debate over whether § 1522 imposes mandatory duties or an authorization; how lower courts resolve that interpretive question will have significant practical consequences for future litigation about the scope of executive power during suspensions of entry.

For students and practitioners, Pacito teaches three operating maxims. First, when the President invokes § 1182(f), courts will usually accord great deference to the formal presidential finding; litigants should not expect to prevail by litigating the factual merits of that finding. Second, statutory and procedural constraints that operate independently of entry (including statutory duties to persons already admitted) remain enforceable, and plaintiffs should press those claims energetically. Third, because the most defendable litigation strategies in this area increasingly turn on statutory interpretation and administrative-law doctrines (not reweighing foreign-affairs judgments), both sides should build robust records focused on statutory text, appropriations and obligations, procedural regularity, and concrete harms rather than primarily on contesting the President’s national-security rationales.

# Statutory Duties to Admitted Refugees: Is § 1522 Mandatory or Merely Authorizing?

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump crystallizes a recurring statutory‑interpretation problem: when Congress uses a mixture of permissive language (“is authorized”), mandatory verbs (“shall”), and limiting conditions (“to the extent of available appropriations”), has it created a judicially enforceable duty or merely a discretionary power for the executive to exercise when convenient? This chapter explains how to analyze that question under ordinary tools of statutory interpretation, illustrates the analysis with the specific provisions of 8 U.S.C. § 1522 at issue in Pacito, weighs the competing readings adopted by the panel majority and by Judge Lee’s partial concurrence/dissent, and provides a practical framework a court (or an advocate) should follow when confronted with similar “authorization vs. command” problems.

At the heart of Pacito is not the President’s power to suspend refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) — the panel accepts that the President enjoys broad authority there — but whether § 1522 imposes a non‑discretionary obligation on the federal government to furnish reception and placement services to refugees admitted to the United States. The majority concluded that, as applied to refugees already admitted and where appropriations exist, the government is not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ APA claim that the executive unlawfully stopped statutorily mandated services; it therefore ordered reinstatement of necessary cooperative agreements for reception and placement. Judge Lee, by contrast, read § 1522 as largely authorizing the executive branch to create refugee programs at its discretion, and thought the district court improperly compelled implementation of what is essentially discretionary policy. The interpretive tensions that produce this divide are familiar: what weight to give the word “shall” when embedded in a section whose title and many subsections use “is authorized,” and how to read contingent phrases such as “in providing assistance” and “to the extent of available appropriations.”

## Textual analysis: ‘Authorization for programs’ vs. operative ‘shall’ clauses in § 1522(a)

The starting point for any statutory‑interpretation exercise is the text. Section 1522 is headed “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees,” and many of its subsections use the phrase “is authorized” or similar permissive formulations. A naïve reading of the heading together with the recurring “is authorized” language supports Judge Lee’s view that Congress granted the executive authority to run refugee resettlement programs but stopped short of imposing a positive command to run them at all times.

Yet the solid intuition generated by the heading and “is authorized” locutions collides with other text in § 1522 that uses mandatory verbs. Section 1522(a)—the provision labeled “Conditions and consideration”—states that “In providing assistance under this section . . . the Director shall, to the extent of available appropriations, make available sufficient resources for employment training and placement . . .” The word “shall” is commonly treated as indicating a mandatory duty, and many courts begin from a presumption that “shall” imposes an obligation unless other textual indicators suggest otherwise. When a statute requires an official to do something “to the extent of available appropriations,” courts typically interpret that limitation as making the duty contingent on funding (i.e., the requirement is operational if appropriations exist), rather than converting a mandatory command into a mere suggestion.

Resolving whether § 1522 is commanding therefore requires harmonizing the surface conflict: does the fact that the program provisions are described as “authorizations” and many operative provisions use “is authorized” rebut the ordinary force of a “shall” command in subsection (a)? A textualist approach examines the immediate statutory context. Subsection (a) explicitly ties its “shall” obligations to “providing assistance under this section.” If assistance is being “provided” (for example, because Congress has appropriated funds for refugee assistance or because the executive has chosen to run programs), subsection (a) prescribes how that assistance must be structured. Put bluntly, the textual grammar supports either of two readings: (1) a contingent mandatory duty—when assistance is provided and funds are available, the executive must provide it in the manner prescribed; or (2) a set of mandatory constraints that are triggered only if the executive chooses to use the permissive authorities in the remainder of the section. The majority in Pacito favored the first reading when appropriations and admissions exist; Judge Lee favored the second, emphasizing the permissive title and the recurring “is authorized” language. Close textual reading alone does not conclusively settle the matter; one must integrate other interpretive aids.

## Qualifiers and triggers: ‘In providing assistance,’ ‘to the extent of available appropriations,’ and ‘as needed’

Three recurring qualifiers in § 1522 are dispositive in many disputes over whether the statute creates enforceable duties: (1) the triggering phrase “In providing assistance under this section”; (2) the funding contingency “to the extent of available appropriations”; and (3) need‑based language such as “as needed.” Each operates in different ways and must be read together.

“In providing assistance under this section” is a textual trigger: it links the detailed requirements in subsection (a) to the core grant of authority in the rest of § 1522. If a court reads that phrase as a conditional — i.e., these requirements only apply if and when the executive elects to “provide assistance” under the programs authorized by the section — then the substantive mandates in subsection (a) are not absolute. The alternative reading, embraced by the Pacito majority for the circumstances before it, treats “in providing assistance” less as an elective trigger and more as a description of the circumstances in which Congress expects federal resettlement efforts to operate: where the government admits and resettles refugees, it must provide certain core reception and placement services. Under this view the presence of actual admitted refugees, combined with appropriations directed to refugee assistance by Congress, satisfies the “providing assistance” condition and thus activates the “shall” commands.

“To the extent of available appropriations” is commonly seen as a limiting clause that prevents courts from imposing obligations that would require spending beyond what Congress has appropriated. It does not, however, nullify statutory obligations when appropriations exist. The critical interpretive question is temporal and scope‑based: is Congress telling the executive branch only that any obligations in § 1522 are limited by the amount Congress chooses to appropriate (a ceiling on performance), or is Congress merely acknowledging that performance is contingent on appropriations but otherwise mandatory if funds are available? Most courts read “to the extent of available appropriations” as creating a funding contingency: the agency need not spend beyond existing appropriations, but when the Congress in fact appropriates funds for refugee programs the statutory duties are operative. This reasoning anchors the majority’s order in Pacito: the panel cited enacted appropriations for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025 and treated those appropriations as sufficient to trigger statutory duties.

“As needed” and similar phrases (e.g., “if necessary”) appear in provisions dealing with unaccompanied minors and with certain alternative projects. These phrases invite a factual inquiry: whether a particular form of assistance is “needed” or “necessary” is a question of circumstance. They are not, by themselves, grant clauses that convert permissive language into an unconditional duty; rather, they define the scope of an otherwise mandatory requirement by tying the duty to an objective or factually determinable condition. Thus subsection (d)(2)(B)’s requirement that the Director “shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary,” is best read as a conditional obligation: if circumstances render such assumption necessary to provide for the child’s immediate care, the Director has a duty to do so.

A careful analysis therefore takes these qualifiers as operational triggers and limits: they narrow the scope of what is mandatory and define when and how a duty must be performed. The legislative choice of qualifiers supports neither a categorical presumption of permissiveness nor an assumption of unconditional mandated spending; rather, their function is to make statutory duties contingent on circumstances that can be evaluated by courts and agencies.

## Specific mandates: unaccompanied minors (§ 1522(d)(2)(B)) and cash/medical support projects (§ 1522(e)(7)(A))

Two provisions generated particular dispute in Pacito: subsection (d)(2)(B), which concerns unaccompanied refugee children, and subsection (e)(7)(A), which addresses alternative projects for cash and medical assistance to recently resettled refugees.

Subsection (d)(2)(B) directs: “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.” Read narrowly, the phrase “if necessary” plainly conveys that the duty to assume responsibility is not automatic but is triggered when necessary to care for the child. The government can therefore argue that the condition precedent—necessity—does not exist in a particular fact pattern, while plaintiffs can point to concrete facts (e.g., an unaccompanied child without sponsors or other caretakers) to demonstrate that necessity exists and thus that the Secretary’s duty is triggered. Judge Lee emphasized that “if necessary” undercuts any broader reading of subsection (d)(2)(B) as creating a sweeping, unconditional duty; the panel majority recognized the conditionality but still thought the undisputed presence of admitted refugees with needs made at least some duties enforceable.

Subsection (e)(7)(A) commands that the Secretary “shall develop and implement alternative projects for refugees who have been in the United States less than thirty‑six months, under which refugees are provided interim support, medical services, support services, and case management, as needed.” Two interpretive points matter here. First, the use of “shall” suggests a requirement to develop and implement projects—i.e., programmatic commitments. Second, the modifier “as needed” applies to the content of those projects (interim support and medical services) rather than to the existence of the projects themselves; it therefore can be read as instructing the Secretary to design programs that supply services that refugees need, rather than telling the Secretary that the programs themselves are optional. Judge Lee stressed that “is authorized” elsewhere in the section makes even the “shall develop and implement” phrasing permissive in operation, while the majority read the clause as a genuine programmatic mandate whose operation is contingent on appropriations and demonstrable need.

One lesson from these two specific provisions is that qualifying phrases require a fact‑sensitive inquiry. “If necessary” and “as needed” are not mere tokens of discretion; they are standards that can be assessed by evidence. Where an agency terminates programs wholesale, leaving refugees without any services, a court can examine whether statutory triggers (admitted refugees present, appropriations available, demonstrable need such as unaccompanied minors lacking guardians) are satisfied and, if they are, whether the agency’s action violates an enforceable duty.

## Titles and headings as interpretive aids and their limits

Statutory headings and titles are routinely invoked as interpretive aids. They offer useful signals about how Congress conceived a statutory scheme, and a title such as “Authorization for programs” naturally suggests a permissive framework. Judge Lee anchored much of her analysis in the title of § 1522 and in the frequent use of “is authorized” across subsections. That approach has intuitive appeal: headings and statutory labels help courts discern whether language should be read as enabling the agency or constraining it.

Nevertheless, headings are subordinate to the statutory text and cannot override clear operative language. Where a provision contains explicit mandatory verbs, courts will not let an isolated heading defeat an otherwise straightforward command. In Pacito the majority pointed to the “shall” verbs in § 1522(a) and the appropriations history as a textual anchor; it treated the heading as informative but not dispositive. The general rule for interpreters, then, is to treat headings as supportive, not controlling. They can tip the balance where the textual indicators are ambiguous, but they do not carry the force of operative legislative text. This principle explains why Judge Lee’s insistence on the title was persuasive in part but insufficient to carry the day for the panel majority.

## Judge Lee’s view: pervasive authorization signals; the panel’s contrary view: enforceable duties once refugees are admitted and funds are available

The disagreement within the Pacito panel is a useful case study in how judges weigh textual nuance against statutory purpose and real‑world conditions. Judge Lee’s partial dissent repeatedly emphasized the structural and textual signals of permissiveness: the section heading, numerous “is authorized” clauses, and the structure that links the mandatory language of subsection (a) to “providing assistance under this section.” Her analysis treats Congress’s choice of authorization language as decisive evidence that it intended to create an enabling statute, not a suite of command obligations that would bind the executive irrespective of policy judgments about refugee admissions and resettlement capacity. On this view, courts are ill‑suited to micromanage resettlement programs; the political branches must retain discretion to determine when and how to implement resettlement services.

The majority, by contrast, took a more pragmatic and functionally textual approach. It read subsection (a)’s “shall . . . to the extent of available appropriations” as creating enforceable obligations where the triggering predicates are present: refugees have been admitted, and Congress has appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance. The majority relied on statutory specificity (the listing of particular services and the authorization of cooperative agreements with non‑profit agencies), historical practice (longstanding cooperative agreements and customary reception and placement services), and congressional accountability provisions (such as § 1523’s reporting requirement) to infer that Congress meant to ensure minimal core services for admitted refugees. Under this approach, “authorization” language in the section title and in particular subsections does not negate specific “shall” duties; instead, the sections operate together such that Congress authorizes programs and, where funds and admissions exist, prescribes certain standards and services that must be provided.

The difference between these approaches is not merely academic. If § 1522 is understood as largely authorizing, then the executive has wide latitude to suspend funding and cooperative agreements; judicial review is limited. If § 1522 contains enforceable duties, then wholesale termination of reception and placement contracts when refugees are already present is subject to meaningful judicial oversight under the APA: an agency cannot simply terminate statutorily required services without reasoned explanation and, where appropriations make obligations operational, without complying with statutory commands.

## How courts should decide and how to apply the analysis to new fact patterns

The Pacito dispute supplies a practical roadmap for litigants and courts confronting similar statutes that contain both permissive and mandatory language.

First, identify the operative verbs and their grammatical scope. Is the mandatory language (e.g., “shall”) addressed to a specified official and does it apply to clearly defined circumstances? If so, the default presumption favors a mandatory meaning unless countervailing textual or structural signals exist.

Second, locate trigger phrases. Determine whether clauses like “in providing assistance under this section” function as conditions precedent (i.e., the duty only arises if the executive elects to provide assistance under the statutory scheme) or as descriptive qualifiers tying the duty to factual circumstances (admission of refugees and availability of funds). Ask whether the predicate condition is present in the case at hand.

Third, analyze funding contingencies. “To the extent of available appropriations” ordinarily means that a duty is limited by funding; it does not mean the duty ceases to exist whenever the executive decides not to spend money. If Congress has appropriated funds directed to the activity, the statute’s substantive requirements are likely operative; the executive may not nullify statutory duties by reallocating appropriations without a reasonable statutory basis.

Fourth, evaluate qualifying standards like “if necessary” and “as needed.” These phrases generally create evidence‑based triggers: they require factfinding. A court reviewing an agency’s termination of programs must examine whether the agency reasonably determined that the condition (necessity or need) was or was not satisfied. Wholesale, unexplained termination is especially vulnerable to judicial invalidation when a statutory standard is susceptible to objective proof.

Fifth, assess structural and purposive indicators. Reporting requirements, delegation to specific officers, detailed lists of services, and historical practice can all be used to infer congressional intent to create minimum programmatic obligations rather than mere permission. Conversely, broad program titles and repeated “is authorized” language that pervades the statutory section weigh in favor of permissiveness, especially when the statute is otherwise spare of specific, concrete directives.

Finally, apply basic canons cautiously. Headings and legislative history can inform ambiguous text but cannot override clear statutory commands. When text is genuinely ambiguous, consider the statute’s purpose and the practical consequences of each interpretation (for instance, whether a permissive reading would allow the executive to frustrate congressional policy by refusing to implement any programs despite appropriations and admissions).

Two brief hypotheticals illustrate application of this roadmap. First, suppose Congress appropriates funds for refugee assistance, the executive admits refugees, and the agency terminates cooperative agreements without explanation. Under the majority’s reasoning in Pacito, a court should examine whether the appropriations trigger § 1522’s “shall” obligations and then whether the agency’s termination is consistent with the statute and reasoned decisionmaking; absent a satisfactory justification, the court may enjoin the termination. Second, suppose no appropriations exist for refugee resettlement in the relevant fiscal year. Under the funding‑contingent reading, § 1522’s substantive duties are not operational, and a plaintiffs’ challenge asserting a mandatory statutory right to services is unlikely to succeed.

## Conclusion: practical consequences and a checklist for litigants

Pacito underscores that the question whether a statutory provision is mandatory or authorizing often turns on close textual parsing combined with practical context — notably whether Congress has appropriated funds and whether the administrative facts satisfy conditionals like “if necessary.” For advocates, the path is clear: marshal textual evidence of mandatory commands (plain “shall” clauses directed at officials), show the presence of statutory triggers (admissions and appropriations), and develop the factual record to prove that qualifying standards (necessity, need) are met. For agencies, the lesson is that wholesale program terminations that leave beneficiaries without statutorily described services invite judicial scrutiny, particularly when Congress has allocated funds and the statute contains specific programmatic prescriptions.

In sum, the interpretive framework for § 1522 is not binary. Congress layered authorization and mandatory elements into the statutory scheme, and a court’s job is to tease apart when the contingency predicates are satisfied so that the “shall” provisions become operational. Pacito’s majority and Judge Lee illustrate the two plausible poles of interpretation: one that views § 1522 as enabling with conditions and another that reads it as imposing enforceable obligations where those conditions are present. Future disputes will turn on the same careful analysis of operative words, triggers, funding, qualifying standards, and the statutory structure as a whole.

# APA Challenges to Funding Suspensions and Terminations of Cooperative Agreements

This chapter uses Pacito v. Trump (9th Cir. interim order, Sept. 12, 2025) to teach how courts analyze agency cessations of programs and terminations of cooperative agreements when statutes may impose duties to provide services. Pacito presented a recurring set of questions that arise whenever an administration abruptly suspends a program or cancels cooperative agreements that third‑party organizations rely on to deliver services to vulnerable populations: (1) is the termination “agency action” subject to the Administrative Procedure Act (APA)? (2) if so, what is the appropriate standard of review and what counts as reasoned decision‑making in the funding context? (3) when do foreign‑policy and national‑security considerations remove action from ordinary administrative review? (4) when does a statute create judicially manageable standards (a “law to apply”) as opposed to committing decisions entirely to agency discretion? and (5) what remedial tools can courts employ — including reinstatement of cooperative agreements — to cure likely statutory violations? Pacito is particularly useful because the panel split the question set: it deferred to executive authority over refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) but concluded that plaintiffs were likely to prevail on their APA claim that the government violated statutory duties under 8 U.S.C. § 1522 by defunding reception and placement services and terminating cooperative agreements. The opinion thus illustrates the limits of deference in foreign‑affairs/immigration contexts and the continuing power of the APA to police statutory compliance over domestic program delivery.

Below we treat each of the subtopics that logically arise from Pacito. The goal is practical: give students the doctrinal tools to analyze whether a funding suspension or termination of cooperative agreements is reviewable and, if so, how courts evaluate the substance of the agency decision and fashion relief.

## Is terminating cooperative agreements “agency action” subject to APA review?

The APA’s definition of “agency action” is capacious. 5 U.S.C. § 551(13) defines “agency action” to include “the whole or a part of an agency rule, order, license, sanction, relief, or the equivalent or denial thereof, or failure to act.” That definition, together with the APA’s cause of action (5 U.S.C. § 706), ordinarily makes a broad range of agency decisions — including grants, contracts, and cooperative agreements — susceptible to judicial review. But the basic inquiry has two parts: standing aside, courts must first decide whether the challenged government conduct is “agency action” and, if it is, whether it is final and thus reviewable under the APA.

Two connected tests control reviewability. First, the decision must constitute “final agency action” under Bennett v. Spear, 520 U.S. 154 (1997). Bennett asks whether the action (a) marks the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking process and (b) is one by which rights or obligations have been determined or from which legal consequences flow. An affirmative, published termination of cooperative agreements that cuts off funding and tells grantees the government will no longer pay plainly meets that test: termination is a consummated decision (a choice has been made) and it produces legal and practical consequences for beneficiaries and contractors. Pacito treated the February 2025 terminations as final and reviewable agency conduct for the purposes of plaintiffs’ APA claims.

Second, even when an action is final, the challenge is barred from review if it is among the narrow classes that the APA excludes. There are two relevant statutory exceptions. Section 701(a)(1) excludes “other statutes” that preclude judicial review and so would deprive courts of jurisdiction. More commonly litigated is § 701(a)(2), which exempts actions “committed to agency discretion by law.” The Supreme Court has emphasized that § 701(a)(2) is a narrow exception: courts should find action “committed to agency discretion” only where “statute is drawn so that a court would have no meaningful standard against which to judge the agency’s exercise of discretion.” Lincoln v. Vigil, 508 U.S. 182, 191 (1993) (quotation cleaned up); see also Heckler v. Chaney, 470 U.S. 821 (1985) (agency refusal to take enforcement action traditionally unreviewable because of lack of judicially manageable standards). The upshot is that not every discretionary funding decision is unreviewable: when Congress sets out substantive duties, clear statutory terms, or criteria Congress expects to be applied, courts can and will review.

Applying those rules to funding terminations and cooperative‑agreement cancellations: when a department issues a definitive termination decision that ends funding and imposes consequences for beneficiaries, the action is final for APA purposes. Whether it is reviewable depends on the statute under which the program operates. If that statute provides standards, duties, or other law to apply, judicial review is available; if the statute leaves the matter to unconstrained discretion, the agency is much more likely to prevail on the § 701(a)(2) exception. Pacito illustrates both possibilities: the panel accepted executive deference to the President’s suspension of admissions under § 1182(f) (a statutory grant of broad national‑security/entry authority), while concluding that § 1522 contains “law to apply” with respect to reception and placement obligations and therefore that the decision to terminate funding for those statutorily described services is reviewable.

A final point: the APA’s definition includes “failure to act.” When agencies terminate funding but also stop performing duties the statute requires (e.g., providing reception and placement services), a plaintiff may challenge the agency’s refusal or failure to perform. Such inaction is reviewable if the statute imposes a non‑discretionary duty; Heckler v. Chaney is often invoked when the claim is an agency refusal to enforce, but it is not an automatic bar. Thus, funding termination can present two cognizable legal claims: (a) a challenge to a discrete, final affirmative act (termination) and (b) a claim that the agency had a statutory duty to take or continue action and unlawfully failed to do so.

## Arbitrary and capricious review: reasoned decision‑making, reliance interests, and program continuity

Once a funding termination or cancellation is subject to review, the governing standard for substantive review of the merits is usually the “arbitrary and capricious” standard of 5 U.S.C. § 706(2)(A). The leading modern articulation is Motor Vehicle Mfrs. Ass’n v. State Farm, 463 U.S. 29 (1983): an agency must examine the relevant data, consider important aspects of the problem, and articulate a rational connection between the facts found and the choice made. An agency changing course must supply a reasoned explanation for its change; it must address “serious reliance interests” and show that either those interests were considered or that countervailing considerations justify disruption. See FCC v. Fox Television Stations, Inc., 556 U.S. 502 (2009); Encino Motorcars, LLC v. Navarro, 579 U.S. 211 (2016).

In the funding/termination context there are a few recurrent themes courts focus upon:

- Reasoned factual record. Termination decisions that rest on record evidence — e.g., a program is not delivering intended results, fraud has been found, or severe mismanagement is established — are far more defensible than blunt edicts that do not articulate a factual predicate. Courts will look for contemporaneous analysis, not post hoc rationalizations. Pacito emphasized that the government told the district court it was “actively preparing a request for proposals” for new resettlement providers but admitted later that it had no idea whether arrangements were in place — a factual lacuna that undermined a claim of reasoned planning.

- Consideration of statutory duties. If the statute prescribes services or responsibilities, the agency must address how termination squares with those duties. State Farm requires the agency to grapple with obvious statutory constraints. In Pacito the Ninth Circuit concluded plaintiffs were likely to succeed on their APA claim because § 1522 contains specific provisions outlining reception and placement responsibilities; the government had not explained how terminating cooperative agreements complied with those statutory obligations.

- Reliance and disruption. Courts frequently require agencies to evaluate reliance interests — the investments, staffing, and client expectations that organizations built on prior policy. Abrupt termination that fails to consider reliance can be arbitrary and capricious. The Supreme Court’s recent turn in Encino (and Fox) made clear that agencies must account for significant reliance interests when changing settled policies. In funding contexts, reliance often includes the statutory beneficiaries’ reliance on planned services (e.g., refugees’ expectation of reception and placement assistance) and third‑party grantees’ contractual and staffing commitments. Pacito highlighted these issues: organizations had historically provided reception and placement services pursuant to cooperative agreements; an abrupt termination demolished the infrastructure needed to serve refugees and raised acute reliance and continuity problems.

- Program continuity and transition planning. When an agency cannot lawfully or practically maintain funding at previous levels, it must still consider continuity, transitional arrangements, and less‑disruptive alternatives. Courts will look for consideration of whether notice, phased reductions, or targeted protections for vulnerable populations were possible. A failure to adopt any transition plan or to assess alternatives is a frequent basis for finding an action arbitrary. Pacito’s directive that the government reinstate cooperative agreements for refugees already admitted to the United States reflects this principle: when statutory duties to admitted refugees exist, the agency cannot simply terminate programs without an adequate plan to ensure delivery of required services.

In sum, arbitrary and capricious review in the funding‑termination context focuses on whether the agency conducted a reasoned analysis that engages statutory duties, relies on evidence, addresses reliance interests, and contemplates less disruptive alternatives and transition measures. An agency whose record resembles an off‑hand policy pronouncement is vulnerable.

## The foreign affairs exception to notice‑and‑comment rulemaking and why many funding terminations remain reviewable

Two different, but sometimes conflated, questions arise about notice‑and‑comment and foreign affairs. First, does APA notice‑and‑comment rulemaking apply to funding decisions and cooperative‑agreement terminations? Second, do foreign‑affairs or national‑security concerns remove otherwise reviewable agency action from judicial scrutiny entirely?

On the procedural side, 5 U.S.C. § 553 governs notice, comment, and publication. Section 553(a) expressly exempts certain categories from the notice‑and‑comment requirement: it exempts “matters relating to military or foreign affairs functions” (subsection (a)(1)) and “matters relating to agency management or personnel or to public property, loans, grants, benefits, or contracts” (subsection (a)(2)). These statutory text exemptions reflect Congress’s judgment that notice‑and‑comment procedures are often impracticable or unnecessary for actions that are fundamentally contractual or implicate foreign‑policy sensitivities.

But the exemptions to notice‑and‑comment do not equate to wholesale unreviewability. APA § 553’s procedural exemptions are narrow in application: they permit agencies to forego notice and comment when the statute so contemplates or when the subject matter fits within the listed categories. Courts will parse both the nature of the action (is it a rule of general applicability?) and the statutory context. Termination of specific cooperative agreements that are essentially contractual and individually targeted is less likely to be treated as rulemaking; such terminations are often governed by separate administrative or contractual procedures and, in any event, are frequently subject to APA review on the merits. Pacito demonstrates this point: even though refugee admissions implicate foreign affairs, the court reviewed the domestic statutory obligations in § 1522 that concern services provided to refugees once they are admitted to the United States — an essentially domestic statutory scheme that does not fall within an absolute foreign‑affairs shield.

On the substantive non‑reviewability side, the foreign affairs and national security context sometimes limits the court’s inquiry into the merits of executive action. The Supreme Court in Trump v. Hawaii (2018) described an exceptionally deferential posture toward presidential judgments on entry and national security under § 1182(f), cautioning against a searching inquiry into the President’s stated national‑security reasons. Courts have often treated core foreign‑affairs powers as presumptively nonjusticiable or deserving of substantial deference. Yet this deference is not limitless: when Congress has enacted domestic statutes that constrain executive action — for example, by imposing specific programmatic duties or domestic protections — courts may review whether the Executive complied with those statutory obligations, even if the wider policy concern involves foreign affairs. Pacito illustrates this boundary: the panel accepted robust deference to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under § 1182(f), but it also held that the government’s termination of resettlement funding pursuant to § 1522 is subject to traditional APA review because § 1522 contains statutory duties applicable to domestic service delivery.

The practical rule is therefore contextual. Actions that are core immigration‑admission decisions or quintessential foreign‑policy judgments (e.g., expelling diplomats, deciding war strategy) may be insulated from meaningful judicial review. But many funding and program‑management decisions — especially those governing domestic service delivery and statutory obligations — are closer to routine administrative functions and remain amenable to ordinary APA review even when they touch on international programs. Exemptions to notice‑and‑comment or heightened deference do not automatically remove all judicial oversight.

## ‘Committed to agency discretion’ vs. ‘law to apply’ when a statute prescribes service levels or responsibilities

A central doctrinal battleground in cases like Pacito is whether the statute at issue leaves agencies with unconstrained discretion or instead creates judicially manageable standards that permit review and relief. This distinction is addressed by two related but different doctrines.

First, the § 701(a)(2) doctrine: the APA does not apply where “agency action is committed to agency discretion by law.” The Supreme Court has repeatedly warned that this exception is narrow — it applies only when “no law” limits the agency or when the statute vests the agency with “unfettered discretion.” See Lincoln v. Vigil (distribution of discretionary grants) and Heckler v. Chaney. Lincoln held that the Secretary of Health and Human Services’ allocation of limited grant funds was essentially unreviewable because the statute placed discretion with the Secretary and left no judicially manageable standards for judicial review. Courts ask: does the statutory text, structure, or context give meaningful criteria or standards by which a court could judge an action? If not, the action may be unreviewable for lack of "law to apply."

Second, the “law to apply” inquiry operates across both Bennett finality and § 706 review, because arbitrary‑and‑capricious review presupposes that there is law for the court to apply. The question is whether Congress prescribed substantive duties, benchmarks, timelines, or other directives. Statutory language matters: “shall” and explicit enumerated duties strongly indicate that Congress intended enforceable obligations; broad authorizations phrased as “may” or “notwithstanding” often indicate discretionary grants.

Pacito hinged on this statutory parsing. Section 1522 is titled “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” and includes language that at first glance appears discretionary — e.g., “to the extent of available appropriations.” That language is often used by agencies to argue that program funding is subject to political appropriations and therefore discretionary. But a careful textual and functional analysis can produce a different conclusion: § 1522(a) and other subsections set out specific programmatic responsibilities — language requiring the Director to make available resources for employment training and placement, to provide English language training opportunities, to ensure cash assistance is structured so as not to discourage self‑sufficiency, and to take responsibility for unaccompanied minors during interim periods. The Ninth Circuit in Pacito concluded that these statutory provisions supplied “law to apply” — there are specific substantive duties and programmatic requirements the court can enforce — so the government’s termination of funding and cooperative agreements could be reviewed under the APA.

Comparative statutory examples help illustrate the difference. Where Congress writes: “The Secretary may provide grants as appropriate,” with no further content, courts are more likely to treat the matter as committed to agency discretion. Where Congress writes: “The Secretary shall ensure that X program provides Y services to persons meeting Z criteria” or “the Director shall make available sufficient resources for employment training…,” the statute provides a substantive benchmark courts can enforce.

Two important caveats follow. First, the presence of qualifying language like “to the extent of available appropriations” does not necessarily render an obligation discretionary. That clause often means Congress recognizes that the program depends on appropriations, not that Congress intended complete deference on how to satisfy statutory duties within available funds. When statutory duties describe substantive obligations, courts will ordinarily assume Congress intended those duties to guide agency action even when tied to appropriations. Second, statutory duties may coexist with agency discretion over implementation; the question is not whether the agency has any discretion at all, but whether there are judicially manageable standards constraining that discretion. Where such standards exist, courts can require agencies to comply with them and to explain how their funding decisions comport with statutory duties.

## Remedial authority: ordering reinstatement of agreements to cure likely statutory violations

If a court finds that an agency likely violated the APA or a statute, what relief can the court grant? Funding cases often create particularly urgent remedial issues because beneficiaries and third‑party providers are immediately harmed when programs are terminated. Courts have two main remedial tools: (1) declaratory and injunctive relief restoring the status quo (which can include ordering the agency to reinstate funding, reinstitute cooperative agreements, or continue payments until the agency complies with law) and (2) vacatur or remand of agency action. In the preliminary injunction context, the standard is equitable: likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm absent relief, balance of equities, and public interest (Winter v. NRDC; though some circuits use a sliding scale; Nken supplies stay factors). On the merits, courts may also order relief to remedy statutory violations under federal‑court powers to enforce federal statutes.

Pacito exemplifies the remedial approach courts will adopt when statutory duties are at stake and the government lacks a plausible immediate alternative. The Ninth Circuit directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States.” That remedial order—narrowly tailored to those already admitted and aimed at ensuring statutory obligations are fulfilled—reflects several remedial principles:

- Narrow tailoring and temporal limits. Courts should and do tailor relief to address the specific statutory violation and avoid unnecessary interference with legitimate executive or foreign‑policy prerogatives. Pacito’s remedy was limited to refugees already admitted and to reinstating agreements to provide statutorily required services; it did not order admissions to proceed nor did it substitute the courts’ judgment for the President’s immigration policy.

- Preservation of statutory rights and avoidance of irreparable harm. When the statutory duty protects vulnerable populations (e.g., refugees already admitted, unaccompanied minors), courts are more willing to order interim relief to prevent irreparable harm. Reinstatement can be an appropriate way to ensure statutory obligations are actually performed while the underlying legal issues are decided.

- Use of equitable discretion rather than micromanagement. Courts typically avoid micromanaging agency programs. Rather than dictating program design, courts often order agencies to comply with statutory duties and to take necessary steps consistent with the statute and subject to further judicial monitoring if necessary. Pacito’s order to reinstate cooperative agreements is an example of restoring the pre‑existing contractual infrastructure rather than prescribing the content of every service.

- Consideration of separation‑of‑powers limits. Courts must respect executive branch responsibilities in areas where the Constitution vests discretion. That is why remedies are often narrow and grounded in statutory enforcement rather than broader policy commands. Pacito demonstrates this balance: the court rejected wholesale interference with admissions policy (deferring to the President under § 1182(f)) but enforced domestic statutory obligations that Congress had imposed.

A final remedial point concerns the form of relief when the government has already terminated cooperative agreements and reallocated funds. Courts can order the agency to reinstate termination, to continue funding, to award contracts, or to remand with instructions to reconsider under the correct legal standard. The particular remedy depends on the record, on whether plaintiff requests specific performance, on any equitable defenses, and on the feasibility of court‑ordered implementation. Pacito favored the pragmatic approach: reinstate agreements to preserve services pending resolution, rather than trying to design new programs from the bench.

## Practical guidance and litigation strategies

For students and practitioners preparing or defending APA challenges to funding suspensions and cooperative‑agreement terminations, the Pacito framework suggests several practical strategies:

- Plaintiffs should frame their claims in statutory terms: show that the statute creates specific duties or programmatic obligations (use of “shall,” enumerated duties, mandated reports, statutory timelines) so that courts will find “law to apply” and reject § 701(a)(2) defenses. Emphasize concrete statutory obligations relating to specific beneficiaries (e.g., reception and placement services for admitted refugees).

- Build evidence of reliance and disruption: collect affidavits and contemporaneous documents showing that organizations and beneficiaries relied on funding, invested resources, and that abrupt termination would and did cause significant harm. Demonstrate a lack of transition planning.

- Document the administrative record: show absence of a reasoned explanation, post hoc rationalizations, or gaps in planning. Agencies are stronger when they produce evidence of deliberation, alternatives considered, and transition measures.

- Anticipate foreign‑affairs and national‑security responses: be ready to distinguish core immigration‑admissions or national‑security actions from domestic program responsibilities. If a challenged action is primarily domestic (service delivery to persons present in the United States, statutory domestic reporting and oversight), stress the domestic statutory framework and the limits of foreign‑affairs deference.

- Seek tailored relief: craft requests for preliminary relief that are narrowly tailored to statutory beneficiaries and that avoid intruding on core executive prerogatives. Courts are more likely to provide narrowly crafted relief (reinstatement for admitted beneficiaries, temporary funding for certain programs) than broad injunctions affecting foreign policy or admissions.

Defendants (agencies or the Executive) should:

- Create and preserve a contemporaneous record for major funding decisions. Explain the factual basis for termination, the alternatives considered, reliance or equity considerations, and transition planning. Where foreign affairs or national security are implicated, articulate the nexus between the decision and those concerns without relying on conclusory assertions.

- Emphasize statutory language granting discretion and, when appropriate, earlier case law (Lincoln v. Vigil, Heckler v. Chaney) showing the court lacks judicially manageable standards. If the statute is discretionary, frame the decision as an exercise of political judgment.

- If relief is likely, propose practical, narrowly tailored interim measures to preserve statutory beneficiaries’ rights (e.g., limited bridge funding, alternative providers) and avoid an adverse injunction forcing reinstitution of complex contractual arrangements without time to implement.

Conclusion

Pacito illustrates the doctrinal line courts must draw between deference in the foreign‑affairs and admission contexts and judicial enforcement of congressional commands about domestic service delivery. Termination of cooperative agreements is usually “agency action” and final when it produces concrete consequences; whether reviewable depends on whether the statute leaves “no law to apply.” Where statutes impose specific duties, agencies must demonstrate reasoned decisionmaking, account for reliance interests, and provide transition plans or face arbitrary and capricious review. When courts find likely statutory violations, they have equitable tools — including narrow orders to reinstate cooperative agreements for protected beneficiaries — to preserve statutory rights without supplanting executive prerogatives in matters of foreign affairs. The lesson for both litigants and agencies is clear: design sound statutory analyses, build a contemporaneous record, consider reliance and continuity, and calibrate remedies to statutory duties and separation‑of‑powers limits.

# Stays Pending Appeal and Tailored Interim Relief

In expedited, high‑stakes litigation—especially where national policy, immigration, and executive power intersect—courts must frequently decide whether to preserve the status quo while an appeal proceeds. The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump provides a compact but instructive example of how appellate courts apply the Supreme Court’s stay framework, balance deference to the President in immigration matters against statutory obligations owed to particular classes of persons, and craft narrowly tailored interim remedies. This chapter uses Pacito to explain (1) how the Nken factors operate (with emphasis on the “most critical” factors); (2) how courts weigh equities and public interest when national security and immigration judgments are at issue; (3) how and why courts carve out limited categories from broad injunctions; (4) how enforcement and supervision mechanisms can make provisional relief workable; and (5) practical lessons about appellate case management—administrative stays, vacatur of prior orders, and iterative clarifications.

## Likelihood of success and irreparable harm as ‘most critical’ factors

The governing framework for a stay pending appeal is Nken v. Holder, which directs courts to consider four factors: (1) the stay applicant’s likelihood of success on the merits; (2) whether the applicant will suffer irreparable harm absent a stay; (3) whether issuance of the stay will substantially injure the other parties; and (4) the public interest. Nken, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009). The Supreme Court described the first two—likelihood of success and irreparable harm—as “the most critical.” Applying Nken requires that courts identify what showing must be made for each factor and then test the movant’s evidence against that standard.

Pacito illustrates both how deferential substantive doctrines can affect the “likelihood of success” inquiry and how the nature of the asserted harms shapes the irreparable‑harm analysis. On likelihood of success, the Ninth Circuit concluded the government was likely to prevail on plaintiffs’ challenge to the President’s suspension of refugee admissions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f). The panel relied on Trump v. Hawaii’s commanding language that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and imposes only a single textual prerequisite—a presidential “finding” that the entry of aliens or any class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” 585 U.S. 667, 685–86 (2018). Where a statutory text expressly gives the President authority and the Supreme Court has pronounced substantial deference, an appellate court assessing a stay should treat the government’s likelihood of prevailing as stronger than it would be in contexts where de novo review applies. In practical terms, counsel seeking a stay in such a context must confront precedent that will compress the range of plausible merits outcomes.

By contrast, the Ninth Circuit also concluded that plaintiffs were likely to prevail on a separate theory—an APA claim based on 8 U.S.C. § 1522’s provision of reception and placement services for refugees. The court thereby split the merits inquiry: it credited the government’s strong showing on the presidential‑authority issue under § 1182(f) while finding a plausible legal basis for plaintiffs’ statutory claim. This serves as an important lesson: “likelihood of success” is claim‑specific. A single case may present claims of different legal character, and the court must analyze the probability of prevailing on each claim the stay implicates.

Irreparable harm requires concrete, non‑remediable injury if interim relief is denied. In immigration and administrative contexts, irreparable harm often takes forms that are statutory, practical, and human—loss of legal status, separation from family, disruptions in health or care, or the removal of contracted services that cannot be replicated later. In Pacito the Ninth Circuit tied irreparable‑harm concerns to the government’s own admissions of uncertainty about its ability to provide statutorily‑mandated reception and placement services: absent interim relief, refugees already admitted would be left without services that Congress contemplated. The court thus found the government had not shown it would suffer irreparable harm from a limited injunction requiring reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees; the harms to plaintiffs (lost services, disruption) were real and immediate.

For litigants and courts, the practical implications are clear. When arguing irreparable harm, parties should (1) explain why monetary relief will not cure the injury; (2) provide specific factual evidence of immediate need or deprivation (e.g., termination notices, government admissions of non‑readiness, testimonies about children or critical services); and (3) connect the injury to legal protections or statutory duties where relevant. Conversely, the party seeking a stay must show that absent a stay it will suffer significant and non‑remediable injury—often by pointing to national security, foreign relations, or other sovereign interests that cannot be compensated later. But where statutory obligations to particular persons exist, courts will scrutinize asserted sovereign harms against the immediacy of individual harms.

## Balancing equities and the public interest in immigration/national security contexts

The third and fourth Nken factors require the court to weigh harms to the parties and the public interest. In national‑security or immigration matters, courts historically grant wide deference to executive policy judgments, and that deference affects how the public interest is evaluated. Pacito demonstrates how courts operationalize that deference while still protecting statutory or constitutional limits.

The Ninth Circuit recognized the breadth of executive authority over entry restrictions, reiterating that § 1182(f) vests the President with “ample power” to impose restrictions and that the text requires only a presidential finding. That recognition informed the court’s judgment that the public interest and the balance of harms favored a stay of the district court’s injunction to the extent it prohibited the President’s suspension of refugee admissions. In other words, when the substantive legal standard is deferential and implicates national security or foreign affairs, the “public interest” factor will often point toward allowing the executive action to proceed—unless the opposing party demonstrates a compelling countervailing interest.

But this deference is not absolute. The Ninth Circuit refused to stay the district court’s order in full because, on the record before it, plaintiffs demonstrated that the government might be violating statutory duties to refugees already admitted. The court balanced sovereign interests in controlling admissions against the public and private interests in ensuring newly admitted refugees receive statutorily mandated services. That balancing led to a tailored result: a stay of the injunctions in their entirety except as to reception and placement obligations for refugees already admitted (and the court directed that cooperative agreements necessary to provide such services be reinstated).

Key takeaways for practitioners and judges: (1) characterize the public interest according to the governing substantive standard—if the operative statute or precedent commands deference, the public‑interest factor will likely favor the executive; (2) present concrete evidence of public‑interest harms (e.g., specific risks to national security, credible foreign policy disruptions) rather than abstract assertions; and (3) when arguing against deference, emphasize statutory text, oversight obligations, or human consequences that the executive cannot evade without legal consequence.

## Structuring limited carveouts (e.g., conditionally approved refugees) and avoiding overbreadth

Carveouts are a common judicial tool for reconciling competing equities in interim relief. Pacito’s sequence of orders shows two important principles in crafting carveouts: precision in defining the covered class and principled predicates for the exception.

The Ninth Circuit’s motions‑panel orders gradually narrowed the district court’s injunction, ultimately preserving a “limited carveout” for individuals who had been conditionally approved for refugee status by U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS) before January 20, 2025. The panel’s early stay denial “to the extent” the injunction applied to those conditionally approved reflected several considerations: (1) a recognition that the equities and reliance interests of applicants who had already secured conditional approval are stronger than those of hypothetical future applicants; (2) a desire to avoid disrupting individual reliance expectations; and (3) the need to avoid blocking the executive’s larger policy decision pending appeal.

Designing such carveouts requires careful attention to both fairness and manageability. The court must define the putative class in administrable terms—dates of approval, specific statutory status, or discrete administrative actions—so that enforcement is feasible and does not require the court to re‑weigh merits on remand. Overbroad injunctions that purport to govern future executive decisionmaking, or that lack clear eligibility criteria, invite appellate reversal or partial stays because they impinge on policy choices. Conversely, excessively narrow carveouts that fail to protect reasonable reliance interests risk leaving vulnerable individuals without recourse.

In Pacito, the appellate court engaged in iterative clarification to avoid overbreadth: it issued a limited denial of a stay with explicit temporal and administrative contours, then later vacated earlier administrative stays and issued a more comprehensive stay subject to reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees. Practitioners should therefore anticipate and litigate over precise definitions: who exactly is in the protected class, what administrative actions count as qualifying, and what documentation will establish membership. Where practicable, litigants should seek (or oppose) definitions that can be implemented from existing administrative records, thereby preserving enforceability and minimizing further litigation over boundaries.

## Supervision mechanisms: enforcement frameworks and magistrate oversight

When courts order tailored interim relief—especially relief that requires administrative action (reinstating contracts, reassigning staff, or providing services)—they often must also build enforcement and supervision mechanisms into their orders. The district court in Pacito did so: following the preliminary injunction, it issued a July 14 order establishing an enforcement framework and appointed a magistrate judge to oversee implementation. The Ninth Circuit’s emergency orders interacted with that framework, temporarily staying the enforcement order and eventually directing reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees already admitted.

Three practical lessons emerge about supervision:

1. Specify the required obligations and the scope of required performance. Orders that direct agencies to “provide reception and placement services” should also identify the ordinary and customary services intended, the population covered (e.g., refugees admitted prior to the suspension), and any temporal limits. The Ninth Circuit in Pacito expressly required the government “to reinstate such cooperative agreements necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States,” and clarified that such services “shall include the usual and customary services that have been afforded such refugees under the prior cooperative agreements.” This specificity reduces dispute about what the order requires.

2. Appoint a neutral supervisor where ongoing administrative activity must be monitored. The district court’s appointment of a magistrate judge to oversee the enforcement framework illustrates a common device: a magistrate or special master can manage implementation (deadlines, reporting, coordination) without burdening the trial judge or the appellate court with minutiae. However, courts must be careful not to impermissibly delegate adjudicative functions; supervision should be tethered to the court’s equitable power to enforce its orders.

3. Build in procedures for compliance monitoring and dispute resolution. Orders should require periodic reports, delineate the evidence necessary to show compliance, and specify prompt procedures for resolving implementation disputes—e.g., expedited motions practice, emergency hearings, or appointment of an independent monitor. These mechanisms increase compliance and make later appellate review more focused.

Judges and counsel should also anticipate and litigate the practicalities of reinstating terminated agreements: what it means to “reinstate” (reactiveffectuate the same terms? reenter negotiations?), whether funds are available, and what interim funding or staffing arrangements are necessary. In Pacito, these practical questions underlay the appellate court’s directive because the government itself disclosed uncertainty about its ability to secure new providers.

## Appellate management: administrative stays, vacatur, and iterative clarification orders

High‑profile, fast‑moving litigation often generates multiple, successive interim orders as courts respond to emergency filings, clarifications, or shifting facts. Pacito’s procedural history—motions panels, administrative stays, partial stays, vacatur of prior stays, and final temporary orders—offers a roadmap for appellate management.

First, motions panels frequently issue temporary relief with limited reasoning to maintain the status quo while fuller panel consideration is prepared. In Pacito, a motions panel initially granted only a partial stay and left a limited carveout intact. Such temporary orders serve triage functions but can create confusion if not swiftly followed by full‑panel disposition or clarifying orders. Practitioners should be prepared to move quickly to seek clarification, to request full‑court consideration, or to press for expedited briefing when interim orders leave important operative questions unresolved.

Second, courts may use administrative stays to pause enforcement of district court orders while the appellate court resolves procedural or managerial disputes. Administrative stays are often shorter and more flexible; they can be vacated or replaced by more reasoned stay orders. Pacito’s sequence—administrative stay followed by an interim order that vacated prior stays and issued a new stay pending resolution of the appeal—illustrates this iterative approach. Parties should track each temporary order closely and anticipate that prior stays may be withdrawn or modified as the appellate court gathers more information.

Third, the appellate court must reconcile competing interlocutory claims and ensure its orders are internally coherent. In Pacito the Ninth Circuit balanced deference on entry suspensions with statutory obligations on resettlement services and accordingly crafted an order staying the district court’s injunctions in their entirety “except as noted below,” then specified reinstatement obligations for admitted refugees. That pattern—stay in large measure, but preserve narrow enforcement where statutory duties are implicated—can be a model for appellate courts seeking to respect lower‑court rulings while protecting serious national policy prerogatives.

Finally, appellate counsel must be strategic about emergency motions. The government in Pacito filed multiple emergency motions and was denied some as moot after iterative orders. Emergency motions should focus on the narrowest obtainable relief, present a clear record-based case for urgency, and anticipate counterarguments about statutory obligations and reliance. Likewise, litigants opposing stays should use the appellate process to foreground concrete harms and narrow remedies that preserve both individual rights and the executive’s prerogatives.

## Practical drafting advice for interim remedies and appellate advocacy

From Pacito flow several pragmatic recommendations for litigants and judges involved in stay and preliminary‑injunction litigation in the national‑policy arena:

- Be claim‑specific in your merits showing. Where different claims have different legal standards, explain likelihood of success on each claim separately. If deference will limit review of one claim, emphasize other claims where review is less deferential.

- Quantify and document irreparable harms. Use affidavits, administrative records, and governmental admissions to show immediacy and non‑remediability.

- Propose or request administrable definitions for any protected class. Dates of approval, specific kinds of agency actions, and documentary thresholds make carveouts practicable.

- Offer practicable enforcement mechanisms. Suggest use of magistrate judges, reporting schedules, and specific metrics of compliance to make orders feasible.

- Anticipate sovereign defenses creatively but concretely. When the government alleges national security or foreign policy harms, require specific evidence of imminent harm and frame relief that minimizes interference with core executive functions.

- Draft relief with an eye to minimal intrusion. Narrow, tailored orders are more defensible on appeal than broad injunctions that second‑guess policy judgments.

- Manage appellate timing: seek expedited briefing early where possible, and be prepared to litigate in multiple interim stages (motions panel, full panel, emergency filings). Keep appellate arguments tightly focused—appellate courts’ resources for provisional orders are limited, and clarity aids quick resolution.

## The role of statutory interpretation and dissenting views

Pacito’s partial concurrence/dissent by Judge Lee highlights the interpretive dispute that often shapes interim outcomes: whether language like “is authorized” and section titles that say “Authorization” indicate permissive congressional intent or whether other statutory provisions containing mandatory language create enforceable duties. Judge Lee read § 1522 as authorizing, not mandating, reception and placement programs; the majority read the statutory scheme differently and ordered reinstatement for admitted refugees.

When seeking or opposing interim relief premised on statutory duties, litigants should frame textual arguments clearly: analyze section headings, verbs (“shall” vs “may” vs “is authorized”), statutory context, and the relevant appropriations language (“to the extent of available appropriations”). Also consider reporting or oversight provisions (e.g., § 1523’s reporting requirement to Congress) as indicators of congressional expectations. Appellate courts—especially in stay contexts—must decide whether the statutory ambiguity tips in favor of the executive’s discretion or requires preservation of statutory protections for a defined group.

In sum, Pacito is a paradigmatic decision about how appellate courts navigate the tension between deference in immigration and national security decisions and judicial protection of statutory or individual rights. It shows that stays pending appeal are not all‑or‑nothing: courts can sustain executive prerogatives while carving out narrowly focused interim relief where statutory duties or concrete harms demand it. The case also underscores the importance of precise, administrable remedies and robust supervision mechanisms so that interim orders are both effective and legally sustainable.

# Separation of Powers and the Youngstown Lens

The Pacito litigation is a useful classroom vehicle for thinking about the separation of powers in practice. At its core this case presents a familiar trio of issues: (1) the scope of presidential authority to restrict entry of non‑citizens (the statutory grant in 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f)); (2) the scope of statutory duties imposed on the executive by the Refugee Act (most importantly 8 U.S.C. § 1522), and the consequences when an executive policy decision collides with such duties; and (3) the limits that appropriations law and impoundment principles place on the Executive’s ability to refuse to spend money that Congress has appropriated. Pacito illustrates how courts sort these competing claims through the Youngstown framework, basic canons of statutory interpretation, doctrines of administrative law, and the law governing the federal purse. This chapter explains those doctrinal tools and shows how they were deployed (and contested) in Pacito so that you can apply them to new fact patterns.

## Youngstown categories and their application to immigration and refugee policy

Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer remains the touchstone for structuring judicial review of presidential action that operates in a domain where Congress has also legislated. The familiar tripartite framework divides presidential action into three categories:

- Category 1: the President acts pursuant to an express or implied authorization of Congress — the Executive stands at its maximum constitutional authority and courts will give considerable weight to the President’s position;  
- Category 2: the President acts where Congress has not spoken — a zone of twilight in which both branches may have concurrent authority and the court’s scrutiny depends on the circumstances;  
- Category 3: the President acts against the express or implied will of Congress — the Executive’s power is at its lowest ebb and the action must be supported solely by his constitutional powers absent congressional authorization.

Apply this framework in immigration/regulatory settings by asking first whether Congress has given the President an explicit statutory grant of authority that authorizes the Executive’s action, and second whether Congress elsewhere has legislated in a way that constrains or contradicts that action. In Pacito the panel divided the issues along those lines. The President pointed to § 1182(f), the century‑old provision that permits the President, “whenever the President finds that the entry of any aliens or of any class of aliens … would be detrimental to the interests of the United States,” to suspend the entry of any class of aliens. That is an express statutory delegation and places the suspension of refugee admissions squarely in Youngstown Category 1 — the Executive’s action is taken pursuant to an express congressional authorization. The panel repeatedly invoked Trump v. Hawaii, where the Supreme Court sustained analogous entry restrictions under § 1182(f) and described that statutory authority as one that “exudes deference” to the President.

But Youngstown analysis does not stop with an initial classification. The existence of one grant of authority (here § 1182(f)) does not necessarily immunize the Executive from all other statutory constraints that Congress has enacted. If another statute imposes duties that the Executive’s policy frustrates, courts must ask whether the President’s exercise of § 1182(f) is nonetheless in conflict with Congress’s will expressed elsewhere — and whether that conflict reduces the action to Youngstown Category 3. In Pacito the court confronted precisely that tension: § 1182(f) provided a clear pathway for suspending admissions, but § 1522 imposed obligations relating to domestic reception and placement services for refugees admitted to the United States. Where the Executive suspends admissions and defunds resettlement activities in a way that prevents the provision of statutorily required services to already‑admitted refugees, the operative question becomes whether the President’s action, though authorized to stop future admissions, transgresses congressionally imposed duties regarding persons already admitted. The panel concluded that on the admissions question the government was likely to prevail (Youngstown Category 1), but that it was not likely to prevail on the claim that the government may refuse to perform statutory obligations to refugees already admitted (which puts the Executive’s conduct into closer proximity to Youngstown Category 3, or at least removes Category 1’s protective force).

When you analyze similar cases, use Youngstown as an organizing framework but resist treating it as a mechanically exclusive test. The key analytic moves are identifying statutory authorizations and statutory constraints, comparing them for conflict or compatibility, and calibrating the level of judicial deference accordingly.

## Delegation via § 1182(f) vs. statutory constraints via § 1522

To understand the interplay between § 1182(f) and § 1522 you must do two things simultaneously: (1) read the delegation to the President to determine what actions it permits; and (2) read the statutory scheme addressing refugee admissions and resettlement to see what duties Congress has imposed on the Executive. The two readings may pull in different directions.

Section 1182(f) is unusually broad on its face. It authorizes the President, after finding that entry of “any aliens or of any class of aliens” would be “detrimental” to the United States, to suspend their entry “for such period as he shall deem necessary.” Congress has thus delegated a sweeping power to the President over immigration admission decisions. When a plaintiff challenges a presidential action taken pursuant to § 1182(f), courts often concentrate on whether the President in fact made the requisite finding, and then — because of statutory deference and the political character of immigration decisions — afford substantial deference to the President’s national‑security and foreign‑policy determinations. That is why courts treating § 1182(f) see the President’s action as operating in Youngstown Category 1 and why the Pacito panel found a likelihood of success on the merits of the suspension of refugee admissions.

Section 1522, by contrast, is part of the Refugee Act of 1980 and sets out Congress’s expectations for domestic resettlement programs and services, including a funding mechanism and a structure under which the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) and other agencies provide reception and placement services. Key features include “shall” language in subsection (a) purporting to impose duties on the Director “to the extent of available appropriations,” specific language authorizing grants and cooperative agreements with public and private non‑profit agencies, and statutory reporting requirements. Historically, the federal government has implemented § 1522 by contracting or entering cooperative agreements with non‑profit resettlement agencies that deliver reception and placement services to refugees already admitted to the United States.

These two statutes thus occupy different functional spaces: § 1182(f) controls who may enter the country; § 1522 requires the Executive to provide specific services to those who have been admitted. When the Executive uses § 1182(f) to suspend admissions it is acting within the sphere Congress created for the President; when the Executive suspends or refuses to perform duties described in § 1522 (e.g., terminating cooperative agreements for reception and placement services), the Executive may be preventing Congress’s statute from operating as intended. Resolving the tension between those statutes requires statutory interpretation and separation‑of‑powers reasoning about whether the President’s delegated authority is broad enough to override, or is constrained by, statutory duties Congress has already imposed.

Pacito illustrates both sides of this coin. The majority panel read § 1182(f) as a commanding grant supporting the suspension, but read § 1522’s provisions — in particular the requirement to provide reception and placement services “to the extent of available appropriations” and the specific language regarding legal and financial responsibility for unaccompanied minors — as creating enforceable statutory obligations that the Executive could not simply refuse to perform. Judge Lee’s partial dissent recognized § 1182(f)’s breadth but read § 1522 as largely permissive, arguing that much of § 1522 uses authorization language (e.g., “is authorized”) and that the “shall” clauses are best read as conditions upon which funding, if provided, must be used. That disagreement centers on statutory interpretation doctrines that courts commonly apply: the significance of the statutory title and headings, the use of “shall” versus “is authorized,” the import of the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifier, and the contextual reading of the statutory scheme.

When approaching similar disputes, first parse the text of each relevant provision with attention to operative verbs, qualifying phrases, cross‑references, and the statute’s place within the larger statutory framework. Then ask whether the circumstances present a true conflict (one statute necessarily prevents the other from operating) or whether the statutes can be read to coexist (e.g., the President suspends admissions prospectively under § 1182(f) while the Executive continues to satisfy § 1522’s duties as to persons already admitted).

## When executive orders collide with statutory duties: supremacy of statute and remedial consequences

A central separation‑of‑powers principle is simple: the Executive cannot nullify statutory obligations by issuing an executive order that conflicts with or frustrates the intent of Congress. When an executive order purports to alter the operation of a statute — for example, by depriving a statutorily protected group of services Congress intended them to receive — courts apply established rules to resolve the dispute.

First, statutory supremacy. If Congress has plainly required a particular program, payment, or duty, the Executive lacks authority to disregard or abrogate that requirement through an executive order. Youngstown Category 3 is the conceptual locus of this rule: when the President acts contrary to the will of Congress, his power is at its nadir and the courts must be prepared to enjoin ultra vires conduct. Pacito shows this principle in operation. The panel concluded the government was unlikely to prevail on the APA claims concerning § 1522 and required reinstatement of cooperative agreements to ensure reception and placement services for refugees already admitted. That relief is a classic example of judicial enforcement of statutory duties: the court did not purport to second‑guess the President’s assessment about future admissions under § 1182(f), but it refused to allow an executive policy to thwart a statutorily mandated domestic resettlement program.

Second, remedies. When courts find that executive action violates statutory requirements or the APA, they typically fashion prospective equitable relief tailored to restore the status quo ante and to ensure compliance with statutory duties. In the preliminary injunction context — the posture of the district court’s initial orders in Pacito — courts weigh the Nken factors (likelihood of success on the merits, irreparable harm, balance of equities, and public interest) in deciding whether to enjoin the government. The Pacito panel applied that framework on stay‑pending‑appeal review; the district court’s initial injunctions were stayed in part while the appellate panel directed the government to reinstate cooperative agreements for admitted refugees because the panel concluded the government had not shown it could meet statutory duties otherwise.

Third, judicial review and remand. When the government terminates programs without reasoned explanation, courts will often remand under the APA for a reasoned explanation or will set aside agency action as arbitrary and capricious. Even where the Executive has discretion, that discretion normally must be exercised consistent with statutory mandates and must be accompanied by reasoned decisionmaking. Pacito rests on that principle: the panel signaled that the government has not provided an adequate record showing that it has fulfilled or can fulfill statutory duties, and thus the court required remedial measures to ensure compliance.

For a student confronted with a hypothetical where an executive order appears to conflict with a statute, the proper sequence is: identify the statutes at issue and their text; determine whether the executive action is authorized or is in conflict; apply Youngstown to determine deference; and, if conflict exists, consider whether judicial relief should be fashioned to vindicate the statute (injunction, reinstatement of programs, remand for explanation). Remember that courts will not lightly substitute their policy judgments for the President’s in matters of immigration and national security, but they will enforce statutory obligations that are unambiguous and mandatory.

## Appropriations and the Anti‑Deficiency Act: operating mandates “to the extent of available appropriations”

Many statutory duties are expressly qualified by appropriations language: Congress commonly writes “to the extent of available appropriations” or “subject to the availability of appropriations.” That qualifying phrase plays a pivotal role in how courts resolve disputes about the Executive’s obligation to fund programs. Pacito turns on exactly this kind of language: § 1522(a) conditions the Director’s responsibilities on “the extent of available appropriations.” How should courts read that qualification?

Start with the ordinary meaning. “To the extent of available appropriations” makes performance contingent on funds being available; it does not, however, mean that the Executive may ignore appropriated funds that Congress has in fact made available. Congress can both authorize and appropriate. Authorization statutes (like § 1522, depending on how it is read) permit the program to be run; appropriations statutes provide the money to run it. If Congress has appropriated funds to accomplish a statutory program, the Executive cannot decline to spend those funds for that purpose without observing the procedures for altering spending (such as seeking a rescission or reprogramming consistent with appropriations law). The key is whether appropriations for the program exist and whether they are lawfully available for the aggrieved statutory purpose.

The Anti‑Deficiency Act (ADA), 31 U.S.C. § 1341 et seq., prohibits federal officers from obligating or expending funds in advance of appropriation or in excess of an appropriation. It also forbids accepting voluntary services for the United States. The ADA thus bars an agency from legally obligating funds if there is no appropriation to support the obligation. Conversely, when Congress appropriates money and the agency has authority to obligate funds for a program, the ADA does not permit the Executive to refuse to obligate appropriated funds simply because of policy disagreement; to do so would be an illegal withholding of funds that Congress intended to be spent.

Train v. City of New York (421 U.S. 775 (1975)) is a canonical case on this point. There, the Secretary of Transportation reduced obligations under a statutory program despite Congress having appropriated funds and having authorized the Secretary to make grants. The Supreme Court held the Secretary could not refuse to carry out the statutory plan when Congress had appropriated money for that purpose. That holding stands for the proposition that the Executive cannot effectively nullify statutory programs by declining to spend appropriated funds without lawful authority to do so.

Pacito signals this dynamic. The panel noted the FY 2025 appropriations for migration and refugee assistance and the subsequent rescission that reduced the total. The presence of an appropriation (even a reduced one) is important: the Executive’s decision to terminate cooperative agreements and to suspend funding for reception and placement services is not proof that appropriations are unavailable to satisfy § 1522 duties. Where the record shows that funds exist and were appropriated, courts will scrutinize the Executive’s decision to stop implementing statutory programs to ensure the decision comports with appropriations law.

A few practical points for applying these principles:

- Determine whether Congress has appropriated funds for the statutory program and whether those funds are currently available (not rescinded or otherwise legally restricted). Legislative history and appropriations committee reports can be relevant but are secondary to the text of the appropriation.

- Ask whether the executive branch has a lawful procedure for redirecting or deferring spending. The President cannot simply impound or permanently withhold appropriation funds; the Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974 and OMB rules set out procedures for deferrals, rescissions, and reprogramming. Those procedures ordinarily must be followed.

- If funds have been lawfully appropriated and are available to fund statutory duties, the Executive must explain any decision not to obligate them. A bare policy disagreement is an insufficient basis to withhold funds that Congress has allocated for a statutory program.

In short, “to the extent of available appropriations” makes obligations contingent on whether Congress has provided money, not a license for the Executive to nullify a statutory program when funds are in fact available.

## Impoundment principles and limits on executive non‑expenditure of appropriated funds

The history of impoundment — and the congressional reaction to perceived executive impoundment — is essential background for Pacito. Impoundment refers to executive branch withholding or delaying the obligation or expenditure of funds that Congress has appropriated. In the Nixon years, Presidents frequently refused to spend appropriated funds for policy reasons; Congress responded with the Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, which sought to constrain executive impoundment by requiring the President to transmit proposed rescissions to Congress and by creating rules governing deferrals.

Legal limits on impoundment derive from a combination of the Anti‑Deficiency Act, Train, and the Budget Act. The Executive may not unilaterally suppress expenditures in a way that frustrates clear congressional intent. If the Executive believes that funds should not be spent for policy reasons, the constitutional and statutory route is to seek a rescission from Congress; absent that, the Executive’s lawful options are limited to temporary deferments within the procedures established by the Budget Act and OMB rules.

Applied to Pacito, those principles mean that the termination of cooperative agreements and the halt in funding for domestic resettlement required careful attention to the appropriations and to the procedures used. If the Executive terminated funding and cooperative agreements without a lawful basis (for example, absent a contemporaneous determination that appropriations were unavailable or without following deferral/rescission procedures), the district court could find that the Executive had unlawfully impounded funds or otherwise failed to carry out a statutory duty. The Pacito panel’s directive that cooperative agreements be reinstated (as necessary to fulfill § 1522 duties) reflects courts’ willingness to remedy situations where the Executive has not shown that appropriation‑based constraints justify non‑spending.

A couple of practical guideposts follow:

- Distinguish between three forms of non‑expenditure: (1) genuine non‑availability of appropriations (e.g., funds were rescinded or otherwise legally restricted); (2) lawful, temporary deferral under Budget Act procedures that have been properly invoked; and (3) unlawful impoundment or refusal to spend appropriated funds for policy reasons without following statutory procedures. Only the first two can be legally sustainable in most circumstances.

- When agencies terminate contracts or cooperative agreements, determine whether they are simply declining to enter into new obligations (which can be lawful if no appropriations exist) or whether they are terminating existing obligations that Congress meant to continue. Termination of already‑valid obligations funded by appropriations raises different legal questions.

- Courts will look for a reasoned administrative record. If the government asserts that funds are not available, the record should explain why: the appropriations that would otherwise fund the program were rescinded, reprogrammed, or otherwise rendered unavailable pursuant to law. Unsupported assertions that the Executive lacks money or that a policy change justifies non‑spending are unlikely to satisfy judicial review.

## Applying the Pacito lessons to future fact patterns

Pacito offers a roadmap for analyzing future disputes that involve presidential directives, statutory duties, and appropriations law. Here is a step‑by‑step approach to analyzing similar problems:

1. Identify the relevant statutes. Does the President rely on an express statutory grant (e.g., § 1182(f))? Does another statute impose duties (e.g., § 1522)? Identify the operative text: “may,” “is authorized,” “shall,” and any appropriations qualifiers.

2. Place the case in the Youngstown frame. If the President acts under an express statutory authorization, you are in Category 1 and give substantial deference to the Executive on that discrete power. If Congress has legislated contrary to the President’s action, treat the situation as moving toward Category 3 and apply stricter review.

3. Read the statutes together. Can the statutes coexist, or do they present an irreconcilable conflict? If they can coexist (for example, the President may suspend future admissions under § 1182(f) while still providing reception services to those already admitted), courts will usually order the Executive to take steps to satisfy both statutes.

4. Examine the appropriations. Has Congress appropriated funds for the statutory duties? Are those funds available? Did the Executive follow the Budget and Impoundment Control Act if it wished to defer or rescind spending? If the funds are available and the Executive has not lawfully impounded them, courts will be skeptical of refusals to spend.

5. Evaluate the administrative record. Under the APA, agencies must provide reasoned explanations for major policy shifts that affect statutory programs. Absent a reasoned explanation tied to statutory constraints or available appropriations, agency action is vulnerable to a finding of arbitrary and capricious or ultra vires conduct.

6. Consider remedies and equitable balancing. If the plaintiff is likely to succeed on the claim that a statutory duty has been violated, equitable relief (injunction, reinstatement of agreements, remand) is a typical remedy, balanced against the national‑security and foreign‑policy interests the Executive identifies.

Pacito thus demonstrates the interplay of statutory interpretation, separation of powers, administrative law, and appropriations law. The case reminds scholars and students that the President’s broad delegated powers in immigration and national security are not unbounded; statutory duties, the availability of appropriations, and the courts’ duty to ensure that the Executive acts within the law all remain potent constraints. Judge Lee’s partial dissent in Pacito highlights the line‑drawing difficulty — many statutory provisions use both permissive and mandatory language and the line between authorization and obligation is often disputed — but the controlling lesson is institutional: the Executive can exercise broad discretion, but courts will enforce clear statutory mandates and the procedural and substantive rules that govern spending of appropriated funds.

# Standing, Justiciability, and the Zone of Interests

This chapter uses Pacito v. Trump (Ninth Circuit stay order, Sept. 12, 2025) to illuminate how courts decide who may sue and what claims they may bring in litigation that challenges refugee admissions policies and related programmatic funding decisions. Pacito is useful because the panel confronted several overlapping justiciability issues in expedited posture: the limits of judicial review of entry restrictions under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), the distinct legal character of claims that target post‑entry services and funding under 8 U.S.C. § 1522, organizational standing when cooperative agreements are terminated, the scope of the APA’s zone‑of‑interests inquiry, the narrow reach of consular nonreviewability in programmatic suits, and mootness questions that arise when agencies change course mid‑litigation.

The lesson of Pacito is a familiar one: not all plaintiffs and not all claims are created equal. A plaintiff’s institutional position (admitted refugee, applicant abroad, NGO) and the legal theory it advances (challenge to an entry suspension, demand for statutorily authorized services, contract/cooperative‑agreement relief) determine whether the federal courts can — and will — redress the alleged wrongs.

## Refugee plaintiffs: injuries to admitted refugees vs. applicants abroad

Standing doctrine begins with the Article III requirements of injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability. Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife supplies the familiar baseline: a plaintiff must show a concrete and particularized injury that is actual or imminent, fairly traceable to the challenged action, and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. Those principles take particular shape in immigration and refugee contexts.

Admitted refugees (those already physically in the United States) typically present the clearest standing. In Pacito, the Ninth Circuit recognized that refugees who had already been admitted or who had been conditionally approved and were imminently to enter have concrete, imminent interests: access to resettlement services, cash and medical assistance, or simply the right to remain in (or enter) the country under existing approvals. Those are injuries readily understood as “personal and individual” and as likely redressable by court orders restoring services or enforcing statutory duties.

By contrast, applicants abroad who lack any concrete government approval or imminence of entry face steeper obstacles. Consular refusals and discretionary entry decisions are often treated as nonjusticiable or at least as presumptively immune from intrusive review; and for applicants without a concrete, government‑recognized entitlement, the injury is frequently speculative. The Pacito stay reflects these differences: the panel found that the government was likely to prevail on challenges to the President’s power to suspend entry under § 1182(f), but it preserved (carved out from its stay) relief for certain non‑citizens who had been conditionally approved for resettlement before the order’s effective date. That carveout shows the court’s willingness to treat conditional approvals as creating a sufficiently concrete, imminent interest to justify judicial relief — a middle ground between admitted refugees and remote applicants abroad.

Practical point for litigators: if you represent refugees abroad, obtain and document any formal government approvals, conditional admissions, or other agency actions that make entry imminent. Those formal markers materially affect redressability and the injury‑in‑fact analysis.

## Organizational standing: diversion of resources and loss of cooperative agreements

Organizations bring two common models of standing: (1) representational/associational standing to sue on behalf of members, and (2) organizational standing premised on a direct injury to the organization itself. The Supreme Court’s decisions in Havens Realty and Friends of the Earth are the textbooks here: an organization may show standing by demonstrating that the defendant’s conduct frustrated its mission and forced it to divert resources to counteract that conduct (Havens), or that the organization has concrete environmental or programmatic interests harmed by the defendant’s actions (Friends of the Earth).

In Pacito, the organizational plaintiffs were resettlement agencies that previously held cooperative agreements with the State Department. When the government terminated those agreements and suspended funding, the organizations alleged immediate operational injuries: loss of contract revenue, the need to divert staff time to mitigate the loss and to assist clients, and damage to their relationships and infrastructure for overseas processing and domestic reception. Those assertions fit comfortably within established organizational‑standing doctrine. Economic injury from lost contracts is classical standing; diversion of resources to protect concrete programs or to fill statutory gaps also demonstrates the type of organizational injury courts recognize.

Two points are worth emphasizing. First, the required showing is fact‑specific: generalized mission frustration is insufficient if the organization cannot show a concrete diversion or a specific lost programmatic opportunity. Second, timing and documentation matter: contemporaneous evidence—board minutes authorizing staff reassignments, accounting showing lost revenue, communications documenting cancelled programs—bolsters the causal connection between the government action and the organizational injury.

## Zone‑of‑interests test under the APA for § 1522 claims

When plaintiffs invoke the Administrative Procedure Act (APA) to challenge agency action (or inaction), courts apply the “zone‑of‑interests” test to determine whether the plaintiff is a proper plaintiff to bring the statutory claim. The test asks whether the plaintiff’s injury falls within the interests sought to be protected by the statute whose violation is alleged. The test is designed to be “modest,” not a demanding merits‑based inquiry, but it is a threshold limitation on APA review.

Pacito turned this inquiry into a central battleground over 8 U.S.C. § 1522, the statutory provision authorizing and outlining federal programs for refugees’ domestic resettlement and assistance. The majority concluded that § 1522, read in context, imposes meaningful duties — not merely discretionary authorizations — with respect to reception and placement services, cash and medical assistance, and certain responsibilities for unaccompanied minors. Because § 1522 speaks directly to the welfare and resettlement of refugees, the court reasoned, refugees and resettlement organizations plainly fall within the law’s protective zone; hence APA review of alleged unlawful defunding or termination of cooperative agreements was warranted.

Judge Lee’s partial dissent highlights the other side of the interpretive divide: the statute’s title (“Authorization for programs”) and repeated language that assistance is “to the extent of available appropriations” can be read as conferring permissive authority rather than imposing mandatory duties. If § 1522 is purely permissive, then plaintiffs may lack an enforceable statutory interest that the APA can vindicate; under that view the zone‑of‑interests test would be fatal to the suit.

The doctrinal point for students and practitioners is twofold. First, zone‑of‑interests analysis is often resolved by close statutory interpretation: where the statute uses “shall” in operational provisions, courts may find enforceable expectations; where the statute is framed as authorization, courts may be hesitant to treat it as imposing mandatory duties. Second, the APA inquiry does not require a plaintiff to show that it is the quintessential beneficiary of the program; it only requires that the interest claimed be among those Congress plausibly sought to protect. In refugee‑services litigation, the statutory text, structure, and historical practice (e.g., longstanding cooperative agreements) inform that plausibility determination.

## Limits of consular nonreviewability in programmatic challenges

The doctrine of consular nonreviewability protects many individualized visa and consular decisions from judicial review. But that doctrine is narrow: it does not license wholesale nonreviewability of programmatic or statutory claims that do not seek to review an individual consular officer’s discretionary visa denial.

Pacito demonstrates the distinction. The panel emphasized deference to the President’s authority under § 1182(f) to suspend entry — invoking Trump v. Hawaii for the proposition that courts should not conduct a searching inquiry into the President’s foreign‑policy judgments about who may enter. Yet the court did not treat this deference as a blanket bar to review of statutory claims about domestic resettlement obligations. The government’s authority to restrict entry is one thing; its statutory obligations to provide post‑entry reception and placement services under § 1522 are another. Thus, a plaintiff’s programmatic challenge to the termination of cooperative agreements or to the cessation of domestic services is not automatically foreclosed by consular nonreviewability or by deference to executive branch entry policies.

In practical terms: when the relief sought aims at restoring statutory benefits or contractual relationships domestically, courts will be more receptive than when plaintiffs try to litigate the merits of an individual visa denial, which is squarely within consular latitude and often insulated from judicial second‑guessing.

## Mootness and voluntary cessation when agencies pivot mid‑litigation

Agency actions in politically charged contexts often shift mid‑litigation. When an agency terminates a program or reverses a policy after suit is filed, defendants frequently argue that the case is moot because the complained‑of conduct ceased. The Supreme Court warns, however, that voluntary cessation moots a case only in “exceptional circumstances” — it must be “absolutely clear” that the wrongful behavior will not recur. Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw and other decisions set a high bar.

Pacito highlights those concerns: after the district court issued an initial preliminary injunction, the government terminated cooperative agreements and later represented it was preparing requests for proposals for new resettlement agencies. The Ninth Circuit was skeptical that the government’s ad hoc representations would render the controversy moot. Accordingly, the panel required the government either to reinstate cooperative agreements (at least for refugees already admitted) or to face continued judicial supervision, because unilateral termination in the face of a pending claim does not, by itself, erase the controversy.

Two related doctrines often rescue plaintiffs from technical mootness. First, the “capable of repetition, yet evading review” exception applies to disputes that are by nature too short in duration to be litigated fully before cessation but are likely to recur. Second, injunctive relief addressing systemic policies is less likely to be moot than requests for narrowly tailored, time‑limited relief. Courts also assess whether the defendant’s cessation was prompted by litigation (suggesting a strategic, temporary move) and whether structural guarantees or commitments exist to prevent recurrence.

Litigators should therefore preserve records showing the defendant’s intent and capacity to resume the challenged conduct: internal memos, budgetary plans, and any evidence that the change was temporary or litigiously motivated. Plaintiffs should also frame relief to address ongoing or recurring harms (e.g., requirements to follow statutory procedures or to maintain minimum services) to reduce mootness risk.

## Conclusion: practical rules of thumb

- Distinguish carefully between challenges to entry suspensions (a realm where § 1182(f) and political‑branch deference loom large) and challenges to domestic programmatic actions (where statutory duties, contractual relationships, and APA review can provide a basis for judicial relief). The former may demand heightened deference; the latter is often amenable to ordinary statutory and administrative review.
- For refugee applicants abroad, secure and document any formal approvals or conditional admissions before framing claims as redressable injuries; such approvals materially strengthen standing.
- Organizations that lose cooperative agreements typically can show concrete injury: lost revenues and the need to divert resources. Document the operational impacts.
- In APA suits, focus early on the zone‑of‑interests question as a threshold issue: show that the statutory text and structure reflect an intent to protect the very interests you assert.
- Do not assume consular nonreviewability immunizes all facets of refugee litigation — it protects individualized visa decisions but does not bar review of domestic programmatic obligations.
- Anticipate and counter mootness arguments by preserving evidence of ongoing impacts and by seeking durable relief that addresses systemic deficiencies rather than ephemeral practices.

Pacito teaches what many doctrinal lines already suggest: standing and justiciability are flexible tools that reflect the character of the relief sought and the nature of the plaintiff. In refugee‑program litigation, understanding those doctrinal contours is the sine qua non of framing a viable claim.

# Statutory Interpretation Toolkit for Refugee Services Cases

This chapter equips you with the interpretive tools lawyers and judges used (and disputed) in Pacito v. Trump to resolve whether 8 U.S.C. § 1522 imposes mandatory duties on the federal government to provide reception and placement services to refugees, or instead simply authorizes such programs. Pacito is a convenient, contemporary lens because the panel split mirrors common interpretive fault lines: textualism versus purposivism, granular grammatical parsing versus structural/functional reading, and skepticism toward agency post hoc justifications following changes in administrative practice. Your task as a student is to master the doctrinal vocabulary and tests that appear in these arguments so you can apply them to new statutory provisions and fact patterns.

We proceed through five interpretive topics that are especially important in refugee-services litigation and agency counseling: (1) modal verbs and force of command, (2) the interpretive value of headings and statutory structure, (3) the meaning of common qualifiers that temper statutory language, (4) the role of legislative history and statutory purpose in refugee law, and (5) how courts evaluate agency interpretations in the post–Loper Bright environment (Skidmore-like “practical persuasion” analysis). At the end of the chapter is a short practical checklist for drafting litigation and administrative arguments.

## Parsing modal verbs: ‘shall’ vs. ‘may’ vs. ‘is authorized to’

Modal verbs and formulaic phrases are the most immediate signals the parties and courts rely on to decide whether Congress imposed a binding duty or granted permissive discretion. Pacito turns on these words.

- The basic starting rule. When Congress uses “shall” in a statutory provision, courts often presume a mandatory command; when Congress uses “may,” the presumption is permissive. But the presumption is rebuttable. Context, structure, and the surrounding text can transform or qualify a facially mandatory “shall” into a contingent or hortatory provision.

- How Pacito applies the rule. The Ninth Circuit majority emphasized that § 1522(a) uses “shall” to describe duties the Director of the Office of Refugee Resettlement must undertake: “The Director shall…make available sufficient resources…” and “shall…insure that cash assistance is made available….” The majority treated those “shall” provisions as expressing congressional obligation—supporting an injunction directing the government to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to furnish those services.

  Judge Lee’s partial dissent, by contrast, placed emphasis on the statutory pattern in the rest of § 1522. Many substantive subsections say the Director “is authorized to” provide certain programs or the Director “may” enter into grants, contracts, or cooperative agreements. From that pattern, Lee argued that Congress intended § 1522 to be a framework authorizing discretionary programs: the “shall” in § 1522(a) sets conditions or aims that should inform program design only “if” the government chooses to fund a program.

- How to analyze modal verbs in practice. When you read “shall,” ask these layered questions:
  1. What is the grammatical subject of “shall,” and is it directing an executive actor?
  2. Are there express qualifications in the same sentence—e.g., “to the extent of available appropriations,” “if necessary,” “as needed”—that plausibly limit compulsion?
  3. Does the “shall” appear in a section labeled with an “authorization” caption or surrounded by other permissive language? If so, is the “shall” best read as a condition upon discretionary action rather than a freestanding duty?
  4. Is there a clear statutory mechanism for enforcement (criminal penalties, private right of action, mandatory reports to Congress), suggesting a mandatory scheme?

  These questions track the Ninth Circuit disagreement: the majority stresses unqualified “shall” language directing programmatic outcomes; the dissent stresses the frequency of express authorizations and the header “Authorization for programs,” which suggest permissive intent.

- Canon of construction and practical tip. When modal verbs conflict within a statutory scheme, apply the whole-text canon—look at how the statute as a whole treats the actor and program. If Congress consistently uses “may” or “is authorized” elsewhere but slips a “shall” into a provision that begins, “In providing assistance under this section…,” the safer reading may be that the “shall” identifies conditions for discretionary funding rather than imposing a free-standing funding duty. But that inference is contextual, not mechanical.

## Effect of headings, structure, and cross-references on operative text

Headings, placement, and the statutory architecture are powerful interpretive tools. They can corroborate what the grammar suggests or pull the meaning in another direction.

- The role of section headings and titles. Section headings themselves are not law, but they are a permissible aid to interpretation. A caption such as “Authorization for programs for domestic resettlement of and assistance to refugees” (the title of § 1522) signals congressional intent to authorize programs. Courts treat such headings as evidence of context—useful but not dispositive. In Pacito, Judge Lee relied heavily on the title to argue that § 1522 authorizes rather than mandates programs. The majority, however, read the title as a backdrop but gave determinative weight to the operative “shall” verbs and to reporting and programmatic detail that point toward mandatory duties when funding exists.

- Structural reading: preambles, “conditions and considerations,” and operative subsections. Many statutes begin with a “conditions and considerations” clause that frames how discretionary powers shall be exercised “in providing assistance under this section.” Focus on whether such preambles are introductory—setting parameters for discretionary action—or whether they are themselves operative commands. In § 1522, the “conditions and consideration” heading is linguistically connected to the rest of the section: it can be read as “if you provide assistance under this section, you shall…,” which supports the dissent’s argument; but the majority reads some of those conditions as mandatory operational duties because they are phrased without express contingency and because they have downstream programmatic counterparts (e.g., reporting and program requirements).

- Cross-references and related provisions. Cross-references can settle ambiguity. If another statute conditions the exercise of the power on appropriations or creates enforcement mechanisms, that can indicate Congress’s design. In refugee law disputes, look to related sections for:
  • appropriations language (do other sections require Congress to appropriate funds?),
  • reporting or oversight requirements (detailed reporting requirements often suggest a mandatory statutory program), and
  • enforcement provisions (if Congress provides remedies or mandates, courts are more apt to treat duties as mandatory).

  In Pacito, the presence of § 1523’s detailed reporting requirement—an annual report to congressional committees—was one of the factors the majority used to support the conclusion that Congress expected the statute to have substantive, ongoing operation rather than mere authorization on paper.

- In practice. Map the statute. Create a table or flowchart: titles → introductory clauses → operative verbs by subsection → cross-referenced provisions (appropriations, reporting, enforcement). That map will show whether the textual ecosystem supports a mandatory or permissive reading.

## Reading qualifiers like ‘as needed’ and ‘to the extent of available appropriations’

Qualifiers are common devices that limit obligations. They are often decisive in disputes over mandatory funding. Three qualifiers appear in § 1522 and in many statutes: “to the extent of available appropriations,” “as needed,” and “if necessary.”

- “To the extent of available appropriations.” This is the archetypal appropriations qualifier. It can mean several things:
  1. Congress is specifying that a substantive duty is conditioned on the availability of appropriated funds—the duty is mandatory but only when funds are actually available.
  2. Congress is signaling that the statute is merely an authorization of programs—a permissive grant of spending authority contingent upon appropriations.
  3. Congress expects the executive to spend appropriated funds consistent with the statute—that is, once Congress actually appropriates funds for the statutory program, the executive must implement the statutory program consistent with the statutory directives.

  Courts parse these possibilities by looking at (a) whether Congress has provided a separate appropriation that unambiguously funds the statutory program and (b) whether the statute manifests an expectation of programmatic operation (detailed program structure, reporting, accountability). In Pacito, the majority noted that Congress had appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025 (albeit subject to subsequent rescission of a portion), and concluded that an appropriations condition did not render the relevant “shall” entirely toothless.

  Practically, a litigant should (i) identify the specific appropriation(s) in the annual appropriations acts that can be traced to the program, (ii) demonstrate whether funds are actually available or have been rescinded, and (iii) show how much discretion the executive has in allocating available funds among competing programs.

- “As needed” and “if necessary.” These phrases often limit a mandatory verb to circumstances in which the action is necessary. Courts interpret them by asking whether Congress intended a standard of judgment for the agency or left the existence of necessity to judicial review. If the phrase is tied to emergency or interim duties (for example, “shall assume legal responsibility…if necessary”), an agency will have some room to determine necessity, but the adequacy of that determination can be reviewed for reasonableness under the Administrative Procedure Act (APA). In Pacito, subsection (d)(2)(B)’s “if necessary” language was central to Judge Lee’s point that the Director’s duties are contingent. The majority read the “if necessary” language as framed narrowly—applying only to certain temporary duties concerning unaccompanied minors—thus not abrogating the broader “shall” obligations elsewhere.

- Practical application. When the text uses qualifiers:
  • Quantify appropriations. Show whether funds were appropriated and whether they were directed to the program at issue.
  • Define “necessity.” In litigation, press the agency to explain the factual basis for claiming that action is not “necessary” (or is necessary).
  • Beware of agency-friendly glosses. An agency’s assertion that funds are unavailable or actions unnecessary should be tested against contemporaneous internal documents and budgetary allocations; inconsistent post hoc explanations are less persuasive.

## Use of legislative history and purpose in refugee legislation

Refugee legislation has both technical elements and a clear humane policy purpose; courts sometimes read the Refugee Act of 1980 and related statutes through that purpose. Legislative history can be a tie-breaker but not a first resort.

- When legislative history helps. If textual indicators are ambiguous or conflicting, legislative history—committee reports, floor debates, contemporaneous executive branch statements—can illuminate congressional intent. In the refugee context, helpful sources include: House and Senate Judiciary Committee reports, the legislative history of the Refugee Act of 1980, and contemporaneous executive communications about how the statute should operate in practice. Courts will look for specific legislative evidence that Congress intended mandatory duties or, conversely, that Congress intended a permissive framework subject to appropriations.

- Purpose and canons. Refugee law is often read against the backdrop of statutory purpose: providing humane, orderly resettlement and preventing statelessness or harm to vulnerable persons. The Supreme Court in Negusie recognized the Refugee Act as a “general rule for the ongoing treatment of all refugees.” Purpose can cut both ways:
  • Purpose can support a mandatory interpretation if Congress’s humanitarian aim would be frustrated by a reading that allows the executive to dismantle resettlement supports without congressional approval.
  • Purpose can support a permissive interpretation if Congress intended to create a flexible program the executive could tailor to foreign policy or resource constraints.

  In Pacito, plaintiffs emphasized the Refugee Act’s remedial purpose and the historical practice of cooperative agreements to show that Congress expected an operational resettlement program. The government pointed to the statute’s appropriations language and the President’s national-security prerogatives to argue for flexibility.

- Limits of legislative history. Be cautious: courts differ in their willingness to rely on legislative history. Some judges treat it as useful but cannot overcome clear statutory language; others give it considerable weight. Use history to fill textual gaps or show contemporaneous understanding by both Congress and the executive.

## Agency interpretations post–Loper Bright: Skidmore weight and practical persuasion

How much deference does an agency’s interpretation deserve? Pacito illustrates how courts evaluate agency explanations—both prior consistent practice and sudden reversals—when deciding whether an agency has permissibly construed a statute or whether the court should enforce statutory duties.

- The deference landscape. Over recent years the Supreme Court has recalibrated judicial deference to agency interpretations. In the post–Loper Bright environment courts have increasingly scrutinized agency positions and frequently treat agency interpretations as deserving persuasive weight rather than automatic controlling deference. Whether on construction of a statute or interpretation of an agency’s own regulation, the guiding inquiry turns on the agency’s expertise, the consistency and contemporaneity of its interpretation, the thoroughness of its reasoning, and the practical consequences of deferring.

- Skidmore factors (practical persuasion). Even where Chevron-style deference is reduced or confined, courts often apply Skidmore v. Swift: an agency’s interpretation may be persuasive depending on its:
  1. Thoroughness of explanation,
  2. Consistency over time,
  3. Formulation process (formal adjudication and notice-and-comment rulemaking weigh more heavily),
  4. Expertise of the agency, and
  5. Fit with statutory design and practical consequences.

  In Pacito the government had a recent, abrupt policy and practice change: termination of longstanding cooperative agreements and suspension of funding. At argument the government could not point to a developed plan for replacing those arrangements. These facts weakened the persuasive force of the government’s present-day assertions that § 1522 is merely permissive: the agency had not furnished a reasoned, contemporaneous explanation tying its actions to statutory aims or budgetary constraints. The majority relied on that absence to conclude the agency had not demonstrated the statutory discretion it claimed; it therefore reinstated agreements to effectuate statutory obligations. Judge Lee, by contrast, found the statutory text and headings sufficient to justify a permissive reading and thought courts should not police policy choices of the executive in a field where the President enjoys constitutional authority.

- Contemporaneity and reversals. A sudden reversal in agency practice without reasoned explanation diminishes the persuasive weight of the new interpretation. Conversely, a long-standing, consistent agency practice that aligns with a permissive reading of the statute can be highly persuasive, especially where the practice was known to and relied upon by Congress.

- Practical litigation posture. When representing plaintiffs challenging an agency roll-back:
  • Emphasize the lack of reasoned decisionmaking and contemporaneous record support for the agency’s position.
  • Present historical practice and program structure to show the agency previously treated the statute as mandatory in effect.
  • Use Skidmore factors to argue that the agency’s new interpretation is unpersuasive.

  When representing an agency defending change:
  • Produce contemporaneous evidence explaining the change (budget analyses, safety assessments, foreign policy memoranda).
  • Tie the reinterpretation to statutory purposes and appropriations constraints; explain why a permissive reading better fits the statute as a whole.
  • Show that practical necessities (national security, diplomacy, emergency funding changes) compelled the action.

## Applying the tools to § 1522 (how the arguments line up)

It is instructive to set out the competing arguments you would make on behalf of each side in Pacito, applying the interpretive tools above.

- Plaintiffs’ textualist (majority) approach. Start with the “shall” verbs in § 1522(a), read without an across-the-board contingency: “The Director shall make available sufficient resources…shall provide refugees with the opportunity to acquire…shall insure that cash assistance is made available…shall insure that women have the same opportunities as men….” Those are programmatic commands. Cross-references buttress the conclusion: § 1522(b) authorizes funding instruments but does not negate obligations; § 1523 requires detailed reporting to Congress on activities under the subchapter—Congress would not impose such accountability if the programs were purely optional. Congress appropriated funds for migration and refugee assistance in FY 2025, so the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualifier does not render the duties hollow. Historical practice—the long-standing cooperative agreements—shows that the statute was implemented as an ongoing program. The government’s abrupt termination, lacking a contemporaneous plan, fails Skidmore factors: the agency’s new interpretation is recent, unexplained, and inconsistent with prior practice.

- Government’s permissive (dissent) approach. Emphasize the statutory title (“Authorization for programs”) and repeated use elsewhere of “is authorized to” and “may” language. Read § 1522(a) as an expression of “conditions and consideration” that apply “in providing assistance under this section”: these are normative constraints for program design, not unconditional mandates. The “to the extent of available appropriations” phrase makes clear that Congress exercised budget control and intended the executive to act when funds are appropriated, not to be forced to create programs. Subsections with “as needed” and “if necessary” language confirm that Congress expected the executive to make discretionary judgments about the scope of assistance. Finally, where a statute implicates the Executive’s foreign policy and border-control prerogatives, courts should refrain from second-guessing policy choices.

- How a court resolves the dispute. The court must weigh text, structure, appropriations history, programmatic features (like reporting), and agency practice and explanations. Given ambiguity, a court will give weight to practical consequences—can the agency accomplish the statutory purposes while suspending the contracts? Can Congress be seen as having intended the Executive to have such unilateral power? The outcome often depends on how persuasive the evidence of congressional intent and contemporaneous agency practice is.

## Practical checklist for litigators and counselors

When you confront a dispute like Pacito, use a disciplined checklist:

1. Textual inventory
   - Identify every instance of “shall,” “may,” “is authorized,” “as needed,” “to the extent of available appropriations,” “if necessary.”
   - Parse who the grammatical subject is and whether the verb is hortatory, conditional, or operative.

2. Structural mapping
   - Note the section title and the header language (e.g., “Conditions and consideration”).
   - Cross-reference related provisions (reporting, appropriations, enforcement).
   - Map programmatic architecture: funding instruments, grant/contract authority, and oversight.

3. Appropriations audit
   - Identify specific appropriations bills and text; determine whether funds were appropriated and if rescissions or reprogramming applied.
   - Prepare a budget timeline to show whether the program had funds when the agency acted.

4. Historical practice and contemporaneous evidence
   - Compile the agency’s historical practice and the existence of longstanding cooperative agreements.
   - Collect contemporaneous internal memos explaining the policy change; absence of such documents is strategically important for plaintiffs.

5. Legislative history and purpose
   - Pull committee reports, floor statements, and floor amendments from the Refugee Act and subsequent amendments.
   - Frame the statutory purpose both as a humanitarian aim and as a policy balancing exercise with foreign policy/defense concerns.

6. Agency interpretive posture
   - If defending the agency, present a well-reasoned explanation—use formal rulemaking or adjudication if possible; otherwise, produce detailed contemporaneous justification.
   - If attacking the agency, highlight inconsistency, lack of reasoned explanation, lack of planning, and reliance on late-breaking policy rationales.

7. Remedies strategy
   - For plaintiffs: build record evidence of irreparable harms to refugees and resettlement organizations; connect textual obligations to specific, remediable programmatic failures; request narrow, tailored injunctive relief.
   - For defendants: show public-interest harms of judicial micromanagement in national-security-sensitive fields and present feasible alternatives to specific injunctive relief.

Conclusion

Pacito is a modern exercise in statutory interpretation where modal verbs, headings, qualifiers, legislative purpose, and the agency’s interpretive posture intersect. There is no single canonical algorithm; instead, careful statutory mapping, a rigorous appropriations audit, and a persuasive account of agency practice are decisive. Master these techniques, and you will be able to litigate or counsel effectively whenever Congress uses garden-variety statutory language—“shall,” “may,” “is authorized”—around sensitive, resource-constrained programs like refugee resettlement.

# Policy, Administration, and Practical Consequences

The Ninth Circuit’s stay order in Pacito v. Trump provides a vivid illustration of how legal disputes over immigration and refugee policy translate into acute administrative and humanitarian dilemmas. The opinion confronts two paired but distinct legal questions: the President’s broad authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f) to suspend classes of aliens from entry, and the statutory architecture that funds and directs domestic resettlement services under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The court’s treatment — treating the admission moratorium as presumptively within executive power while simultaneously concluding that the government may not be free to abandon post‑admission resettlement obligations — produces an operational problem that agencies must plan for and courts must manage: how to suspend admissions without creating a dereliction of care for refugees already in the United States or conditionally admitted abroad.

This chapter examines the operational and humanitarian effects of a pause in admissions coupled with continuing statutory duties, and the capacity challenges agencies face when law and policy diverge. The material is doctrinal and practical. It uses the Pacito litigation to show how statutory text, judicial deference, injunctive relief, and appropriations law converge to shape administrative choices. It also develops governance principles and concrete risk‑management options that agencies and their local partners commonly confront when admissions slow or stop.

## Maintaining a resettlement infrastructure during admissions pauses

A legal suspension of admissions — even if lawfully executed under § 1182(f) — does not automatically extinguish the statutory or contractual frameworks that enable refugees’ domestic reception and placement. Section 1522 contemplates a federal resettlement infrastructure: competitive or cooperative awards to public and private nonprofit agencies, case management systems, employment and language programs, and state and local coordination. When a President suspends refugee admissions, the pipeline of new arrivals may be interrupted, but the organizational and legal scaffolding that receives, places, and supports refugees remains for those already admitted or conditionally approved. That was the crux of the district court’s injunction and the panel’s partial reversal: the government cannot assert a policy choice to cease admissions while simultaneously asserting that it has no responsibility to provide statutorily described reception and placement services for refugees who are already in the United States or lawfully arriving.

Administratively, maintaining a basic resettlement infrastructure during a pause in admissions requires explicit decisions about scope and funding. Contracts, cooperative agreements, and grants that funded reception and placement (R&P) typically include performance periods, deliverables, and billing mechanisms tied to caseloads. If admissions stop, caseloads fall, and cooperative partners face imminent fiscal shortfalls. Unless the agency has authority to reprogram funds, use carryover balances, or draw on supplemental appropriations or other sources, the agency may be forced to terminate agreements. But the legal consequences of termination depend on whether the underlying statutory regime imposes mandatory duties. If a court — as the Pacito panel suggested — interprets § 1522 as imposing nondiscretionary obligations for certain cohorts (for example, refugees admitted in the period immediately before the suspension), then termination can produce litigation risk and injunctive relief forcing reinstatement. Agencies must therefore weigh the legal risk of termination against authorization-based arguments and practical fiscal constraints.

Operationally, the prudent administrative response is to preserve a minimum, scalable infrastructure — a skeleton of staff, data systems, and cooperative relationship — that can continue to deliver essential functions (case tracking, emergency cash and medical assistance, housing placement pipeline management, and coordination with states and local service providers) while minimizing costs. That approach obliges agencies to design contracting instruments with contingency provisions: bridge agreements or short-term extensions, termination-for-convenience clauses with phased wind‑down, and options for reduced scope of work that permit core services to continue even when new arrivals are paused.

## Continuity of care for admitted refugees: housing, employment, language services

Continuity of care is the human heart of resettlement law. Section 1522 enumerates a bundle of services — employment training and placement, English language training, cash and medical assistance for refugees in their first 36 months, and special care for unaccompanied minors — that are designed to promote early economic self‑sufficiency and protect vulnerable populations. These services are not merely bureaucratic outputs; they are time‑sensitive interventions that, if interrupted, can produce harm to refugees’ housing stability, health, and labor market integration.

From a programmatic perspective, certain services must be delivered on schedule. Employment and language training are most effective when offered soon after arrival; delays decrease labor market outcomes and slow the path to self‑sufficiency. Temporary housing and initial cash assistance are essential to preventing homelessness or medical crisis during the critical first weeks and months. Unaccompanied minors require immediate placement and protective care, the legal contours of which Congress recognized in § 1522(d)(2)(B). The statutory text and regulatory framework therefore create an implicit temporal structure: early interventions are qualitatively different from long‑term integrations and cannot simply be deferred until admissions resume.

When an agency contemplates pausing admissions, it must therefore triage services. One institutionally sensible principle is to prioritize continuity of time‑dependent services for refugees already admitted or imminently arriving under pre‑existing approvals. That principle matches the district court’s injunction in Pacito and the panel’s directive that cooperative agreements “necessary to provide the reception and placement services described in § 1522 to refugees who have been admitted to the United States” be reinstated. Even where appropriations are constrained, agencies can seek to prioritize expenditures within the statutory “to the extent of available appropriations” qualification by documenting the compelling needs of admitted refugees, notifying appropriations committees, and requesting emergency supplemental funds or permissive reprogramming.

Ensuring continuity also requires coordination with local service providers and state and municipal agencies. Housing authorities, public health clinics, school systems, and workforce partners often serve as the frontline of refugee integration. Agencies should put in place memoranda of understanding and communication protocols to preserve those local relationships — a task that is administratively simple but politically and legally consequential when federal funding is cut. Failure to maintain these links can cause gaps in services that are difficult and costly to remediate.

## Risk management for agencies: termination vs. bridge mechanisms

Agencies faced with a policy decision to halt admissions must confront immediate legal and contractual risks. The choice to terminate cooperative agreements or to keep them in force through reduced or bridge funding carries different legal exposures and administrative costs.

Termination raises contract law and administrative law risks. Terminating cooperative agreements without observing statutory constraints and without adequate justification may expose the government to claims under the APA (arbitrary and capricious action), breach of contract litigation, and injunctive relief restoring funding or compelling performance. The Pacito panel relied on APA principles to order reinstatement of cooperative agreements for admitted refugees — an example of how courts can use equitable relief to protect vested statutory expectations and reliance interests. Agencies confronted with such risk must carefully document the legal basis for termination, including budgetary necessity, alternative means of compliance, and consultative steps taken with grantees, and consider the equitable factors courts apply when evaluating preliminary injunctions and stays (likelihood of success on the merits; irreparable harm; balance of equities; public interest), as articulated in Nken v. Holder and applied in the Pacito stay.

Bridge mechanisms offer an alternative risk management path. Bridge agreements, short‑term extensions, and phased reallocation clauses allow agencies to preserve continuity while managing costs. From a contracting perspective, agencies should build contingency clauses into RFPs and cooperative agreements that permit temporary scope reductions, phased staffing, or the suspension of intake activities while preserving essential services. Where existing contracts lack these clauses, agencies should consider executing short-term modification agreements, using available contract administrative tools to avoid abrupt cessation. Bridge mechanisms also reduce political fallout and protect vulnerable populations during policy transitions.

A further risk-management tool is clear recordkeeping and interbranch communication. If an agency intends to curtail services due to policy choices, it should build an evidentiary record demonstrating how appropriations constraints, programmatic priorities, and statutory authorities were considered and why certain services can or cannot be maintained. Such documentation is not merely good governance; it can determine whether a court views the agency’s action as a permissible exercise of discretion or an arbitrary revocation of statutory obligations.

## Intergovernmental impacts: state and local partners, private sponsors, and NGOs

The resettlement system is not purely federal. Its success depends on an ecosystem of state and local governments, nonprofit resettlement agencies, volunteer networks, and private sponsors. A suspension of admissions and any associated federal defunding therefore has ripple effects that extend well beyond the Department of State or HHS headquarters.

Local resettlement agencies typically operate on thin margins. Their budgets commonly rely on a mix of federal cooperative agreement dollars, private donations, and in‑kind contributions. When federal funding is withdrawn, NGOs may respond with short‑term fundraising, volunteer mobilization, or redeployment of staff. But these are stopgap measures: sustained resettlement operations require predictable funding. The forced termination of cooperative agreements thus threatens organizational viability, staff retention, and institutional knowledge. In Pacito, the organizational plaintiffs were precisely such agencies, and the district court’s injunction recognized that terminating their funding would jeopardize the services they provide to admitted refugees.

State and local governments also feel the impact. Housing authorities, school districts, and local health providers carry the immediate cost of service provision when federal programs lapse. Although some states are sympathetic to continued resettlement and may adopt complementary programs, many lack the fiscal capacity to absorb large numbers of arrivals in the face of federal retrenchment. Private sponsorship models can offset federal reduction in some instances, but they too are limited by volunteers’ bandwidth and by legal restrictions on what private sponsors may be expected to deliver.

The interdependency of actors suggests two administrative solutions. First, federal agencies should consult and coordinate with state and local partners before implementing admissions pauses or program terminations. Consultation reduces surprises and allows for contingency planning at the local level. Second, agencies should explore temporary cost‑sharing arrangements or pass‑through grants to local partners to ensure continuity of critical services — a legally and politically feasible mechanism when federal appropriations permit such flexibility.

## Budgetary planning amidst rescissions and shifting executive priorities

Appropriations law is central to understanding how far agencies may go in sustaining or suspending services. Section 1522 repeatedly uses the phrase “to the extent of available appropriations,” signaling that Congress’s appropriation choices constrain executive action. But that qualification is not a blank check for unilateral defunding. Agencies must grapple with the mechanics of budget availability, rescissions, and anti‑deficiency limitations.

Pacito’s factual matrix included specific appropriations context: for FY 2025 Congress appropriated $3.178 billion for migration and refugee assistance, subsequently reduced by $800 million in a rescissions statute. The availability of funds, timing of rescissions, and the agency’s internal budgeting decisions determine whether contracts can lawfully be funded. Agencies must therefore maintain a clear nexus between statutory obligations, appropriations law, and contract obligations. Where appropriations are inadequate, agencies should provide Congress with timely notice and exercise available administrative flexibilities (e.g., carryover of unobligated balances, reprogramming within statutory limits, or requesting emergency supplemental appropriations).

From a risk perspective, invoking appropriations constraints as a basis for termination requires clear documentary support. The anti‑deficiency statute limits obligations in the absence of appropriations; nonetheless, a bare assertion that funding is insufficient will be insufficient if unobligated balances or reprogramming authority exists. Moreover, courts often expect agencies to use available statutory mechanisms to mitigate harm to beneficiaries before resorting to contract terminations.

Budget planning should therefore anticipate policy shifts. Agencies that operate in politically volatile domains ought to maintain contingency reserves, negotiate flexible contracts with NGOs (including options for reduced scope), and build triggers for ramp‑down and ramp‑up that minimize service interruptions. Budget officials should also coordinate closely with program leaders to ensure that legal interpretations of statutory duties (such as whether § 1522 is mandatory or permissive) are reconciled with fiscal strategy and with ongoing communications to grantees and appropriations committees.

## Applying the Pacito dispute: practical governance lessons and legal tests

Pacito emphasizes several doctrinal touchstones with immediate governance consequences. First, the court’s invocation of Trump v. Hawaii shows how expansive § 1182(f) is: courts give substantial deference to presidential findings that entry of a class of aliens is detrimental to national interests. For agencies, this means that pauses in admissions grounded in § 1182(f) will seldom be overturned on the merits. But that executive latitude does not automatically excuse neglect of statutory duties to persons already admitted.

Second, the debate over statutory interpretation of § 1522 — whether it creates mandatory duties or is merely an authorization for discretionary programming — is pivotal. The majority’s reading, which the Pacito panel applied in ordering cooperative agreements reinstated for admitted refugees, treats at least portions of § 1522 as imposing non‑discretionary duties to ensure the immediate care and assistance of refugees already in the United States. Judge Lee’s concurrence/dissent highlights the counterargument: the statute’s title and many provisions use the term “authorize,” suggesting permissive rather than mandatory obligations. For administrators, this split in judicial interpretation signals litigation risk and suggests prudence: absent a clear statutory reading in one’s favor, agencies should not assume unfettered discretion to defund or cease services for admitted refugees without exposing themselves to injunctive relief.

Third, equitable remedies play a major role. The district court’s preliminary injunctions and the Ninth Circuit’s directed reinstatement of some cooperative agreements show that courts can and will tailor relief to preserve statutory expectations and to prevent irreparable harm, even while respecting executive prerogatives on admissions. The four Nken factors — with particular emphasis on likelihood of success and irreparable injury — will remain the lodestar in stay and injunction settings. Agencies should therefore assess not just the legal strength of their position, but the practical likelihood that their actions will cause irreparable harm to vulnerable populations, which can tip the balance toward court‑ordered relief.

Operationally, this jurisprudence suggests a conservative administrative posture when admitting refugees is paused: preserve essential services for those already in the system; create bridge agreements and phased termination protocols; document appropriation constraints and interbranch communications; and coordinate with state, local, and nonprofit partners to ensure continuity of critical housing, medical, and child protection services.

## Conclusion: reconciling legal authority and administrative duty

The Pacito litigation demonstrates a central paradox of refugee governance. The President’s power to curtail entry under § 1182(f) is broad and receives judicial deference, yet the federal government simultaneously stands behind a statutory commitment — reflected in § 1522 and associated regulations — to provide immediate reception and placement services to recently admitted refugees. When admission policy and statutory care obligations diverge, agencies and courts become arbiters of how to allocate scarcity without abdicating legal and humanitarian responsibilities.

For administrators, the principal lesson is one of anticipatory governance: design contracts and budgets with contingencies; prioritize time‑sensitive services for admitted refugees; document and communicate appropriation limitations to Congress; and build operational flexibility to scale services down and back up without creating gaps that courts will seek to cure. For lawyers and judges, Pacito reaffirms that broad executive authority on entry does not erase statutory commitments to persons already within the United States or lawfully arriving, and that equitable remedies remain potent tools for protecting vulnerable populations when policy changes endanger continuity of care.

# Pacito’s Place in the Doctrinal Landscape and Future Directions

The Ninth Circuit’s interim order in Pacito v. Trump crystallizes a key doctrinal posture that the federal courts are likely to take in the next phase of litigation over presidential immigration policymaking: robust deference to a President’s statutory authority to suspend entries under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), coupled with a willingness to enforce statutory obligations that Congress has placed on the executive for those already admitted to the United States. Pacito thus functions as a synthesis: it reaffirms the high degree of judicial deference described in Trump v. Hawaii when the President finds entry would be “detrimental to the interests of the United States,” but it also draws a line at the executive’s ability to defeat otherwise mandatory statutory duties—here, the reception-and-placement obligations in 8 U.S.C. § 1522—by terminating cooperative agreements and cutting off services for refugees already admitted. This chapter unpacks Pacito’s doctrinal holdings, the tests the court applied, and the practical and doctrinal consequences for future litigation, executive action, and legislation.

## Reaffirmation of Hawaii’s deference on § 1182(f) entry suspensions

Pacito begins where modern § 1182(f) jurisprudence now resides: with Trump v. Hawaii’s articulation of a heavily deferential standard. In Trump, the Supreme Court explained that § 1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President, furnishing “ample power” to suspend entries in addition to the otherwise enumerated INA provisions. Pacito’s motions panel invoked the same language and applied the same logical constraint: the “sole prerequisite” under § 1182(f) is a presidential finding that entry of a class of aliens “would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” Once the President makes that finding in an executive order, Pacito treats the court’s role as circumscribed; it stated that courts cannot undertake “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” That language signals continued judicial reluctance to substitute judicial assessments for presidential judgments about national security, foreign affairs, and related immigration concerns.

Two points about the nature of that deference are worth emphasizing for doctrinal application. First, the deferential posture is statutory—grounded in the text and structure of § 1182(f)—not an abdication of judicial review. Courts will still confirm the existence of a formal presidential “finding” and whether the authority invoked is textually available. But the inquiry does not typically extend to searching review of the empirical or policy premises underlying the finding. Second, deference is contextual: it is strongest where the President purports to act pursuant to a clear, textually granted statutory power to regulate entry, and where national security and foreign-relations considerations are implicated. By contrast, Pacito shows that deference will not permit the executive to fold otherwise applicable domestic statutory obligations into the presidential decision and thereby nullify them.

In practice, therefore, future challenges to presidential entry suspensions under § 1182(f) face a steep uphill path. Litigants should anticipate that courts will limit judicial review to whether the President invoked a statutory mechanism correctly, whether the measure is reasonably within the scope of that mechanism’s textual grant, and whether any nonjusticiable political-question barriers apply. Proof that the President’s paper finding was merely a pretext, or that the order was adopted without any factual tether at all, will be the difficult but necessary route to more searching review. Conversely, litigation that focuses on collateral executive actions (funding cuts, agency terminations, programmatic changes) rather than the core admission determination is more likely to survive the deference wall.

## Boundary-setting on executive defunding when statutes impose service obligations

Pacito’s most significant doctrinal contribution is the boundary it draws between the President’s power to suspend entries and the executive’s statutory obligations toward refugees already in the United States. The panel recognized that Congress, through 8 U.S.C. § 1522 and related provisions, created a statutory regime for “reception and placement” services: employment and language training, cash and medical assistance, and special care duties for unaccompanied children. The court concluded—at least for the preliminary posture and for purposes of a stay pending appeal—that the government was not likely to prevail on the plaintiffs’ APA claim that the executive’s termination of cooperative agreements unlawfully frustrated those statutory duties. Accordingly, the court ordered the reinstatement of those agreements necessary to provide services to refugees who had been admitted.

Two doctrinal threads explain why the court reached this result, and both have wider application.

First, statutory interpretation. Where Congress uses mandatory language—“shall”—and pairs it with programmatic detail (what services must be provided, by whom, and in what form), courts are predisposed to read the text as imposing substantive duties on the executive. Pacito emphasized the specific obligations in § 1522 (employment training and placement, English language instruction, cash assistance administered to promote self-sufficiency, parity for women, obligations toward unaccompanied minors, and projects for cash and medical assistance). Although § 1522 contains phrases like “to the extent of available appropriations,” the majority reasoned that those qualifications modulate the intensity of obligations but do not automatically convert substantive mandates into mere authorizations. The practical consequence is important: statutes that prescribe programs, beneficiaries, and administrative responsibilities can create judicially enforceable duties even when funding is subject to appropriations.

Second, the court treated the executive’s programmatic contractions as reviewable agency action under the Administrative Procedure Act. The termination of cooperative agreements was not dismissed as a purely political decision insulated from review; instead, it was treated as a concrete change in the executive’s administration of a statutorily authorized program that could frustrate statutory obligations. That approach brings into play the spectrum of APA doctrines—failure to follow required procedures, arbitrary-and-capricious review of abrupt policy shifts, and enforcement of mandatory statutory duties—and permits courts to enjoin agency action that undermines clear statutory aims, especially where vulnerable populations are involved.

For lawyers and judges, Pacito therefore sets out a dividing line: pre-entry policy (suspension of admissions) will normally be judged under the Arabia of deference owed to presidential findings under § 1182(f); post-entry program administration that implicates specific statutory obligations will be judged under ordinary statutory-interpretation and administrative-law principles. That syntactic divide is consequential when counsel frames claims: pleading and proof strategies that highlight concrete statutory commands and their frustrated implementation (rather than abstract policy disagreements about who may enter) will be more successful at enlisting judicial review.

## Strategic implications for plaintiffs and the government in crafting records and remedies

Pacito is a roadmap for litigation strategy on both sides. Plaintiffs who hope to enjoin executive actions should differentiate pre-entry claims from post-entry operational claims and structure their records accordingly.

For plaintiffs:
- Focus on statutory duties and the administrative record. Pacito shows that a detailed record demonstrating how agency actions (terminations of cooperative agreements, defunding, cancellation of support services) interfere with statutory mandates is powerful. Collect and present documentary evidence of terminated agreements, communications showing abrupt termination without transition, evidence that alternative providers are not ready, and affidavits showing concrete harms to admitted refugees.
- Emphasize discrete, enforceable statutory commands. Claims that the executive has violated specific provisions that use mandatory language (“shall”) and identify duties or beneficiaries are more judicially cognizable than broad policy complaints. Seek to prove both the textual basis and the practical consequences of noncompliance.
- Seek narrow, program-specific equitable relief. Pacito’s remedy—to direct reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception-and-placement services for admitted refugees—illustrates the power of carefully tailored injunctive relief that respects the broader policy prerogatives of the political branches while protecting statutory rights of those already within the United States.
- Preserve exigent evidence of irreparable harm. Even where courts give substantial weight to presidential findings on entry, they still weigh irreparable injury in stay and preliminary-injunction analyses. For admitted refugees, personal affidavits describing lost services, delay in medical care, or loss of benefits will help establish the kind of irreparable injury that many courts find persuasive.

For the government:
- Build a robust administrative record if invoking § 1182(f). Although Pacito reiterates deference, it also underscores that courts will treat a presidential finding as formalistic if the record is empty. Documentation of the predicate facts, internal interagency assessments, and reasoned explanation for decisions will make it harder for courts to find the President’s finding a mere pretext.
- Avoid abrupt terminations that leave statutory duties unfulfilled. The court in Pacito reacted to the government’s sudden termination of cooperative agreements and expressed concern that no ready replacement existed. Even where the President may lawfully suspend admissions, the executive should preserve continuity of services for admitted persons unless a reasoned plan exists. Maintaining or phasing out agreements with notice and transition plans reduces the risk of judicial intervention on APA grounds.
- Use clear appropriation and policy-based explanations where funding changes. Where the government relies on the qualification “to the extent of available appropriations,” it should document budgetary constraints, identify what services can be funded, and delineate the statutory consequences of any funding shortfalls. An opaque or ad-hoc claim that funds are unavailable will be vulnerable in court.
- Consider narrowly tailored remedies and settlements. Where judicial intervention is likely, agencies can negotiate court-supervised transition plans for service provision that meet statutory minima. Such negotiated remedies reduce litigation risk and permit the executive to pursue its broader policy goals while meeting statutory obligations.

Remedially, Pacito shows courts will not hesitate to craft targeted equitable orders—reinstatement of cooperative agreements, specific program directives—so long as the remedies are tailored to statutory duties and do not attempt to micromanage policy decisions that fall squarely within presidential authority.

## Potential Supreme Court review and circuit splits to watch

Pacito is poised to invite further appellate and possibly Supreme Court attention because its core holdings push against two longstanding tensions: executive flexibility in immigration policymaking and judicial enforcement of statutory duties toward noncitizens already present in the United States. Several doctrinal issues are likely to generate circuit splits and ultimately to attract certiorari.

Key questions likely to generate division:

1. The scope of judicial review of § 1182(f) determinations. Some judges and circuits may read Trump v. Hawaii as effectively insulating § 1182(f) presidential findings from any meaningful review; others may insist on a minimal but substantive record of factual basis for such findings. Pacito falls in the latter camp on procedural sufficiency (requiring an adequate administrative record in practice) while endorsing deference to the presidential finding’s substance. Circuits that reject any meaningful review could conflict with Pacito.

2. Whether § 1522 creates enforceable, mandatory duties. Judge Lee’s partial dissent in Pacito takes the opposite view: § 1522 and its programmatic provisions are authorizations, not compulsions. That interpretive split—mandatory statute versus permissive authorization—presents a pure statutory-interpretation conflict that different circuits could resolve differently, especially given the “to the extent of available appropriations” qualification. A split over the mandatory/authorization point would be a natural vehicle for Supreme Court review because it implicates separation of powers (what duties Congress can impose on the executive) and the role of courts in enforcing statutory programs.

3. The reach of APA review when the executive changes programmatic operations tied to immigration policy. Some circuits may hold that core immigration decisions are nonreviewable political questions; others may treat programmatic changes that affect individuals inside the United States as ordinary agency actions subject to APA review. A circuit split over justiciability and the proper scope of APA review in immigration-adjacent program changes would be significant.

4. The role of appropriations language in converting statutory commands into discretionary duties. If circuits disagree about how to interpret “to the extent of available appropriations,” the Supreme Court may be asked whether that language unambiguously makes statutory duties contingent on appropriations or whether it simply modulates the extent of performance but leaves the duty intact.

If the Supreme Court takes up any of these questions, expect briefing and argument to turn on text-and-structure arguments, the nature of agency discretion, and the special role of the political branches in national-security and foreign-affairs domains. The Court may be especially interested in carving out the “admission vs. post-admission” distinction: even if the executive has great latitude in deciding who the United States admits, does that latitude extend to refusing to perform statutory duties for those already present? Pacito suggests the answer is “no,” a proposition that could produce doctrinal clarification at the highest level.

## Legislative options: clarifying § 1522’s mandatory scope and funding mechanisms

Pacito also highlights the levers that Congress possesses if it wishes to reduce litigation and provide clearer guidance to the executive. Two broad legislative approaches are available: clarification of substance (what services must be provided and to whom) and clarification of funding (how those services are to be financed and when the executive may stop paying).

Substantive clarifications:
- Make statutory duties explicit and categorical where Congress wishes them enforced. Rewriting key provisions in § 1522 to say “The Director shall provide [specified services] to refugees admitted to the United States” without caveats would reduce ambiguity and strengthen judicial enforcement.
- Define the trigger events and covered populations. Congress can specify whether obligations run to refugees conditionally approved abroad, refugees physically present in the United States, unaccompanied minors in transit, or other defined classes. Clear definitions will preclude disputes over standing and temporal scope.
- Require minimum continuity plans for changes. Mandating that, before program terminations, agencies must publish a transition plan that preserves services for a set period or until substitute providers are in place would prevent abrupt service cessations like those that prompted Pacito.

Funding and appropriations clarity:
- Create mandatory appropriation lines or trust funds. Instead of authorizing programs “to the extent of available appropriations,” Congress can appropriate dedicated funds for reception-and-placement services, which would reduce the executive’s ability to avoid program obligations through budgetary maneuvers. Such mandatory funding raises constitutional and political considerations (Congressional control of the purse), but it is the most direct means to ensure program continuity.
- Enact emergency contingency funds. A standby appropriation could be used to meet statutory obligations during short periods when other funds are withdrawn for policy reasons, thereby preventing immediate service collapses.
- Require notice to appropriations committees. If the executive wants to reallocate or rescind funding for refugee programs, it must provide advance notice and a justification to the Committees on Appropriations, together with a plan to avoid interruption of statutorily mandated services. This would enhance congressional oversight and create administrative friction to deter abrupt cuts.

Congress may also consider more surgical solutions: procedural requirements (notice-and-comment before terminating cooperative agreements), reporting obligations with specific metrics, and criminal or civil penalties for agencies that violate statutorily defined duties (the last is politically charged and constitutionally fraught, but it is a legislative lever).

By clarifying both the substantive duties and the funding mechanisms, Congress can reduce the kinds of statutory-interpretation disputes Pacito exposed and provide agencies with clearer guidance on how to reconcile competing policy objectives.

## Practical litigation and doctrinal developments to watch

Looking forward, several doctrinal ripples from Pacito merit close attention.

1. The admission/post-admission divide will be litigated repeatedly. Future plaintiffs will press APA and statutory claims that focus on concrete harms to persons already admitted. Defendants will continue to assert broad § 1182(f) authority. How courts define the line between admission policy and program administration will shape outcomes.

2. The role of “to the extent of available appropriations” will generate doctrinal work. Courts will have to develop standards for evaluating executive invocation of funding constraints—what proof is necessary to sustain a claim that funds are genuinely unavailable, and how should courts assess agency efforts to reallocate monies?

3. Procedural prerequisites will be litigated. Where programmatic changes are effected through administrative mechanisms (terminations of cooperative agreements, cancellation of grants, or revisions of agency guidance), courts will examine whether notice-and-comment or other procedures were required and whether the agency’s action was arbitrary and capricious given reliance interests and abruptness.

4. Standing, mootness, and scope-of-relief litigation will follow. Pacito’s carveouts (protecting certain classes of refugees and services) suggest that careful record-development about who is affected and how services have been interrupted will be decisive in shaping both relief and appellate outcomes.

5. The intersection with constitutional doctrine remains alive but secondary. Pacito resolved disputes primarily through statutory and administrative law tools, but litigants may continue to press Due Process or other constitutional claims (especially where the termination of services results in particularly severe harms). The courts’ willingness to entertain such claims may vary across circuits.

## Conclusion

Pacito is best read as a pragmatic reconciliation of two familiar but competing imperatives. On the one hand, it reaffirms the deference long accorded to presidential determinations about entry under § 1182(f). On the other hand, it asserts that the executive cannot use that deferential cloak to nullify detailed statutory obligations Congress placed on the government to protect and assist refugees already present in the United States. For practitioners, Pacito clarifies that litigation should separate the entry decision (where judicial relief will be difficult) from programmatic implementation (where courts will enforce clear statutory duties). For policymakers, Pacito points toward legislative fixes that can reduce future litigation by clarifying statutory commands and funding structures. And for the judiciary, Pacito invites a finer-grained approach to immigration-adjacent disputes: give space for the political branches on who may enter, but protect statutory rights and administrative accountability once people are within the United States’ borders.

# Problems and Hypotheticals

This chapter uses the Ninth Circuit’s interim Pacito opinion to frame practical problems that test your ability to bring together statutory interpretation, Administrative Procedure Act reviewability and standards, and remedial design. Each hypothetical presents a realistic factual variation that implicates the same core doctrinal tensions the Pacito court confronted: the breadth of the President’s authority under 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f); the content and enforceability of Congress’s statutory directions for refugee resettlement in 8 U.S.C. § 1522; and the scope of judicial supervision of executive action that terminates cooperative agreements and grants. For each fact pattern below, work through the legal issues the court must address, identify the relevant rules and tests, sketch how the parties will argue, and describe plausible judicial dispositions and remedial contours consistent with Pacito.

## Hypo 1 — Agency pauses all § 1522 grants citing “as needed” — is there law to apply?

Fact pattern. The Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) issues a one‑paragraph memo: “Effective today HHS will pause all discretionary grants and cooperative agreements made under 8 U.S.C. § 1522. The Department interprets the statutory phrases ‘as needed’ and ‘to the extent of available appropriations’ to permit a complete, temporary pause.” The pause halts cash assistance, English language training, and other reception‑and‑placement services to thousands of refugees already admitted to the United States. Several resettlement organizations and recently admitted refugees sue under the APA and § 706(1) and (2), seeking a declaration that the memo is unlawful and an injunction requiring the agency to resume grant payments.

Step 1 — Identify the statutory question: mandate or authorization? The first and most important move is statutory interpretation. Pacito turned on competing readings of § 1522: the majority treated § 1522 as imposing binding duties that the executive branch must satisfy for refugees already admitted; Judge Lee read § 1522 principally as an authorization—i.e., permission to establish programs when appropriations are available. In this hypo the tension is the same. Your analysis should begin with the text of § 1522: notice the repeated use of “shall” in § 1522(a) together with qualifying language “to the extent of available appropriations,” and the use of “authorized” in subparts that describe specific programs. Context matters: compare the section title (“Authorization for programs…”) and the operative provisions. Can the “shall” in § 1522(a) be read as a condition on discretionary programs (mandatory instructions about how to run programs if they are funded), or does it create independent, enforceable duties regardless of whether Congress or the executive has chosen to fund and run those programs?

Step 2 — Consider the Canon of Construction and Contextual Signals. Courts will apply ordinary tools of interpretation: canons concerning titles and section headings (which are helpful but not dispositive), the significance of “to the extent of available appropriations,” and the statutory scheme as a whole. Pacito’s majority found that certain obligations in § 1522—for example, care for unaccompanied minors—create legally enforceable duties. In your memo you should explain why a court might treat some subsections as mandatory (where the text is precise and the underlying program addresses vulnerable persons already on U.S. soil) and others as discretionary (program design and outreach overseas).

Step 3 — Is there final agency action subject to APA review? If HHS simply issues a general policy memo pausing grants, plaintiffs must show that this memo constitutes “final agency action” under Bennett v. Spear: the action must mark the consummation of the agency’s decisionmaking and determine rights or obligations or create legal consequences. A categorical pause that cuts off funding and is immediately operative likely will be treated as final—it causes concrete harm (lost services) and is not merely tentative. Pacito treated termination of cooperative agreements as a decisive step with legal consequences and ordered reinstatement in part because of the statutory provisions.

Step 4 — APA Standards. If the agency action is final, the court will review for compliance with the APA. Two principal paths: (a) the agency failed to follow required procedures (notice-and-comment, if the pause is a substantive rule), and (b) the policy is arbitrary and capricious—i.e., the agency failed to consider important aspects of the problem, offered no rational connection between facts and decision, or ignored statutory obligations. In Pacito the court found that the government had not demonstrated it could meet statutory duties and required reinstatement at least for already‑admitted refugees; analogously, plaintiffs here will argue the pause ignores statutory commands and is arbitrary because HHS did not evaluate how statutory duties to admitted refugees would be satisfied.

How the arguments will be framed
- Plaintiffs’ arguments. Textual reading of § 1522 that gives content to mandatory language; Bennett finality; arbitrary and capricious review because the agency failed to consider the consequences for refugees already admitted and has not explained how statutory duties will be met; irreparable injury and emergency relief for vulnerable populations; the agency cannot use the “to the extent of appropriations” clause to escape obligations where funds exist or were appropriated for the fiscal year.
- Government’s arguments. § 1522 is permissive; the memo is an exercise of discretion; the agency acted within the statutory authorization and to preserve resources; the decision is committed to agency discretion by law (5 U.S.C. § 701(a)(2)) or is policy-making not subject to judicial second-guessing; lack of finality if the memo is a preliminary internal statement.

Plausible outcomes consistent with Pacito
- Court finds the memo to be final agency action and reviews it. If the statutory text and circumstances show statutory duties to refugees already admitted, the court could enjoin the pause as arbitrary and capricious with limited scope—requiring resumption of services for refugees already in the United States while allowing the agency discretion regarding new awards. This mirrors Pacito’s direction to reinstate cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted.
- Alternatively, a court persuaded by the government’s reading may treat § 1522 as discretionary, find the memo within agency discretion, and deny relief, leaving plaintiffs to pursue political channels or seek injunctive relief on non‑statutory grounds (e.g., constitutional claims if any).

Key practical lessons for litigants
- Plaintiffs should develop a record showing the concrete reliance and reliance interests of refugees, existing appropriations earmarked or previously allocated, and the operational consequences of a pause.
- The government should document decisionmaking, explain budgetary constraints, and identify any competing statutory obligations or appropriations limitations that make continuation impossible without violating the Antideficiency Act.

## Hypo 2 — President narrows admissions by nationality under § 1182(f) with sparse findings — apply Hawaii analysis

Fact pattern. The President issues an order suspending admission of refugees from Nation X for 90 days, citing § 1182(f). The order recites a single sentence: “I find that entry of refugees from Nation X would be detrimental to the interests of the United States.” No supporting factual findings, reporting, or assessment accompanies the order. Plaintiffs challenge under the APA and the Constitution, arguing the order fails even the modest judicial check announced in Trump v. Hawaii and described in Pacito.

Legal framework — what Pacito tells us about § 1182(f)
Pacito applied the Hawaii framework: the statute requires only that the President “find” entry would be detrimental; courts must accord substantial deference. The Ninth Circuit emphasized that “the sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’” and that the language “exudes deference,” quoting Trump v. Hawaii. But deferential review is not no review: the President’s action must fall within the statutory language (suspension of “entry”) and must not require courts to probe the merits of national‑security determinations beyond the statutory prerequisite. Moreover, there are constitutional limits—if the action is motivated by invidious discrimination (e.g., religious animus), constitutional scrutiny can apply (Trump travel‑ban litigation is instructive).

How to litigate sparse findings
- Plaintiffs’ approach. Plaintiffs should argue that while courts do not demand a full classified record, there must be some minimal factual showing or administrative record tying the suspension to identifiable risk—evidence that Nation X poses specific security risks or that existing vetting is inadequate relative to demonstrated risks. They can rely on precedent that asks for a “facially legitimate and bona fide” justification (Kleindienst v. Mandel) for exclusion decisions, and emphasize that a one‑sentence conclusion is conclusory and may be arbitrary and capricious if reviewable under the APA (assuming agency delegation or that the order is not nonreviewable presidential action).
- Government’s approach. The government will emphasize executive primacy in national security and immigration, contend that the President's single‑sentence “finding” satisfies § 1182(f)’s sole statutory prerequisite, and stress that courts should not probe the sufficiency of the President’s factual determinations. It may submit classified declarations or ex parte material to the district court for in camera review to buttress the finding without revealing sensitive sources.

Standards of review and possible outcomes
- Deference under Trump v. Hawaii is powerful but not absolute. If the government provides at least a minimal record—classified or unclassified—showing particularized risk or explanation, courts will likely uphold the suspension. Pacito’s interim order suggested courts should not engage in a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” Thus an order with sparse but plausible support or a classified submission will likely survive review.
- Conversely, if the record is literally the single sentence and the suspension impacts persons with no plausible connection to national security risk (e.g., refugees vetted and approved, or a country with no history of terrorism), a court could find the action arbitrary or procedurally deficient if reviewable. For constitutional claims, evidence of discriminatory intent (statements, pattern of targeting) could trigger heightened scrutiny and defeat the suspension.

Practical considerations: use of classified evidence and remedial scope
- The government’s practical strategy is to supply classified affidavits in camera to show the factual basis without public exposure; courts accepting such submissions have often afforded deference.
- Remedies should be tailored. Pacito’s interim ruling allows that the presidential authority may be broad but does not absolve the executive of its statutory duties to individuals already admitted or conditionally approved. A court could enjoin the suspension insofar as it affects refugees who have already received approvals and have reliance, while deferring to the government for future admissions decisions.

## Hypo 3 — Partial rescission mid‑year; must ORR continue existing case management?

Fact pattern. Congress enacts a mid‑fiscal‑year rescission that reduces funds for refugee resettlement by $600 million and attaches a rider forbidding expenditure of those rescinded sums. The Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR) announces it will not pay for ongoing case management for refugees beyond the current month. Refugee service providers and refugees sue, asking the court to order ORR to continue case management for refugees admitted earlier in the fiscal year.

Statutory and appropriations law conflicts
- “To the extent of available appropriations.” A key phrase in § 1522 is that programs are to occur “to the extent of available appropriations.” The interplay of a statutory “shall” and appropriations restrictions forces courts to address whether a statutory duty survives a lawful rescission. Pacito acknowledged appropriations numbers and a rescission but nevertheless directed reinstatement of cooperative agreements for refugees who have been admitted, suggesting that statutory responsibilities to those already admitted carry weight even amid funding reductions.
- The Antideficiency Act and constitutional separation of powers. A court cannot order an agency to spend money that Congress has lawfully rescinded. Courts remedy unlawful executive action with injunctions reinstating prior agency behavior only when funding legally permits. If Congress has lawfully rescinded the funds with proper enactment, ORR may lack funds to continue case management. But the timing and legality of the rescission matter: if the rescission unlawfully targeted an appropriated obligation or violated statutory requirements (e.g., if the rescission was improperly enacted or limited to retroactive amounts beyond statutory authority), plaintiffs could seek relief.

Analytical steps for the court
1. Determine whether the rescission is in force and legally effective. A mid‑year rescission is presumptively valid if it is a product of regular order and properly enacted. If the rescission was accompanied by a clause suspending the obligation to spend previously obligated funds, the agency may be unable to comply with a court order that requires new expenditures.
2. Determine whether expenditures for case management were already obligated under binding cooperative agreements. Contract obligations or cooperative agreements signed before the rescission might create payment obligations that are not extinguished by later rescission of funds for future obligations. If the government had binding obligations, the court may order performance or payment unless the rescission was lawful and retroactively eliminated those obligations.
3. Reconcile statutory duty and appropriations. If § 1522 imposes mandatory duties for those already admitted (Pacito majority’s view), then a court may find that the executive was required to plan and obligate funds for ongoing services and that a rescission that frustrates those statutory duties may be unlawful if Congress intended otherwise.

Possible judicial outcomes
- If the rescission is valid and there are no preexisting contractual obligations that bind the government to continue discrete payments, a court will likely be unable to order ORR to spend money Congress has rescinded. The court may, however, fashion alternative equitable relief—e.g., ordering the agency to prioritize available funds for the most vulnerable or to provide nonmonetary assistance that does not require new appropriations.
- If the plaintiffs show that the agency had binding commitments or that the rescission is unlawful or misapplied, a court could order continuation of services for already admitted refugees, limited in time and scope, and leave prospective obligations to Congress.

Practical remedial design
- Where appropriations constraints bind, courts should avoid ordering the executive to create new spending. Instead, a practical remedy is an injunction requiring the agency to allocate remaining lawful funds in a manner that gives priority to statutorily protected categories (e.g., unaccompanied minors) and to provide transitional assistance for a reasonable period to avoid immediate harm.
- Courts can also retain jurisdiction to monitor compliance and to adjust remedies if Congress acts or if additional funds become available.

## Hypo 4 — Termination‑for‑convenience clauses in cooperative agreements and APA reviewability

Fact pattern. The State Department has cooperative agreements with ResettleCo that include a standard termination‑for‑convenience clause allowing the government to terminate with 30 days’ notice. The Department invokes that clause and terminates the cooperative agreement immediately with no explanation. ResettleCo sues, asserting the termination is arbitrary and capricious under the APA and that the Department violated its statutory obligations under § 1522 by failing to ensure reception and placement services for admitted refugees.

Is termination of cooperative agreements reviewable?
- Final agency action and the contract context. Not every contractual decision by the government is an agency action reviewable under the APA; but if the termination is made by an agency and has concrete legal consequences—termination of services, loss of funds, and downstream statutory impacts—Bennett’s finality test is likely satisfied. Pacito treated the termination of cooperative agreements as an action with legal consequences and directed reinstatement for a certain category of refugees.
- Contract remedies versus APA remedies. The government emphasizes the termination‑for‑convenience clause as private law that allocates risk; typically, a private contractor’s remedies would be contractual (Breach, reprocurement costs, bid protests, claim before the contracting officer). But the presence of a statutorily mandated obligation (if § 1522 is read as mandatory) gives rise to overlap: a business plaintiff may have primarily contract law remedies, while third parties (refugees) injured by the termination may seek APA review focused on statutory compliance.

Arbitrary and capricious review in the termination context
- An agency that terminates for convenience is not free to act capriciously. The APA’s arbitrary and capricious standard requires agencies to consider relevant factors and avoid unexplained flip‑flops. If the agency terminates because of policy reasons tied to legitimate statutory management considerations, it may be within its discretion. But if the termination is pretextual—intended solely to frustrate statutory protections for refugees—or made without considering obligations under § 1522 for currently admitted refugees, the termination can be set aside.
- Procedural obligations attached to cooperative agreements matter. Some cooperative agreements require notice, hearings, or mitigation steps before termination. The agency’s failure to comply with its own procedures can be a basis for APA relief.

How litigants should frame claims
- Plaintiffs (contractor and refugees) should build a record: timing of termination, whether termination was used to cut off services to already‑admitted refugees, internal communications showing pretext, and whether termination disregarded statutory duties. For refugees, the key claim is that the agency’s termination violated § 1522 duties and was arbitrary because the agency made no plan to replace services.
- Government’s defenses: the clause is a contractual right, contracting officers must be given deference, the decision is committed to agency discretion, and the agency acted to serve a broader policy objective (budgetary reallocation or reorganization).

Outcome scenarios
- Court fashions split relief: it may find the termination is final agency action reviewable under the APA and set aside the termination to the extent it prejudices statutory duties for refugees already admitted, while leaving the remainder of the termination intact and preserving the government’s contractual discretion for future planning. This is closely aligned with Pacito’s remedy ordering reinstatement of cooperative agreements necessary to provide reception and placement services to refugees who have been admitted.
- Alternatively, if the court treats the termination as an ordinary contractual exercise or finds no statutory duty to the refugees, the relief may be limited to contract damages for the contractor and no broader remedy for refugee plaintiffs.

Practical tips
- Contractors should preserve claims under the Contract Disputes Act and also plead APA claims if termination implicates statutory obligations and public‑interest harm.
- Third‑party plaintiffs should emphasize statutory language and reliance interests and seek preliminary relief that restores services for currently admitted refugees pending full review.

## Hypo 5 — Unaccompanied minors in transit during an entry suspension — scope of § 1522(d)(2)(B)

Fact pattern. The President suspends refugee admissions generally for 60 days. At the time the suspension takes effect, several unaccompanied minors are in transit to the United States—on flights with approved resettlement visas but not yet physically at border control—and their overseas processing agencies ask whether ORR must assume legal and financial responsibility under § 1522(d)(2)(B): “During any interim period while such a child is in the United States or in transit to the United States but before the child is so placed, the Director shall assume legal responsibility (including financial responsibility) for the child, if necessary, and is authorized to make necessary decisions to provide for the child’s immediate care.”

Statutory interpretation and the meaning of “in transit”
- Plain meaning and temporal coverage. Pacito emphasized that § 1522(d)(2)(B) explicitly covers unaccompanied minors “in transit to the United States.” The statute thus anticipates situations where children are en route but not yet placed in ORR custody. A court inclined to enforce statutory text will read “in transit” broadly to include children physically en route on aircraft or other conveyances bound for U.S. points, and who are reasonably expected to enter. This reading is consonant with protective, child‑welfare‑oriented statutory purposes.
- The “if necessary” qualification. The statutory duty is conditional—“shall assume legal responsibility…if necessary.” The “if necessary” clause gives the Director some discretion to intervene only when other mechanisms are inadequate. But “if necessary” is not empty; it requires agency assessment and explanation. For example, if commercial carriers or resettlement partners can provide continuous care, the Director might conclude ORR assumption is unnecessary; if not, ORR must act.

Operational complications with a suspension of entry
- Does a suspension of entry extinguish the category of “in transit”? A suspension that bars “entry” does not necessarily erase the child’s status as “in transit.” Congress drafted § 1522 mindful of contingencies, and Pacito noted the statute’s explicit text. If the suspension stops flights or border processing, the effect on ORR obligations depends on the nature of the suspension: if entry is halted by presidential proclamation under § 1182(f), the government may assert that the children cannot legally enter and therefore need not be taken into custody. Plaintiffs will counter that statute requires action to protect vulnerable minors and that the agency must assume responsibility until the children are either admitted, returned safely, or otherwise placed.
- Burdens of proof and emergency relief. For minors physically in transit, a court is likely to treat the issue as an emergency because of the heightened vulnerability of children. Plaintiffs should seek immediate declaratory relief that ORR must evaluate individual cases and assume responsibility where necessary.

Litigation strategies and likely judicial approaches
- Plaintiffs should seek individualized determinations: judges are more comfortable ordering agency processes (a narrow order to assess each child’s needs and decide whether ORR must assume responsibility) than broad programmatic mandates. Pacito’s majority imposed a narrow reinstatement obligation for certain refugees; similarly, courts may order ORR to exercise its statutory judgment in a reasoned manner for each child in transit.
- The government should document operational constraints and may propose a contingency plan: for children in transit, the agency could provide interim funding to carriers or resettlement partners to secure care without formally assuming legal responsibility—if statute permits. But ORR must show whether the plan satisfies the “if necessary” criterion or explain why formal assumption of legal responsibility is unnecessary.

Possible judicial outcomes
- The court orders ORR to make individualized determinations and to assume legal and financial responsibility where the agency cannot ensure the minors’ safety or welfare through other means—consistent with § 1522(d)(2)(B)’s protective purpose and Pacito’s deference to statutory obligations for admitted refugees.
- If the government can show safe alternative arrangements without ORR taking on legal responsibility, the court may allow such arrangements but retain jurisdiction for oversight and require periodic reporting.

Policy and practical implications
- The “in transit” language functions as a safeguard: courts will treat unaccompanied minors as a high‑priority group. Agencies should adopt clear procedures to evaluate “necessity” and document decisions to avoid arbitrary outcomes and litigation.
- Prosecutorial and immigration authorities must coordinate with ORR to prevent gaps where children are left without responsibility simply because of a suspension order.

***

Concluding guidance for exam essays and litigation memos

1. Start from the text, but do not stop there. Pacito demonstrates that textual cues (e.g., “shall,” “authorized,” “to the extent of appropriations,” and “in transit”) are determinative in framing the plausibility of statutory duties. Yet context, purpose, and statutory scheme are critical to refining the textual interpretation.

2. Distinguish categories of claim. Claims that challenge the President’s suspension of entry under § 1182(f) raise separation‑of‑powers and national security deference questions; claims that challenge agency suspensions or terminations of cooperative agreements under § 1522 raise APA and statutory‑compliance questions. Treat each claim with the distinct standard and remedies that the relevant doctrine supplies.

3. Seek tailored remedies. Pacito’s remedial approach is instructive: the court enforced statutory duties for a defined class (refugees already admitted or conditionally approved) without ordering the President’s policy reversed wholesale. In practice, the most sustainable relief is narrowly tailored—reinstatement for those already admitted, individualized determinations for unaccompanied minors, and monitoring of implementation—rather than broad programmatic injunctions that run directly into appropriations constraints and separation‑of‑powers concerns.

4. Build the administrative record. Plaintiffs must assemble evidence of reliance, concrete harms, and the agency’s failure to consider statutory duties. The government should prepare reasoned explanations and, where national security is invoked, appropriate classified justifications or declarations showing a rational predicate.

These hypotheticals are designed to force you to deploy the statutory‑interpretation tools and the APA’s procedural and substantive standards in a messy intersection of immigration policy, appropriations law, contract law, and child‑welfare concerns—precisely the mix the Pacito court balanced in its interim decision.
