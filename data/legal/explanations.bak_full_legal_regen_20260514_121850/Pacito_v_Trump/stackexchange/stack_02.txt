Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Pacito v. Trump

### What standard of review applies to the President’s § 1182(f) findings post-Hawaii?
Question:
The order says the court cannot engage in a “searching inquiry” into the President’s justifications and quotes Hawaii’s deferential language. Is this effectively rational-basis review? How does the court decide whether the President has “undoubtedly fulfilled” the sole prerequisite (a detriment finding) without probing pretext or evidence?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel applies the extremely deferential review that the Supreme Court spelled out in Trump v. Hawaii — not ordinary rational‑basis review, but a near‑noninquiry focused on two narrow legal questions: (1) did the President make the statutory “finding” that §1182(f) requires, and (2) does the President’s action exceed any textual limit in the statute. The court accepts the President’s stated finding as sufficient (“the President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here”) and refuses to engage in a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” That is deference to the existence and legal scope of the finding, not an assessment of whether the finding is factually true under a typical merits test.

Explanation of the controlling doctrine and how the opinion applies it
- The governing precedent is Trump v. Hawaii, which the panel cites repeatedly. Hawaii teaches that §1182(f) “exudes deference” to the President and that courts should not undertake a “searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.” The panel quotes that language and frames its analysis around it. See opinion’s recitation: “We observed that the authority under which the President issued Executive Order No. 14163, 8 U.S.C. § 1182(f), ‘exudes deference’ …” and later: “we cannot engage in ‘a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.’ Hawaii, 585 U.S. at 686.”  

- The panel identifies the statutory test as a single, textual prerequisite: the President must “find[] that the entry of any aliens … would be detrimental to the interest of the United States.” The court treats that requirement as a threshold, formal element of the statute: “The sole prerequisite set forth in § 1182(f) is that the President ‘find[]’ … The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here.” In other words, the court’s review is limited to whether the statutory form/condition exists, not to a searching factual review of the merits that led the President to the finding.

- That limited review is distinct from classic rational‑basis review. Rational‑basis inquiry normally asks whether a government action is rationally related to a legitimate purpose and can involve looking at factual record and competing justifications. Here, by contrast, the court (following Hawaii) declines to weigh the persuasiveness of the President’s national‑security or foreign‑policy reasons, and instead asks only whether the President acted within the text of §1182(f). The opinion says as much: the court concluded that “the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.” That is legal, text‑based review — not a fact‑intensive balancing test.

How the court decides “undoubtedly fulfilled” without probing pretext or evidence
- The phrase “undoubtedly fulfilled” signals acceptance of the President’s formal finding as legally sufficient on its face. Practically, the panel looks for (a) the President’s formal statement or proclamation that he has made the finding required by §1182(f), and (b) whether the proclamation stays within the statute’s textual bounds (e.g., applies to “entry” of a class of aliens and does not contravene other clear statutory limits). Because the statute vests the President with “broad discretion” and contains a clear, single prerequisite (a finding), the court will not probe the factual basis for that finding — it will accept the President’s stated determination unless the action exceeds the statute’s text or other controlling limits. See opinion: “The sole prerequisite … The President has undoubtedly fulfilled that requirement here,” and later: “the language of § 1182(f) is clear, and the [Executive Order] does not exceed any textual limit on the President’s authority.”

- In short, the court performs a narrow, legalistic check: (1) Is there a formal, cognizable finding? (2) Does the President stay within the textual scope of §1182(f)? If both answers are yes, the court will not excavate the evidence behind the President’s assertion or treat the finding as subject to a searching factual inquiry. That is precisely what the panel does here — it finds the formal elements present and concludes the government is likely to prevail on that statutory claim.

What this is not
- This is not an open‑ended rational‑basis or pretext inquiry. The court expressly refuses to engage in “a searching inquiry into the persuasiveness of the President’s justifications.”  
- It also does not mean the President’s actions are immune from all judicial review. The panel distinguishes the §1182(f) question from other statutory or administrative claims (e.g., APA claims about providing resettlement services under §1522), where ordinary review may apply. The opinion illustrates that deferential §1182(f) review is narrow and does not displace other statutory obligations that the government may have to third parties.

Bottom line for practice
- After Hawaii, and as reflected in this opinion, a court reviewing a §1182(f) suspension will apply extreme deference: it will verify that the President made a formal statutory finding and will check only whether the executive action exceeds the statute’s text or other clear legal limits. Courts will not undertake a searching factual or “persuasiveness” inquiry into the President’s national‑security or foreign‑policy justifications.
