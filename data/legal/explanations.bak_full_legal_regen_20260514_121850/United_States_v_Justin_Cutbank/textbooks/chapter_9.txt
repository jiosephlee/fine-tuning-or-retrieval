Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

Chapter 9: # Harmless Error and Cumulative Error in Criminal Appeals

The appellate inquiry into whether trial errors require reversal often determines the fate of a criminal conviction. Appellate courts must balance two competing imperatives: protecting a defendant’s constitutional and statutory rights to a fair trial, and avoiding needless retrials when errors were harmless in the sense that they did not affect the outcome. This chapter explains the principles governing harmless-error review and the related cumulative-error doctrine, illustrates how the allocation of burdens varies depending on the nature of the error, and shows how courts apply these principles in practice—particularly in firearms prosecutions in which physical evidence and strong testimonial proof often diminish the prejudicial effect of trial errors. The Eighth Circuit’s opinion in United States v. Cutbank, decided October 7, 2025, provides a recent, concrete example of these doctrines in action and will be used throughout the chapter to illuminate the tests and their application.

## Non‑constitutional harmless error: “substantially swayed” / “substantially injurious effect” standard

Not all errors at trial are constitutional errors. Many claimed errors involve the trial court’s discretionary evidentiary rulings, the admission of certain witnesses or documents, and other non‑constitutional matters. When the claimed error is non‑constitutional, appellate courts apply a harmless‑error inquiry designed to determine whether the error affected the fairness, integrity, or outcome of the trial.

Eighth Circuit precedent (and the Cutbank opinion) frames this inquiry in pragmatic terms: a non‑constitutional evidentiary error warrants reversal only if the defendant shows the jury “may have been substantially swayed” by the improperly admitted evidence or, said another way, that the error had a substantial or injurious effect on the jury’s verdict. This language echoes the Supreme Court’s harm formulations (for direct review and habeas contexts) such as the “substantial and injurious effect or influence” standard articulated in Kotteakos v. United States and used in habeas review decisions like Brecht v. Abrahamson. The Eighth Circuit often uses the shorthand that reversal is required only if the improperly admitted evidence “substantially swayed” the jury in reaching its verdict (see United States v. DeMarce and Keys). This is an inherently case‑specific judgment that turns on the record as a whole.

Applying this test requires the appellate court to assess the nature of the error, the centrality of the erroneously admitted or excluded evidence to the government’s theory, and the strength and character of the remaining evidence supporting guilt. Factors that mitigate the prejudicial impact of an error include (1) overwhelming independent evidence of guilt (for example, reliable physical evidence such as DNA or fingerprints linking the defendant to the instrumentality of the crime), (2) other admissible testimony that established the same facts, (3) curative jury instructions, and (4) the peripheral or cumulative nature of the erroneously admitted material. Conversely, errors that affect the prosecution’s central theory or relate to a contested factual issue are more likely to be deemed prejudicial.

Cutbank illustrates this approach. The defendant mounted a cluster of challenges to evidentiary rulings—whether lay witnesses could testify they had seen him possess the sawed‑off rifle before the charged incident; whether the jury should have been instructed about hearsay; whether testimony about a witness’s sister being killed by a gun was prejudicial; and whether a police report could be read into evidence for impeachment. The Eighth Circuit did not accept the defendant’s cursory objections as showing prejudice. The court held that the district judge’s rulings did not “substantially sway” the jury because the government’s testimonial and physical evidence—DNA on the firearm, victim identifications, the discovery of the rifle on the likely path between two houses, the victim’s phone recovered nearby, and officers’ testimony about the defendant’s conduct—was substantial. Thus, even if one or more evidentiary rulings were erroneous, the error was harmless under the “substantially swayed” standard.

Practical lesson for practitioners and students: when pressing a harmless‑error claim on appeal in a non‑constitutional setting, the appellant must show more than that a trial ruling was mistaken; the appellant must demonstrate that the error likely had a meaningful effect on the jury’s verdict. Appellate briefing should therefore focus tightly on the record and on why the remaining evidence was insufficient to sustain the verdict in light of the error.

## Constitutional harmless‑error standard: harmless beyond a reasonable doubt

Constitutional errors—violations of the Sixth Amendment right to counsel, the Confrontation Clause, the privilege against self‑incrimination, the right to an impartial judge, or other fundamental protections—trigger a more exacting standard. Where a constitutional error is properly preserved at trial, the government bears the burden of proving beyond a reasonable doubt that the error did not contribute to the guilty verdict. This is the Chapman v. California standard: only when the government demonstrates that there is no “reasonable possibility” that the error affected the verdict will the conviction stand.

Why the stricter standard? Constitutional protections are treated as so fundamental to a fair trial that a higher standard guards against nullifying those protections by mere evidentiary happenstance. Chapman draws a bright line: preserved constitutional errors require the government to overcome a heavy burden.

There are two important practical wrinkles. First, the label “constitutional error” matters—some trial errors implicate statutory or evidentiary rules rather than constitutional rights, and those invoke the less demanding “substantial prejudice” inquiry discussed above. Second, when a defendant fails to preserve a constitutional objection at trial, the appellate court ordinarily reviews for plain error under the standard of United States v. Olano: the defendant must show (1) an error, (2) that is plain (clear or obvious at the time of appeal), (3) that affects substantial rights (usually showing a reasonable probability of a different outcome), and (4) that the court should exercise its discretion to correct the error because it seriously affects the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings. Plain‑error review, while demanding, still permits reversal of constitutional error in exceptional cases even when not preserved.

Finally, not all constitutional errors are subject to harmless‑beyond‑a‑reasonable‑doubt review. Structural errors—those that defy analysis by harmless‑error principles because they affect the framework within which the trial proceeds (for example, total deprivation of counsel, a biased judge, racial discrimination in jury selection)—are treated as reversible per se. A defendant need not show prejudice for structural errors.

In Cutbank, the defendant raised Confrontation Clause and hearsay concerns. The Eighth Circuit found no Confrontation Clause problem because the relevant declarant (the victim) testified in person at trial, satisfying the constitutional guarantee. Thus, the Chapman standard did not come into play. This shows that courts often decide threshold constitutional questions before addressing harmlessness.

## Cumulative‑error doctrine: aggregation and the requirement of substantial prejudice

Multiple errors that are individually harmless may, in combination, require reversal if their cumulative effect undermines confidence in the verdict. The cumulative‑error doctrine embodies the intuition that several small errors can, when aggregated, amount to a trial so flawed that the conviction cannot stand. Nonetheless, courts are cautious in applying this doctrine. The central question is whether the aggregate effect of the errors “substantially swayed” the jury or otherwise deprived the defendant of a fundamentally fair trial.

The doctrine has boundaries. First, cumulative‑error review is applied only to errors; it does not aggregate non‑errors (i.e., correct rulings). Second, courts will not aggregate multiple harmless discretionary rulings if those rulings were correct in law or limited in effect. Third, the cumulative effect must be meaningful: courts typically require a showing of substantial prejudice comparable to that demanded when considering a single harmful error. The Eighth Circuit’s decisions—illustrated in Keys and invoked in Cutbank—are representative: aggregation leads to reversal only when the combined errors produced substantial prejudice to the defendant’s right to a fair trial.

How do courts evaluate cumulative prejudice? Analysts look at the number and type of errors, whether they implicated the same factual issue or different portions of the government’s case, whether overlapping admissible evidence re‑established the points the errors undermined, and whether the errors involved constitutional rights (which raises the bar for harmlessness). Courts also place heavy weight on the strength of the government’s evidence—the stronger the independent proof of guilt, the less likely that the collective effect of errors altered the verdict.

Cutbank demonstrates the cumulative‑error analysis in practice. The defendant claimed multiple evidentiary rulings were erroneous, but the Eighth Circuit concluded the cumulative effect did not produce substantial prejudice. The panel emphasized the substantial physical and testimonial evidence of guilt and reasoned that even if some rulings were marginally erroneous, the aggregate effect did not undermine confidence in the verdict. The court’s conclusion exemplifies the typical outcome in cases where the government has strong independent proof: the aggregate of trivial or peripheral errors rarely compels reversal.

Practical lesson: when asserting a cumulative‑error claim, defense counsel must identify not only multiple rulings that were improper but also explain how those rulings together altered the evidentiary landscape in a way that raises a serious doubt about the result. General assertions that “errors stacked up” without record‑based analysis are unlikely to succeed.

## Burden allocation and record‑driven analysis

A hallmark of harmless‑error and cumulative‑error review is that it is intensely record‑driven. Appellate courts decide whether an error was prejudicial by examining the trial transcript, exhibits, jury instructions, and the nature and timing of objections. The procedural posture and preservation of objections determine both the standard of review and the party that bears the burden.

The allocation of burdens proceeds along these lines:

- Preserved constitutional error: government bears the burden to prove harmless beyond a reasonable doubt (Chapman).
- Preserved non‑constitutional error: appellant must show prejudice under the applicable harmless standard (circuit variations exist, but Eighth Circuit law frames this as showing the jury may have been “substantially swayed”).
- Unpreserved error (constitutional or non‑constitutional): plain‑error review applies; appellant must show the error was plain, affected substantial rights, and that correction is necessary for fairness—this is the most demanding route for defendants on appeal.

Appellate courts give substantial deference to trial courts’ factual findings and to discretionary evidentiary rulings. When a district court adopts a presentence report or other factual statements without objection, appellate courts accept those adopted facts as a proper part of the record; the Cutbank panel relied on the district court’s adoption of the PSR for sentencing findings. Similarly, when a trial judge gives a contemporaneous or curative instruction, appellate review recognizes the trial judge’s superior position to assess how the jury received the evidence.

Because harmless‑error review is fact‑intensive, appellant briefs must marshal the trial record; assertions divorced from record citations are unsatisfactory. An appellate court has no basis to find prejudice where the record demonstrates otherwise, and conversely, a well‑developed record of error and its consequences creates a path to reversal.

Cutbank affords multiple examples of record‑driven reasoning. The defendant’s appellate brief was criticized for lacking legal authority and for failing to develop arguments supported by record citations; the court treated the contentions as undeveloped and disposed of them on the merits by reference to the record (victim testimony, DNA, physical recovery of the rifle, and the PSR). The lesson is plain: appellate courts expect a careful, record‑based showing of how a disputed ruling affected the outcome.

## Application in firearms trials with strong physical and testimonial evidence

Firearms prosecutions often present the classic setting in which harmless‑error and cumulative‑error doctrines play out. A typical federal felon‑in‑possession case will feature a mix of physical evidence (the firearm itself, DNA or fingerprints on the weapon, photographs, shell casings, serial numbers) and testimonial evidence (victim identifications, eyewitness accounts of possession, officers’ observations). When the physical and testimonial evidence is strong and consistent, courts are less likely to find that an evidentiary error—such as a narrowly admitted prior‑possession statement by a witness or limited testimony about a witness’s prior statements—had a substantial effect on the verdict.

Two practical points about firearms cases deserve emphasis:

1. Prior possession testimony often lies at the heart of the government’s case. Federal courts routinely recognize that testimony that a defendant possessed the same weapon on previous occasions is not necessarily “other‑acts” propensity evidence under Rule 404(b); rather, it may be intrinsic and probative of possession, ownership, and continuity. The Eighth Circuit in United States v. Battle (and the Cutbank opinion following Battle) explains that prior possession of the same firearm tends to establish later possession because it illuminates control and continuity. Consequently, such testimony is less likely to be deemed unfairly prejudicial and more likely to survive harmless‑error scrutiny.

2. Physical evidence such as DNA linking a defendant to the firearm is highly probative and strongly mitigates the prejudicial effect of peripheral evidentiary errors. If DNA or fingerprints on the gun match the defendant, if the weapon was recovered along the defendant’s likely path of flight, and if multiple witnesses identify the weapon and the defendant’s prior familiarity with it, appellate courts will often find that any isolated evidentiary misstep did not “substantially sway” the jury.

Cutbank illustrates both points. The government presented DNA evidence connecting the defendant to the sawed‑off Marlin .22 rifle, eyewitness identification by the victim and another housemate, recovery of the gun and the victim’s phone along the likely path between locations, and testimony regarding the defendant’s flight and resistance—including biting an officer and grabbing a weapon. Against that record, the Eighth Circuit found that the aggregate effect of the defendant’s complaints about evidentiary rulings did not produce substantial prejudice. The court also affirmed the district court’s pretrial ruling that lay testimony about prior observations of the same sawed‑off rifle was intrinsic and admissible to establish possession and continuity, underscoring that prior possession testimony in firearms cases often serves a permissible and probative function.

For practitioners: in a firearms prosecution, challenge to an evidentiary ruling will have a higher chance of reversal when the contested evidence bears directly on a disputed element that is not otherwise corroborated by physical or other strong evidence. Conversely, where the government has multiple independent proofs of possession, appellate courts will usually treat peripheral evidentiary errors as harmless.

## Conclusion: practical strategy for litigants and students

Harmless‑error and cumulative‑error doctrines require careful, record‑bound argumentation. For defense counsel:

- Preserve objections where possible, and make clear the constitutional basis of any objection that implicates fundamental rights.
- If an objection is preserved, develop the record to show how the admitted evidence impacted the jury’s view of contested issues; if the error is not preserved, be prepared to show plain error and its effect on substantial rights.
- In appellate briefs, focus on the specific portions of the record that demonstrate prejudice; bare assertions will fail.
- In cases with strong physical evidence (common in firearms prosecutions), tailor arguments to show why even overwhelming evidence may not cure particular errors—e.g., demonstrate that the error affected a threshold credibility finding or eliminated a legitimate defense theory.

For appellate courts and students studying their work, the Cutbank opinion is a model of typical appellate analysis: the court identified the nature of each claimed error, characterized it as constitutional or non‑constitutional (or not error at all), applied the appropriate standard, and reviewed the full record to decide whether the error (alone or in combination) undermined confidence in the outcome. The central thread through all of these analyses is the same: harmless‑error and cumulative‑error review is not abstract; it is an outcome‑sensitive, record‑bound inquiry that asks whether the asserted errors, individually or collectively, were likely to change the result. Where the record contains strong, independent proof of guilt, courts will generally find that errors—particularly peripheral evidentiary rulings—were harmless.
