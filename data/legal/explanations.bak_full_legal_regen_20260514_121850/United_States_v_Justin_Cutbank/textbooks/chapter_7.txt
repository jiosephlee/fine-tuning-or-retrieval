Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

Chapter 7: # Impeachment with Prior Inconsistent Statements: Rule 613, Authentication, and Police Reports

This chapter explains how a litigant may use a witness’s prior inconsistent statement to impeach testimony under Federal Rule of Evidence 613, the often-overlooked but critical authentication requirement under Rule 901, and the particular problems that arise when the impeaching “statement” is embedded in a police report. The Eighth Circuit’s decision in United States v. Cutbank provides a compact illustration of these issues: the defendant sought to read portions of a police report into the record to impeach a government witness, the district court required authentication and sustained objections, and the Court of Appeals affirmed, relying on settled Eighth Circuit law—including United States v. Cumbie—that extrinsic impeachment evidence must be authenticated before admission. That factual posture is the vehicle for a doctrinal roadmap lawyers and judges will use repeatedly: Rule 613’s two-step foundation for extrinsic impeachment, the multiple ways to authenticate a written statement, and the alternatives to reading a police report into evidence when authentication or hearsay concerns bar that route.

## Rule 613(a)-(b): foundation requirements and extrinsic proof

Rule 613 governs impeachment with prior inconsistent statements. Its operative structure is straightforward in theory but consequential in practice.

- Rule 613(a) requires that a party confronting a witness may use a prior statement to show inconsistency. It calls for a procedure: the examiner may, but need not, show the witness the writing or make them hear the statement while they are being examined. The point is to put the witness on notice and to give the jury a clear basis for assessing credibility.

- Rule 613(b) imposes an important limitation on the use of extrinsic evidence to prove a prior inconsistent statement: extrinsic evidence is not admissible to impeach a witness with a prior inconsistent statement unless (1) the witness is given the opportunity to explain or deny the statement and (2) the opposing party is afforded an opportunity to examine the witness about it. In short, before the trier of fact may be shown a document or other item that purports to contain a prior inconsistent statement, the witness must be given a chance to address the apparent inconsistency on the stand (or previously have had that opportunity), and the opposing party must be able to cross-examine about the inconsistency.

These rules are designed to balance the truth-seeking function of impeachment with fairness to the witness and the opponent. If the witness can be presented with the statement and either adopts it or disavows it, the jury has the benefit of seeing the statement while also hearing the witness explain. If the witness is not on the stand or the examiner cannot get the witness to address the statement, courts are rightly suspicious of allowing juries to see out-of-court materials that may be unreliable.

In practice, the Rule 613(b) requirement is often satisfied by first asking the witness, on cross-examination, whether they made the prior statement or whether the document accurately records their prior words. Only if the witness disclaims or fails to recollect does the proponent offer extrinsic proof. The rule thus encourages the direct route—asking the witness about the prior statement—before resorting to document admission.

Applying these principles to the Cutbank facts: defense counsel sought to read portions of a police report that recorded a witness’s earlier statements to an investigator. The district court allowed questioning about what the witness had told police but sustained objections to reading the police report into the record because the report was not authored by the witness and lacked foundation. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, emphasizing that extrinsic impeachment evidence must be authenticated—i.e., Rule 613(b)’s requirements were not satisfied simply by counsel’s attempt to read the report aloud.

## Authentication under Rule 901: who can authenticate; adoption by the witness

Admission of any writing or recorded statement as extrinsic evidence requires authentication under Rule 901(a): the proponent must produce evidence sufficient to support a finding that the item is what the proponent claims it to be. Authentication is a preliminary question for the court, and the methods to authenticate are several.

Common methods courts accept include:

- Testimony of a witness with knowledge: A witness who personally wrote the document, made the notation, or observed the making of the document may testify that it is what it purports to be. In the impeachment context, the simplest authentication is the impeached witness’s own testimony that the document correctly records a prior statement they made (often called adoption). If the witness testifies, “Yes, that is what I told the officer,” the report or the specific portions can be authenticated through the witness.

- Adoption by conduct or declaration: A witness may authenticate by adopting or affirming the substance of a writing or recording. Physical or verbal adoption—“I remember saying that” or signing a statement—provides a sufficient basis for finding authenticity.

- Testimony of the author or custodian: If an investigator or officer wrote the report, that author can authenticate the report by testifying that it was prepared contemporaneously and accurately recorded the witness’s words. A custodian of records can likewise authenticate business records (see discussion below) by laying the business-record foundation.

- Distinctive characteristics or circumstantial evidence: When a writing has distinctive markings, dates, or other characteristics that uniquely identify it, a court may accept such circumstantial proof as authentication (e.g., a recorded phone call with a known caller ID pattern, a photograph that plainly depicts the scene). Rule 901(b) lists these non-exclusive methods.

Eighth Circuit law, as applied in Cumbie and cited in Cutbank, frames these principles with a practical rule: extrinsic evidence offered to impeach a witness with a prior inconsistent statement must be authenticated. Where the report is not authored by the witness and no other witness establishes that the writing accurately records the witness’s statement, a district court may require additional foundation—often the investigator’s testimony—before allowing the document into evidence.

Some practical illustrations:

- Best practice when seeking to use a police report to impeach a witness is to ask the witness on the stand, “Did you tell Officer Smith on January 3 that X?” If the witness answers yes or adopts a writing, the report is authenticated as to those portions the witness admits.

- If the witness disclaims or has poor recollection, counsel may call the officer who prepared the report and ask whether the notes accurately reflect what the witness said. The officer’s testimony can authenticate the report and bring it within the scope of Rule 613(b)’s allowance for extrinsic proof (if the other requirements are met).

- If the report cannot be authenticated in those ways, the proponent should not attempt to read it aloud to the jury; the court is likely to sustain hearsay and authentication objections, as in Cutbank.

## Police reports: hearsay problems and authorship; limits of reading from reports

Police reports create a particular tangle of concerns: they are hearsay-heavy (containing out-of-court statements by victims, witnesses, or third parties), they are often authored by someone other than the person whose earlier statements they record, and they combine narrative, opinions, and raw statements in a single document.

Two distinct problems arise when counsel attempts to use a police report for impeachment:

1. Hearsay within hearsay. A police report typically contains statements by witnesses (e.g., “Victim said X”) memorialized by an officer. Those witness utterances are out-of-court statements and thus hearsay if offered for their truth. If the report is offered only to show prior inconsistent statements for impeachment, the offering party may claim the piece is not being admitted for its truth but solely to show inconsistency. When the witness adopts the specific portion, the statement becomes admissible for impeachment under Rule 613 and may also be admissible substantively under Rule 801(d)(1)(A) if the prior inconsistent statement was given under oath at a prior proceeding. But if the report is offered when the witness has not adopted or is unavailable, the inclusion of third-party statements creates hearsay within hearsay that must satisfy an exception for each layer.

2. Authorship and foundation. Many police reports are drafted by investigators who paraphrase or summarize a witness’s statement. Unless the witness testifies that the report accurately reflects their words, or the officer who prepared the report testifies to that effect, the court is likely to treat the report as an unauthenticated document authored by a third party. Reading such a report into the record is usually impermissible: it would be an extrinsic writing containing hearsay and lacking sufficient authentication.

The Cutbank court’s practical approach is emblematic: the district court permitted defense counsel to question the witness about prior police interviews (eliciting the substance of what she had told police), but it sustained objections when defense counsel attempted to read parts of the investigator’s report into evidence without establishing that the report was the witness’s own statement or that the investigator/writer would authenticate it. The Court of Appeals approved this limitation—authentication was required before extrinsic impeachment could be accomplished by reading the report to the jury.

What are the common judicial responses to this problem?

- If the witness adopts the writing, courts admit it (subject to other rules). Reading it then serves both impeachment and, in some cases, substantive purposes.

- If the witness does not adopt the writing but the officer who prepared the report is present, the proponent may call that officer to authenticate the report and testify that the report accurately records the witness’s words. That authentication may admit the report as extrinsic impeachment evidence under Rule 613(b).

- If the officer is not present, counsel can attempt to admit the report under a hearsay exception (business or public records) if the proponent can lay the appropriate foundation. That route raises its own complications because internal witness statements within a police report are often not admissible under the public-record exception if they contain investigatory opinions or accusatory narrative (see Rule 803(8) limitations and authorities construing it).

- If none of the above works, recorded recollection (Rule 803(5)) may be a fallback: have the witness read and adopt their prior written or recorded statement as a recorded recollection, but the rule’s foundation is strict.

A final point on police reports: trial judges have wide discretion to prevent juries from being exposed to hearsay-filled documents that may confuse the issues or unfairly prejudice a party. That discretion is particularly robust where the party proffering the report has not met authentication or hearsay-exception foundations.

## Alternative routes: recorded recollection (Rule 803(5)), prior sworn testimony (Rule 801(d)(1)(A)), public/business records (Rules 803(6), 803(8)) and their constraints

When a proponent cannot authenticate a police report as containing the witness’s own words, several alternative doctrines may permit use of prior statements—each with particular prerequisites and limits.

1. Recorded recollection—Rule 803(5).
   - Elements: the witness once had personal knowledge of the matter; the witness made or adopted a record when the matter was fresh in memory; the record accurately reflects the witness’s knowledge; and the witness now has insufficient recollection to testify fully and accurately.
   - Effect: the writing may be read into evidence (but generally is not received as an exhibit unless offered by an adverse party).
   - Practical points: counsel should establish on the record that the witness cannot now recall specifics even after examination and that the record was made contemporaneously. Recorded recollection is an evidentiary lifeline but is narrow: if the witness can recall now, the rule does not apply.

2. Prior sworn testimony—Rule 801(d)(1)(A) (nonhearsay).
   - If a witness’s prior inconsistent statement was made under oath at a prior trial, hearing, or other proceeding and the witness currently testifies and is subject to cross-examination about the earlier statement, the prior statement is not hearsay and can be admitted both for impeachment and substantive use. The crucial elements are (a) prior oath, (b) a prior proceeding (not merely a police interview), and (c) opportunity for cross-examination in the earlier proceeding (or being subject to the prior proceeding’s cross and the current opportunity for cross).
   - This exception is often unavailable where the prior statement is a police interview or an investigator’s report.

3. Business records—Rule 803(6).
   - A police report might be admitted as a business record if it was made in the regular course of the reporting entity’s business, by a person with knowledge, at or near the time of the event, and if the proponent lays the proper foundation by calling a custodian or other qualified witness.
   - Trap: a police report may qualify as a business record of a law enforcement agency, but statements within the report recounting third-party declarations are hearsay within hearsay and require their own hearsay exception. If the report contains the witness’s own prior statement and the report is otherwise admissible as a business record and properly authenticated, courts may admit it as impeachment. However, courts are often skeptical about admitting accusatory or investigative conclusions under business-record theory.

4. Public records—Rule 803(8).
   - Matters observed pursuant to a duty imposed by law and recorded by public officials may be admissible. But the exception excludes “matters observed by law-enforcement personnel in criminal cases” if admitted against a defendant in a criminal case, and courts are generally cautious with police investigatory reports, particularly as to complaints, conclusions, and opinions. Many courts exclude the narrative portions of police reports as contrary to the spirit of the exception.

Each of these alternatives has its own strategic implications. For instance, seeking a recorded recollection admission requires careful on-the-record proof that the witness cannot now recall despite being shown the writing; attempting to admit a police report as a business or public record requires calling a custodian or author to establish foundation and to address hearsay-within-hearsay. The Eighth Circuit’s jurisprudence, as seen in Cutbank and Cumbie, encourages lawyers to choose a path that will permit the necessary foundation to be established at trial rather than attempting to short-circuit the authentication step.

## Eighth Circuit’s insistence on authentication before admitting extrinsic impeachment (e.g., United States v. Cumbie)

The Eighth Circuit has repeatedly emphasized the authentication requirement for extrinsic impeachment evidence. United States v. Cumbie, cited by the Cutbank court, is a leading example: the court held that while extrinsic evidence may be used to impeach by prior inconsistent statement, such extrinsic evidence still must be authenticated. Cumbie and its progeny require proffers sufficient to permit a reasonable juror to find that the document accurately represents the witness’s prior statement.

The Cutbank decision applied that rule to a common fact pattern: defense counsel sought to read portions of an investigator’s report into the record to impeach a government witness who had testified inconsistently. The district court allowed cross-examination about what the witness had told police (eliciting inconsistent statements orally from the witness), but disallowed introduction of the investigator’s report absent authentication. The Eighth Circuit approved: the proponent had not shown that the report was authored by the witness, adopted by the witness, or accurately recorded the witness’s words; thus the court did not err in rejecting the attempt to admit the report in lieu of live foundational testimony.

Key lessons from the Eighth Circuit line:

- Authentication is not a mere formality; courts will require the proponent to affirmatively connect the document to the witness or to the maker of the document.

- Cross-examination about a prior statement is often the simpler route. If the witness can be confronted with the prior statement and either adopts it or explains it, impeachment is accomplished without introducing the underlying document.

- Where the witness disavows a prior statement and the proponent wants the jury to see the contemporaneous record, the proponent must furnish authentication—either the witness’s adoption, the investigator’s testimony, or a proper hearsay exception/foundation for a business/public record. Absent that, trial courts should exclude the report as unauthenticated.

- Appellate courts reviewing such rulings apply an abuse-of-discretion standard; judges have substantial latitude to protect juries from unauthenticated, hearsay-laden materials.

## Practical trial strategies and common pitfalls

1. Always try direct confrontation first. Put the prior statement to the witness on the stand. If they admit, you are done on authentication; the writing is effectively adopted for impeachment purposes (and sometimes substantive use). Rule 613 favors this approach.

2. If the witness disavows and you want the jury to see the exact language, prepare to authenticate the writing. Call the author (investigator/officer) or a custodian to lay the business-record foundation. Anticipate and address hearsay-within-hearsay issues.

3. Consider recorded recollection if the witness genuinely cannot recall. Lay the careful foundation required by Rule 803(5). Be mindful that recorded recollection allows the writing to be read into evidence but not necessarily admitted as an exhibit.

4. Don’t assume police reports are freely admissible. Many jurisdictions scrutinize narrative reports and exclude opinions or summaries that invade the jury’s province or are hearsay.

5. If you lack a foundation witness, think about alternative evidence: body-worn camera footage, contemporaneous text messages, call logs, photos. These items typically present fewer hearsay/authentication headaches than narrative reports.

6. Preserve the record. If the court excludes a report you intended to use, make a proffer outside the presence of the jury (offer of proof) that establishes what you would have shown and by whom—this is essential should you later challenge the ruling on appeal.

7. Beware of Confrontation Clause issues in criminal cases. A prior testimonial statement by a declarant is subject to the Sixth Amendment unless the declarant testifies at trial and is subject to cross-examination. A police report that contains testimonial statements from a witness who does not testify may therefore be constitutionally inadmissible for substantive purposes; impeachment under Rule 613, however, raises distinct Confrontation Clause questions that hinge on whether the declarant testifies and is available for cross-examination.

## Conclusion: rules-of-thumb for the courtroom

The Eighth Circuit’s treatment in Cutbank—endorsing the district court’s insistence on authentication before allowing a police report to be read into evidence for impeachment—illustrates stable doctrine:

- Rule 613(b) permits extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement only after the witness has been afforded an opportunity to explain or deny the statement and the opposing party has an opportunity to examine the witness about it.

- Rule 901 requires authentication of any extrinsic document offered to prove a prior inconsistent statement; absent adoption by the witness or testimony from the document’s author/custodian, a court may exclude the document.

- Police reports often contain hearsay and third-party statements; they are not a shortcut for impeachment unless properly authenticated or otherwise admissible under a recognized exception (recorded recollection, business or public records, prior sworn testimony).

For trial practice: confront the witness directly first; if the witness disavows, be ready to authenticate through the author or a custodian, or to resort to recorded recollection or other admissible evidence. Failure to do so invites exclusion, and appellate courts will generally defer to the trial court’s exercise of evidentiary gatekeeping power.
