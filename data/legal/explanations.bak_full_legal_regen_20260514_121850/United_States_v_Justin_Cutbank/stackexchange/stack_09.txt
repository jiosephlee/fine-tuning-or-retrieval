Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

### Can unobjected-to PSR facts alone support a § 2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement for 'another felony offense'?
Question:
The panel emphasized that the district court adopted PSR facts 'without objection,' and on that basis found the gun was used in connection with another felony. Under Rule 32(i)(3) and Eighth Circuit practice, does failure to object equal admission for sentencing purposes? Is there any limit on relying solely on unobjected PSR assertions to establish the 'in connection with' element, given the preponderance standard and due process concerns?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — in this case the Eighth Circuit upheld a §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement based solely on PSR facts the district court “adopted, without objection.” Under the court’s practice, unobjected-to PSR factual statements are accepted for sentencing and can satisfy the “in connection with another felony” element; the court affirmed the enhancement because the PSR (and adopted facts) established the connection and no timely objection was made.

Why (legal doctrine and the opinion’s reasoning)
- The Guidelines provision and commentary: USSG §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) applies where a defendant “used or possessed any firearm or ammunition in connection with another . . . felony offense,” and note n.14(C) expressly provides the enhancement can apply “regardless of whether a criminal charge was brought, or a conviction obtained.” The panel relied on that rule (Opinion, § III).
- PSR-adoption practice: the opinion states that “the district court adopted, without objection, the PSR’s factual statements” and, on that basis, concluded the court did not err in finding the facts establish the enhancement (Opinion, § III). The court cited United States v. Dunn for the applicable standard of review (Opinion, § III).
- Practical effect: where the district court adopts PSR facts and the defendant lodges no objection, the court may rely on those facts at sentencing to support guideline enhancements — and the Eighth Circuit will not reverse absent clear error in the district court’s factual finding (Opinion, § III; citing Dunn).

Limits and due-process concerns (what the opinion shows and what it does not)
- What this opinion holds: because the PSR facts were adopted without objection, the court found no error in applying the four-level enhancement here. The opinion does not announce a categorical rule insulating every unobjected PSR assertion from review; it affirms that adopted, unobjected facts can be the basis for a guidelines finding in a particular case (Opinion, § III).
- What the opinion does not decide: the panel did not analyze any broader constitutional due-process problem or set a boundary saying a court may rely on any unobjected PSR allegation regardless of source or quality. The opinion does not discuss whether (and when) an adopted PSR assertion that is materially untested, unreliable, or inherently incredible would still be permissible — it simply affirms the sentencing determination here where substantial evidence supported the enhancement and no objection was made (Opinion, §§ I–III).
- Procedural reminder from the opinion: the district court must correctly calculate the Guidelines range and consider §3553(a) factors; appellate review presumes a within-guidelines sentence is reasonable if there is no significant procedural error (Opinion, § III citing Gall, Rosales‑Mireles, and related precedent).

Takeaway for practice
- If the district court adopts PSR facts and the defendant fails to object, the Eighth Circuit will treat those facts as accepted for sentencing purposes and they can support a §2K2.1(b)(6)(B) enhancement (as in this case).
- To prevent a PSR statement from being dispositive, timely and specific objections at sentencing are required so the court must resolve disputes and make the necessary factual findings on the record. The opinion demonstrates the practical consequence of failing to do so: the enhancement stood.
