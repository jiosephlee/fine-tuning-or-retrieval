Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

### How does cumulative-error review operate when individual evidentiary rulings are harmless or non-errors?
Question:
The court cited Keys to say it won’t overturn a conviction based on cumulative effect absent substantial prejudice. If each ruling is either not error or harmless, how can the cumulative-error doctrine ever provide relief? What threshold showing of combined prejudice is required in the Eighth Circuit, and how is it assessed when many rulings involve similar themes (e.g., prior sightings of the gun)?
Answer:
Short answer
In the Eighth Circuit cumulative-error review only helps a defendant who can show that the combined effect of trial errors produced “substantial prejudice” — i.e., the errors, taken together, “may have been substantially swayed” the jury. If each ruling is either correct or harmless (meaning it did not influence the verdict or had only a slight effect), the cumulative‑error doctrine ordinarily does not provide relief unless the aggregate of those rulings meaningfully changed the trial’s outcome. The Cutbank opinion applies this exact standard: because the government’s testimonial and physical evidence was substantial, any individual evidentiary rulings were harmless or non‑prejudicial, and the court concluded the cumulative effect did not produce the requisite substantial prejudice. See DeMarce and Keys citations in the opinion.

What the opinion says (and the governing standard)
- The panel reiterates the harmless‑error framing: the court “will reverse only if the jury may have been substantially swayed by the improperly admitted evidence.” United States v. DeMarce, 564 F.3d 989, 997 (8th Cir. 2009) (quoted in the opinion). The opinion repeats that an error is harmless if it “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” Id. at 998 (quoted).
- For cumulative error the opinion cites Keys: “We will not overturn a conviction based on the cumulative effect of trial errors unless there is substantial prejudice to the defendant.” United States v. Keys, 918 F.3d 982, 985 (8th Cir. 2019) (quoted in the opinion).

How that operates in practice (as shown here)
- Burden and showing: the defendant must do more than point to several rulings that were close calls. He must show the cumulative impact plausibly changed the verdict — not merely that there were multiple, technically imperfect rulings. Cutbank could not “clear this hurdle” because the record showed substantial government proof (witness identifications, DNA on the gun, physical items found along the route), so any individual evidentiary rulings “did not influence or had only a slight influence on the verdict.” (Opinion, Section II, last two paragraphs.)
- When each error is harmless: if the court finds each challenged ruling was either correct or harmless, cumulative‑error review typically adds nothing. The opinion reflects that approach: it treats the individual rulings under ordinary abuse‑of‑discretion/harmless‑error review first, and then rejects cumulative prejudice because the overall record was strong.
- Repetitive/similar rulings (e.g., multiple references to prior sightings of the gun): repetition can matter only if the repetitions, taken together, materially increased the risk of an unfair trial. But in Cutbank the court accepted that prior sightings were intrinsic and admissible (Battle, quoted) and, even if there had been borderline errors, the physical evidence and DNA made any cumulative effect immaterial. Thus repetitive testimony that merely reinforced a strong, independently supported theory of guilt did not meet the “substantial prejudice” threshold.

Bottom line for litigation strategy
- To prevail on cumulative‑error review in the Eighth Circuit you should (1) preserve and identify the individual errors; (2) show that each error had at least some prejudicial tendency; and (3) explain how those tendencies combined to produce a realistic probability the jury’s verdict was affected. If the government’s proof is strong and the errors were minor or correctable, the court will conclude the cumulative effect was insufficient, as the Cutbank court did.
