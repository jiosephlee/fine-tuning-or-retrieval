Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: United States v. Justin Cutbank

### Did the district court need to make explicit § 3553(a) findings to reject Cutbank’s variance argument?
Question:
The panel said the court reviewed the PSR and memoranda, noted § 3553(a) factors that could warrant a variance, and thus was presumed to have considered and rejected the defense’s arguments (citing Masood and Maluoth). When does Eighth Circuit law require more explicit discussion of particular § 3553(a) factors or arguments (e.g., mitigation, unwarranted disparities, overstatement of criminal history) to allow meaningful appellate review?
Answer:
Short answer
Under this opinion (and controlling Eighth Circuit precedent the opinion cites), the district court need not recite each § 3553(a) factor or analyze every argument point‑by‑point so long as the record makes clear the court considered the PSR, the parties’ sentencing submissions, and the § 3553(a) factors. By contrast, a more explicit explanation is required when the record does not show the court considered the materials or when the defendant raises specific, nonfrivolous grounds for a variance that the record suggests were overlooked (or the sentence is outside the Guidelines and the court has committed a procedural error). The Cutbank opinion explains and applies this baseline.

What the opinion says (key points)
- The court began by noting the settled framework: the district court must correctly calculate the Guidelines, then consider whether § 3553(a) factors warrant a departure or variance. (Opinion § III, citing Gall and Rosales‑Mireles.)
- A within‑Guidelines sentence is presumptively reasonable on appeal. (Opinion § III, citing Goodhouse.)
- The district court “noted its review of the PSR and sentencing memoranda and reviewed § 3553(a) factors that could warrant a variance.” Because of those statements, the panel applied the Masood presumption that the court “was aware of [the defendant’s] arguments, and we therefore presume that the district court considered and rejected them.” (Opinion § III, quoting Masood.)
- The court rejected any need for a “mechanical recitation” of factors: “it simply must be clear from the record that the district court actually considered the § 3553(a) factors in determining the sentence.” (Opinion § III, quoting Maluoth.)

Putting that together: when an explicit discussion is required
1. When the record does not show the court considered the PSR and parties’ submissions. Cutbank turned on the court expressly stating it had reviewed the PSR and memoranda; absent such a showing, appellate courts cannot presume consideration and will require a clearer explanation. (Opinion § III.)
2. When the defendant raises specific, nonfrivolous arguments (e.g., particular mitigation evidence, an unwarranted‑disparity claim, or an assertion that the PSR overstates criminal history) and the record suggests the court ignored or failed to address those points. The panel’s reliance on Masood and Maluoth implies the presumption of consideration collapses where the sentencing record is silent or ambiguous about whether the court engaged with the defendant’s concrete arguments. (Opinion § III; Masood/Maluoth citations.)
3. When a court commits a procedural error (e.g., miscalculates the Guidelines or fails to provide any meaningful sentencing explanation for a non‑Guidelines sentence). The opinion reiterates that before reviewing substantive reasonableness, an appellate court must ensure there was no significant procedural error. (Opinion § III, citing Rosales‑Mireles and Gall.)

How this applies to the examples you listed
- Mitigation evidence: If the district court’s oral or written statement shows it reviewed mitigation submissions (PSR, defense memo) and referenced § 3553(a), the Eighth Circuit will usually presume the court considered and rejected mitigation arguments (as in Cutbank). If the mitigation is unusually powerful, or the defendant points to specific mitigating facts the record shows the court ignored, the appellate court will demand a clearer explanation.
- Unwarranted disparities: Same rule — a general statement that the court considered § 3553(a) and the parties’ memoranda is usually sufficient for a within‑Guidelines sentence. But a defendant asserting a concrete disparity (with case citations, comparators, or statisticized proof) that the district court does not acknowledge may trigger the need for a more specific response.
- Overstated criminal history: If the PSR is adopted and the court says it reviewed the PSR, the Eighth Circuit will presume the court considered challenges to the PSR. If the defendant preserved a concrete objection and the record shows the court never addressed that objection, appellate review will expect a clearer ruling.

Bottom line
Cutbank reiterates the Eighth Circuit’s practical rule: no “mechanical recitation” of every § 3553(a) point is required; a clear record that the court reviewed the PSR and parties’ submissions and considered § 3553(a) suffices for a within‑Guidelines sentence. But when the record is silent, ambiguous, or shows the court overlooked specific, preserved arguments (or when there is a procedural error), the court must say more to permit meaningful appellate review. (Opinion § III, with citations to Gall, Rosales‑Mireles, Masood, and Maluoth.)
