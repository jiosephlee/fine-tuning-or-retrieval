Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

### Why could the Second Circuit decide fair use on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion without discovery?
Question:
The panel says fair use may be "so clearly established by a complaint" as to support dismissal at the pleading stage (citing TCA Television). Here, the court relied on the videos and the broadcast segments themselves. How does the incorporation-by-reference/judicial notice doctrine let the court look past the complaint’s characterizations and evaluate the works directly without converting to summary judgment? What are the limits on doing this, especially when transformative purpose turns on a "reasonable observer" standard after Warhol?
Answer:
Short answer
The Second Circuit relied on the videos and the JKL segments because those materials were properly before the court (either attached/incorporated or subject to judicial notice) and because the fair‑use question could be resolved as a legal determination from those materials alone. The court invoked the familiar Rule 12(b)(6) authorities (DiFolco, TCA Television) that allow a court to look at documents integral to the complaint without converting to summary judgment, and it stressed that it only needed “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments]” to decide fair use (Cariou). The limit is that a court may only do this where the materials themselves—viewed objectively—establish fair use as a matter of law; if determining transformation requires resolving disputed factual issues about how a reasonable observer would perceive the works, dismissal is inappropriate.

Why the court could look at the works without converting to summary judgment
1) Rule 12(b)(6) authorities the opinion relies on. The panel began by restating the standard for a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal: it may consider “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.” (Opinion quoting DiFolco v. MSNBC, 622 F.3d at 111.) The court also cited TCA Television for the proposition that fair use “may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal . . . at the pleading stage.” (Opinion citing TCA Television, 839 F.3d at 178.)

2) Judicial notice /registrations. The opinion expressly noted the court may take judicial notice of copyright registrations published in the Copyright Office’s registry (citing Island Software & Comput. Servs., Inc. v. Microsoft, 413 F.3d 257, 261). The court used those registrations to establish publication timing (Opinion, ¶ discussing factor four).

3) The court relied on the works themselves. The panel said it “need[ed] only consider ‘the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue’ to decide ‘the question of fair use’,” and then decided the fair‑use factors from those materials (citing Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d at 707). That statement explains the procedural move: the operative works were integral to the complaint (the complaint discussed and depended on them), were either attached or incorporated, and thus the court could examine them on a 12(b)(6) record.

Why that is legally sound (doctrine + reasoning)
- Incorporation-by-reference / integral documents doctrine lets a court consider documents the complaint relies on without converting to summary judgment because the complaint’s allegations invite judicial consideration of the documents’ contents. The opinion follows that line (DiFolco).
- Judicial notice of certain public records (copyright registrations) is permitted and was used here to resolve a discrete factual assertion (publication date) that bears on factor four.
- The court treated fair use as a mixed law‑and‑fact inquiry that sometimes can be resolved as a matter of law when the comparison of works themselves plainly demonstrates the statutory factors — exactly the situation the panel found here. The opinion repeats the settled rule that fair use can be “so clearly established by a complaint” that dismissal is appropriate (TCA Television), and then applies the four statutory factors to the originals and the segments.

Limits — when the court cannot do this on a 12(b)(6) record
- No fact‑finding on disputed extrinsic facts. A court may not rely on extra‑record facts, disputed factual allegations, or competing witness accounts at the pleading stage. If resolution of fair use turns on contested facts outside the four corners of the works or on testimonial evidence, the case cannot be decided on 12(b)(6).
- The “reasonable observer” / transformation point after Warhol. The opinion acknowledges that transformation turns on whether “a reasonable observer” would perceive the secondary use as adding a new purpose or character, not on the parties’ subjective intent (citing the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision). That means:
  - If the objective comparison of the original video and the JKL segment makes plain, on its face, that the segment adds a different communicative purpose (criticism, lampoon, commentary), the court can decide transformation at the pleading stage.
  - But if transformation depends on contested contextual facts or how a reasonable observer would see the work in light of extrinsic evidence (e.g., disputed broadcast context, surrounding statements, or paratextual materials not before the court), then the reasonable‑observer inquiry requires factual development and cannot be resolved by looking only at the submitted works.
- Narrow use of judicial notice. Judicial notice can establish facts that are not reasonably subject to dispute (e.g., registration date). It cannot be used to prove disputed inferences about how audiences perceived the work; those are typically for later fact‑finding.

How the Santos opinion applied those limits
- The panel concluded no factual disputes needed resolution because the complaint itself described the works and their use, and the works (videos + segments) clearly showed an added purpose (mockery/criticism). The court therefore applied the four statutory factors to the materials and held fair use barred the claims as a matter of law.
- The opinion stressed objective assessment: the reasonable‑observer test governs and the complaint’s own allegations (that Santos intended uplifting messages while defendants intended sarcastic commentary) made clear which purpose a reasonable observer would infer. Given that, the court found no need for evidentiary development.

Takeaway for litigants and students
- Courts may examine works attached to or incorporated by a complaint (and certain public records) on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion and decide fair use if the statutory factors can be resolved from those materials alone.
- But when transformation or other fair‑use factors turn on contested facts about perception, context, or intent that are not resolved by the works themselves, dismissal is improper; discovery or summary judgment will be necessary. The Santos opinion rests on the former situation — the works and the complaint’s own descriptions were sufficient to show transformation as a matter of law.

### How did the court treat Santos’s claim that Kimmel ‘instigated’ the creation of the videos when analyzing transformative use?
Question:
Santos argued that because Kimmel submitted the prompts, he effectively designed the videos’ original purpose, undermining a finding of transformation. The court rejected this, emphasizing an objective reasonable-observer perspective under Warhol. Why doesn’t the requester’s role in shaping the original work’s content defeat a finding that the later broadcast use had a different purpose or character? Are there cases where the instigator’s role does matter to the first fair-use factor?
Answer:
Short answer: The Second Circuit rejected Santos’s “instigator/designer” argument. The court held that whether Kimmel prompted the videos does not defeat a finding of transformation because the governing inquiry asks what a reasonable observer would see the original works as conveying, not what the requester or the maker subjectively intended. The opinion relies on Warhol (and Supreme Court guidance) and declines to treat the requester’s role as dispositive.

Why — doctrinal explanation tied to the opinion
- The court frames the first fair-use factor around “transformative” use, which turns on whether the later work “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” (citing Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021)).  
- It then rejects Santos’s contention that Kimmel “instigat[ed]” and thus “designed” the originals, explaining that transformation is determined by “what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user.” The court quotes the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision: the subjective intent of the user (or the subjective interpretation of a court) does not determine the purpose of the use. (Op. citing Warhol, 598 U.S. at 544.)  
- Applying that test, the court accepts the District Court’s factual finding that a reasonable observer would view Santos’s Cameo videos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while the JKL segments had the contrary purpose (sarcastic criticism — “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money”). Because the public-facing purposes differ, the court concluded the JKL use was transformative and that the first factor strongly supports fair use.

On bad faith and the instigator argument
- Santos also argued Kimmel’s deception (using fake names) showed bad faith that undermines fair use. The court rejects that too. It notes Harper & Row’s old statement that “fair use presupposes good faith,” but observes subsequent Supreme Court decisions have been skeptical about giving bad faith a determinative role (citing Campbell and Google). More importantly, the complaint itself indicates defendants’ purpose was critical commentary, not a commercial supplanting of Santos’s market, so the court saw no basis to deny fair use on bad-faith grounds.

Are there cases where an instigator’s role matters?
- The opinion does not identify any decisions that treat a requester/instigator’s role as dispositive of transformation. Instead, it relies on Warhol’s reasonable-observer rule and the line of authority treating subjective intent (of user or rights-holder) as irrelevant to the characterization-of-purpose inquiry. The court also cites Harper & Row only to note historical language about good faith but explains that later precedent has undercut the significance of bad faith in the fair-use analysis. Thus, within this opinion the court does not recognize the instigator argument as a route to defeat transformation.

Bottom line
- Under this opinion, the fact that Kimmel prompted or “instigated” the Cameo videos did not defeat a finding of transformative use because the test is objective (what a reasonable observer would perceive) rather than subjective (who conceived the original purpose). The court declined to treat the requester’s conduct as controlling, and it pointed to precedent that limits the relevance of subjective intent or bad faith in the fair-use inquiry.

### Does alleged bad faith or deception in obtaining a copy negate fair use after Campbell and Google v. Oracle?
Question:
Santos argued Kimmel’s use of aliases on Cameo showed bad faith that should weigh against fair use (citing Harper & Row's 'good faith' language). The panel called that language questionable after Campbell and Google v. Oracle and found the complaint showed a purpose of commentary/criticism. In practical terms, does deception ever meaningfully tip the first factor or overall balance against fair use in the Second Circuit, and what kind of record would be needed for that?
Answer:
Short answer: Almost never, and only in a narrow set of circumstances. Since Campbell the Supreme Court and the Second Circuit have treated the Harper & Row “good faith” language as at best of limited weight; alleged bad faith or deception will only meaningfully hurt a fair‑use defense if the evidence shows the secondary use is not transformative and/or is intended to usurp the original author’s market (or otherwise make the use fall within the core proprietary interests the Copyright Act protects). Mere trickery in obtaining a work—without more—does not ordinarily defeat fair use in the Second Circuit.

Why (doctrine and the Santos opinion)
- Harper & Row stated that “fair use presupposes good faith and fair dealing.” Santos, however, quotes the panel’s rejection of that rigid rule: the court notes Campbell’s “skepticism about whether bad faith has any role in a fair use analysis” and Google v. Oracle’s further observation that that skepticism is “justifiable” because “copyright is not a privilege reserved for the well‑behaved.” Santos v. Kimmel, slip op. at pp. ___ (citing Campbell, Google). The panel therefore treated Harper & Row’s good‑faith language as weakened.
- The panel then made the practical point: even if bad faith were relevant, Santos’s complaint affirmatively pleaded that Kimmel’s use was for commentary/criticism (a paradigmatic transformative purpose), and the complaint did not plausibly allege an intent to “supplant” Santos’s commercially valuable rights. The opinion: “We reject Santos’s argument regardless of the relevance of good or bad faith,” and the first fair‑use factor “strongly supports a finding of fair use.” (Opinion ¶¶ explaining Harper & Row, Campbell, Google; and the first‑factor analysis.)
- The Second Circuit therefore treated bad faith as at most a subsidiary consideration that cannot override objective evidence of transformation and lack of market usurpation.

What a plaintiff would need to allege/produce to make bad faith matter in practice
If a plaintiff hopes to use deception/bad faith to defeat fair use in the Second Circuit, the record must do more than show mere trickery in obtaining the material. The plaintiff needs evidence linking the deception to one (or both) of two decisive showings:

1) The secondary use is not transformative (or would be improbably viewed as such by a reasonable observer):
   - Evidence that the defendant intended and intended the public to use the work for the same expressive purpose as the original (not a different purpose), undermining any claim of transformation.
   - Internal communications or planning documents showing the defendant commissioned/induced the creation of the content specifically to repackage and broadcast the exact same expressive content, rather than to comment upon it.
   - Admissions or a consistent pattern showing the defendant’s real purpose was to reproduce the original’s expressive value rather than to add new meaning or message.

2) The defendant intended to usurp a traditional or plausible licensing market for the original (market‑usurpation):
   - Evidence that there is (or was foreseeable) a licensing market for the very kind of reuse performed, and that the defendant sought to avoid licensing by deception.
   - Emails, invoices, or business plans showing defendants treated the acquired recordings as a commercial substitute for licensed access (e.g., selling clips, creating compilations sold/licensed to third parties).
   - Proof of actual market harm or a plausible, non‑speculative market substitution (not just reputational harm or criticism).

Put another way: allegations that the defendant lied to obtain material will tip the scales only if those lies are tied to a non‑transformative commercial plan to use the material as a substitute for the original or to strip the work of its original communicative content and exploit it commercially. Absent those ties—where, as here, the complaint itself frames the secondary use as critical commentary and shows no plausible market usurpation—the Second Circuit will not treat deception alone as dispositive.

How that applied in Santos v. Kimmel
- The panel found the complaint itself established a transformative purpose (commentary/criticism) and no plausible market usurpation; thus the alleged aliases/deception did not change the fair‑use outcome. The court explicitly rejected Santos’s attempt to rely on Harper & Row’s good‑faith language and affirmed that even if bad faith were relevant, the complaint’s own allegations undercut any showing that defendants sought to supplant Santos’s rights. (Opinion at the fair‑use discussion.)

Practical tip for litigation
- If you want bad faith to matter, plead and (for summary judgment/trial) prove the link between deception and either (a) a non‑transformative reuse, or (b) an intent and ability to exploit a licensing/market substitute. Scattered allegations of dishonesty without that nexus are unlikely to overcome transformation and lack of market harm.

Conclusion
Deception can matter, but only rarely and only when tied to non‑transformative intent or market usurpation. In the Second Circuit after Campbell and Google, mere bad faith in obtaining material is insufficient by itself to negate fair use.

### Why did commentary about Santos’s behavior (not the works themselves) still count as ‘transformative’ commentary?
Question:
The opinion frames the JKL segments’ purpose as commenting on Santos’s willingness to "say absurd things for money," which targets Santos rather than critiquing the expressive content of each Cameo video. Under Campbell, parody targets the original work, while satire can be more attenuated. How does Second Circuit law treat commentary that uses a work to criticize the subject or creator rather than the work’s expression—why is that still sufficiently transformative here?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the fair-use inquiry asks whether the secondary use adds a new purpose or character that a reasonable observer would perceive as different from the original — not whether the secondary user is literally criticizing the expressive choices of the original work. The JKL segments plainly did that: they used short Cameo clips as vehicles to lampoon Santos’s willingness “to say absurd things for money,” which a reasonable observer would see as adding a new, critical purpose. Under Second Circuit precedent (as the opinion explains), that kind of re‑contextualizing commentary is sufficiently transformative to weigh strongly for fair use.

Why (and how) the opinion says so — doctrine and textual support
- Transformative purpose is the central concern for the first fair‑use factor. The court reiterates the familiar test: whether the secondary use “merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” (Opinion quoting Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021).)
- The court applies the “reasonable observer” lens: transformation turns on how a reasonable observer would perceive the use, not on the subjective intent of either party. The opinion cites the Supreme Court’s admonition that subjective intent does not control and relies on Andy Warhol/Goldsmith for this point. The court therefore rejects Santos’s effort to equate his stated purpose in making the Cameo videos with the purpose of the JKL segments. The complaint itself, the court notes, describes the Cameos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while the JKL segments’ purpose (per the District Court and the complaint) was to comment on Santos’s readiness to say absurd things for money. That difference is what makes the use transformative (Opinion ¶ discussing reasonable observer and App’x 15, 235).
- The court also recognizes the distinction recognized in Campbell: parody targets the original work’s expressive content, while satire often targets something else (a public figure, institution, etc.) and may therefore be more attenuated. The opinion (and the authority it cites) treats such satire-style re‑use as capable of being transformative where it adds new expression, meaning, or message — here, mockery of Santos — rather than simply republishing the Cameos for the same purpose. (Opinion references Campbell and the Harper & Row/Google lineage in discussing intent and bad faith.)
- Practical result in the opinion: because the JKL segments used the Cameo clips to add a new critical message about Santos (a different purpose and character), the first fair‑use factor “strongly supports” fair use. The opinion then proceeds to consider the other factors and finds no displaced market, reinforcing that the uses were non‑superseding.

Takeaway for similar cases
- You don’t need to be directly critiquing the artistic form or content of a work to be transformative. Using a work as a vehicle to comment on its subject or creator can suffice, provided the secondary work adds new meaning or message that a reasonable observer would perceive as distinct.
- That is precisely the court’s conclusion here: the JKL segments recontextualized the Cameo videos to communicate a new, satirical message about Santos, and that recontextualization is the essence of the transformation the fair‑use doctrine protects.

### How did the court handle the third fair-use factor when Kimmel allegedly used the entirety of the Cameo videos?
Question:
The opinion does not dwell on factor three, but it seems the show aired full videos or substantial portions. In Second Circuit practice, when is taking an entire work permissible because it is 'reasonable in relation to the purpose' (e.g., for commentary or criticism)? What reasoning would justify minimal discussion of factor three here?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel treated factor three (amount/substantiality) as subordinate here because the other factors—especially the first (purpose and character)—clearly favored fair use. The court explained that Kimmel’s uses were transformative criticism/commentary, and that there was no plausible market usurpation under factor four; those conclusions made detailed analysis of the quantity used unnecessary. At the pleading stage, where the complaint itself and attached materials show a transformative, non‑market‑supplanting use, copying even an entire short Cameo video can be permissible.

Why that reasoning fits the law (and the opinion)

- Second Circuit approach to amount/substantiality. The amount-and-substantiality inquiry focuses on whether the portion taken is reasonable in relation to the user’s transformative purpose. The opinion applies the familiar multi‑factor framework (17 U.S.C. § 107) and emphasizes that the “purpose and character” inquiry turns on transformativeness—“whether the new work merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” Op. at 5–6 (quoting Andy Warhol Found., 11 F.4th at 37). When a use is transformative commentary or criticism, taking the amount necessary to make that commentary can be justified; the court therefore focused on whether Kimmel’s segments added new expressive content rather than on a granular tape‑count of seconds taken.

- How the opinion applied that to the Cameo videos. The panel concluded that a reasonable observer would see Santos’s videos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while Kimmel’s broadcasts used them for sarcastic criticism—i.e., a different purpose. Op. at 6–7. The court therefore found the first factor “strongly supports a finding of fair use.” Op. at 7. Because the complaint itself and the attached registrations and clips make that transformativeness apparent, the court treated the other factors (including amount) as less critical to the ultimate fair‑use determination.

- Market effect (factor four) cut the other way as well. The court rejected Santos’s market‑harm theory, explaining that fair use asks whether the second work “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” Op. at 8 (quoting Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 189 and Campbell, 510 U.S. at 592). Finding no plausible allegation that Kimmel’s segments competed with or supplanted Santos’s market for the Cameo videos, the fourth factor supported fair use and reduced the importance of a detailed factor‑three showing.

- Procedural posture matters. The court repeatedly framed its decision as a dismissal under Rule 12(b)(6) based on the complaint and materials attached or incorporated by reference. Op. at 4, 8–9. Where the pleadings themselves establish transformativeness and lack of market usurpation, the Second Circuit has allowed fair use to be resolved at the pleading stage without an exhaustive factor‑three analysis. The panel echoed that approach: “Because we need only consider ‘the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue’ to decide ‘the question of fair use,’ Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013) … we affirm.” Op. at 9.

Putting it together: minimal discussion of factor three was justified here because (1) the complaint and exhibits showed Kimmel used the videos for a clearly transformative purpose (criticism/sarcasm), (2) the complaint did not plausibly allege market usurpation, and (3) under Second Circuit practice a dominant finding on factors one and four can make a fine‑grained amount/substantiality analysis unnecessary at the pleading stage—even when the secondary user aired entire short works.

### Why did the court find no cognizable market harm under factor four despite Santos’s claim of lost licensing value?
Question:
The panel says we ask whether the secondary use 'usurps the market ... by offering a competing substitute' (citing Hachette) and notes Campbell’s point that creators are unlikely to license criticism. If Santos sells Cameo videos for money, why doesn’t a broadcast that repurposes them for ridicule count as market substitution or at least impairment of a derivative licensing market? What makes the JKL use non-substitutable for buyers of Cameo videos?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the fourth fair‑use factor asks whether the secondary use “usurps” the market for the original work by offering a competing substitute, and the court concluded (based on the nature of Santos’s Cameo videos and the JKL segments) that Kimmel’s broadcasts do not operate as a market substitute for purchasers or licensees of personalized Cameo videos. The opinion relies on Hachette and Campbell to explain that criticism/parody uses occupy a different market function from the original product and therefore ordinarily do not create the kind of cognizable market harm that defeats fair use.

Why the court reached that conclusion (with citation to the opinion)

- Legal standard applied: The court framed the fourth factor as whether the secondary use “usurps the market for the first by offering a competing substitute.” It quoted Hachette Book Group, 115 F.4th at 189 for that principle and cited Campbell v. Acuff‑Rose, 510 U.S. 569, 592 (1994), for the related point that creators of imaginative works are unlikely to license critical reviews or lampoons of their own productions (Op. at §I, para beginning “We are equally unpersuaded…” and the immediately following sentences).

- How that standard fits these facts: Santos’s pleading itself described the JKL segments’ purpose as “to comment on the willingness of Santos . . . to say absurd things for money” (Op. at §I). The court emphasized that fair‑use transformation is judged by what a reasonable observer would perceive, not the subjective intent of the parties (citing Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith). The court accepted that Santos intended his Cameo videos to convey “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity” (App’x 15), whereas JKL repurposed those videos into critical, mocking commentary. Because the JKL segments perform a different communicative function — critique/parody — they do not compete with or substitute for the market for Santos’s original personalized videos (Op. §I, para beginning “Santos also contends…” and later where Hachette and Campbell are cited).

- Complaint contradicts a claim of market usurpation: The court also noted the complaint’s own allegations undermined any suggestion the defendants sought to “supplant” Santos’s commercially valuable rights; instead the complaint paints defendants as motivated by criticism and commentary (Op. §I, second paragraph after discussing bad faith; citing Swatch and Authors Guild to the same effect). That factual framing made it implausible to infer the kind of market displacement required by factor four.

- Practical reason why the JKL use is non‑substitutable: Personalized Cameo videos function as paid, individualized messages (sent to specific recipients and conveying positive encouragement). A national broadcast that edits and repackages such videos for ridicule is not a functional substitute for a consumer who would pay for an original Cameo message; it serves a different expressive purpose and a different audience. The court relied on this market‑function distinction (Campbell’s point that creators will not license critical lampoons) to conclude Santos failed plausibly to allege the sort of licensing market harm that defeats fair use (Op. §I, quoting Campbell and Hachette).

Bottom line: The court found Santos’s allegation of lost licensing value insufficient because fair‑use market harm requires usurpation of an actual or likely licensing market by a competing substitute. Transformative criticism/parody that repurposes the work for a different communicative function (as the JKL segments do) does not plausibly function as such a substitute, so factor four did not weigh against fair use.

### How did the court determine that the Cameo videos were 'published,' and why does that matter to factor two?
Question:
The court took judicial notice of copyright registrations showing the videos were published the same day they were created and cited the §101 definition. Is it proper to treat statements on the registration about publication as true at the 12(b)(6) stage, or just notice of what the registration asserts? Do privately delivered, paywalled Cameo videos qualify as 'published' under §101, and how does their being published affect the factor-two analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
- The court relied on the Copyright Office registrations (App’x 160–69) and the statutory §101 definition of “publication” to find the Cameo videos were published the same day they were created. It did so by taking judicial notice of the registrations—as allowed by Island Software & Comput. Serv., Inc. v. Microsoft Corp., 413 F.3d 257, 261 (2d Cir. 2005) (see opinion at pp. __) — and treating their stated registration information as an adjudicative fact for the 12(b)(6) record.
- At the pleading stage a court may judicially notice the fact of a registration and the contents of the registration as published in the Copyright Office registry; that gives a plaintiff’s complaint less room to dispute the registration date/entry, but it is notice of the registration and its entries, not a merits determination of every factual claim. The Santos opinion treated the registrations as establishing that the works were “published” on the creation date (App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101).
- Whether a given privately delivered, paywalled Cameo video is legally “published” under §101 depends on whether the distribution constituted a general dissemination to the public (or an offering to distribute to a group resulting in dissemination). Private, limited transfers ordinarily do not qualify as “publication” under the statute; the opinion, however, did not analyze Cameo’s access rules in depth — it relied on the registrations’ publication statements.
- Publication matters to fair use factor two because unpublished works have stronger protection against unauthorized appropriation; a finding that the videos were published reduces (but does not eliminate) the second factor’s weight in favor of the copyright holder. Even with publication, the court concluded the overall fair-use analysis favored defendants.

Explanation and authorities from the opinion

1) Judicial notice of registrations at 12(b)(6)
- The opinion follows settled Second Circuit practice allowing courts on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion to take judicial notice of “copyright registrations[] as published in the Copyright Office’s registry.” The opinion cites Island Software, 413 F.3d at 261, and repeats that method (see citation at I; and the court’s statement that it may take judicial notice of registrations). The court therefore treated the registration entries (including the registration/publication date lines in App’x 160–69) as part of the record it could consider in deciding the motion to dismiss.
- Important nuance: judicial notice of the registration and its contents means the court may accept as indisputable the fact that a registration exists and what the registration says (e.g., the listed date of publication). It is not a merits ruling that every factual assertion in a registration is true in the sense of resolving contested factual disputes at summary judgment or trial. The opinion uses the registrations to establish publication timing as a pleaded fact for purposes of the fair-use analysis.

2) §101 “publication” and the Cameo videos
- The opinion expressly links the registrations to the statute: “the relevant copyright registrations establish that the videos were published on the same day they were created. See App’x 160–69; 17 U.S.C. § 101.” (Opinion, I.) That reference invokes the statutory definition of “publication” in §101 as the benchmark for what “published” means for copyright purposes.
- Section 101’s operative concept is dissemination to the public (or an offering to distribute to a group) as opposed to private, limited transfers. The court did not walk through a separate factual inquiry into Cameo’s delivery model (private message, paywalled access, etc.) in this opinion; instead it relied on the registration statements identifying the works as published on a given date.

3) Do privately delivered, paywalled Cameo videos qualify as “published”?
- The opinion does not definitively hold on the general question whether all Cameo-style deliveries are “publication” under §101. It treats these particular registration entries as establishing publication for the record before it. In general doctrinal terms (drawn from §101 as invoked in the opinion): a truly private transmission to a defined, limited recipient group will ordinarily be “unpublished” for copyright purposes; broader dissemination or an offering to distribute that results in public access will be “publication.” Because the court relied on the registrations rather than litigating the underlying access facts, a different factual record (or a registration that did not assert publication) could yield a different conclusion.

4) Why publication matters to factor two (the “nature of the copyrighted work”)
- The court cites the registrations’ publication finding when addressing the fair-use factors (Opinion, I). Factor two asks whether the work is of a type entitled to greater protection; unpublished works traditionally receive greater protection against appropriation than published works. Thus, a finding that a work is published reduces the degree to which factor two favors the copyright owner.
- Even so, publication is only one input. The opinion shows that despite the registrations’ showing of publication the court found the first factor (transformative purpose) and the fourth factor (no market usurpation) strongly favored fair use, and the published status did not prevent the court from concluding that fair use barred the infringement claim. In short: publication moves factor two toward the defendant but does not control the overall fair-use balance.

Practical takeaway for a law student
- At 12(b)(6) a court can and will take judicial notice of Copyright Office registrations and rely on the registration entries (dates, deposited titles, etc.) as record facts. That allows the court to accept a registration’s statement that a work was “published” in evaluating fair use.
- But judicial notice of a registration is not a substitute for factfinding about whether a particular method of delivery legally constitutes “publication” under §101; privately delivered/paywalled material may or may not be “published” depending on the access/dissemination facts. The Santos opinion relied on the registrations rather than resolving that factual question on the merits, and publication remained only one piece of the fair-use analysis.

### Why didn’t the court reach any First Amendment analysis beyond fair use?
Question:
Given the expressive, critical nature of the JKL segments, I expected a First Amendment discussion. Instead, the court treats fair use as dispositive. Is fair use considered the constitutional accommodation for speech interests within copyright, making separate First Amendment balancing unnecessary unless fair use is inadequate? Under what circumstances would the Second Circuit add a First Amendment layer beyond the §107 analysis?
Answer:
Short answer
The panel treated fair use as dispositive because, applying the §107 factors to the facts pleaded, fair use plainly barred Santos’s copyright claim. The opinion follows settled Second Circuit practice that fair use is the ordinary, context-sensitive mechanism for accommodating expressive interests in copyright; where the fair‑use inquiry resolves the dispute (as it did here — transformative purpose, little market harm), there is no need to perform a separate First Amendment balancing. The court would add an explicit First Amendment analysis only if the fair‑use framework did not adequately resolve the constitutional free‑speech concern — i.e., if fair use were not clearly dispositive on the record.

Why the court stopped at fair use (with citations to the opinion)

- The opinion treats fair use as the doctrinal vehicle that protects commentary and criticism in copyright disputes. It does not say fair use supplants the First Amendment, but it shows the court’s view that the §107 analysis embodies the relevant accommodation for expressive uses: transformation and lack of market usurpation are the copyright-side standards that vindicate speech interests in many cases.

The court thus resolved the dispute within copyright doctrine rather than conducting separate First Amendment analysis.

Two indicia in the opinion support this:
  - The court emphasizes that fair use is flexible and context‑sensitive and can resolve disputes on the record where the §107 factors are clear (citing TCA Television and Cariou). That implies that when those factors are not clear, the court may need to consider additional constitutional grounds.
  - The opinion distinguishes statutory fair‑use doctrine from other concerns (e.g., it rejects treating defendant bad faith as automatically fatal), showing a preference to resolve cases within copyright doctrine unless a separate constitutional claim is presented or necessary.
- Put another way: if a claim raised serious, unresolved First Amendment questions that §107 could not answer (for example, where a plaintiff plausibly alleged that a use both enabled market usurpation and chilled speech, or where the statutory factors yield a close result at summary judgment or trial), the court would likely engage in explicit constitutional analysis. The Santos opinion does not reach that point because the statutory factors were one‑sided in the defendants’ favor.

Bottom line
The Second Circuit simply applied §107 and found the JKL segments plainly fair use — transformative criticism and no plausible market usurpation — and so had no occasion to perform an extra First Amendment balancing. The court’s approach is consistent with treating fair use as the primary, often dispositive, doctrinal vehicle for accommodating speech interests in copyright cases; only when fair use cannot resolve the dispute would the court add a separate constitutional layer.

Explanation tied to the opinion
- Jurisdictional basis: The opinion opens by identifying the case as an appeal from “a final judgment of the United States District Court for the Southern District of New York” dismissing Santos’s claims, and the Court proceeds to review that dismissal. That language (appeal from final judgment) is the paradigmatic § 1291 posture. The Court does not invoke interlocutory jurisdictional statutes (e.g., § 1292), nor any statutory writ — it treats this as a straight appeal from final judgment.

- Ability to affirm on an alternative ground: The opinion itself demonstrates the principle. The District Court dismissed on two principal bases: (1) copyright claims barred by fair use, and (2) state-law claims either preempted or failing on the merits. That is a textbook instance of an appellate court affirming on an alternative ground supported by the record and governed by de novo legal review.

  - De novo review: The Court relied on de novo review of legal issues arising on a Rule 12(b)(6) record (as the opinion states), which makes it appropriate to resolve legal defects not reached below so long as resolution does not depend on disputed facts.

  - Avoiding unwarranted factfinding: The Court declined to reach preemption — a question that might have required more layered analysis — but did not invent factual findings; it relied on the pleadings and registrations.   - Preservation and fairness: Although the opinion does not elaborate on preservation doctrine, its structure (noting de novo review, citing the complaint and incorporated documents, and explicitly stating it “need not resolve” preemption) illustrates the prudential constraint: appellate courts prefer not to decide new issues when doing so would be unfair to a party or would require evidence beyond the record.

Takeaway for future cases

- That flexibility is strongest for pure legal questions decided de novo on the existing record (as here with Rule 12(b)(6) issues); it is limited when meaningful factual development would be necessary or when deciding a new issue would prejudice a party who lacked notice or an opportunity to develop the record.

### Why apply Illinois law to the Cameo Terms-of-Service claim if Santos was not a party to that contract?
Question:
The court applied Illinois law (the ToS governing-law clause) to hold Santos wasn’t a third-party beneficiary. How do courts select governing law when the plaintiff is a non-signatory asserting third-party rights? Is it routine to honor the contract’s choice-of-law clause to determine if a third party can enforce it, or is a separate choice-of-law analysis usually required?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. When a plaintiff who is not a contracting party seeks to enforce contractual rights as a third‑party beneficiary, courts commonly apply the governing law the parties chose in the contract to decide whether the plaintiff has enforceable third‑party rights. That is what the Second Circuit did here: it invoked the ToS’s choice‑of‑law clause (Illinois law) and, under Illinois law, rejected Santos’s third‑party‑beneficiary theory because Illinois presumes against conferring benefits on non‑contracting third parties.

Why (legal doctrine and reasoning)
- A contract’s choice‑of‑law clause governs interpretation of the contract and the parties’ rights under it. When the threshold question is whether anyone outside the signatories may enforce the contract, that is a question about the contract’s scope and intent — ordinarily answered under the law the contracting parties selected.
- The Santos opinion follows that approach: it begins the breach‑of‑contract analysis by saying the Terms of Service are governed by Illinois law and then applies Illinois third‑party‑beneficiary doctrine. See Opinion, II (first paragraph) (“Under Illinois law, which governs the Terms of Service, there is a ‘strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.’” (quoting Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur)).
- The court used a core Illinois rule — that implication of third‑party beneficiaries must be “so strong as to be practically an express declaration” — to reject Santos’s claim. The opinion also relied on an internal feature of the contract (that other provisions expressly create third‑party rights) to show that the parties knew how to confer third‑party benefits when they intended to do so. See id. (noting “other provisions . . . do contain third‑party beneficiary language, indicating that when the parties . . . intended to allow provisions . . . to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly”).

What this means in practice
- It is routine for courts to honor a contract’s choice‑of‑law clause when deciding whether a non‑signatory is a third‑party beneficiary, because that inquiry is a contract‑interpretation question for the governing law the parties selected.
- The opinion demonstrates the routine application: rather than conducting a freestanding or multi‑factor choice‑of‑law inquiry, the court applied the ToS’s specified Illinois law and then applied Illinois precedent (Barry) to the beneficiary question.
- That routine approach has limits (not addressed or altered in this opinion): if the enforceability question raised independent tort or extra‑contractual issues governed by different contacts or public‑policy concerns, a court might need a broader choice‑of‑law analysis. But in Santos the court treated the beneficiary issue as an ordinary contract‑law question for the ToS’s governing law and resolved it under Illinois law.

Bottom line
The Second Circuit’s reasoning is straightforward: apply the contract’s governing‑law clause to interpret the contract and determine whether the parties intended to confer third‑party enforcement rights. Under the Illinois law specified in the Cameo ToS, Santos’s status as a non‑signatory did not create an enforceable third‑party beneficiary right.

### What made Santos’s implied-in-fact contract theory inadequate under New York law at the pleading stage?
Question:
The panel cites Caniglia and DG & A Management to say Santos failed to plead 'essential terms' and a 'meeting of the minds' that Kimmel would adhere to Cameo’s ToS. What specific terms would need to be pleaded to establish an implied-in-fact promise in this context (e.g., scope of permitted use, commercial restrictions, attribution)? Can industry custom or the platform’s standard practices supply those terms without express assent?
Answer:
Short answer
Santos’s implied‑in‑fact theory failed because he pleaded no concrete agreement—no “meeting of the minds”—about the key terms that would convert a Cameo transfer into an enforceable bargain with Kimmel, and he did not allege facts showing those terms were supplied or accepted by the parties. Under New York law (as the panel applied Caniglia and DG & A), an implied‑in‑fact contract must plead the essential terms with non‑conclusory factual detail; Santos pled only the existence of Cameo’s Terms of Service and that videos were submitted, not the specific promises Kimmel allegedly made or accepted.

What the complaint needed to allege (the “essential terms”)
To state an implied‑in‑fact license/promise in this platform context, the complaint must allege the operative terms with enough specificity that a court can identify what was promised and that the parties agreed. That generally includes (at minimum):

- the grant or license scope: what uses Kimmel was permitted to make (e.g., “public broadcast on television,” “use in news/criticism,” “limited personal, noncommercial display,” etc.);
- any temporal or geographic limits: duration of the permission and where it applied;
- commercial restrictions or permitted commercial exploitation: whether resale, editing, broadcast, sublicensing, or monetization were allowed;
- exclusivity or sublicensing rights: whether Santos retained exclusive control or whether defendants could sublicense or transfer the material;
- attribution and moral‑right terms (if relevant): whether Santos required credit or restrictions on derogatory uses;
- consideration or bargained‑for exchange (what Santos received and what was promised in return); and
- any mechanics for acceptance/notice: how a user manifested acceptance (e.g., messages, platform confirmations, negotiated terms).

Why Santos’s pleading fell short under the cited authorities
- Caniglia (cited by the panel): requires pleading “the essential terms of the parties’ ... contract, including those specific provisions of the contract upon which liability is predicated.” The panel found Santos “did not ‘allege, in nonconclusory language, . . . the essential terms’” (App’x 22 ¶¶78–79 and Caniglia citation). Santos therefore failed to identify which provisions of the Cameo ToS (or which additional terms) Kimmel allegedly promised to follow.
- DG & A (cited by the panel): requires factual allegations showing “an indication of a meeting of minds” that the defendant would comply with the asserted terms. The panel held Santos did not plausibly allege any such meeting of minds that Kimmel agreed to adhere to Cameo’s ToS (App’x 22; DG & A citation).

Can industry custom or platform practice supply missing terms?
Possibly, but only if pleaded with factual specificity. Industry custom and platform norms can fill gaps when a plaintiff pleads facts showing:

- those customs are uniform and well‑established in the relevant market;
- the parties knew or should have known those customs applied to their transaction; and
- the defendant acted in a manner consistent with accepting those customary terms (e.g., by following platform workflows that incorporate the terms, communicating acceptance, or negotiating around the terms).

What the opinion requires here is more than asserting “industry practice” or attaching Cameo’s ToS. The panel stressed (1) Santos was not a party to the ToS and (2) the ToS itself contains third‑party beneficiary language only where the drafter expressly intended it—showing courts require clear indicia that non‑parties were intended beneficiaries. Thus, to rely on custom or platform practice, Santos would have needed to plead facts showing either (a) Kimmel affirmatively agreed (by message, express acceptance, or conduct) to be bound by the specific ToS provisions that protect Santos, or (b) the industry/platform practices are so clearly and consistently applied that a reasonable user would understand the particular restrictions and that Kimmel accepted them in ordering and receiving the video.

Practical pleading roadmap (what a viable complaint would allege)
- Quote or attach the exact ToS provisions Santos relies on and explain how they protect a third‑party creator (or allege a separate promise by Kimmel mirroring those provisions).
- Allege concrete communications or conduct from Kimmel showing assent (e.g., messages acknowledging limits on use, platform confirmations visible to both parties, or Kimmel’s acceptance of specific restrictions).
- Plead the specific uses Kimmel made that violated those terms (e.g., “broadcast on national television without permission,” “edited for parody and monetized”), tying each to an identified breached term.
- If invoking industry custom, plead what the custom is, why it governs this transaction, and factual steps by the parties showing they acted consistent with that custom.

Bottom line
Under Caniglia and DG & A (as applied by the panel), Santos failed at the pleading stage because he did not identify the essential contractual terms or allege a plausible meeting of the minds that Kimmel agreed to be bound by the Cameo ToS (or equivalent restrictions). Industry custom or platform practices can supply terms, but only when the complaint pleads facts showing the customs actually governed the parties’ exchange or that the defendant accepted those terms. Without those factual allegations, an implied‑in‑fact contract claim under New York law is inadequate.

### Why did the fraudulent inducement claim fail for lack of 'out-of-pocket' loss, and what would count as such a loss here?
Question:
The court, applying Lama Holding and Connaughton, found no actual pecuniary loss. If Santos spent time making the videos and alleges he was tricked into doing so under false pretenses, why isn’t the value of his time or the price differential between a personal Cameo and a commercial broadcast license an out-of-pocket loss? What allegations would satisfy New York’s 'out-of-pocket' rule in a case like this?
Answer:
Short answer
The claim failed because New York law requires a pleaded, concrete pecuniary loss from the fraudulent inducement (the “out‑of‑pocket” rule), and Santos’s complaint did not allege any such loss. Saying he was “tricked” into making videos and spent time doing so is not, as pleaded, an actual pecuniary injury under Lama Holding and Connaughton. To survive, Santos would have needed to allege a specific monetary loss fairly traceable to his reliance on defendants’ misrepresentations — for example, unreimbursed production costs, a forgone licensing fee or cancelled contract he would have obtained but for the deception, or other measurable expenditures or lost revenue caused by the fraud.

Why the complaint failed under Lama Holding / Connaughton
- Lama Holding (cited in the opinion) establishes that New York fraudulent inducement damages are governed by the “out‑of‑pocket” rule: the plaintiff must allege actual pecuniary loss resulting from reliance on the false statement. The panel says Santos “failed to allege that he suffered any actual out‑of‑pocket loss” and therefore the fraud claim was rightly dismissed. Op. § II (last paragraph) (quoting Lama and Connaughton citations).
- Connaughton, also cited, confirms the point: New York law requires a real economic loss caused by the fraud (not merely hurt feelings, wasted time without pecuniary consequence, or an abstract loss of a nonmonetary expectation). The opinion applies that standard and concludes Santos did not plead such a loss. Op. § II.

Why Santos’s alleged losses (time and price differential) did not suffice as pleaded
- Time spent: The opinion treats Santos’s allegations about being “tricked into” producing videos and spending time on them as insufficient because they do not identify a pecuniary outlay or loss. Mere time or effort is not automatically an “out‑of‑pocket” monetary loss under Lama/Connaughton unless the complaint ties that time to a concrete economic harm (e.g., unreimbursed production expenses, loss of billable hours with a quantifiable value, or lost income).
- Price differential between a personal Cameo and a commercial broadcast license: The opinion implicitly rejects the idea that a hypothetical license value alone satisfies the rule unless pleaded as an actual economic loss. Santos did not allege that he was deprived of a licensing fee he actually would have received, that he attempted to license the clips and was precluded from doing so, or that he lost a specific licensing opportunity or revenue stream. Without allegations that defendants’ conduct resulted in an actual, provable forfeited license fee or other monetary loss, the “price differential” is an abstract theory of loss, not an out‑of‑pocket injury.

What allegations would have satisfied New York’s out‑of‑pocket rule here
To plead a viable fraudulent inducement claim under Lama and Connaughton, Santos would need to allege specific, traceable monetary losses caused by his reliance on defendants’ misrepresentations. Examples grounded in the facts of this case would include:
- Unreimbursed production costs: itemized expenses (equipment rental, studio time, props, travel, crew payments) he incurred to make the videos that he would not have incurred but for defendants’ misrepresentations. Allegations should state amounts and causal connection.
- Lost licensing revenue or forgone deal: a plausible allegation that Santos had an extant or likely licensing opportunity for commercial use of the videos (with an expected fee), that defendants’ broadcast preempted or destroyed that opportunity, and a quantifiable amount of lost revenue (or a specific license fee he would have obtained).
- Payment or fee diversion: allegations that Santos would have charged a commercial licensing fee for broadcast use (distinct from Cameo’s personal‑message pricing), that he did not receive such a fee, and a concrete estimate of that fee tied to industry norms or his historical licensing practice.
- Out‑of‑pocket reliance expenditures: money actually paid in reliance on defendants’ promises (for example, if Santos had to hire third‑party editors or pay expedited fees because he believed the request was personal, and those payments were lost).
- Actual diminution in market value: plausible allegations showing measurable diminution in the videos’ commercial value (with a dollar estimate), caused by defendants’ conduct and not by normal market forces.

Form and specificity: the opinion relies on pleading deficiencies. So to survive dismissal the complaint should do more than assert that defendants “tricked” him and that he “lost value.” It must allege concrete expenditures or lost revenues, with factual detail linking those losses to defendants’ misrepresentations — the sort of nonconclusory, quantifiable allegations the panel says were missing. See Op. § II (discussion rejecting Santos’s conclusory pleading and affirming dismissal for failure to allege actual out‑of‑pocket loss).

Bottom line
Under Lama and Connaughton, New York fraudulent inducement damages require an actual pecuniary loss. Santos’s allegations about time spent and speculative price differentials, as pleaded, did not identify a specific monetary loss caused by the defendants. To survive, he would have needed to plead concrete monetary expenditures or a specific lost revenue/licensing opportunity traceable to the alleged fraud.

### Could the district court have declined supplemental jurisdiction over the state-law claims after dismissing the federal copyright claims?
Question:
Because the court held the copyright claims barred by fair use at the pleading stage, could (or should) the district court have dismissed the state-law claims without prejudice under 28 U.S.C. § 1367(c)? On appeal, does the Second Circuit’s merits affirmance of the state-law dismissals implicitly approve the exercise of supplemental jurisdiction, or is that issue waived/harmless here?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes—the district court could have declined to exercise supplemental jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1367(c) after disposing of the federal copyright claims. But the Second Circuit’s opinion does not meaningfully fault that exercise of jurisdiction because the panel affirmed the state-law dismissals on their merits; any error in retaining jurisdiction would therefore be harmless (or moot) here.

Explanation and reasoning

- The statutory rule. Section 1367(c) gives district courts discretion to decline supplemental jurisdiction when the district court has dismissed all claims over which it had original jurisdiction. That is a discretionary doctrine (often applied under the familiar considerations of judicial economy, convenience, fairness, and comity). The question whether a district court should have exercised that discretion is ordinarily a procedural one for the district court.

- What happened in this case. The district court dismissed Santos’s federal copyright claims at the pleading stage (finding fair use). The district court also dismissed his state-law claims (either as preempted or for failure to state a claim). On appeal the Second Circuit (1) affirmed that the copyright claims were barred by fair use, and (2) independently concluded “that each of Santos’s state law claims failed to state a claim” and affirmed the dismissal of those claims. - Why § 1367(c) would have been available but is not outcome-determinative. It would have been a permissible course for the district court, after resolving the federal claims, to decline to exercise supplemental jurisdiction and dismiss the state-law claims without prejudice under § 1367(c). That is within the district court’s discretion. But the Second Circuit did not reverse the state-law dismissals on jurisdictional grounds — instead it affirmed them on the merits (finding the breach and fraud claims deficient under state-law standards). Because the appellate court independently sustains the state-law dismissals, any error by the district court in retaining supplemental jurisdiction would be harmless for purposes of the disposition: the state claims would be dismissed under the panel’s merits analysis regardless of how jurisdiction had been exercised.

- Practical consequence for appellate review. When an appellate court affirms state-law dismissals on their merits, it effectively obviates any reversible error that might have attended the district court’s decision to retain or decline supplemental jurisdiction. Here the panel’s explicit merits rulings (fair use bars the federal claims; state claims fail to state a claim) mean that plaintiffs obtain no relief even if the district court had instead dismissed the state claims without prejudice under § 1367(c). Thus the jurisdictional issue is not necessary to the Court’s disposition and is functionally harmless in this appeal.

Concluding point: If you were litigating this below, invoking § 1367(c) to seek dismissal without prejudice would be a sensible tactical move (to preserve state claims for refile in state court). But on appeal from a merits dismissal of the state claims, the Second Circuit’s affirmance of those merits rulings renders any question about the district court’s discretionary exercise of supplemental jurisdiction immaterial to the outcome.

### How does Melendez’s 'extra element' test for preemption apply to contract-based restrictions on use of copyrighted works?
Question:
The panel avoids deciding preemption but notes Santos’s view that his contract claims are 'qualitatively different.' Under Second Circuit law, when do contractual promises (e.g., not to broadcast commercially, or to limit uses) add the 'extra element' that avoids § 301 preemption? Would specific, bargained-for use restrictions in a ToS or side agreement typically survive preemption if adequately pleaded?
Answer:
Short answer: Under Second Circuit precedent (Melendez), a contract claim avoids § 301 preemption only when the contract imposes an “extra element” — a qualitatively different duty — that is not merely a restatement of the bundle of exclusive rights Congress gave copyright owners (reproduction, distribution, public performance, derivative works, etc.). A specific, bargained-for use restriction (for example, an express promise not to broadcast commercially or to limit certain downstream uses) will typically survive preemption only if the plaintiff pleads (1) a concrete contractual obligation that is distinct from the copyright monopoly, (2) that the obligation was actually owed to the plaintiff (e.g., the plaintiff is a party or an intended third‑party beneficiary, or there is a plausible implied-in-fact contract), and (3) damages or remedies that flow from breach of that contractual obligation rather than from the defendant’s exercise of the copyright owner’s exclusive rights.

Why — the doctrine and standard (as applied in Santos)
- Melendez’s “extra element” test (as the Santos panel cites Melendez) asks whether the state-law claim rests on an additional element beyond making, distributing, or publicly performing the copyrighted work. If the additional element creates a qualitatively different obligation, the claim is not preempted. The Santos opinion acknowledges that characterization (Santos’s claim is “qualitatively different,” Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022)), but the panel did not resolve preemption because it disposed of the state-law claims on other grounds.
- The practical import: promises that create contractual duties unrelated to copyright’s exclusive rights — for example, express confidentiality promises, affirmative covenants about how a recording may be used (e.g., “not for broadcast,” “for private use only,” or “only for noncommercial distribution”), or other obligations that regulate behavior beyond mere copying/use — are the kind of “extra element” that can avoid preemption under Melendez.

How that works in pleading and proof (why many ToS/side‑agreement claims fail)
- Specificity of the contract term. The plaintiff must plead the actual, specific contractual term that creates the extra obligation (not just a conclusory allegation that “uses were limited”). The Santos panel rejects Santos’s breach‑of‑Cameo‑Terms claim because he is not a party to those Terms and points to no “express declaration” making him an intended third‑party beneficiary; under the applicable law the court applied a strong presumption against third‑party benefits and found no plausible basis to enforce the Terms as to Santos. In short: pleading must show that the promise existed and was directed to the plaintiff.
- Relationship to copyright rights. The contractual promise must be conceptually distinct from the copyright owner’s exclusive rights. A clause that merely replicates a copyright prohibition (e.g., “you may not reproduce or broadcast this work” stated as a contractual restatement of statutory rights) is unlikely to supply the required “extra element.” By contrast, a bargained-for limitation that regulates non-copyrightal aspects of use (time windows, geography, method of exhibition, confidentiality, agreed attribution, etc.) is more clearly “qualitatively different.”
- Pleading damages tied to contract breach. Courts look to whether the injury alleged flows from a breach of the contract obligation, not simply from the exercise of rights that would violate the Copyright Act. The Santos opinion illustrates a related pleading failure: the court dismissed the implied-contract and fraudulent-inducement claims for lack of nonconclusory allegations of essential terms and for failure to allege cognizable out-of-pocket loss.

Bottom line for ToS or side agreements
- Specific, bargained-for use restrictions can survive preemption in the Second Circuit if pleaded with sufficient particularity to show (a) a definite contractual obligation owed to the plaintiff (party status or clear third‑party beneficiary or plausible implied‑in‑fact contract), (b) that the obligation is not merely a restatement of statutory copyright rights but adds a qualitatively different duty, and (c) damages or remedies traceable to the contract breach rather than to the copy/use itself.
- But boilerplate or vaguely alleged ToS claims, or claims by non-parties who fail to allege entitlement to enforce the term (as the Santos panel found here), will typically be dismissed either for failure to state the contractual elements or (if properly pleaded but substantively duplicative of copyright rights) as preempted.

References to the opinion
- The panel cites Melendez for the “qualitatively different”/“extra element” principle (II, opening paragraph) but declines to decide preemption because Santos’s contract claims failed on state‑law pleading grounds.
- The court dismissed the Cameo Terms claim because Santos was not a party and identified no “express declaration” that the Terms applied to noncontracting third parties (II, ¶ on Cameo Terms), and dismissed the implied‑in‑fact contract claim for failing to plead essential terms or a meeting of the minds (II, ¶ on implied contract).
- The outcome shows that even where the Melendez test could save a contract claim from preemption in theory, plaintiffs still must adequately plead the contractual basis and damages under state law to survive dismissal.

If you’re drafting or evaluating such a claim: plead the operative contract language (or concrete facts supporting third‑party beneficiary/implied contract status), explain how the obligation differs from the copyright monopoly (identify the extra element), and allege damages tied to that contractual breach.

### Why did the court rely on a 'reasonable observer' perception of the Cameo videos’ original purpose rather than Santos’s own stated intent?
Question:
The opinion cites Warhol to discount subjective intent and notes Santos’s own complaint described the videos as conveying 'hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.' How does the 'reasonable observer' approach operate at the pleadings stage when a plaintiff insists his actual purpose matched the defendant’s characterization? Does the court’s ability to view the works themselves override contrary conclusory allegations?
Answer:
Short answer: Because under Warhol/Goldsmith and settled fair-use doctrine the “purpose” inquiry is objective — what a reasonable observer would view the work as doing — and at the 12(b)(6) stage a court may look to the works themselves (and the complaint’s own allegations/exhibits) to resolve an issue that is facially dispositive. Santos’s bare, conclusory assertion about his subjective intent cannot override the reasonable-observer inference the court drew from the videos and the complaint.

- The complaint’s own allegations cut against Santos. The court pointed out that Santos’s complaint itself described the videos as conveying “feelings of hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity.” Op. § I (citing App’x 15). That allegation, combined with the content of the videos and the JKL segments, supported the view that a reasonable observer would not see the originals as already serving the parody/criticism function Kimmel used them for.

- What courts may consider at the pleading stage. The panel reiterated the Rule 12(b)(6) framework: it may consider “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference.” Op. § DISCUSSION (citing DiFolco). Relying on Cariou and TCA Television, the court emphasized that “we need only consider ‘the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue’ to decide ‘the question of fair use’” and concluded fair use was clear on the face of the complaint. Op. § I (quoting Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d 694, 707 (2d Cir. 2013)).

- Why Santos’s contrary conclusory allegation lost. The court treated Santos’s claim that he intended the same purpose as Kimmel’s use as a conclusory allegation that cannot overcome an objective reading of the works plus the complaint’s own descriptive language. In short, where the works (and the complaint) plainly show the secondary use is transformative and no plausible competing inference exists, courts can decide fair use on a motion to dismiss. Op. § I (citing TCA Television and Hachette Book Grp.).

Takeaway for pleading practice
- If you want to preserve an argument that your work’s original communicative purpose matches the defendant’s later use, you must plead nonconclusory, fact-specific allegations showing why a reasonable observer would perceive that purpose (and, where possible, attach the works or precise descriptions). Mere assertions of subjective intent will not defeat a fair-use dismissal when the works themselves and other complaint allegations point the other way.

### Did the court’s finding that defendants aimed to 'comment and criticize' foreclose any secondary liability theories against ABC and Disney?
Question:
The panel affirmed dismissal across the board once fair use applied. If fair use is a complete defense to direct infringement, does that necessarily defeat vicarious/contributory theories against the broadcaster and parent company? Are there scenarios where a secondary-liability theory could survive even if the use is ultimately held fair?
Answer:
Short answer: In this case, yes—the panel’s fair‑use ruling effectively foreclosed Santos’s secondary‑liability theories as pleaded. The opinion treats fair use as an affirmative defense that “excuses what might otherwise be considered infringing behavior,” and because the court concluded the JKL segments were fair use it disposed of the copyright claims (and thus left no underlying direct infringement on which contributory or vicarious liability could rest). See pp. 6–8 (fair use as an affirmative defense and application of §107), and the Court’s holding that “fair use bars Santos’s copyright claims even at the pleading stage.”

Why that follows from the opinion
- The panel framed fair use as excusing otherwise infringing conduct (citing Hachette Book Grp., 115 F.4th at 179) and applied the four §107 factors to conclude the broadcast was fair use. Once the Court concluded the defendant’s use was noninfringing as a matter of law, there was no predicate infringement left for secondary‑liability theories to attach to. The opinion affirms dismissal “of Santos’s copyright claims” because “fair use bars” them. (Opinion pp. 6–8 and conclusion.)

What the opinion does and does not decide
- The opinion does not separately analyze or cite precedent on contributory or vicarious liability; it resolves the case by finding fair use and dismissing the copyright claims. That procedural outcome means the panel did not need to (and did not) adopt a standalone holding about every possible theory of secondary liability.

When a secondary‑liability claim could survive (consistent with the opinion’s reasoning)
- The opinion’s logic points to the limiting principle: secondary liability depends on a viable underlying infringement claim. Thus, under the reasoning here, secondary liability could survive only if there is a plausible predicate direct infringement that fair use does not negate. Put differently:
  - If a defendant’s conduct were held not to be fair use (or if fair use applied to some defendants/uses but not others), then contributory or vicarious claims could proceed.
  - If a secondary actor engaged in independently wrongful acts beyond merely airing or hosting allegedly fair uses—acts that themselves meet the elements of infringement or fall outside §107’s protection—then secondary liability might be viable.
  - Likewise, if factual differences showed the use was not transformative or did supplant Santos’s market (the very tests the opinion applies), the predicate infringement would remain and secondary theories could be pursued.

Finally, note what the panel explicitly rejected here: Santos’s attempt to defeat fair use by pointing to Kimmel’s alleged bad faith. The Court declined to treat subjective bad faith as dispositive and found the complaint itself showed defendants were motivated by “comment and criticism,” a finding that supported fair use (pp. 6–8). That factual posture—use characterized as transformative commentary and thus noninfringing—was the basis for dismissing all copyright claims in this case, including any that would depend on a showing of underlying infringement.

So: the panel’s fair‑use ruling did, in practical effect, foreclose secondary‑liability theories here because it eliminated the predicate infringement. But if the facts or law showed no fair use (or showed additional wrongful conduct by the secondary defendant), secondary‑liability theories could still survive in another case.

### Does the possibility that Kimmel could be a co-author or joint author of the videos (by crafting prompts) affect the fair-use analysis?
Question:
Santos argued Kimmel 'designed' the videos’ purpose by submitting the prompts. If, hypothetically, Kimmel’s contributions rose to the level of joint authorship (or an implied license existed), would that change the fair-use calculus, or simply eliminate infringement altogether? How do courts treat authorship or implied-license arguments when the same facts are used to claim the secondary use is non-transformative?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes — but they are different routes to the same end. If Kimmel actually were a joint author of the Cameo videos, or had an (enforceable) implied license from Santos to use them, there would be no infringement to defend against and the fair‑use inquiry would be beside the point. By contrast, arguing that Kimmel “designed” the videos’ purpose is a different kind of argument when used to defeat fair use: courts do not treat a defendant’s claimed role in creating the underlying work as dispositive of whether the defendant’s later use is “transformative.” The Santos opinion illustrates both points.

Why (and how) the opinion draws the line
- The opinion treats Santos’s “designer”/authorship argument as a fair‑use contest about purpose and character, not as an ownership question. Santos argued Kimmel “designed” the videos by submitting prompts and therefore the JKL segments were not transformative. The court rejected that framing: transformation is judged from the perspective of a reasonable observer, not by the subjective creator/secondary‑user intent. See slip op. (Sect. I) (citing Andy Warhol Found. v. Goldsmith and noting “whether a secondary use is transformative turns on what a reasonable observer thinks, not the subjective intent of the copyright holder or that of the secondary user”).
- The court therefore resolved Santos’s argument under the first fair‑use factor (purpose/character) and found the JKL segments strongly transformative (critical/satirical commentary) even accepting Santos’s pleading that he intended uplifting messages. The court relied on the reasonable‑observer standard to reject Santos’s attempt to convert Kimmel’s prompting into a non‑transformative purpose. Slip op. Sect. I.

What the opinion does (and does not) decide about co‑authorship or implied license
- The opinion does not find or adopt a co‑authorship or implied‑license ruling in favor of Kimmel; it reaches only the fair‑use defense on the pleadings. It therefore does not hold that co‑authorship or an implied license existed. See overall disposition (affirming dismissal on fair use and dismissing state claims on other grounds).
- The opinion does, however, treat Santos’s state‑law contract and implied‑contract claims separately and dismisses them for failure to plead the necessary elements under applicable state law (no pleading of enforceable third‑party benefit or essential terms of an implied contract). Slip op. Sect. II. That indicates the court saw the licensing/contract path as a different legal theory from the fair‑use inquiry and concluded Santos hadn’t plausibly stated it.

Practical implication for litigation strategy (as reflected in the opinion)
- If a defendant can prove joint authorship or an implied license in fact, that is a straight‑forward defense: no infringement, no fair‑use determination necessary. The opinion implies that ownership/licensing issues are proper grounds to eliminate infringement where they are adequately pleaded and proven, but it did not reach such a factual finding here.
- If the defendant instead relies on their role in creating the original work to argue the later use is non‑transformative, the court will apply the reasonable‑observer/transformativeness test rather than accept the user’s claimed purpose as dispositive. Santos attempted that strategy and the court rejected it on the pleadings.

Bottom line
- Co‑authorship or an actual (enforceable) implied license, if proven, would negate liability (no need to litigate fair use).  
- But merely arguing that the defendant “designed” the videos or submitted prompts to show the later broadcast use is non‑transformative will not substitute for ownership/licensing proof; courts (as here) will apply the reasonable‑observer standard in the fair‑use analysis and treat authorship/licensing as distinct issues to be pleaded and proven under copyright and contract principles.
