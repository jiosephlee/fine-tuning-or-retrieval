Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

Chapter 9: # Platform Terms and Third-Party Beneficiary Doctrine

The dispute in Santos v. Kimmel provides a useful entry point into a recurrent problem in the law of online creator marketplaces: when, if ever, a person who is not a party to a platform agreement may sue to enforce its terms. George Santos alleged that Jimmy Kimmel breached Cameo’s Terms of Service when he used the platform to request personalized videos and then exploited those videos on television. The Second Circuit did not reach whether the Copyright Act preempted Santos’s state-law claims because it concluded, under the choice-of-law clause, that Illinois contract law governed the Cameo Terms and Illinois law disfavors enforcement of contracts by nonparties. The court relied on the Illinois rule that a nonparty may not enforce contract provisions unless the contract manifests an intent to confer a benefit on that third party so strong as to be “practically an express declaration.” That doctrine—its rationale, its mechanics, and its drafting workarounds—is the subject of this chapter.

This chapter explains (1) the Illinois presumption against conferring benefits on noncontracting third parties and how courts apply that presumption; (2) the decision in Barry v. St. Mary’s and the “practically an express declaration” standard; (3) how to read platform Terms of Service (TOS) when there are separate user and talent or creator agreements and why explicit third‑party beneficiary clauses in some parts of a contract can be read as a negative implication for the rest; (4) the strategic role of choice‑of‑law provisions; and (5) practical drafting advice for platform operators and creators who want to allocate rights in user‑uploaded content, authorize broadcast reuse, and preserve enforceable creator protections.

## Third‑party beneficiary presumption against enforcement under Illinois law

At common law, a contract ordinarily confers rights only upon the contracting parties. The law of third‑party beneficiaries creates an exception: where the contracting parties intend that a benefit conveyed by their bargain inures to a third party, that third party may have enforceable rights. But jurisdictions differ sharply on how readily they will infer such an intent from contract language and surrounding circumstances.

Illinois sits on the more restrictive end of that spectrum. Illinois courts begin with a strong presumption that a contract does not confer enforceable benefits on noncontracting third parties. The presumption reflects judicial reluctance to rewrite bargains by imposing contractual obligations for the benefit of persons who did not exchange consideration, negotiate the terms, or assume risks. Practically, the Illinois rule means that a third party who was not a party to the relevant agreement faces an uphill evidentiary burden: the plaintiff must point to language in the contract that unambiguously manifests a direct intent to benefit that third party, not merely to incidental effects that the contract will have on others.

Functionally, courts distinguish two categories of beneficiaries. An incidental beneficiary is someone who will gain from the contract’s performance but whom the contracting parties did not intend to benefit in a legally enforceable way. An intended beneficiary is someone the parties expressly or demonstrably meant to benefit. Illinois requires a clear showing of the latter. Thus, for claimants who rely on a platform TOS to supply legal rights against another platform user (for example, to prevent reuse or to extract compensation), Illinois law will typically demand contractual language that names them or otherwise unmistakably establishes their status as intended beneficiaries.

This rule has concrete consequences in the platform context. Many creator marketplaces operate on a two‑party model: a user (requester) clicks to accept the platform’s TOS when making a request; the creator (talent) separately engages through the platform’s interface but is not a signatory to the requester's acceptance. If the TOS do not manifest an intent to create enforceable third‑party rights in the talent, Illinois law will likely deny enforcement to any nonparty creator who later finds the requester’s conduct objectionable.

## Barry v. St. Mary’s and the requirement of an express declaration of intent to benefit

The Santos court relied on Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur, an Illinois appellate decision that is often cited for the proposition that a contractual implication of third‑party beneficiary status must be “so strong as to be practically an express declaration.” Barry crystallizes the Illinois approach: courts will not infer a third‑party right from ambiguous or generalized language; rather, the contract must contain wording that shows the parties intended to confer enforceable rights on the third party—language approaching an express naming or unmistakable identification.

Why such strictness? Barry and its progeny reflect several policy concerns. First, enforcing third‑party rights can impose unanticipated obligations on contracting parties who did not bargain with the third party. Second, allowing casual or implied third‑party beneficiary claims would invite litigation from persons affected incidentally by many commercial contracts. Third, the doctrine of consideration and privity historically limits contractual enforcement to parties who have supplied consideration; a strict third‑party‑beneficiary rule preserves that limiting principle unless parties clearly decide otherwise.

Applying Barry to the fact pattern in Santos, the Second Circuit emphasized that the Cameo Terms did not contain an express declaration that certain provisions were intended to benefit creators such as Santos. The court also noted that other provisions of the Terms did, in fact, include explicit third‑party beneficiary language. That asymmetry was important: when a contract explicitly confers third‑party rights in some contexts but omits them elsewhere, courts will often treat the omission as deliberate. Illinois courts will therefore infer that the contracting parties knew how to grant third‑party beneficiary status and did so only where they intended; the absence of such language elsewhere defeats a nonparty’s attempt to enforce the contract.

A few practical lessons follow from Barry. First, the inquiry is text‑centric: a plaintiff who wants to rely on a contract to obtain third‑party enforcement should look first for explicit beneficiary language—naming, identifying, or otherwise unmistakably describing the intended beneficiary—or for similarly strong indicia of intent (e.g., the contract imposes an obligation whose whole purpose is to benefit the third party). Second, courts will scrutinize the contract as a whole: the presence of explicit third‑party clauses in some parts of the TOS weakens arguments that other, silent parts are intended to be enforceable by nonparties.

## Reading TOS structures: separate user and talent agreements; explicit third‑party beneficiary clauses elsewhere as negative implication

Platform agreements are not monoliths. Large creator marketplaces like Cameo typically have multiple agreements and interfaces: a general Terms of Service, a User Agreement governing those who buy services, a Talent Agreement governing creators who offer services, and ancillary policies (privacy policy, content rules, payment terms). These separate documents interact; courts will read them as a whole to determine rights and obligations.

Where a platform sets up separate contracting flows—one for requesters and one for creators—the words used in each flow are material. A common architecture: the requester is required to accept (clickwrap) a User Agreement that grants the platform certain rights and imposes obligations on the requester; creators accept the platform’s Creator or Talent Agreement that grants the platform a license to host and distribute the creator’s work and lays out compensation and dispute procedures. If the requester’s agreement is silent about granting direct rights to creators, and the creator’s agreement is the only place where the creator’s rights are spelled out, Illinois courts will often treat the creator as a contracting party to the creator agreement and not as a third‑party beneficiary of the requester’s agreement.

Santos illustrates a related interpretive rule: when a contract contains express third‑party beneficiary language in some provisions but not in others, that express inclusion can be read as an implicit exclusion elsewhere. The Second Circuit observed that “other provisions of the Terms of Service do contain third‑party beneficiary language, indicating that when the parties to that agreement intended to allow provisions of the contract to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly.” That negative implication matters: if a platform wants to preserve flexibility to grant certain protections to creators without making them parties to every requester contract, the platform should be deliberate about where it includes express third‑party‑beneficiary language.

For creators, the practical significance is plain: do not assume that clicking through a platform interface entitles you to enforce requester promises. Creators should (a) read both the requester-facing and creator-facing agreements; (b) ensure the creator-facing agreement contains the protections you want (licensing terms, payment obligations, ownership provisions, consent for reuse); and (c) consider using platform features that convert the requester’s assent into an enforceable obligation to you—such as an explicit clause in the User Agreement naming creators as intended beneficiaries, or a mechanism that makes creators co‑parties to the transaction.

From a litigation standpoint, courts will analyze the objective language and structure of the TOS. The presence of an express consent or beneficiary clause will be decisive; in its absence, courts like those in Illinois will be reluctant to impose third‑party rights.

## Choice‑of‑law provisions and their strategic significance

Choice‑of‑law clauses in platform agreements are not mere boilerplate. The governing-law clause determines which state’s substantive contract doctrines will decide whether nonparties may enforce contract provisions. Platforms often select a jurisdiction perceived to be business-friendly or predictable (e.g., Delaware, New York, Illinois); those choices affect a host of doctrinal questions, including third‑party‑beneficiary rules, enforceability of clickwraps, contracting formalities, and statutes of limitations.

The Santos opinion demonstrates the real-world impact of such clauses. The District Court (and the Second Circuit on review) applied the governing Illinois law named in the Cameo Terms to Santos’s claim. Under Illinois law, the strong presumption against conferring benefits on third parties supplied the doctrinal basis to reject Santos’s ability to enforce the requester-facing TOS. Had the TOS specified New York law, the inquiry might have differed; New York courts apply the intended-beneficiary test but have sometimes been more permissive in inferring intent from the contract and surrounding circumstances. Thus, choice‑of‑law selection is a deliberate strategic decision that shapes the enforceability of rights.

Practical implications for drafters:

- Platforms. Select governing law with an eye to the doctrines you want to control. If you prefer a restrictive approach to third‑party enforcement (limiting suits by nonparties), name a state whose law articulates a firm presumption against third‑party beneficiaries. Also consider forum selection and arbitration clauses to reduce litigation risk.

- Creators and requesters. Beware that governing-law clauses can neutralize favorable state doctrines. If you as a creator prefer a jurisdiction more likely to find third‑party beneficiary status, negotiate for that governing law in the creator agreement or an agreement that directly binds requesters to the creator.

Courts sometimes decline to enforce choice‑of‑law clauses when enforcement would violate fundamental public policy or when the clause is procedurally unconscionable. But in ordinary commercial contexts, courts will respect a clear choice‑of‑law clause and apply the chosen state’s law to resolve third‑party‑beneficiary disputes.

## Practical drafting: allocating rights in user‑uploaded content, broadcast reuse, and creator protections

The Santos decision highlights a drafting gap that often exists in creator marketplaces: platforms routinely obtain licenses from creators to host and distribute content but may not secure clear, parallel assurances from requesters that permit downstream uses—broadcast, excerpting, or parody—of content created through the platform. When an improper use occurs, creators who are not parties to the relevant requester‑facing agreement can be left without a contractual remedy unless the TOS affirmatively creates such a remedy.

Below are practical drafting recommendations for platforms, creators, and high‑value requesters to reduce ambiguity and allocate risk.

1. Be explicit about who is a contracting party and who has enforceable rights.

- If the platform intends creators to be able to enforce certain promises made by requesters, include an express third‑party beneficiary clause in the User Agreement that identifies creators (or a defined class of creators) as intended beneficiaries. Sample language:
  “Creator(s) shall be third‑party beneficiaries of the Requester Agreement. Requester acknowledges and agrees that the Creator(s) are express intended beneficiaries of the promises contained herein and may enforce those promises directly against Requester.”

- Alternatively, make creators co‑parties to the transaction (for example, by generating a mini‑agreement that both requester and creator must accept at the point of ordering).

2. Specify the scope of licenses and reuse rights clearly.

- Grant language should be precise about allowed uses: noncommercial/commercial, broadcast, promotional, sublicensing, derivative works, geographic scope, and duration. Example:
  “Creator hereby grants Platform and Requester a nonexclusive, worldwide, transferable license to reproduce, distribute, publicly perform, display, and create derivative works of the Delivered Video in any media now known or hereafter developed, for the term of the copyright, solely for the purposes specified in the Request.”

- If broadcast reuse is permitted, include explicit permission and, where appropriate, a separate compensation mechanism for certain classes of reuse (e.g., television broadcast or network reuse).

- Define the right to edit. If the platform or requester can edit creator content for broadcast or commentary, state whether alterations are permitted and whether the creator’s moral rights are waived.

3. Address publicity, privacy, and right of publicity.

- Ensure that the requester warrants that they have obtained all necessary releases and consents for any person appearing in the video and that the requester indemnifies the creator/platform for claims arising from unauthorized uses.

- Where creators supply content that uses their voice/likeness, include a clause where the creator grants the platform and its licensees rights to use the creator’s name, portrait, or voice, but also provide guardrails on derogatory or demeaning uses if that is a concern.

4. Allocate risk with representations, warranties, and indemnities.

- Requesters should represent that their intended use of the delivered content complies with law and platform policies and that they will not use the content in a way that violates the creator’s rights beyond the scope of the license.

- Include mutual indemnities for breaches of representations: the requester indemnifies for misrepresentations about consents; the creator indemnifies for warranty breaches about originality.

- Consider a “safe harbor” mechanism: if a requester wants to use content beyond the platform’s typical use cases (for example, to include it in a nationally broadcast TV segment), require requester to accept an enhanced license and additional payment and to sign an express acknowledgement.

5. Use express third‑party beneficiary language sparingly but deliberately.

- If the goal is to empower creators to enforce requester obligations, include express beneficiary language. If the goal is to limit creators’ enforcement and keep dispute resolution in a streamlined channel (e.g., arbitration with the platform), do not include such language.

- Make the platform’s intentions clear through definitions and exceptions. For instance, define “Creator” in the User Agreement and expressly state whether creators are intended beneficiaries of particular covenants (e.g., “payment,” “no-derogatory-use”).

6. Design the user experience to create enforceable consent.

- A clickwrap is far stronger than browsewrap. For key rights transfers or third‑party beneficiary grants, require explicit affirmative assent (e.g., checkboxes, signature, or dual acceptance by both requester and creator).

- Consider integrating an in‑transaction confirmation: when a requester orders a video, present a short rider that both parties accept, stating the permitted uses, any fees for certain reuses, and whether the creator will be an intended beneficiary.

7. Consider a modular approach to rights and compensation.

- Permit creators to select a default license for standard marketplace transactions, with options to opt into broader rights (e.g., broadcast, merchandising) for additional fees.

- Provide transparent labeling and pricing for “broadcast rights” or “network rights” to reduce downstream disputes.

8. Protect creators’ moral and reputational interests.

- If creators object to uses that are derogatory or imply endorsement, include contractual protections (e.g., a covenant not to use the creator’s content in a defamatory or falsely endorsing manner) or an opt‑out right for certain sensitive uses, with compensation adjustments.

9. Draft with litigation in mind.

- If you want potential enforcement by nonparties, include clear naming of who may enforce, a statement of intent, and a damages/remedy clause that contemplates enforcement by the beneficiary (including injunctive relief and statutory damages, where applicable).

- If you want to funnel disputes to the platform, include an explicit dispute-resolution clause that requires administrative exhaustion before litigation and clarifies who may participate.

10. Watch choice‑of‑law and jurisdiction language.

- Pick governing law deliberately to reflect the jurisprudence you prefer on third‑party beneficiaries and clickwrap enforceability.

- Coordinate governing law across the suite of documents (User Agreement, Creator Agreement, Privacy Policy). Inconsistent governing-law clauses create interpretive friction and invite forum shopping.

## Practical checklist and takeaways

- For creators who want enforceable protections: do not assume platform TOS protect you unless they expressly say so. Seek a creator agreement, request express third‑party beneficiary language in relevant requester agreements, insist on clear licensing and reuse language, and require affirmative assent where needed.

- For platforms that want to limit creators’ ability to sue requesters: draft requester agreements without express third‑party beneficiary language, but recognize that omitting protections for creators can create reputational and operational risks if content is widely reused. Balance legal insulation with marketplace integrity.

- For both sides: a clear user experience that surfaces key rights to both requesters and creators at the point of transaction reduces disputes. Explicit, narrow grant language (what is licensed, to whom, for what uses, for how long, and for what compensation) is far preferable to ambiguous, catchall phrases.

- Choice of law matters: pick it to align with your policy goals, but be realistic about enforceability and public‑policy limits.

Santos v. Kimmel underscores the practical legal consequence of drafting choices made before any dispute arises. Courts will look first to the text and structure of the TOS: who is named, what language is used, whether the parties used express third‑party beneficiary clauses in some places but not others, and what governing law the contract selects. For creators and platforms alike, the lesson is practical and immediate: rights and remedies in the real world turn largely on careful contracting.
