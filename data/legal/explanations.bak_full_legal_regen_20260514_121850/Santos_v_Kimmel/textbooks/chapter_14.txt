Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

Chapter 14: # Notes, Questions, and Hypotheticals

This chapter uses Santos v. Kimmel as the launching point for exercises that test how small factual changes can push a dispute across the line from fair use dismissal and state-law failure-to-plead into viable claims. The Second Circuit’s opinion supplies the governing legal framework on fair use and on when state-law claims survive (or are displaced) by federal copyright law. Below we explore six fact-variants that commonly arise in practice, explain the doctrinal pivot points, and pose the precise factual showings a plaintiff would need to survive early dismissal.

## What if the show aired the videos without commentary, in full, and monetized them exclusively on social platforms?

Santos was dismissed because Kimmel’s use was overwhelmingly “transformative” — the purpose and character of the JKL segments was to mock and comment on Santos’s willingness to say absurd things for money — and because the other fair-use factors cut in favor of the defendants. That conclusion rests on the particular mixture of transformation (criticism, satire) and the limited market-harm risk created by the segments.

Change the facts so that the show instead runs each Cameo video in its entirety, with no critical framing or commentary, and republishes them on defendant-controlled social accounts where the defendants monetize views (ads, sponsorships, or paid promotions). Under Section 107, this factual shift affects at least three of the four fair-use factors in predictable ways.

First, purpose and character: a verbatim broadcast of the entire video, without commentary or context, is far less likely to be viewed as “transformative.” The Second Circuit emphasizes the reasonable-observer inquiry: what does the secondary use add, and does it alter the original with a different purpose or message? When the secondary use merely republishes the work to reach an audience and extract ad revenue, it “supersedes the objects of the original creation” rather than adds new expression or meaning. That pushes the first factor strongly against fair use.

Second, amount and substantiality: airing the whole work weighs against fair use. The use of the entire creative work is presumptively non‑fair when the entire expression is copied and there is no strong justification for doing so to accomplish a transformative purpose.

Third, market effect: republishing the videos on monetized social platforms creates a real risk of usurpation. If viewers can access the same content on the defendants’ high‑traffic platforms, the secondary distribution functions as a substitute for the creator’s licensing opportunities (e.g., the market to license clips, or direct-pay opportunities for full videos). The Supreme Court and the Second Circuit draw a line between critical uses (which courts have recognized as unlikely to form a conventional licensing market) and untransformed republishing that competes with the original creator.

Taken together, these changes make fair use a much weaker defense. A complaint alleging that the defendant ran the unaltered, monetized videos could survive a motion to dismiss if the plaintiff alleges that defendants extracted commercial value by offering the same expressive content to the public and that the defendants’ platforms were a realistic substitute for plaintiff’s marketplaces. In short: airing in full and monetizing likely tips the balance away from fair use and toward liability.

Questions for students: What evidence would work to show that the defendants’ social posts served as substitutes for plaintiff’s market? Would view counts or advertising revenues be necessary at the pleading stage, or could plausibly pled allegations about platform reach suffice? How would a plaintiff allege licensing value or lost licensing opportunities plausibly?

## What if Santos included a visible license term or watermark restricting broadcast use in each video?

Embedding a license term (for example, a short text on the video — “Not for broadcast” — or a watermark stating “Licensed for private use only”) changes the legal texture of the dispute, but it does not automatically convert a copyright defense problem into a win for the copyright holder. The effect of such notice operates on several doctrinal axes: fair use, bad-faith inquiries, and contract or licensing claims.

On fair use: fair use remains an objective legal doctrine. Courts look to the use’s purpose and character, the amount copied, the nature of the work, and market effect. A visible “no-broadcast” notice does not negate fair use automatically, but it can be relevant evidence. The Supreme Court has been skeptical of treating subjective bad faith as dispositive in the fair-use calculus (see Campbell’s and later decisions’ caution about treating “bad faith” as decisive), but objective evidence of purposeful evasion can still matter. A notice makes it harder for a defendant to credibly argue that it thought the use was noncopyrightous or that it believed it had a right to republish. Thus notice tends to cut against a defendant on the first factor (purpose/character) insofar as it undermines claims of benign intent, and it may influence a court’s view of overall fairness.

On contract/licensing: an embedded license notice could function as a unilateral license term, but the enforceability of that term depends on the parties’ relationship. If the recipient of the video (or the platform through which the video is delivered) expressly accepts the license term in a way that satisfies contract formation requirements — offer, acceptance, and consideration — then the license may be enforceable. A platform-wide Terms of Service, however, is typically a contract between the user and the platform; a notice within a media file does not by itself create privity between the uploader and every downstream viewer. Where a defendant individually negotiated with the creator or explicitly agreed to license restrictions in messages or a separate contract, the embedded term has more force.

On remedies: a clear visible license or watermark strengthens claims for breach of contract or breach of an implied license if the defendant’s conduct created the requisite contractual relationship or if the defendant’s acceptance of the uploader’s conditions can be plausibly alleged. It may also reinforce equitable claims (e.g., injunction) because it lessens any equitable defense based on laches or reasonable misunderstanding.

Practical note for a plaintiff: plead the notice’s text, its location on the video, and the recipient’s subjective knowledge (e.g., that the defendant watched the video and the watermark was visible) to make it concrete. The more the complaint plausibly shows the defendant saw and ignored the notice, the stronger the argument that fair use should not be resolved in their favor at the pleading stage.

## Would a narrowly tailored right of publicity claim avoid preemption and survive fair use in this context?

Right-of-publicity claims are conceptually distinct from copyright. Copyright protects original expression; the right of publicity protects the commercial appropriation of an individual’s identity (name, persona, likeness) to exploit a commercial value. Whether a narrow right-of-publicity claim survives (1) copyright preemption and (2) any “defenses” available to the defendant (including expressive‑use defenses) turns on two questions.

First, preemption: federal copyright law preempts state-law claims that are equivalent to copyright’s exclusive rights (17 U.S.C. § 301). The test asks whether the state claim protects rights that are qualitatively the same as those protected by copyright and whether the state remedy is no broader than the rights granted by copyright. A right-of-publicity claim that does not seek to control expressive elements that are the same as the copyrighted expression — for example, it seeks to redress commercial exploitation of a person’s persona distinct from any claimed exclusivity in the creative content itself — is less likely to be preempted. Put differently, if the complaint centers on unauthorized commercial exploitation of Santos’s identity (separate from the expressive content of the particular videos), it may survive preemption because it vindicates a different interest: control of commercial use of one’s persona.

Second, survivability against an expressive‑use defense (sometimes manifesting as First Amendment protection): courts often balance the individual’s publicity right against the public’s interest in free expression. In the 2d Circuit, and in many other jurisdictions, when the use is expressive (commentary, parody, news reporting), courts apply a balancing test — sometimes characterized as a “transformative use” inquiry similar in flavor to the copyright test — to determine whether the public‑interest value outweighs the commercial appropriation. Where the use is highly transformative (adds significant new expression, meaning, or message), the First Amendment tends to protect the defendant; when the defendant uses the plaintiff’s identity primarily for commercial exploitation and the work is not meaningfully transformative, the plaintiff’s publicity interest prevails.

Applying these principles to a “Will Santos Say It?” scenario: if Santos pleads a narrow publicity claim alleging that Kimmel used Santos’s name and likeness as a commercial draw for ad‑supported segments and social posts — independent of any claimed ownership in the videos’ expressive elements — that claim may be pled as qualitatively different from a copyright claim and therefore avoid preemption. Whether it survives summary judgment or trial depends on the expressive character of the broadcast. The use at issue in Santos was, arguably, expressive (satirical commentary), which is the exact context where defendants frequently win under First Amendment balancing. But if the defendant’s broadcast was unaccompanied by commentary, was used as pure advertisement bait, and was monetized directly as a commercial substitute for Santos’s own market, the publicity claim would be stronger.

Takeaway: a well‑pleaded, narrow publicity claim that seeks to protect the commercial value of the plaintiff’s persona and does not attempt to assert exclusive rights over the expressive content of the video has a plausible path around copyright preemption. It then faces — and may lose to — First Amendment expressive‑use defenses depending on the particular application’s transformative character and commercial nature.

## If Kimmel edited the videos to fabricate statements Santos did not make, how would that affect factors one and four?

Fabrication is a complicating fact with implications across multiple doctrines. Two fair-use factors are most directly implicated: purpose/character (factor one) and market effect (factor four). Fabrication also raises separate torts (defamation, false-light privacy, and possibly fraud) that may provide independent remedies.

On factor one (purpose and character): editing a creator’s videos to fabricate statements changes the second work’s relationship to the original. If the edits are designed to misrepresent Santos’s own expression — e.g., welding unrelated clips together to make him appear to say things he did not — a reasonable observer might not view the secondary use as commentary but as a deceptive creation that supplants or supplants the authentic message. The court’s transformative inquiry asks whether the new work “adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” Fabrication may add a new (false) message, but that message is not a commentary on the original work; it is an altered portrayal that distorts the author’s expressive intent. Courts have been skeptical of protecting mischievous or misleading uses under the banner of “transformation” when the new work merely manipulates the original to create a false impression.

Therefore, fabrication tends to undermine claims of transformativeness. The defendant cannot evade liability simply by asserting that the altered work offers “new meaning” if that new meaning is a falsification of the original speaker’s message. That is especially true where the fabricated content damages the speaker’s public reputation or supplants his authentic expression.

On factor four (market effect): fabrication increases the risk of market harm in two distinct ways. First, it can diminish the market for the original creative works because consumers will not rely on or purchase the creator’s authentic messages if misleading, fabricated versions proliferate. Second, the fabricated versions may usurp licensing opportunities: for example, news outlets or social channels may choose to share the fabricated clip rather than seeking out or licensing the original content. Fabrication therefore increases the plausibility of a substitution argument and corresponding market harm.

Aside from copyright, fabrication can open the door to other tort claims that the Santos opinion did not reach. If a defendant edits content to create false statements, a plaintiff may have a viable defamation or false‑light privacy claim (depending on jurisdiction and whether the plaintiff is a public figure) and possibly a claim for fraudulent inducement if the defendant’s pre-broadcast misrepresentations caused the plaintiff to act to his detriment. Those tort claims are not precluded by copyright because they vindicate distinct interests.

In short: fabrication weakens a fair-use defense and strengthens both the case for market harm under the fourth factor and allegations that the defendant acted deceptively, inviting non‑copyright remedies.

## Assume a detailed, mutually acknowledged message exchange agreeing to abide by TOS — does an implied-in-fact or express contract claim now lie?

The Santos opinion rejected a breach-of-TOS claim largely because Santos was not a party to the platform’s Terms of Service and because the complaint lacked facts showing a binding implied‑in‑fact agreement between the creator and the defendants. But change the factual record to include a detailed, mutually acknowledged message exchange in which the defendants explicitly state that they reviewed the Cameo Terms of Service, agree to abide by them, and promise not to broadcast or distribute the videos beyond the intended private audience. How do contract doctrines apply?

Express contract: If the messages constitute an offer (defendant’s promise), acceptance (creator’s assent), consideration (the creator’s promise to deliver a video in exchange for payment by the defendant or otherwise provided consideration), and sufficiently definite terms, an express contract likely exists between the creator and the defendant. Courts look to objective manifestations of assent; modern case law treats electronic messages like other writings: an exchange of emails or texts can create a binding contract if the parties agree on essential terms. A message in which the defendant acknowledges the platform’s TOS and expressly promises not to broadcast would be strong evidence of express agreement. The presence of consideration is typically satisfied where the creator provides a paid service.

Implied‑in‑fact contract: Where the communications are less formal, courts evaluate whether the parties’ conduct and surrounding circumstances evidence a meeting of the minds. If the defendant requests a personalized recording, the creator records and delivers it, and the defendant pays (or promises to pay), courts can infer an implied-in-fact contract whose terms include customarily expected conditions like the intended audience and license scope. Explicit message acknowledgements strengthen the inference; indeed, messages that incorporate TOS terms or refer to them can supply the terms of an implied contract.

Third‑party beneficiary issues: An alternative route is showing that Santos was an intended third‑party beneficiary of the platform’s TOS. The Santos opinion noted the strong presumption against conferring rights on nonparties under Illinois law (the choice-of-law that applied to the platform Terms). But if the TOS expressly designates third‑party beneficiaries or the messages between Kimmel and Santos state that the TOS protections apply for Santos’s benefit, the “strong presumption” can be overcome. Express language in the TOS or in the bilateral messaging creating an express contractual obligation to individuals like Santos would be decisive.

Practicalities and enforcement: If there is a clear, bilateral contract between Kimmel and Santos limiting use to non-broadcast or private distribution, a breach-of-contract claim can proceed independently of copyright law. The defendant’s fair‑use defense to a copyright claim is irrelevant to breach-of-contract unless the contract purports to surrender rights that are not within the parties’ power to bargain away. Contract remedies (damages, injunctions) are available subject to standard defenses (consent, waiver, lack of consideration). Thus, a detailed, mutually acknowledged message exchange can create an express contract, or at minimum supply the facts needed to plead an implied‑in‑fact contract with particular terms that will survive a Rule 12(b)(6) motion.

Questions for students: What specific terms must the messages contain to make the contract sufficiently definite? How would formation be affected by platform intermediaries or by TOS provisions that purport to disclaim third‑party obligations? How would you plead waiver or modification by subsequent conduct?

## What out-of-pocket loss could be plausibly alleged if Santos declined higher-paying requests due to time spent on Kimmel’s prompts?

The Santos court affirmed dismissal of the fraudulent inducement claim because the complaint failed to allege any out‑of‑pocket loss — a necessary element under New York law for such a claim. But a different set of allegations about foregone opportunities could plausibly state an out‑of‑pocket loss.

Under New York law, a fraudulent inducement plaintiff must plead a pecuniary loss caused by reasonable reliance on the defendant’s misrepresentation. Out‑of‑pocket damages are the classic form of reliance damages: money the plaintiff actually spent, or money the plaintiff could have earned but lost because of reliance.

In the hypothetical where Santos alleges that, based on Kimmel’s solicitations (and perhaps false representations about how the videos would be used), he declined other offers that would have paid more, the complaint must do more than plead a conclusory statement that he “lost” opportunities. To be plausible, the complaint should allege:

- Specific offers that were declined: identify the other requesters (if possible), the dates and approximate times, the higher fees that were offered, and the fact that the creator declined those offers in reasonable reliance on the defendant’s representations or promises.
- Causation and reliance: show that Santos reasonably relied on the defendant’s representations (e.g., the defendant said the videos would only be used privately, and Santos relied on that promise) and that reliance caused him to decline other paid opportunities.
- Quantifiable loss: plead the amount of the foregone fees (e.g., “Santos declined a $5,000 Cameo request on April 1 because he had agreed to produce Kimmel’s request that evening”); if there is a pattern, aggregate the lost sums.
- Non-speculative connection: explain why Santos could not have done both (time constraints, scheduling, or exclusivity commitments), and why the lost opportunities are not merely hypothetical.

If Santos can plausibly allege the foregoing, courts have allowed out‑of‑pocket reliance damages to survive. The complaint must avoid vague or conclusory statements about lost business; specificity is required to show a real pecuniary injury. Alternative measures of damages could also include costs incurred in producing the Kimmel videos (equipment, assistants, studio hire) that would be recoverable as out‑of‑pocket expenditures if the defendant’s fraudulent inducement caused the creator to undertake the costs.

One caveat: if the plaintiff is a public figure (as Santos was), damages for reputational harms are harder to recover under certain torts; but out‑of‑pocket, pecuniary reliance losses do not require a showing of reputational injury and so are the most straightforward path to survive a motion to dismiss on fraud claims.

Question for students: How would you plead reliance where the defendant’s misrepresentation was implicit (e.g., use of a fake account to solicit videos), rather than an explicit promise about distribution? What kinds of documentary evidence would strengthen the plaintiff’s claim at summary judgment?

---

These hypotheticals illustrate how relatively subtle factual changes alter the balance between competing doctrines: copyright fair use, contract formation, preemption, right-of-publicity law, and tort damages. The Santos decision highlights several recurring lessons. First, courts resolve fair use by focusing on what a reasonable observer would see as the new use’s purpose and market effects. Second, statutory preemption presents a barrier to state-law claims that merely duplicate the rights of copyright; narrowly tailored claims that vindicate distinct interests (persona, contract promises) can survive. Third, pleading matters: specificity about the defendant’s conduct, the plaintiff’s losses, and any contemporaneous agreements will often be dispositive at the Rule 12 stage. Use these hypotheticals to practice translating fact patterns into the legal elements each cause of action requires, and to think about the types of evidence — messages, view counts, licensing offers, and payment records — that will matter as the case develops.
