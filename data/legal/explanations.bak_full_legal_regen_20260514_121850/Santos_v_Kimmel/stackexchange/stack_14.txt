Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

### How does Melendez’s 'extra element' test for preemption apply to contract-based restrictions on use of copyrighted works?
Question:
The panel avoids deciding preemption but notes Santos’s view that his contract claims are 'qualitatively different.' Under Second Circuit law, when do contractual promises (e.g., not to broadcast commercially, or to limit uses) add the 'extra element' that avoids § 301 preemption? Would specific, bargained-for use restrictions in a ToS or side agreement typically survive preemption if adequately pleaded?
Answer:
Short answer: Under Second Circuit precedent (Melendez), a contract claim avoids § 301 preemption only when the contract imposes an “extra element” — a qualitatively different duty — that is not merely a restatement of the bundle of exclusive rights Congress gave copyright owners (reproduction, distribution, public performance, derivative works, etc.). A specific, bargained-for use restriction (for example, an express promise not to broadcast commercially or to limit certain downstream uses) will typically survive preemption only if the plaintiff pleads (1) a concrete contractual obligation that is distinct from the copyright monopoly, (2) that the obligation was actually owed to the plaintiff (e.g., the plaintiff is a party or an intended third‑party beneficiary, or there is a plausible implied-in-fact contract), and (3) damages or remedies that flow from breach of that contractual obligation rather than from the defendant’s exercise of the copyright owner’s exclusive rights.

Why — the doctrine and standard (as applied in Santos)
- Melendez’s “extra element” test (as the Santos panel cites Melendez) asks whether the state-law claim rests on an additional element beyond making, distributing, or publicly performing the copyrighted work. If the additional element creates a qualitatively different obligation, the claim is not preempted. The Santos opinion acknowledges that characterization (Santos’s claim is “qualitatively different,” Melendez v. Sirius XM Radio, Inc., 50 F.4th 294, 302 (2d Cir. 2022)), but the panel did not resolve preemption because it disposed of the state-law claims on other grounds.
- The practical import: promises that create contractual duties unrelated to copyright’s exclusive rights — for example, express confidentiality promises, affirmative covenants about how a recording may be used (e.g., “not for broadcast,” “for private use only,” or “only for noncommercial distribution”), or other obligations that regulate behavior beyond mere copying/use — are the kind of “extra element” that can avoid preemption under Melendez.

How that works in pleading and proof (why many ToS/side‑agreement claims fail)
- Specificity of the contract term. The plaintiff must plead the actual, specific contractual term that creates the extra obligation (not just a conclusory allegation that “uses were limited”). The Santos panel rejects Santos’s breach‑of‑Cameo‑Terms claim because he is not a party to those Terms and points to no “express declaration” making him an intended third‑party beneficiary; under the applicable law the court applied a strong presumption against third‑party benefits and found no plausible basis to enforce the Terms as to Santos. In short: pleading must show that the promise existed and was directed to the plaintiff.
- Relationship to copyright rights. The contractual promise must be conceptually distinct from the copyright owner’s exclusive rights. A clause that merely replicates a copyright prohibition (e.g., “you may not reproduce or broadcast this work” stated as a contractual restatement of statutory rights) is unlikely to supply the required “extra element.” By contrast, a bargained-for limitation that regulates non-copyrightal aspects of use (time windows, geography, method of exhibition, confidentiality, agreed attribution, etc.) is more clearly “qualitatively different.”
- Pleading damages tied to contract breach. Courts look to whether the injury alleged flows from a breach of the contract obligation, not simply from the exercise of rights that would violate the Copyright Act. The Santos opinion illustrates a related pleading failure: the court dismissed the implied-contract and fraudulent-inducement claims for lack of nonconclusory allegations of essential terms and for failure to allege cognizable out-of-pocket loss.

Bottom line for ToS or side agreements
- Specific, bargained-for use restrictions can survive preemption in the Second Circuit if pleaded with sufficient particularity to show (a) a definite contractual obligation owed to the plaintiff (party status or clear third‑party beneficiary or plausible implied‑in‑fact contract), (b) that the obligation is not merely a restatement of statutory copyright rights but adds a qualitatively different duty, and (c) damages or remedies traceable to the contract breach rather than to the copy/use itself.
- But boilerplate or vaguely alleged ToS claims, or claims by non-parties who fail to allege entitlement to enforce the term (as the Santos panel found here), will typically be dismissed either for failure to state the contractual elements or (if properly pleaded but substantively duplicative of copyright rights) as preempted.

References to the opinion
- The panel cites Melendez for the “qualitatively different”/“extra element” principle (II, opening paragraph) but declines to decide preemption because Santos’s contract claims failed on state‑law pleading grounds.
- The court dismissed the Cameo Terms claim because Santos was not a party and identified no “express declaration” that the Terms applied to noncontracting third parties (II, ¶ on Cameo Terms), and dismissed the implied‑in‑fact contract claim for failing to plead essential terms or a meeting of the minds (II, ¶ on implied contract).
- The outcome shows that even where the Melendez test could save a contract claim from preemption in theory, plaintiffs still must adequately plead the contractual basis and damages under state law to survive dismissal.

If you’re drafting or evaluating such a claim: plead the operative contract language (or concrete facts supporting third‑party beneficiary/implied contract status), explain how the obligation differs from the copyright monopoly (identify the extra element), and allege damages tied to that contractual breach.
