Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

### Why apply Illinois law to the Cameo Terms-of-Service claim if Santos was not a party to that contract?
Question:
The court applied Illinois law (the ToS governing-law clause) to hold Santos wasn’t a third-party beneficiary. How do courts select governing law when the plaintiff is a non-signatory asserting third-party rights? Is it routine to honor the contract’s choice-of-law clause to determine if a third party can enforce it, or is a separate choice-of-law analysis usually required?
Answer:
Short answer
Yes. When a plaintiff who is not a contracting party seeks to enforce contractual rights as a third‑party beneficiary, courts commonly apply the governing law the parties chose in the contract to decide whether the plaintiff has enforceable third‑party rights. That is what the Second Circuit did here: it invoked the ToS’s choice‑of‑law clause (Illinois law) and, under Illinois law, rejected Santos’s third‑party‑beneficiary theory because Illinois presumes against conferring benefits on non‑contracting third parties.

Why (legal doctrine and reasoning)
- A contract’s choice‑of‑law clause governs interpretation of the contract and the parties’ rights under it. When the threshold question is whether anyone outside the signatories may enforce the contract, that is a question about the contract’s scope and intent — ordinarily answered under the law the contracting parties selected.
- The Santos opinion follows that approach: it begins the breach‑of‑contract analysis by saying the Terms of Service are governed by Illinois law and then applies Illinois third‑party‑beneficiary doctrine. See Opinion, II (first paragraph) (“Under Illinois law, which governs the Terms of Service, there is a ‘strong presumption against conferring benefits to noncontracting third parties.’” (quoting Barry v. St. Mary’s Hosp. Decatur)).
- The court used a core Illinois rule — that implication of third‑party beneficiaries must be “so strong as to be practically an express declaration” — to reject Santos’s claim. The opinion also relied on an internal feature of the contract (that other provisions expressly create third‑party rights) to show that the parties knew how to confer third‑party benefits when they intended to do so. See id. (noting “other provisions . . . do contain third‑party beneficiary language, indicating that when the parties . . . intended to allow provisions . . . to be enforced by third parties, they said so expressly”).

What this means in practice
- It is routine for courts to honor a contract’s choice‑of‑law clause when deciding whether a non‑signatory is a third‑party beneficiary, because that inquiry is a contract‑interpretation question for the governing law the parties selected.
- The opinion demonstrates the routine application: rather than conducting a freestanding or multi‑factor choice‑of‑law inquiry, the court applied the ToS’s specified Illinois law and then applied Illinois precedent (Barry) to the beneficiary question.
- That routine approach has limits (not addressed or altered in this opinion): if the enforceability question raised independent tort or extra‑contractual issues governed by different contacts or public‑policy concerns, a court might need a broader choice‑of‑law analysis. But in Santos the court treated the beneficiary issue as an ordinary contract‑law question for the ToS’s governing law and resolved it under Illinois law.

Bottom line
The Second Circuit’s reasoning is straightforward: apply the contract’s governing‑law clause to interpret the contract and determine whether the parties intended to confer third‑party enforcement rights. Under the Illinois law specified in the Cameo ToS, Santos’s status as a non‑signatory did not create an enforceable third‑party beneficiary right.
