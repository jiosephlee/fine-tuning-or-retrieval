Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

### Why did commentary about Santos’s behavior (not the works themselves) still count as ‘transformative’ commentary?
Question:
The opinion frames the JKL segments’ purpose as commenting on Santos’s willingness to "say absurd things for money," which targets Santos rather than critiquing the expressive content of each Cameo video. Under Campbell, parody targets the original work, while satire can be more attenuated. How does Second Circuit law treat commentary that uses a work to criticize the subject or creator rather than the work’s expression—why is that still sufficiently transformative here?
Answer:
Short answer: Because the fair-use inquiry asks whether the secondary use adds a new purpose or character that a reasonable observer would perceive as different from the original — not whether the secondary user is literally criticizing the expressive choices of the original work. The JKL segments plainly did that: they used short Cameo clips as vehicles to lampoon Santos’s willingness “to say absurd things for money,” which a reasonable observer would see as adding a new, critical purpose. Under Second Circuit precedent (as the opinion explains), that kind of re‑contextualizing commentary is sufficiently transformative to weigh strongly for fair use.

Why (and how) the opinion says so — doctrine and textual support
- Transformative purpose is the central concern for the first fair‑use factor. The court reiterates the familiar test: whether the secondary use “merely supersedes the objects of the original creation, or instead adds something new, with a further purpose or different character.” (Opinion quoting Andy Warhol Found. for Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith, 11 F.4th 26, 37 (2d Cir. 2021).)
- The court applies the “reasonable observer” lens: transformation turns on how a reasonable observer would perceive the use, not on the subjective intent of either party. The opinion cites the Supreme Court’s admonition that subjective intent does not control and relies on Andy Warhol/Goldsmith for this point. The court therefore rejects Santos’s effort to equate his stated purpose in making the Cameo videos with the purpose of the JKL segments. The complaint itself, the court notes, describes the Cameos as conveying “hope, strength, perseverance, encouragement, and positivity,” while the JKL segments’ purpose (per the District Court and the complaint) was to comment on Santos’s readiness to say absurd things for money. That difference is what makes the use transformative (Opinion ¶ discussing reasonable observer and App’x 15, 235).
- The court also recognizes the distinction recognized in Campbell: parody targets the original work’s expressive content, while satire often targets something else (a public figure, institution, etc.) and may therefore be more attenuated. The opinion (and the authority it cites) treats such satire-style re‑use as capable of being transformative where it adds new expression, meaning, or message — here, mockery of Santos — rather than simply republishing the Cameos for the same purpose. (Opinion references Campbell and the Harper & Row/Google lineage in discussing intent and bad faith.)
- Practical result in the opinion: because the JKL segments used the Cameo clips to add a new critical message about Santos (a different purpose and character), the first fair‑use factor “strongly supports” fair use. The opinion then proceeds to consider the other factors and finds no displaced market, reinforcing that the uses were non‑superseding.

Takeaway for similar cases
- You don’t need to be directly critiquing the artistic form or content of a work to be transformative. Using a work as a vehicle to comment on its subject or creator can suffice, provided the secondary work adds new meaning or message that a reasonable observer would perceive as distinct.
- That is precisely the court’s conclusion here: the JKL segments recontextualized the Cameo videos to communicate a new, satirical message about Santos, and that recontextualization is the essence of the transformation the fair‑use doctrine protects.
