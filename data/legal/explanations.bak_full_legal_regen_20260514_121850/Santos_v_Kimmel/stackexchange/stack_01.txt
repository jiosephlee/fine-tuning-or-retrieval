Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Santos v. Kimmel

### Why could the Second Circuit decide fair use on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion without discovery?
Question:
The panel says fair use may be "so clearly established by a complaint" as to support dismissal at the pleading stage (citing TCA Television). Here, the court relied on the videos and the broadcast segments themselves. How does the incorporation-by-reference/judicial notice doctrine let the court look past the complaint’s characterizations and evaluate the works directly without converting to summary judgment? What are the limits on doing this, especially when transformative purpose turns on a "reasonable observer" standard after Warhol?
Answer:
Short answer
The Second Circuit relied on the videos and the JKL segments because those materials were properly before the court (either attached/incorporated or subject to judicial notice) and because the fair‑use question could be resolved as a legal determination from those materials alone. The court invoked the familiar Rule 12(b)(6) authorities (DiFolco, TCA Television) that allow a court to look at documents integral to the complaint without converting to summary judgment, and it stressed that it only needed “the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments]” to decide fair use (Cariou). The limit is that a court may only do this where the materials themselves—viewed objectively—establish fair use as a matter of law; if determining transformation requires resolving disputed factual issues about how a reasonable observer would perceive the works, dismissal is inappropriate.

Why the court could look at the works without converting to summary judgment
1) Rule 12(b)(6) authorities the opinion relies on. The panel began by restating the standard for a Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal: it may consider “the facts alleged in the complaint, documents attached to the complaint as exhibits, and documents incorporated by reference in the complaint.” (Opinion quoting DiFolco v. MSNBC, 622 F.3d at 111.) The court also cited TCA Television for the proposition that fair use “may be so clearly established by a complaint as to support dismissal . . . at the pleading stage.” (Opinion citing TCA Television, 839 F.3d at 178.)

2) Judicial notice /registrations. The opinion expressly noted the court may take judicial notice of copyright registrations published in the Copyright Office’s registry (citing Island Software & Comput. Servs., Inc. v. Microsoft, 413 F.3d 257, 261). The court used those registrations to establish publication timing (Opinion, ¶ discussing factor four).

3) The court relied on the works themselves. The panel said it “need[ed] only consider ‘the original version of [the videos] and the [JKL segments] at issue’ to decide ‘the question of fair use’,” and then decided the fair‑use factors from those materials (citing Cariou v. Prince, 714 F.3d at 707). That statement explains the procedural move: the operative works were integral to the complaint (the complaint discussed and depended on them), were either attached or incorporated, and thus the court could examine them on a 12(b)(6) record.

Why that is legally sound (doctrine + reasoning)
- Incorporation-by-reference / integral documents doctrine lets a court consider documents the complaint relies on without converting to summary judgment because the complaint’s allegations invite judicial consideration of the documents’ contents. The opinion follows that line (DiFolco).
- Judicial notice of certain public records (copyright registrations) is permitted and was used here to resolve a discrete factual assertion (publication date) that bears on factor four.
- The court treated fair use as a mixed law‑and‑fact inquiry that sometimes can be resolved as a matter of law when the comparison of works themselves plainly demonstrates the statutory factors — exactly the situation the panel found here. The opinion repeats the settled rule that fair use can be “so clearly established by a complaint” that dismissal is appropriate (TCA Television), and then applies the four statutory factors to the originals and the segments.

Limits — when the court cannot do this on a 12(b)(6) record
- No fact‑finding on disputed extrinsic facts. A court may not rely on extra‑record facts, disputed factual allegations, or competing witness accounts at the pleading stage. If resolution of fair use turns on contested facts outside the four corners of the works or on testimonial evidence, the case cannot be decided on 12(b)(6).
- The “reasonable observer” / transformation point after Warhol. The opinion acknowledges that transformation turns on whether “a reasonable observer” would perceive the secondary use as adding a new purpose or character, not on the parties’ subjective intent (citing the Supreme Court’s Warhol decision). That means:
  - If the objective comparison of the original video and the JKL segment makes plain, on its face, that the segment adds a different communicative purpose (criticism, lampoon, commentary), the court can decide transformation at the pleading stage.
  - But if transformation depends on contested contextual facts or how a reasonable observer would see the work in light of extrinsic evidence (e.g., disputed broadcast context, surrounding statements, or paratextual materials not before the court), then the reasonable‑observer inquiry requires factual development and cannot be resolved by looking only at the submitted works.
- Narrow use of judicial notice. Judicial notice can establish facts that are not reasonably subject to dispute (e.g., registration date). It cannot be used to prove disputed inferences about how audiences perceived the work; those are typically for later fact‑finding.

How the Santos opinion applied those limits
- The panel concluded no factual disputes needed resolution because the complaint itself described the works and their use, and the works (videos + segments) clearly showed an added purpose (mockery/criticism). The court therefore applied the four statutory factors to the materials and held fair use barred the claims as a matter of law.
- The opinion stressed objective assessment: the reasonable‑observer test governs and the complaint’s own allegations (that Santos intended uplifting messages while defendants intended sarcastic commentary) made clear which purpose a reasonable observer would infer. Given that, the court found no need for evidentiary development.

Takeaway for litigants and students
- Courts may examine works attached to or incorporated by a complaint (and certain public records) on a Rule 12(b)(6) motion and decide fair use if the statutory factors can be resolved from those materials alone.
- But when transformation or other fair‑use factors turn on contested facts about perception, context, or intent that are not resolved by the works themselves, dismissal is improper; discovery or summary judgment will be necessary. The Santos opinion rests on the former situation — the works and the complaint’s own descriptions were sufficient to show transformation as a matter of law.
