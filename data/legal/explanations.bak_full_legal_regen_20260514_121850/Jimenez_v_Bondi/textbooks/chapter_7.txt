Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

Chapter 7: # Standards of Review and Appellate Procedure

This chapter explains how federal courts review agency decisions in immigration cases—particularly denials of asylum and withholding of removal—and how procedural doctrines such as issue preservation, the scope of the administrative record, harmless error, and remand interact with those standards. The Tenth Circuit’s opinion in Jimenez v. Bondi (No. 24‑9573) illustrates these concepts in a routine but instructive setting: the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) affirmed an Immigration Judge (IJ) and the court denied review under the familiar substantial‑evidence standard. Read together, the statutory text, circuit precedent, and the Jimenez opinion teach practical and doctrinal rules that govern appellate advocacy and judicial decisionmaking in removal cases.

## Jurisdiction and scope: 8 U.S.C. § 1252; consolidated review

Review of final orders of removal is governed by 8 U.S.C. § 1252. Section 1252(a)(1) provides that courts of appeals have jurisdiction to review final orders of removal, and § 1252(b)(9) centralizes review in the courts of appeals for “a petition for review of an order of removal” while limiting piecemeal collateral attacks. The statutory scheme creates a single consolidated vehicle in the court of appeals for challenges arising from a removal order (for example, denials of asylum, withholding, and protection under the Convention Against Torture), subject to particular exhaustion and jurisdictional provisions elsewhere in the INA.

Jimenez illustrates these jurisdictional mechanics. The petitioners asked the Tenth Circuit to review the BIA’s denial of their asylum and withholding claims. The court began by invoking § 1252 and circuit precedent (Singh v. Bondi) to establish that it had jurisdiction to entertain a consolidated review of the immigration claims presented in the administrative record. That is the ordinary route for challenging an IJ/BIA removal order: the court of appeals evaluates the administrative proceedings as a unit, not by treating each stage as a separate litigation.

A crucial corollary to the consolidated review is the limited scope of review: courts generally review only “the administrative record on which the order of removal is based.” 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A). That provision requires litigants to present their case to the agency and to rely on the evidence assembled in the administrative proceedings; it also constrains the court’s factfinding capability. If a party wants a court to consider material outside the record, the party must normally persuade the court to remand to the BIA so that the agency can receive and assess the new evidence. Jimenez follows this rule: the Tenth Circuit limited itself to the administrative record that the IJ and BIA had developed.

Practical point for advocates: because the court receives only the administrative record, litigants must present their best factual and legal case at the agency level and preserve issues properly—see the sections below on issue preservation and waiver.

## Substantial‑evidence review of BIA factfinding; deference on credibility versus sufficiency

The critical substantive standard in most immigration appeals is the “substantial evidence” standard. The courts of appeals review the BIA’s factual determinations under this deferential test: a factual finding is “conclusive” unless the record compels a contrary conclusion. As the Tenth Circuit explained in Jimenez (quoting Rivera‑Barrientos and other precedent), the reviewing court must uphold an adverse factual determination unless “any reasonable adjudicator would be compelled to conclude to the contrary.” In practice this means that the reviewing court does not reweigh evidence or substitute its judgment for that of the agency when the record supports more than one permissible view.

This deference governs credibility determinations and factbound questions—whether the petitioner suffered past persecution, whether a persecutor was motivated “on account of” a protected ground, whether government protection was unavailable, and whether internal relocation is reasonable. In Jimenez the BIA (and the IJ) found that, even when taken together, the threats and related incidents did not rise to the level of persecution; the Tenth Circuit upheld that decision because the record contained evidence supporting the BIA’s interpretation of the cumulative facts. The court emphasized that petitioner’s concession of no physical harm and the character of the threats made it reasonable for the agency to conclude the mistreatment did not amount to persecution.

But the substantial‑evidence standard does not apply to legal questions. Pure questions of law (for example, whether a particular legal test should govern or how statutory language should be interpreted) are reviewed de novo. That distinction—de novo review of law and substantial‑evidence review of facts—is central. When an outcome depends on the application of a legal standard (for instance, the legal meaning of “particular social group” or the interpretation of nexus requirements), the court will review whether the agency applied the correct legal test. When an outcome hinges on whether the petitioner’s testimony was believable or whether the incidents alleged meet a fact‑intensive threshold (for example, whether the harm is sufficiently severe or recurring), the court applies substantial‑evidence review.

Credibility findings receive particular deference because they are quintessentially factual. A credibility determination based on demeanor, internal consistency, and corroboration is reviewed for substantial evidence. If the agency’s credibility determination rests on a legal error—if, for example, the agency applied an incorrect legal standard or excluded relevant corroboration improperly—then the reviewing court will scrutinize that aspect as a legal question and may remand.

Jimenez demonstrates both points. The IJ found petitioners credible, but nevertheless concluded their harm did not rise to persecution; the court treated those factual conclusions as within the agency’s domain and sustained them under the substantial‑evidence standard.

## Reviewing BIA decisions with reference to IJ reasoning

The BIA’s opinion is the primary document for appellate review, but the administrative record typically contains the IJ’s decision, which often contains more detailed factual findings and reasoning. When the BIA issues a short opinion—affirming an IJ’s decision without a fresh, full exposition—courts routinely consult the IJ’s decision to understand the basis for the BIA’s ruling. Uanreroro v. Gonzales is the leading example and is cited in Jimenez: when a single member of the BIA “affirm[s] the IJ’s decision,” a reviewing court adjudicates the BIA’s decision but is “not precluded from consulting the IJ’s more complete explanation of those same grounds.”

This practice has two consequences. First, the IJ’s written findings remain important even if the BIA’s summary affirms; a reviewing court will look to the IJ’s analysis to determine whether the BIA’s conclusions rest on substantial evidence or a legal mistake. Second, litigants must preserve arguments and develop the record at both levels: an argument not adequately presented to the IJ or to the BIA is unlikely to be revived on appeal simply because the IJ articulated helpful legal reasoning.

In Jimenez the BIA issued a summary affirmation, and the Tenth Circuit explicitly consulted the IJ’s fuller explanation to assess whether the BIA’s factfinding satisfied the substantial‑evidence test. That approach enabled the court to evaluate both the agency’s factual conclusions and its application of law without remanding for further explanation.

Practical point for students: when reading agency opinions, always read the IJ decision where present. It often contains the contours of the agency’s factual basis and legal reasoning and can be decisive on appeal.

## Issue preservation and waiver on petition for review

Issue preservation is a threshold matter in immigration appeals. Courts generally will not consider an argument raised for the first time on petition for review; a petitioner must present the issue to the agency (to the IJ and then to the BIA) so that the agency has an opportunity to address the matter. Section 1252(d) and related doctrines embody the principle that the administration should be allowed to address and correct alleged errors before judicial review. Failure to press a claim at the agency stage typically results in waiver and forfeiture on appeal.

Jimenez contains several concrete examples of preservation and waiver. Petitioners did not appeal the denial of protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT) or their due process claim, and they therefore “do not appeal” those findings to the court; the Tenth Circuit accordingly declined to consider them. Similarly, petitioners had not briefed an argument based on membership in a particular social group and thus the court limited its review to the political‑opinion nexus; the omitted argument was treated as waived. The court also noted that petitioners “haphazardly briefed” other points, which weakened their appellate claim. In short, a claim not pressed in the agency proceedings or not developed in the appellate brief is unlikely to survive scrutiny.

Practical advice for advocates: preserve every argument you intend to raise on appeal. Raise it at the merits hearing before the IJ, renew or reframe it on appeal to the BIA, and brief it carefully in the petition for review. If you abandon a claim at any stage, you should expect waiver.

A few nuances complicate the general rule. For constitutional claims and questions of law, some statutes and precedent ensure review even where an issue has not been exhaustively briefed, but those exceptions are narrow. Moreover, an agency’s failure to adjudicate a claim at all—or a jurisdictional error by the agency—may constitute grounds for immediate judicial review even if the party failed to preserve a particular argument. But as a practical matter, the preservation rule governs the majority of ordinary removal appeals.

## Harmless error and remand principles in immigration appeals

When the agency errs—procedurally or legally—a reviewing court must decide whether the error requires reversal or whether it is harmless. The harmless‑error doctrine asks whether the error affected the petitioner’s “substantial rights” or otherwise had a prejudicial impact on the outcome. If the error is harmless, the court may affirm despite the irregularity; if the error is prejudicial, the court will typically vacate or remand.

In immigration appeals, harmless error and remand analysis are informed by the substantial‑evidence standard. If the agency applied the correct legal standard and the record would have compelled the same outcome notwithstanding the misstep, a court may deem the error harmless and deny relief. Conversely, where the agency applied the wrong legal standard, conflated legal and factual questions, overlooked material evidence, or reached a conclusion unsupported by the administrative record, the court commonly remands for the agency to reconsider under the correct standard.

Jimenez illustrates these principles by the converse: the court found no legal error that would require remand; the agency’s factual findings were supported by the record, so no prejudicial error existed. By contrast, a case in which the BIA misapplies a legal test for persecution or nexus—e.g., by equating political motive with a mere “tangential” factor without explaining why evidence of mixed motives fails the statutory test—would ordinarily be remanded for further agency analysis.

Remand is especially appropriate where the BIA fails to address material arguments or omits necessary findings. Because the BIA is the agency with national jurisdiction and expertise, courts defer to its resolution of factual conflicts and its expertise in applying immigration law, but they also require reasoned decisionmaking. If the BIA’s opinion fails to offer an adequate basis for review (for example, if the BIA ignored an argument that could change the outcome), the appellate court will often remand rather than supply missing reasons itself.

Practical tips on seeking remand or affirmance on harmless‑error grounds:
- If you represent a petitioner, identify and emphasize in the record the evidence that would require reversal if considered properly. If an IJ or the BIA overlooks that evidence, argue that the omission was prejudicial and ask for remand.
- If you represent the government, demonstrate how any error was harmless because the record unambiguously supports the agency’s result (the Jimenez opinion shows that courts will affirm when the record contains evidence that reasonably supports the BIA’s conclusions).
- When seeking relief based on extra‑record evidence, present a convincing reason why the evidence was unavailable before the agency and why the BIA should consider it on remand.

Finally, courts sometimes address the interplay of discretion and remand. Asylum is a discretionary remedy; even when an applicant establishes refugee status, the Attorney General retains discretion whether to grant asylum. Courts generally lack authority to reverse or direct grant of purely discretionary relief, although they may remand for the agency to exercise discretion if legal or procedural errors infected the agency’s discretionary denial.

## Practical implications and closing observations

The Jimenez opinion is a representative example of how courts synthesize statutory text, agency findings, and record constraints to decide removal appeals. Several takeaways follow:

- Jurisdiction under § 1252 allows consolidated review of removal claims, but the court’s review is ordinarily confined to the administrative record developed before the IJ and the BIA.

- Fact‑intensive questions—credibility, past persecution, nexus, government protection, and internal relocation—are reviewed for substantial evidence. Appellate courts will affirm if reasonable adjudicators could reach the agency’s conclusion.

- Legal questions are reviewed de novo; when a legal error infects an agency’s factfinding (for example, an incorrect legal test), remand is usually required.

- Where the BIA’s opinion is terse and it “affirm[s] the IJ,” courts will consult the IJ’s fuller explanation to discern the basis for the agency’s ruling.

- Issue preservation matters. Claims not raised before the agency or not properly briefed on appeal are typically waived.

- Harmless‑error and remand doctrines are the mechanisms courts use to correct agency mistakes without overstepping. If the error could have affected the outcome, remand is the common remedy; if the error is harmless in light of the record, the court may affirm.

For students and practitioners, the practical lesson is plain: build a complete administrative record, raise and preserve every viable legal theory at each administrative level, and anticipate the deferential posture a court will adopt on factual issues. With those prerequisites met, appellate arguments can focus on legal error, inadequacy of agency reasoning, and instances where the record—rather than mere disagreement—compels reversal.
