Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

Chapter 1: # Orientation to U.S. Protection Law: Asylum, Withholding, and CAT

This chapter introduces the statutory framework, institutional actors, and core doctrines that govern humanitarian protection claims in the United States: asylum, withholding of removal, and protection under the United Nations Convention Against Torture (CAT). A working command of these concepts — the statutory elements, burdens of proof, evidentiary tests, and the roles of the Department of Homeland Security (DHS), immigration judges (IJs), the Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA), and the federal courts of appeals — is indispensable before analyzing individual decisions. We use the Court of Appeals decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (10th Cir. 2025) to illustrate how these rules are applied in practice and to show common pitfalls in framing a protection case.

## Statutory bases: 8 U.S.C. §§ 1101(a)(42)(A), 1158, 1231(b)(3) and 8 C.F.R. pt. 1208

The INA defines the starting point for asylum law. Section 1101(a)(42)(A) defines a “refugee” as any person who is outside his or her country of nationality and who is unable or unwilling to return because of past persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution on account of race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group, or political opinion. Section 1158 implements asylum relief: it authorizes the Attorney General to grant asylum to eligible applicants and prescribes procedural rules for applications. Section 1231(b)(3) sets a different protection — withholding of removal — which bars return to a country where the alien’s life or freedom would be threatened on account of one of the same five protected grounds. Regulations implementing asylum, withholding, and CAT protections appear primarily in 8 C.F.R. part 1208.

Although the statutory categories of protected grounds are narrowly enumerated, the statutory language hides important complexities. “Persecution” is not defined in the INA; courts and the BIA have developed the concept through precedent and regulation. The well‑founded fear standard, the distinction between past and future persecution, the particularity and social visibility inquiries for “particular social group,” and the “central reason” test for nexus (see 8 U.S.C. § 1158(b)(1)(B)(i)) are all interpretive constructs that flow from the statutory core. The regulations codify procedures (for example, 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13 addresses changed country conditions and internal relocation) and define key terms in administrative practice.

Jimenez v. Bondi provides concrete applications of these statutory touchstones. The court framed the claim under § 1101(a)(42)(A) and § 1158 for asylum and under § 1231(b)(3) for withholding, and relied on regulatory guidance (for example, on internal relocation and changed country conditions) to resolve contested issues such as whether the petitioners suffered “persecution,” whether the harm was “on account of” a protected ground, and whether the government was unwilling or unable to protect them.

## Institutional structure: DHS, IJs, the BIA, and the courts of appeals

The contemporary adjudicative sequence for asylum and related protection claims typically begins with DHS. DHS (through U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services for affirmative asylum applications, or through immigration enforcement components for defensive applications raised in removal proceedings) institutes removal proceedings and the filing of applications. When the claim is raised defensively before removal, an immigration judge (an Executive Office for Immigration Review adjudicator) conducts the merits hearing, receives evidence, makes credibility and factual findings, and issues a decision.

Appeals from an IJ’s decision go to the Board of Immigration Appeals, which reviews legal and factual determinations and issues precedential or non‑precedential decisions. A single BIA member may affirm an IJ’s reasoned decision; where that happens, courts of appeals generally review the BIA’s decision but may consult the IJ’s opinion for its fuller factual exposition. The final step for judicial review is an appeal to the appropriate court of appeals under the judicial review provisions of 8 U.S.C. § 1252; such review is constrained by the statutory standards that govern what counts as final agency action and by limitations on claims that may be raised.

In Jimenez, DHS initiated removal proceedings, the IJ heard the family’s asylum and withholding applications and denied relief, and a single BIA member affirmed. On appeal to the Tenth Circuit, the court reviewed the BIA’s decision under the familiar administrative‑law rubric: legal questions are reviewed de novo in many respects, but findings of fact — including credibility determinations and the existence of persecution — are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard. The court explicitly limited its review to the administrative record, consistent with 8 U.S.C. § 1252(b)(4)(A). This institutional path — DHS → IJ → BIA → court of appeals — is the standard procedural ladder for defensive protection claims.

Understanding the roles and incentives of each actor is important for litigation strategy. IJs and the BIA are fact‑finders operating within the Executive branch; they must apply legal precedents but are not courts. The courts of appeals play a supervisory role with deferential review on factual questions, which means that a well‑developed administrative record and targeted legal arguments are essential where the outcome hinges on fact‑intensive inquiries like nexus, persecution, and government protection.

## Relief versus protection: discretionary asylum, mandatory withholding, and CAT protection

Asylum, withholding of removal, and CAT relief are distinct remedies that serve overlapping but different functions.

Asylum is discretionary. An applicant must first establish eligibility by proving refugee status under § 1101(a)(42)(A) — a showing that the applicant was persecuted in the past or has a well‑founded fear of future persecution on account of a protected ground — and then the Attorney General (now acting through delegated officials) may exercise discretion to grant asylum. Because asylum is discretionary, adverse discretionary factors (e.g., criminal history, security concerns, delay in seeking protection) can defeat relief even when statutory eligibility is established. In Jimenez, the court’s denial of asylum rested on the conclusion that the applicants failed to meet the statutory eligibility thresholds; because the threshold was not met, the court did not reach the discretionary balancing inquiry.

Withholding of removal under § 1231(b)(3) is mandatory relief for noncitizens who meet the statutory standard: the government may not remove an alien to a country where the alien’s life or freedom would be threatened on account of a protected ground. The practical consequence is important: withholding protects against removal but does not confer the broader benefits associated with asylum (such as eligibility for adjustment to lawful permanent residence). The evidentiary standard for withholding is higher than for asylum — the applicant must generally show it is “more likely than not” that he would face persecution (or, under some caselaw formulations, that there is a clear probability of persecution) — and withholding, unlike asylum, is not discretionary; if the standard is met, the government must withhold removal.

CAT protection is distinct from both asylum and withholding. CAT obligations arise from treaty and implementing regulations (8 C.F.R. § 1208.16), and require the applicant to establish that it is more likely than not that he would be tortured if returned to the proposed country of removal. CAT protection applies irrespective of nexus to a protected ground and is primarily concerned with the nature and probability of torture by the state or persons acting with the state’s acquiescence. CAT relief is also non‑derivative and is available only when the statutory and regulatory standards are met.

Jimenez illustrates the differences in practice. The family applied for asylum (discretionary), withholding (mandatory if the higher standard were met), and CAT (separate standard and inquiry). The IJ and BIA denied all three; the Tenth Circuit addressed the asylum and withholding claims, rejecting them on statutory grounds (insufficient persecution, insufficient nexus, and insufficient evidence government was unwilling or unable to protect). The court did not need to reach CAT because petitioners did not press the CAT claim on appeal; but the opinion clarifies that CAT standards and withholding standards differ and require separate proof.

## Core burdens and standards: preponderance for asylum eligibility; well‑founded fear; more‑likely‑than‑not for withholding and CAT

Understanding burdens of proof and their substantive content is central to protection law.

Asylum eligibility requires proof by a preponderance of the evidence that the applicant is a “refugee.” That means the applicant must persuade the adjudicator that it is more likely than not, when all evidence is weighed, that the applicant meets the statutory refugee definition — either because she suffered past persecution on account of a protected ground, which gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of future persecution, or because she has a well‑founded fear of future persecution on account of a protected ground.

“Well‑founded fear” is a hybrid standard: it has a subjective component (the applicant sincerely fears persecution) and an objective component (a reasonable person in the applicant’s situation would fear persecution). Courts have interpreted “well‑founded” in different ways, but the operative practical rule is that an applicant can meet the standard either by showing past persecution (which creates a rebuttable presumption of future persecution) or by showing a significant possibility of future persecution. The Tenth Circuit in Jimenez emphasized that past persecution gives rise to a rebuttable presumption of future persecution, but the board and courts will carefully scrutinize whether the underlying harm actually constitutes persecution.

What counts as “persecution”? The INA does not define it, and courts have generally required something more than harassment, discrimination, or sporadic threats; persecution normally requires the infliction of suffering or harm considered serious and offensive — physical harm, prolonged detention, torture, or severe economic deprivation when inflicted for a protected reason. Courts evaluate incidents cumulatively; repeated non‑severe harms can, in aggregate, constitute persecution. But threats, standing alone, rarely satisfy the persecution element. In Jimenez, the Tenth Circuit concluded that the pattern of threats, a violent encounter at the factory, an assault on an employee, a menacing interaction with the petitioners’ son, and attendant psychological anxiety nonetheless did not rise to past persecution. The court relied on precedent holding that threats, even when repeated, and psychological distress alone are frequently insufficient.

Withholding of removal requires a higher standard of proof: the applicant must show it is “more likely than not” (i.e., a greater than 50% probability) that his life or freedom would be threatened in the proposed country of removal on account of a protected ground. This burden is commonly described as the “clear probability” standard arising from Supreme Court precedent. The higher standard reflects withholding’s mandatory nature and its narrower consequences (it prevents removal but does not afford the broader benefits of asylum). As a consequence, even applicants who meet the asylum standard may fail to meet the standard for withholding if the evidence does not cross the higher probability threshold.

CAT protection likewise demands a “more likely than not” showing that the applicant would be tortured if returned. But unlike asylum and withholding, CAT protection does not require a nexus to a protected ground; the inquiry focuses on the probability of torture, and whether the torture would be inflicted by or with the acquiescence of a public official. The scope of “torture” is defined in regulations and treaty practice and generally requires the intentional infliction of severe pain or suffering by or at the instigation of government actors.

In Jimenez, the court explained these burdens and their relationship: because the petitioners failed to establish asylum’s lower standard (no past persecution; no well‑founded fear on account of a protected ground), they could not meet the higher standard for withholding. The court did not decide CAT because petitioners did not press that claim on appeal; but it underscored that CAT would require a separate, non‑derivative showing (and a higher probability showing).

## Nexus and causation: “at least one central reason” and mixed‑motive cases

Establishing that persecution is “on account of” a protected ground — the nexus requirement — is often the most litigated element. The statutory text and caselaw require that a protected ground be at least one central reason for the persecution. Courts have made clear that the protected ground need not be the sole reason, but it must be a substantial and central motivating factor; the harm cannot be merely incidental or tangential to a dominant non‑protected motive.

When a persecutor has mixed motives, courts ask whether a protected ground was at least one central reason for the harm. The “at least one central reason” formulation (as recited in Jimenez) is a demanding standard. Evidence that the abuse was motivated primarily by personal disputes, criminal motives (e.g., extortion, property disputes), or general criminality will often undermine a claim of nexus to a protected ground. The record must include particularized evidence tying the persecutor’s actions to the protected characteristic: explicit statements, consistent patterns targeting others who share the protected trait, or credible inferences from objective circumstances.

Jimenez shows the application of this rule. The petitioners claimed persecution on account of political opinion — their membership in and campaigning for the opposition Liberal Party. The IJ and BIA found, and the Tenth Circuit agreed, that the petitioners’ targeting was predominantly a response to Jimenez’s legal intervention in a property dispute and to that intervention’s adverse effect on a criminal actor’s revenue and security. The escalated threats occurred only after Don Rafa (a narco‑trafficking leader) learned that Jimenez had investigated the property and allegedly caused searches and seizures. The men who threatened Jimenez accused him of being a “snitch” for the prosecutor’s office, and the threats appeared tied to that alleged role rather than to his political activities. The petitioners’ continuing political campaigning earlier in the election — despite prior threats — and the temporal connection between the property dispute and the intensification of harassment further supported the agency’s conclusion that political opinion was tangential. The decision illustrates how courts weigh motive evidence and why generalized assertions of political views, without a clear causal connection to the persecutor’s conduct, often fail.

## Government protection: reporting, notice, futility, and systemic failure

A required element of many asylum and withholding claims is that the persecution is inflicted by actors the state is unable or unwilling to control. The “unable or unwilling” inquiry considers whether the national government could have protected the applicant and whether the applicant sought state protection or whether doing so would have been futile or dangerous.

Key components of the government‑protection analysis:

- Notice and opportunity to respond: Courts look for evidence that the government had notice of the persecution and a real opportunity to act. If an applicant never reported the persecution, the agency has no record that the government had an opportunity to intervene, and courts will give weight to the absence of reporting. That absence can be excused, however, where reporting would have been futile or dangerous.

- Evidence of police or judicial actions: The government’s actual efforts — arrests, investigations, protective custody, enforcement actions against perpetrators — are probative. Conversely, evidence of systemic corruption, collusion between officials and persecutors, or a documented pattern of non‑response can support a conclusion that the state is unwilling or unable to protect.

- Individual versus systemic failures: Isolated incidents of unhelpful local officers are less persuasive than widespread government inaction. Courts require a showing of systemic inability or unwillingness, or evidence that the applicant would face particularized risk and that reporting would not have been effective.

In Jimenez, the petitioners did approach police officers and received discouraging responses: one officer reportedly told Jimenez that “nothing could be done” and that the family should leave. Yet the court found those anecdotal interactions insufficient to prove systemic governmental failure. Moreover, important facts undercut the petitioners’ theory that the persecutors were aligned with the government: law enforcement had attempted searches and seizures at the property associated with the persecutor, indicating some degree of governmental action against the criminal enterprise. The petitioners also had not exhausted or documented attempts to seek help beyond their immediate neighborhood or via the petitioners’ law‑enforcement connections. The court therefore concluded the record supported the agency’s determination that the Colombian government was not unable or unwilling to control the persecutors.

This application shows two practical lessons: first, claimants should document efforts to seek official protection (police reports, victim‑witness statements, correspondence), or explain convincingly why such efforts were impossible or dangerous; second, generalized assertions of corruption or collusion require evidence of systemic failure or a reasoned explanation why the particular victim would not receive protection.

## Internal relocation: feasibility and the government’s rebuttal

If an applicant cannot show past persecution or a well‑founded fear of persecution in the applicant’s home location, the government may rebut a presumption of a well‑founded fear by showing either (1) a fundamental change in country conditions such that the applicant no longer has a well‑founded fear, or (2) the applicant could avoid future persecution by relocating to another part of the country, if internal relocation is reasonable. The regulation implementing this rule is 8 C.F.R. § 1208.13(b)(1)(i), and courts analyze whether (a) alternative locations are accessible, (b) the applicant could reasonably settle there without undue hardship, and (c) relocation would meaningfully reduce the risk of persecution.

The relocation inquiry is case specific and considers ties to the proposed relocation area, availability of housing and employment, language and cultural barriers, and the reach of the persecutor. It is not enough for the government to say, in the abstract, that relocation is possible; it must show by a preponderance of the evidence that relocation is reasonable.

In Jimenez, the IJ and the BIA found that internal relocation was feasible. The petitioners had family in Colombia who remained unharmed, and Jimenez’s father continued to work in the criminal justice system, which made relocation to another area of the country more plausible. The family had in fact moved temporarily to the mother’s home in the Bogota countryside for several weeks without harm. The threatening men told the petitioners to leave the neighborhood but had not ordered them to leave the country. These facts allowed the agency and the court to accept internal relocation as a reasonable alternative, thus undercutting the petitioners’ fear of future persecution.

For practitioners and students, this emphasizes that demonstrating the unavailability of safe internal relocation often requires showing that the persecutor’s reach is nationwide, that the persecutor is aligned with authorities throughout the country, or that specific hardship (economic, administrative, social) would render relocation unreasonable.

## Derivative asylum; rider respondents; non‑derivative nature of withholding and CAT

Family structure matters in protection claims, but the rules differ for asylum, withholding, and CAT.

Asylum allows for derivative benefits: certain family members can be included on a principal applicant’s asylum application and derive asylum status if the principal applicant is granted asylum. Children, spouses, and qualifying dependents can be “rider respondents” in defensive proceedings; their claims hinge on the principal’s eligibility and the family relationship. In contrast, withholding of removal and CAT protection are non‑derivative in the sense that each person seeking those forms of protection must establish eligibility on his or her own. A parent’s success on withholding or CAT does not automatically cloak children or a spouse; each must demonstrate the higher requisite standard.

In Jimenez, the two minor sons proceeded as rider respondents and derivative beneficiaries of Jimenez’s asylum application. That is why the Tenth Circuit’s findings against Jimenez and his wife on asylum eligibility directly affected the children’s claim to derivative asylum. Because the family failed to meet the asylum standard, the children could not derive asylum through Jimenez. With withholding and CAT, the court noted that petitioners had not carried their burdens and, because withholding requires a higher showing, the asylum failure logically foreclosed withholding. The non‑derivative aspect of CAT means even if an applicant’s family member applies successfully, any other family members seeking CAT relief would still need to establish the CAT standard individually.

For students, the distinction means that litigation strategy must treat family members differently depending on the relief sought: derivative asylum may allow economies of proof and a focus on the principal applicant’s case, whereas withholding and CAT pleadings must ensure each individual’s circumstances and risk are separately articulated and proven.

## Appellate review and practical consequences

Finally, remember how substantially deferential review affects case outcomes. Factual findings by the IJ and BIA — including credibility determinations, whether harm rises to persecution, whether a protected ground was a central reason for the harm, and whether internal relocation is reasonable — are reviewed under the substantial‑evidence standard. This means that the courts of appeals will not reweigh evidence or substitute their view for the agency’s where reasonable adjudicators could reach different conclusions. Thus, advocacy before the IJ and BIA should aim to build a clear, well‑documented factual record that ties the claimed mistreatment directly to a statutory ground and anticipates the government protection and internal relocation arguments.

The Jimenez opinion exemplifies these dynamics. The petitioners presented credible testimony and documentary evidence of threats and stress, but the IJ and BIA — and ultimately the Tenth Circuit — found that the evidence did not cross statutory thresholds. The decision reinforces several practical lessons: threats alone are often insufficient; demonstrating nexus requires concrete ties between persecutor motive and the protected ground; documenting attempts to seek government protection (or explaining why such attempts would have been futile) strengthens claims; and showing that internal relocation is infeasible requires persuasive evidence of persistent risk or lack of safe alternatives.

Conclusion

This orientation has laid out the statutory frameworks, the institutional processes, and the principal doctrinal tests that govern asylum, withholding, and CAT protection. Jimenez v. Bondi serves as a useful case study of how these rules operate — and where many claims fail. Mastery of the statutory definitions, the burdens of proof, and the evidentiary strategies to establish persecution, nexus, government protection failures, and the infeasibility of internal relocation is essential for both academic analysis and effective advocacy in protection cases.
