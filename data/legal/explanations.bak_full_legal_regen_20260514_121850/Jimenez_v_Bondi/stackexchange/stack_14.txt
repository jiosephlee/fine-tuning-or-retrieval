Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

### How does the court treat unbriefed theories (like the 'particular social group' of 'social leaders')—waiver, forfeiture, or failure to exhaust?
Question:
The panel says petitioners didn’t brief PSG arguments and limits review to political opinion. Is that characterized as waiver in the court of appeals or failure to exhaust at the agency? What’s the doctrinal consequence—outright abandonment on appeal, or could the court remand if the BIA had addressed PSG but petitioners failed to brief it here?
Answer:
Short answer: The panel treated the unbriefed particular‑social‑group theory as abandoned on appeal—i.e., an appellate forfeiture/waiver of the argument—rather than as an agency exhaustion problem requiring remand. The court therefore limited its review to the only protected ground the petitioners actually argued (political opinion).

Why: The opinion says so in plain terms. The panel notes that “Petitioners, however, did not brief any arguments regarding the existence of their particular social group nor did they establish their membership in such a group.” It then states, “Accordingly, we limit our review to whether Petitioners establish a nexus between their alleged persecution and their political opinion.” Those two sentences reflect the classic appellate‑law consequence of failing to brief an issue: the issue is not preserved for appellate decision and the court will not consider it.

Doctrinal framework and consequence
- Appellate forfeiture/waiver: When a party fails to present or develop an argument in its opening brief, a court of appeals ordinarily treats the point as forfeited and declines to consider it. The panel applied that approach here: because petitioners did not brief the PSG claim (and did not establish membership), the court declined to reach it and resolved the case on the ground the petitioners did press (political opinion). The opinion’s “we limit our review” language is the operational effect of waiver.
- Not framed as agency exhaustion: The opinion does not treat the problem as a failure to exhaust administrative remedies (i.e., something that would implicate whether the BIA had a chance to consider the claim and whether remand is appropriate). The court reviewed the BIA’s decision under its ordinary standards (substantial evidence review of the administrative record) and refused to entertain the unbriefed PSG argument because it was not briefed on appeal.
- Practical consequence: The claim is effectively abandoned on appeal. The court denied relief without remanding to the BIA to address the PSG theory. In this opinion the panel did not remand or say the BIA had failed to consider a PSG claim such that remand would be required; instead it resolved the case on the ground the petitioners actually argued and supported.

What to watch for (if you litigate this issue)
- If petitioners want the court to consider a theory (e.g., a particular social group), they must either (a) develop and brief it on appeal, or (b) ensure the BIA clearly addressed it in its decision so there is a developed administrative ruling for the court to review. Absent that, the appellate court may treat the theory as forfeited and decline to consider it—precisely what happened here.
- If the BIA has expressly decided a theory below but the petitioner fails to brief it on appeal, some courts have nonetheless reviewed the BIA’s reasoning sua sponte in limited circumstances. This opinion, though, shows the panel chose not to do so and limited review to the briefed ground.

Bottom line: The Tenth Circuit treated the unbriefed “social leaders” PSG as abandoned on appeal (forfeiture/waiver) and limited its review accordingly; it did not treat the omission as an exhaustion defect requiring remand.
