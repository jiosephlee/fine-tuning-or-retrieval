Title: Blog about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

# Private Violence, Public Alignments: Proving State Complicity in Colombia

## Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Jimenez v. Bondi (Oct. 7, 2025) is a useful primer on how U.S. courts evaluate claims that criminal actors who commit private violence are effectively arms of the state. The case turns on three tightly related questions that arise again and again in asylum and withholding adjudications from countries with strong criminal nonstate actors and fraught state institutions: Was the applicant persecuted; if so, was the persecution on account of a protected ground; and was the state unable or unwilling to protect the applicant? The court denied relief on all three prongs, but the opinion’s treatment of the “unable or unwilling” inquiry is the most consequential for litigants and adjudicators confronting claims that private persecutors are aligned or colluding with government actors.

Why pay attention? Because many asylum seekers from Colombia and similar countries do not allege that a soldier or policeman dragged them out of their house. Rather, they allege that organized criminal groups—paramilitaries, guerrillas, or narco-traffickers—targeted them, and that these groups have connections to, or de facto protection from, the state. How courts assess whether the state’s connection to those groups is sufficient to defeat a claim of government protection is determinative. Jimenez illustrates both the limits of what a single, factbound record can show and the kinds of country-conditions and case-specific proof that will be required to demonstrate state complicity or acquiescence.

## What the court did: a close reading of the record

The family in Jimenez presented a mixed record. They were Liberal Party canvassers and supported a rival presidential candidate, and the husband had worked as a provisional lawyer and intern at the prosecutor’s office. The harassment escalated after he confronted the tenant of a disputed property who turned out to be a local narco-trafficking leader known as “Don Rafa.” Threatening brochures, in-person threats at the factory, an incident at the son’s school, and a colleague’s earlier kidnapping and murder prompted the family to flee to the United States.

The BIA and IJ concluded, and the Tenth Circuit affirmed, that three independent defects doomed the claim. First, the mistreatment—threats and psychological harms without any lasting physical injury—did not rise to persecution as the court has construed that threshold in previous cases. Second, the primary motive for the threats was not political opinion but retaliation for Jimenez’s interference with Don Rafa’s economic interests and property, so the political-opinion nexus was at best tangential. Third, and most relevant here, the record showed affirmative law-enforcement activity against the property in question (several searches and seizures) and did not show systemic government inaction or collusion. The court relied on petitioner testimony that a couple of individual officers were dismissive, but it held that anecdotal unhelpfulness was not enough to prove the Colombian government was “unable or unwilling” to protect the family, especially when the government had in fact sought to police the property. In short, the evidence favored a conclusion that state actors had notice and at least some responsiveness, undermining a claim that filing a police report would have been futile.

The practical effect: Jimenez’s factual sequence—escalating threats that track closely to a discrete property dispute with a local criminal leader, along with evidence that police had acted against that property—was insufficient to show state complicity or effective absence of protection.

## What the record shows — and what it does not

The Tenth Circuit’s dispositive factual finding was that government response to the underlying criminal enterprise existed in the record: the premises were allegedly subject to searches and closures by health and police authorities. That factual finding is important; it directly undercuts a theory of state acquiescence. The court also emphasized the lack of evidence that petitioners sought help beyond a few neighborhood officers or that they reported the prior politically motivated threats. From the court’s perspective, without a showing of systemic police apathy or political collusion—rather than isolated instances of unhelpful officers—the legal standard for “unable or unwilling” was unmet.

But it is equally important to note what the record lacked. There was no documentary evidence that police or prosecution units systematically protected Don Rafa, no proof of investigations that were opened and then intentionally shut down, no forensic linkage between named government officials and the persecutors, and no expert or country-conditions evidence showing a pattern of collusion in that locality. Absence of such evidence is a predictable Achilles’ heel in many asylum cases—courts are reluctant to infer systemic corruption from isolated anecdotes.

The lesson: when government actors have taken some, even imperfect, enforcement steps against the perpetrators or premises tied to the attackers, petitioners need stronger, more systemic proof to show that those actions were superficial, pretextual, or insufficient because of collusion higher up the chain.

## How to prove state complicity or acquiescence — what the record needs

Jimenez is a reminder that proving the state is “unable or unwilling” to protect someone—particularly where the persecutors are private criminal actors—requires more than a few hostile officers and a claimant’s subjective fear. Successful claims typically combine case-specific evidence with country-conditions proof. Key categories of proof that courts and adjudicators often find persuasive include:

- Contemporaneous reports and official records. Police reports, complaints filed with prosecutors, documentation of investigations that were opened and later closed without reasonable explanation, and administrative records showing inaction after notice are powerful. They show notice, opportunity to act, and actual government choices.

- Evidence of non-enforcement patterns. Reliable country-conditions materials (U.S. State Department reports, credible NGO reports, local press investigations) that show chronic non-enforcement, impunity rates, or patterns of law-enforcement refusal to pursue organized-crime actors in the relevant region help transform an anecdote into a pattern.

- Evidence linking perpetrators and officials. Documents, testimony, or investigative reporting showing ties—financial, familial, or political—between named persecutors and identifiable state actors can demonstrate that enforcement was not just weak but compromised.

- Evidence of targeted impunity. Proof that other victims who reported similar crimes were ignored, threatened, or worse, or that complaints were systematically dismissed, supports a finding that the government will not protect the applicant.

- Expert affidavits. Well-sourced expert declarations that situate the particular incidents in the broader context of local collusion or political alliances can bridge gaps between a claimant’s individualized story and country-wide patterns.

- Corroboration of direct threats and incidents. Photos, contemporaneous witness statements, medical or school records (e.g., therapy notes for a child), or statements from coworkers who can attest to threats or to perpetrators’ identifications strengthen credibility and help establish the sequence of events that produced the claim.

Without such layered proof, courts will often credit the fact that some state action occurred—a search, a closure, an arrest—or that the applicant did not take available steps to seek broader protection. That is precisely what happened in Jimenez.

## How asylum’s “unable or unwilling” standard differs from CAT’s “acquiescence” standard

The Jimenez opinion did not reach the Convention Against Torture (CAT) claim on the merits, but the case highlights an important doctrinal point courts and advocates must keep in mind: the “unable or unwilling” test used in asylum and withholding cases and the “government acquiescence” standard under CAT are related but distinct.

For asylum/withholding, petitioners must show persecution on account of a protected ground and that the government is “unable or unwilling” to control the persecutor. Courts assess governmental response and look for notice, opportunity, and demonstrable failure to protect. Evidence of some state action—especially action against the same criminal enterprise—can be fatally persuasive for adjudicators unless petitioners show that enforcement was a sham or that the state tacitly supported the persecutors.

CAT’s standard is different in emphasis. CAT protection turns on whether torture would be inflicted by or with the consent or acquiescence of public officials. Courts have sometimes required evidence of the government’s intent to permit or tolerate torture, or at least that officials were aware and willfully blind to likely torture. While there is overlap—both standards probe governmental engagement—CAT often requires stronger proof of deliberate tolerance or complicity in the particular acts of severe harm. In practice this means that even where an asylum claim fails because the government’s overall response appears at least partially effective, a CAT claim might still succeed if there is convincing evidence that the particular persecutors operate with actual acquiescence from identifiable officials or that the state would be willfully blind to the torture the applicant would face upon return.

The practical implication is that advocates should not abandon CAT claims simply because they cannot establish asylum’s political-nexus prong. But they must be prepared to offer evidence that gets closer to proving state knowledge and consent to the specific torturous conduct.

## Downstream effects and practical takeaways

Jimenez will likely be cited by IJ’s, the BIA, and courts in future cases from Colombia and elsewhere for two propositions. First, where private violence is temporally tied to a discrete, non-political dispute (here, a property confrontation and threats over “revenue and security”), courts will scrutinize nexus and often find political opinion secondary. Second, where the record contains evidence of some government action against the perpetrators or locations in question, adjudicators will demand stronger proof of systemic collusion or targeted impunity to find state “inability or unwillingness.”

For practitioners that means early and aggressive evidence-gathering. Obtain police reports, complaint numbers, filings with prosecutors, and any local administrative records. Assemble reliable country-conditions reports focused on the specific region and the named groups. Secure declarations from investigators, journalists, or NGOs who have direct knowledge of regional patterns of collusion. Corroborate threats and incidents with witnesses, contemporaneous messages, or other documentary material. And do not rely solely on a client’s reported belief that the persecutors are “aligned” with the government—translate that belief into objective, verifiable evidence.

For adjudicators and courts, Jimenez is a cautionary reminder to distinguish isolated municipal failures from systemic state complicity while remaining sensitive to how local power-brokers can render state protections effectively unavailable. The record-based approach the Tenth Circuit applied is defensible, but it should not become a pretext for dismissing hard-to-prove allegations of collusion where credible, corroborated evidence of officials’ ties to perpetrators exists.

## Conclusion

Jimenez offers a factbound lesson in how difficult it can be to win asylum or withholding claims premised on private violence without robust documentary and country-conditions support. The presence of some official enforcement activity against the perpetrators’ property—combined with sparse evidence of systemic non-enforcement or collusion—was fatal to the claim. For future claimants from Colombia and similar contexts, the path to demonstrating state “unable or unwilling” status requires assembling both granular, case-specific proof and broader evidence of patterns of impunity or collusion. Absent that dual showing, courts will be reluctant to infer that private violent actors operate with effective governmental protection.
