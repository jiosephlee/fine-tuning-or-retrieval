Title: Blog about the opinion: Jimenez v. Bondi

# When Politics Is Tangential: Mixed‑Motive Nexus After Canon Jimenez v. Bondi

## Why this case matters

The Tenth Circuit’s decision in Canon Jimenez v. Bondi is a useful, and in some ways cautionary, reminder of how difficult it can be for asylum seekers to satisfy the “nexus” requirement when private actors with mixed motives target them. At issue in the appeal was whether threats and harassment aimed at a Colombian family—who had been active in partisan campaigning and whose husband had professionally confronted a local narco‑trafficker about property—were “on account of” the family’s political opinion. The court affirmed the Board of Immigration Appeals’ (BIA) conclusion that the persecutor’s predominant motive was economic and retaliatory—protecting criminal revenue and security after Jimenez’s interference—rendering any political animus merely “tangential.” That conclusion defeated the applicants’ claim under the statutory requirement that a protected ground be “at least one central reason” for persecution.

The ruling has important implications for future asylum claims that arise where criminal actors, corruption, or anti‑corruption activities intersect with political activism. It underscores the evidentiary and framing challenges in mixed‑motive cases—particularly where the applicant’s conduct could plausibly have provoked private criminal retaliation for reasons other than political opposition.

## The court’s reasoning in a nutshell

The Tenth Circuit applied the familiar three‑part test for past persecution: harm rising to persecution, causation (nexus) to a protected ground, and government inability or unwillingness to control the persecutor. While the court found that the petitioners suffered threats and frightening incidents, it concluded these harms—taken in the aggregate—did not meet the jurisdiction’s established baseline for “past persecution.” More centrally for the nexus discussion, the court held that substantial evidence supported the BIA’s determination that the persecutor’s main reason for targeting Jimenez and his family was that Jimenez’s involvement in a property dispute interfered with the trafficker’s revenue and security interests. That interference, not the family’s partisan work for a rival presidential candidate, was the dominant motive.

The court relied on several fact‑based signals: the escalation of threats began only after Jimenez confronted the tenant (who was linked to the trafficker), the persecutors explicitly accused Jimenez of being a “snitch” and of working for the prosecutor’s office, and the timing and content of the threats connected the harassment more closely to the property dispute than to the family’s prior campaigning. Against that backdrop, the court saw Petitioners’ political activity as tangential rather than a central causal reason. The opinion reiterates the settled principle that when a persecutor has multiple motives, at least one protected ground must be a central reason for the harm—not merely present or subordinate.

## Putting the ruling in doctrinal context

The decision tracks established Tenth Circuit doctrine—drawing on Rivera‑Barrientos and related precedent—about the “one central reason” standard for nexus. It also reflects an established reluctance to construe private‑actor violence as persecution on a protected ground unless the evidence shows a clear, central causal link to the protected characteristic or opinion.

There are two doctrinal points to highlight. First, mixed‑motive analysis remains fact‑intensive and deferential: courts evaluate whether the administrative record compels a different conclusion, and they do not reweigh credibility where substantial evidence supports the BIA’s reading. That means strategic evidentiary development at the administrative hearing stage is critical.

Second, claims premised on anti‑corruption, whistleblowing, or law‑enforcement‑adjacent activities face a particular challenge: persecutors will often couch retaliation as a response to interference with criminal economics or security rather than as punishment for political opinion. When applicants have engaged in both partisan activity and actions that might directly threaten private criminal interests, adjudicators may view the latter as the motivating factor. The Tenth Circuit’s opinion reinforces that inferential gap.

## Practical consequences and downstream effects

For practitioners and advocates, Canon Jimenez signals several practical realities. If an applicant’s political activity is temporally distant, diffuse, or shared with many others in an area—while confrontations with criminal actors are proximate, individualized, and tied to specific interference with criminal enterprises—adjudicators may find the political explanation weak. Equally, when private actors articulate non‑political reasons for violence (e.g., punishing a “snitch” or protecting revenue), decisionmakers may credit those motives absent strong, contrary evidence.

The decision may also be cited in future cases as support for a narrow reading of political‑opinion nexus where criminal‑economic motives are plausible. That could make it harder for clients who combine community leadership, anti‑corruption activities, or legal work with overt partisan campaigning to obtain asylum unless they can develop record evidence tying persecutors’ statements, patterns, or organizational incentives directly to political animus.

## Strategies for proving nexus in mixed‑motive settings

Canon Jimenez does not foreclose asylum grants in mixed‑motive cases, but it points to what litigants must show to get past the “one central reason” hurdle. A few practical strategies follow from the opinion:

- Seek contemporaneous statements or objective indicators of political animus. Persecutors’ own words—even if ambiguous—are potent. When the persecutor explicitly references the applicant’s political activities, that substantially shores up a nexus claim. Record any threats or materials that tie the violence to partisan identifiers (party membership, campaign role, slogans) rather than to business or private disputes.

- Emphasize timing and escalation tied to political acts. If threats or violence begin immediately after a political speech, protest, or campaign action, highlight that temporal link. Here, the court stressed that the threats escalated after a property confrontation; litigants should document and argue the reverse where the facts allow.

- Build structural context—show that the persecutor targets political actors systematically. Evidence that the group targets rivals of a political faction, or has a pattern of suppressing partisan opponents, helps place individual incidents in a political pattern rather than in isolated criminal retaliation.

- Distinguish professional or anti‑corruption activity from neutral private disputes. When an applicant’s professional role involved representing victims, reporting crimes, or cooperating with law enforcement on matters that overlap with public‑interest lawyering or civic political action, emphasize how those roles are political in your client’s country insofar as they represent opposition to entrenched power or policy.

- Use corroboration beyond testimony: police reports, contemporaneous news articles, NGO reports, and expert country‑condition evidence can show that local criminal actors operate as political enforcers or that certain groups are informally allied with political factions—countering the inference that motives were purely economic.

## Conclusion

Canon Jimenez v. Bondi reaffirms that mixed‑motive asylum claims require careful factual and doctrinal navigation. Where private persecutors have plausible nonpolitical reasons to retaliate, courts will look for strong, specific evidence that political opinion occupied a central causal role. For practitioners, the case underscores the need to anticipate mixed‑motive in the record and to marshal concrete indicators—statements, timing, patterns, and corroboration—that tie feared persecution squarely to a protected ground.
