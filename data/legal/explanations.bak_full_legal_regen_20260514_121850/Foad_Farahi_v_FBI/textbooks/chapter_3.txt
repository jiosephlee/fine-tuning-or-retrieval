Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

Chapter 3: # “Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes”: The Pratt–Campbell Standard

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) shields from disclosure a variety of records that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” That threshold phrase—“compiled for law enforcement purposes”—is the gatekeeper for all Exemption 7 claims. If an agency cannot carry the threshold showing, the court need not consider downstream questions about interference with enforcement proceedings, disclosure harms, or segregability. The D.C. Circuit’s recent decision in Farahi v. FBI (2025) demonstrates how courts apply the familiar Pratt–Campbell line of authority to the modern facts of counterterrorism and long-running national-security investigations. This chapter explains the doctrinal test that flows from Pratt v. Webster and related cases, clarifies the deference accorded to specialized law-enforcement agencies such as the FBI, describes what kinds of investigations qualify as “compiled for law enforcement purposes” (with particular attention to counterterrorism and intelligence-driven inquiries), and offers practical guidance for how agencies can and courts should articulate the required nexus without unduly compromising ongoing investigations.

## Pratt v. Webster: the rational-nexus and possible-violation requirements

Pratt v. Webster established the basic structure of the “compiled for law enforcement purposes” inquiry that courts still apply today. The test is twofold. First, the agency must establish a rational nexus between the records sought and one of the agency’s law-enforcement duties. Second, there must be a connection between the individual or incident that is the subject of the records and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. The point of the test is straightforward: FOIA Exemption 7 was designed to protect information assembled in the service of governmental law-enforcement activity, not to shelter purely administrative materials or general policy work.

The “rational nexus” prong is not a demanding one in form. The agency need not prove criminality or a likelihood of prosecution; it must show only a rational relationship between the records and one of the agency’s law-enforcement responsibilities. This requirement prevents agencies from invoking Exemption 7 for materials that have only an attenuated connection to enforcement—personnel management files, policy memoranda, or routine regulatory work unconnected to a discrete investigatory function. But the showing is also not hyper-technical: documents created to investigate allegations, gather or analyze evidence, identify subjects, or otherwise advance an investigation typically satisfy the nexus requirement.

The “possible violation” prong ensures that the agency’s investigatory focus is on legal wrongdoing or security harms, not merely on general intelligence-gathering or administrative curiosity. Courts have required a showing of a connection between the subject (an individual or incident) and a plausible criminal or security risk. The agency need not demonstrate proof of a crime; a reasonable basis to suspect a violation—or a concern that the subject presents a security risk—is sufficient to place the materials within Exemption 7’s protective ambit. Thus Pratt contemplates a fact-sensitive review: the agency’s declarations and the surrounding circumstances must supply enough factual detail for a court to evaluate whether the requisite connection exists.

Pratt’s demands are deliberately balanced: they guard against casual invocation of Exemption 7 for materials unrelated to enforcement, while recognizing that an agency need not disclose the entire investigative theory or evidence to show that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes. Courts therefore look to the agency’s affidavits, the factual background in the public record, and any other non-sensitive contextual material to determine whether Pratt’s two elements are satisfied.

## Campbell v. DOJ and Clemente v. FBI: deference to specialized law-enforcement agencies

The D.C. Circuit has been clear that the degree of deference an agency receives in meeting the Pratt test depends in part on the agency’s character. In Campbell v. DOJ, the court emphasized that when a specialized law-enforcement agency like the FBI invokes Exemption 7, the agency’s determinations about whether records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes are entitled to a “fair measure of deference.” The reasoning is simple: specialized agencies possess institutional expertise about their own investigatory programs, their statutory missions, and the nature and significance of the records in their custody. Courts therefore accept proffers from those agencies with more latitude, so long as the agency’s declarations provide a sufficient factual predicate for judicial review.

Clemente v. FBI applies that principle to the modern FOIA context, affirming that the FBI can rely on its institutional knowledge, combined with factual declarations from agency personnel, to satisfy the Pratt threshold. But deference is not abdication. Campbell and Clemente reinforce that even the FBI must provide declarations that reasonably describe how the records relate to law-enforcement duties and the basis for believing the records concern a possible violation or security risk. Conclusory, boilerplate assertions are insufficient; the agency’s proffer must give the court some basis to evaluate the claim.

Farahi illustrates how the deference principle operates in practice. The court accepted that the FBI, as a specialized law-enforcement agency with counterterrorism responsibilities, is entitled to deference—and that deference supports the agency’s showing when the declarations, taken together, establish the nexus to law-enforcement duties and to possible violations. But the opinion also makes clear that the agency’s declarations must provide enough detail to permit judicial scrutiny: the FBI’s public declaration, together with a more detailed ex parte declaration, furnished the necessary factual foundation. Where an agency produces both public and classified or ex parte material that complements the public descriptions, the court will give the agency’s claims weight—especially when national-security concerns are implicated—but the agency still bears the burden of persuasion.

## What counts as “compiled for law enforcement purposes”: counterterrorism and intelligence-driven investigations

A perennial question is whether intelligence-driven investigations—particularly counterterrorism inquiries that borrow techniques from intelligence agencies—qualify as law-enforcement compilations under Exemption 7. The D.C. Circuit’s practice is to treat investigations led by law-enforcement agencies (especially the FBI) as law-enforcement compilations, even when they involve classified methods, foreign intelligence, or national-security considerations. The key inquiry is whether the records were assembled “in furtherance of” the agency’s law-enforcement duties.

Counterterrorism investigations, hybrid criminal-intelligence matters, and investigations that straddle national-security and criminal-law domains are routinely treated as falling within Exemption 7’s scope when a law-enforcement agency compiles the records and has a demonstrable law-enforcement purpose. The court’s decision in Farahi underscores this point: the FBI’s investigation into a person suspected of links to terrorist organizations, recruitment activity, and ties to convicted or detained terrorism suspects was squarely within the FBI’s law-enforcement remit. That the investigation engaged national-security concerns did not push the records outside Exemption 7; rather, it reinforced the importance of deference and of allowing the FBI to protect information assembled for investigative purposes.

What this means in practice is that documents generated by law-enforcement personnel, compiled to identify subjects, analyze networks, preserve evidence, or support prosecutions or other enforcement actions—regardless of whether the subject matter is conventional criminality or national-security related—will tend to qualify as “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” Conversely, materials compiled primarily for policy advice, administrative decisionmaking, or external diplomatic functions will not qualify unless the agency can point to a direct law-enforcement nexus.

Agencies should therefore frame their declarations to emphasize the investigatory function of the records: who compiled them, for what investigatory tasks (e.g., evidentiary collection, target analysis, case development), and how those tasks fall within the agency’s statutory law-enforcement duties. When investigations are hybrid in nature, stating that the record was compiled by a law-enforcement division, as part of an investigation intended to support possible enforcement proceedings, will typically be sufficient under Pratt—so long as the agency avoids pure boilerplate and supplies a factual predicate.

## Applying the threshold in practice: articulating the individual/incident connection to security risks

Applying Pratt’s second element—the connection between the individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation—requires concrete, fact-focused description. An agency cannot simply say, “these records concern a national-security investigation,” and rest on deference. Courts have repeatedly instructed agencies to provide a factual narrative—without disclosing sensitive intelligence—that ties the subject to a plausible law-enforcement interest.

The agency’s declaration should identify, in non-sensitive terms, the nature of the suspected wrongdoing or risk, the relationship between the subject and the alleged or reasonably suspected actors or incidents, and the role the records play in the investigation. For example, saying that the records “document the subject’s recruitment activities for an organization designated as a foreign terrorist organization” or “contain witness statements and field reports about the subject’s ties to indicted co-conspirators” furnishes a concrete link between the individual and a possible violation. Courts have accepted such descriptions when they avoid revealing the operational specifics that would themselves undermine an investigation.

Farahi provides a concrete model of acceptable practice. The FBI’s public declaration asserted that the records were compiled in connection with investigations into “national security and international terrorism,” and the ex parte declaration supplied additional detail about the nature and scope of the investigation. The district court and the D.C. Circuit judged the public declaration adequate when supplemented by the ex parte material, and concluded the agency had plausibly tied the records to a law-enforcement duty and to a possible security risk. The case also illustrates the practical interplay between public and classified materials: an agency can satisfy Pratt while withholding operational detail, so long as the public description, taken with the fact that the agency is a specialized law-enforcement body, gives the court a foundation to find the requisite connection.

Practitioners should note common pitfalls. Vague, conclusory assertions—“these records relate to potential wrongdoing”—are unlikely to satisfy a judicial reviewer, particularly in long-stale investigations. Declarations that merely recite the statutory language of Exemption 7 without factual support will not carry the agency’s burden. Likewise, assertions that the records were compiled for “national-security” reasons without any description of the subject’s alleged connection to that risk are susceptible to challenge. To withstand scrutiny, the agency must show that the records were used or intended to be used in furtherance of a law-enforcement function focused on a particular individual or incident that plausibly implicated federal law or security concerns.

## Using public and ex parte details to establish the threshold without compromising investigations

FOIA litigation involving national security often forces an uncomfortable tradeoff: the agency must give enough information to permit meaningful judicial review while not disclosing the very operational details that, if revealed, would endanger ongoing proceedings. The jurisprudence allows—indeed contemplates—the use of layered submissions: a public declaration that provides a non-sensitive factual framework, and a classified or ex parte declaration that supplies the sensitive particulars for the court’s in camera review.

Farahi demonstrates how that layered approach works. The FBI submitted a public declaration from a high-ranking records official that described the general purposes of the investigation and attested that the records were law-enforcement in nature. To supply the underlying factual detail without exposing it publicly, the FBI filed an ex parte sealed declaration that the district court reviewed in camera. The court relied on the aggregate record—public and ex parte—to conclude that the Pratt threshold was satisfied. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit affirmed that approach: an agency may rely on ex parte declarations to supply the detailed factual foundation required, and a court need not recite its in camera analysis on the public docket when doing so would disclose the sensitive material itself.

A few lessons flow from this practice. First, agencies should prepare public declarations that are as informative as possible without disclosing operational specifics: identify the agency component responsible for the records, describe the general nature of the investigation (e.g., counterterrorism, transnational criminal enterprise), and explain the law-enforcement function the records serve. Second, agencies should be ready to supplement those public proffers with classified or ex parte affidavits that the court may review in camera. Those sealed declarations should provide the factual particulars—who the targets are, what investigative steps have been taken, how the records fit into the case theory—so that the court can perform a meaningful review. Third, agencies must update stale declarations. Farahi required the FBI to confirm that the investigation remained ongoing; an agency that relies on older affidavits risks a finding that it has not carried its burden.

For litigants and courts, the practice raises difficult fairness questions. FOIA requesters may be handicapped when they cannot see the ex parte material, and they may have limited opportunity to rebut the agency’s classified assertions. The courts have acknowledged this tension, but they have also explained that there is nothing novel about ex parte submissions in national-security FOIA cases: when the only way to assess the government’s privilege claim is to examine the sensitive material itself, in camera review by the judge is the appropriate procedure. To balance the competing interests, courts will press agencies to make their public declarations as informative as they safely can and will scrutinize ex parte declarations to ensure they are not merely conclusory.

## Practical drafting and litigating guidance

For agency counsel:
- Draft public declarations that identify the compiling component, describe the nature of the investigation and the law-enforcement duties implicated, and provide non-sensitive factual predicates tying the records to suspected wrongdoing or security risks. Avoid boilerplate Exemption-7 language alone.
- Prepare thorough ex parte or classified declarations for in camera review that supply the sensitive factual details necessary to satisfy Pratt. Explain clearly why those details cannot appear on the public record.
- Update declarations when litigation is protracted. Courts expect agencies to account for the passage of time and to confirm whether investigations remain current.

For FOIA requesters:
- Press agencies on the adequacy of public declarations and on whether the public proffer, without the sealed material, is precise enough to permit judicial review.
- When possible, show why the public record undercuts the agency’s claimed nexus (e.g., evidence that an agency component did not compile the documents for enforcement purposes).
- Challenge conclusory or stale affidavits and demand renewal or supplementation, as Farahi did successfully in part at the district-court level.

For courts:
- Give appropriate deference to specialized law-enforcement agencies, particularly where national-security concerns are implicated, but insist on a sufficient factual showing under Pratt.
- Use in camera review of ex parte material where necessary to permit meaningful judicial review while minimizing public disclosure of sensitive information.
- Require updates when litigation has been lengthy and consider whether a narrowly tailored public description could be supplemented without revealing operational details.

## Conclusion

The Pratt–Campbell standard defines the FOIA Exemption 7 threshold as a pragmatic, fact-sensitive test. Agencies must show a rational nexus between the records and an agency law-enforcement duty, and a connection between the subject or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. Courts give a “fair measure of deference” to specialized law-enforcement agencies like the FBI, especially in counterterrorism and national-security contexts, but that deference does not excuse conclusory assertions. Farahi v. FBI illustrates the modern application of the standard: a layered public/ex parte proffer, careful factual description (even if some details are reserved for in camera review), and timely updates can carry the agency’s burden under Pratt; failure to supply those elements may compel further scrutiny or supplementation. Understanding how to articulate the nexus without betraying investigative secrets is therefore the central practical challenge in Exemption 7 litigation—and the recurring theme of the Pratt–Campbell jurisprudence.
