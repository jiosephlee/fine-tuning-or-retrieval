Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

Chapter 8: # Procedural Architecture of FOIA Litigation

Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation is, in many respects, litigation of a distinctive procedural species. The statute creates a highly regimented administrative process, allocates burdens of proof, and invites courts to resolve disputes largely on the paper record assembled by agencies. This chapter examines the procedural building blocks that govern most FOIA suits: when a requester may sue; how courts resolve disputes on summary judgment; the scope and limits of appellate review and judicial deference; how litigants preserve and forfeit claims; the role of sealed and ex parte filings in protecting sensitive materials while enabling review; and the remedies and remands courts employ when agency submissions are insufficient. Each section draws on the Farahi v. FBI litigation to illustrate how these procedural doctrines play out in practice.

## Exhaustion and timing: when a requester may sue

FOIA requires agencies to respond to requests within a prescribed period and gives requesters a cause of action if an agency fails to comply. Under the statute, an agency must determine whether to comply “within 20 working days” after receipt of a request, subject to tolling and certain statutory extensions (for consultations, for “unusual circumstances,” and for administrative appeals). If the agency does not make a timely determination, the requestor may treat the delay as a constructive denial and bring suit in federal court. The statutory text and regulations thus create a firm procedural trigger: a plaintiff may sue after the statutory period lapses without an adequate response, or after an adverse determination has issued.

Practically, FOIA litigation often begins after an agency misses the statutory deadline. Farahi’s suit is a canonical example: he sued after the FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding to his request for his FBI file. Long after suit commenced, the agency completed its document review and moved for summary judgment. That procedural posture — suit filed based on a missed deadline, followed by successive agency disclosures and withholdings — is common. It underscores a key lesson: FOIA ripeness is mechanical in the sense that the requester may initiate litigation once the statutory deadlines or administrative steps have been exhausted, but the merits will usually be litigated only after the agency has produced the record it intends to rely on.

Two corollaries are worth stressing. First, administrative exhaustion in FOIA differs from exhaustion doctrines in other statutory schemes (for example, administrative law requiring exhaustion of agency rulemaking processes): FOIA contemplates prompt judicial review once the statutory response period lapses or an adverse determination is issued. Second, requesters should preserve and document administrative steps (the date of the request, any tracking numbers, communications about extensions) because courts will focus on whether the requester diligently pursued his administrative remedy or simply rushed to court without giving the agency a chance to comply within the statutory framework.

## Summary judgment in FOIA: record-based resolution via declarations

FOIA is predominantly a paper-intensive statute; the typical case is resolved on motions for summary judgment. The reason is simple: the controlling question is almost always whether the agency’s withholding was authorized by a statutory exemption, and that question can usually be resolved by comparing the withheld documents (or accurate descriptions thereof) to the statutory exemptions. Pursuant to Rule 56, courts routinely decide FOIA cases on summary judgment, with the agency bearing the burden of proving that an exemption applies.

Because exculpatory or incriminating facts are rarely disputed in FOIA litigation, the evidentiary device for resolving the exemption question is the agency affidavit or declaration. These declarations (sometimes called Vaughn affidavits when they supply item-by-item descriptions) must provide sufficient factual and logical detail to permit meaningful judicial review. The agency’s declaration should identify the records at issue, the exemption(s) claimed, and the specific reasons why each exemption applies. It should also address segregability: whether any nonexempt material can reasonably be released and, if so, whether the agency has produced it.

Farahi illustrates both the centrality of agency declarations and the pitfalls that arise when time passes. The FBI initially supported its summary-judgment motion with a declaration that described the law enforcement nature of the records and asserted that disclosure could interfere with pending enforcement proceedings. The district court, mindful that years had elapsed since that declaration, denied summary judgment without prejudice and ordered updated declarations. The court’s approach reflects a recurring practice: courts will accept agency affidavits as the primary record for review, but they insist on contemporaneous, specific explanations and will require supplementation when the passage of time or lack of detail raises legitimate concerns.

A few doctrinal points about declarations are particularly important for students and practitioners. First, specificity matters. General, conclusory assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” or that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere” will not always suffice; courts ask for a rational connection between the asserted harms and the specific information withheld. Second, courts will often accept in camera or sealed submissions when disclosures would reveal the very secrets the agency seeks to protect — but agencies cannot rely exclusively on unreviewable assertions. Public summaries that provide enough detail for the adversary and the court, accompanied by sealed elaborations for the court’s eyes only, are the norm in cases implicating law enforcement or national security. Third, segregability is not a ritual; agencies must explain why redactions, rather than wholesale withholding, would still permit meaningful disclosure without undermining protected interests. In Farahi the FBI argued that meaningful segregable material was not reasonably separable because nonexempt material was interspersed with exempt material line-by-line, an argument the district court accepted after reviewing the agency’s sealed submissions.

## Appellate review: de novo standard and deference to agency expertise where appropriate

When a district court disposes of a FOIA case on summary judgment, appellate courts review the ruling de novo. That de novo review means that the appellate court reexamines whether the agency satisfied its statutory burden to justify withholding under a claimed exemption. The appellate court independently evaluates the sufficiency of the record, including agency declarations and any in camera materials, to determine whether the statutory criteria are met.

But de novo review does not mean a blank slate. In FOIA cases, especially ones implicating law enforcement or national security, courts recognize a constrained form of deference to agency expertise. The D.C. Circuit has long explained that the FBI and other law-enforcement agencies are entitled to “a fair measure of deference” in invoking exemptions that bear on ongoing investigations or security matters. That deference manifests in two ways. First, courts accept that agencies — by virtue of their mission — have superior access to facts about ongoing investigations, the significance of particular records, and the potential for adverse effects from disclosure. Second, courts are often willing to accept agency factual assertions (for example, that an investigation exists or that a particular disclosure would “tip off” targets) so long as the declarations are reasonably specific and nonconclusory.

Farahi exemplifies this balancing act. The D.C. Circuit applied de novo review to the district court’s grant of summary judgment, but it also emphasized that the FBI’s invocation of Exemption 7(A) merited a “fair measure of deference.” The court relied on the agency’s public and sealed declarations to conclude that the withheld materials were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The opinion shows how appellate courts will independently review whether the statutory elements are satisfied while simultaneously acknowledging that agencies have institutional competence that justifies relying on their specialized assessments — particularly where national security or counterterrorism are involved.

Students should internalize the practical implication: an appellate court will scrutinize whether agency declarations are detailed and timely, but it will not substitute its own judgment for the agency’s where the agency has explained the connection between the information withheld and the asserted harm. The appropriate posture is de novo review with an appreciation for agency expertise, not blind deference.

## Forfeiture and issue preservation: raising challenges to categories and segregability

A separate but recurrent procedural theme in FOIA practice concerns the preservation of issues and the consequences of forfeiture. Courts will not entertain on appeal arguments a party failed to press sufficiently in the district court. This rule applies with special force in FOIA cases, where agencies often justify withholding on multiple grounds and where the district court may resolve the dispute after in camera review or after issuing detailed rulings based on the entire record.

Two instructive decisions illuminate the point. Gallant v. NLRB articulated that when an agency withholds files in categories (rather than item-by-item), those categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to allow a court to assess the propriety of each claimed exemption. If the agency provides distinct categories, the challenger must identify how and why those categories are inadequate; a generalized attack on the categorization will often fail. In Khine v. DHS, the D.C. Circuit reiterated that parties who fail to raise specific challenges to the partitions or descriptions used by the agency forfeit those issues on appeal.

The Farahi opinion underscores the practical bite of these rules. Farahi objected on appeal to the way the FBI defined categories of withheld records, arguing they were overbroad. The court refused to entertain that argument because Farahi did not specify why the FBI’s categories were insufficient or how the agency’s designations were legally defective — he had forfeited the point by not developing it adequately below. The lesson is clear: litigants must press specific, developed arguments in the district court about the sufficiency of categories, the content of the Vaughn index, and segregability; merely asserting in general terms that categories are broad or that segregable material must exist is unlikely to preserve an appellate claim.

For practitioners, the takeaway is tactical: early in the district-court litigation, force the agency to explain categories and to produce a detailed Vaughn index or to allow in camera review. If the agency resists, crystallize objections on the record. On appeal, courts will look to whether the challenger presented a meaningful, focused challenge below.

## Protective orders, sealed filings, and structuring the record to permit meaningful review

FOIA’s twin goals — transparency and protection of legitimately secret information — often collide. The procedural response is a menu of tools: public declarations that summarize key facts, Vaughn indices that describe records with enough specificity to permit review, sealed or ex parte declarations that provide the detailed factual foundation the agency cannot disclose publicly, and in camera review as a safety valve. Courts have developed practices intended to balance the requester’s right to a fair adversarial process against the agency’s interest in preventing disclosure.

Farahi presents a prototypical posture. The FBI filed both public and ex parte sealed declarations. The district court allowed the ex parte filings; Farahi did not oppose the agency’s motion for sealed and ex parte submissions. The district court relied on the sealed declarations in concluding that the records were not reasonably segregable and that Exemption 7(A) applied. On appeal the D.C. Circuit stressed that while ex parte filings impose a handicap on requesters, they are sometimes necessary to permit the court to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret. The court also noted that the requester’s failure to object to ex parte submissions undermined his later challenge to the court’s reliance on them.

Several practical rules emerge. First, when an agency moves to file ex parte or sealed declarations, a requester should carefully consider whether to consent; if the requester objects, the district court must decide whether the public interests favor an in camera or public resolution. Second, agencies should, when feasible, provide a public declaration that summarizes the basis for withholding without compromising security; the public declaration facilitates meaningful adversarial testing and appellate review. Third, Vaughn indices or categorical descriptions should be sufficiently detailed and organized to allow the court to perform its function; courts will sometimes demand better indexing or invite in camera review when the public record is inadequate. Finally, when national security or ongoing investigations are implicated, sealed submissions and in camera review are routine; but the agency still bears the burden to justify secrecy, and courts will scrutinize the sealed materials themselves.

## Remedies and remands: when courts order refreshed or more detailed submissions

When agency submissions are stale, conclusory, or otherwise insufficient, courts frequently do not proceed to summary judgment in the agency’s favor; instead they deny the motion without prejudice or remand for further agency action or supplementation. Remedies available to courts include ordering the agency to produce refreshed declarations, to provide a more detailed Vaughn index, to disclose segregable material, or to submit documents for in camera review. Courts sometimes enter partial orders — permitting disclosure of redacted records while preserving certain materials — or they may order further fact-finding about whether proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated” for purposes of Exemption 7(A).

Farahi offers a good map of the remedial landscape. The district court initially denied the FBI’s summary-judgment motion without prejudice, explicitly because the prior declarations were old and the court needed updated information to determine whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. The FBI supplied supplemental public and sealed declarations, and the district court then granted summary judgment in the agency’s favor. On appeal the D.C. Circuit affirmed, explaining that courts commonly require updated declarations when significant time has passed and that a short, contemporaneous declaration can be understood as an update to an earlier detailed affidavit rather than as a new, stand-alone justification.

A few practical rules flow from these outcomes. First, staleness is a real procedural defect; agencies must be prepared to update declarations that are several years old. Second, a district court’s remedial posture often depends on the litigation history: a court may grant an agency leave to supplement its record and then reassess; it may also order disclosure of segregable portions if the agency cannot carry its burden. Third, the scope of a remand or order for supplementation can vary widely; sometimes courts seek only a brief confirmation that nothing has changed, while at other times they demand a fuller, contemporaneous exposition of the factual bases for the claimed exemptions. Finally, the prospect of remand counsels practitioners to think ahead: agencies should assemble contemporaneous records about the status of investigations, and requesters should consider asking for a narrow set of declaratory rulings or targeted discovery if an agency’s justifications appear facially inadequate.

Conclusion

FOIA litigation is governed by a distinctive procedural architecture that channels disputes into summary-judgment proceedings, sharpens the debate around agency declarations and segregability, and requires litigants to preserve and press specific arguments at the district court level. Farahi v. FBI illustrates many of these features: the typical motion-practice arc (initial agency declarations, a district court’s demand for updated information, submission of sealed and public declarations, and appellate de novo review tempered by deference) and the practical consequences of failing to preserve particular arguments. Mastery of FOIA procedure therefore requires attention to timing and exhaustion rules, careful construction and contestation of agency declarations, strategic choices about sealed filings, and vigilant issue preservation to avoid forfeiture on appeal.
