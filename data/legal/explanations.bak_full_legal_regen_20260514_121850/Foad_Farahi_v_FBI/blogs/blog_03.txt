Title: Blog about the opinion: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

# Segregability in the Shadow of National Security: How 10,750 Pages Yielded “No Meaningful Information”

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI is a quiet but doctrinally significant reaffirmation of two of FOIA law’s core, tension-filled principles: the government’s broad deference in national-security and law-enforcement contexts, and the statutory duty to release “any reasonably segregable portion of a record.” At stake is a familiar but still urgent question—when an agency invokes Exemption 7(A) to shield law‑enforcement files, how stringently must courts police the government’s segregability showing, especially where the withheld materials are voluminous and the declared harms implicate active counterterrorism investigations?

On the facts, the FBI located roughly 10,750 pages and 80 CDs responsive to a requester’s FOIA demand for his own file and withheld most of those records under Exemption 7(A). The district court initially required the FBI to update stale declarations; after supplemental public and ex parte filings, it concluded that (1) the records were compiled for law enforcement purposes; (2) release could be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings; and (3) meaningful, nonexempt material was not reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit affirmed.

The opinion matters because it illustrates how the Court of Appeals applies two doctrines—“inextricably intertwined”/non‑segregability and deference to national‑security‑adjacent agency declarations—in a high‑volume, counterterrorism context. The result is a practical, if unsurprising, outcome: thousands of pages can be withheld in bulk when the agency convinces a court that the benign-looking snippets within those pages are so enmeshed with sensitive law‑enforcement material that disclosure would itself reveal substantive intelligence or investigative contours.

## The court’s reasoning, in short

The court’s analysis proceeds along three tracks that track the statutory language of Exemption 7(A) and the segregability requirement.

First, the court accepted that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” The FBI’s public declaration (Hardy) and an ex parte, sealed declaration provided sufficient detail about the investigation’s nature—ties to terrorism, named associations, and the connection to prosecutions—to establish the requisite “nexus” between the records and a law‑enforcement function. The majority underscored that courts traditionally afford the FBI “a fair measure of deference,” particularly where national‑security concerns are implicated.

Second, the court concluded that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that were “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The opinion is careful on timing: it recognizes that stale or conclusory assertions of ongoing investigations raise skepticism (citing CREW), but it treats the agency’s later Bender declaration as an update—not a standalone assertion—supplementing the earlier, more detailed Hardy affidavits. The court therefore accepted the agency’s showing that active investigations remained and that disclosure could tip off targets, jeopardizing evidence gathering.

Third—and the piece that will draw the most attention—the court upheld the district court’s segregability determination. The court relied on precedent (Mead, Juarez, Perioperative) permitting withholding where nonexempt material is “randomly interspersed line-by-line throughout the document” and therefore not “reasonably segregable.” The D.C. Circuit emphasized that the FOIA’s aim is disclosure of information, not of raw words, and that where the small chunks of nonexempt content cannot be extracted without revealing the substance of the exempt portions, the agency may withhold in bulk. The district court’s review of a sealed ex parte submission (describing why segregation would itself injure investigations) carried the day, and the appellate panel accepted that reliance.

## An analytical perspective: mosaics, granularity, and the danger of functional non‑segregability

There is force to the court’s reasoning. Where the subject of a FOIA request is an active national‑security investigation, releasing even apparently innocuous lines can reveal investigative techniques, priorities, timelines, or relationships among individuals that adversaries could exploit. The “mosaic” theory—fear that small pieces, when combined, yield significant intelligence—has intuitive appeal in counterterrorism contexts, and courts have long allowed agencies to make carefully supported showings along those lines.

But Farahi also demonstrates how the segregability obligation can be displaced by two structural features: volume and secrecy. Volume cuts both ways—large document sets increase the likelihood that some neutral facts are embedded somewhere, but they also make it practically difficult for courts to parse publicly what could be releasable without compromising sensitive details. Secrecy intensifies the problem: when an agency supplies ex parte, sealed declarations to make the segregability case, the requester cannot mount a public rebuttal and the court cannot explain, in an open opinion, why it accepted the agency’s view. The circuit recognizes this asymmetry and cites the necessity of sealed submissions in certain cases—but that necessity tends to entrench deference and narrows the practical reach of FOIA’s disclosure requirements.

Put bluntly, “line-by-line interspersion” and “inextricably intertwined” reasoning can functionally swallow segregability when combined with national‑security deference and sealed affidavits. A powerful agency can assert that disclosure of isolated phrases would enable targets to infer investigative connections or reveal methods; absent a robust public record, courts will often credit that assertion.

## Doctrinal implications and possible downstream effects

The Farahi decision reinforces precedents that tilt the balance toward nondisclosure in national‑security and terrorism-related FOIA disputes. Two doctrinal consequences are worth flagging.

First, the decision tightens the upper bound on what courts will consider “reasonably segregable” where disclosures are alleged to risk facilitating law‑enforcement harms. Courts will accept detailed—but sealed—justifications that explain how even minimal disclosures would be sensitive. That could mean fewer piecemeal disclosures from large law‑enforcement compilations going forward, especially in cases involving counterterrorism, organized crime, or intelligence‑adjacent investigations.

Second, the decision underscores an evidentiary path for agencies: combine a public affidavit that establishes the investigative nexus and a sealed ex parte submission that provides the granular rationale for non‑segregability and explains why public release of segregated snippets would itself cause the asserted harms. That combination was decisive here—Hardy’s public affidavit laid the groundwork; the sealed material made the segregability case.

Both implications are unsurprising; what is more consequential is the practical dynamic this creates. Requesters will find it harder to obtain meaningful disclosures from large FBI files; lower‑resource requesters and small nonprofits will be particularly disadvantaged because they lack the resources to litigate and to generate alternative evidence undermining sealed claims. Moreover, the use of ex parte declarations, while sometimes unavoidable, may increase, making FOIA litigation more opaque and judicial explanations less instructive for future parties.

## How courts and requesters can cabin overbroad non‑segregability claims

The opinion does not close off workable steps that courts and requesters can take to preserve FOIA’s disclosure function while respecting legitimate law‑enforcement concerns.

First, courts should insist on a high degree of specificity in public declarations before accepting that sealed submissions are needed. The D.C. Circuit here accepted the agency’s initial public declarations as sufficient, but courts can press agencies to provide categorical public descriptions that allow meaningful, adversarial testing of the asserted harms—without exposing operational details. For example, a public declaration could specify categories of information that are sensitive (e.g., “identities of confidential human sources,” “technical collection methods,” “timelines of ongoing operations”) and explain at a high level why those categories pervade the records. That kind of public, categorical specificity enables the requester to challenge overbreadth while limiting operational disclosure.

Second, courts should require agencies to produce a detailed Vaughn index (or its equivalent) in camera whenever practicable. The Vaughn index should be organized such that it allows the court to evaluate segregability line-by-line if necessary, but it may be submitted under seal with a clear public summary. The goal is to avoid relying solely on ex parte narrative affidavits; a court that has a detailed, itemized map of the withheld material is better positioned to separate the wheat from the chaff and to explain its rationale on the public record where possible.

Third, courts can adopt structured sampling techniques. When agencies claim that nonexempt material is randomly interspersed across thousands of pages, a court can order the agency to disclose representative samples of smaller subsets (e.g., particular file types, time periods, or document custodians), perhaps initially in camera and then redacted for public release if feasible. Sampling reduces the piecemeal‑catch‑all problem: agencies cannot claim that every line is sensitive if representative samples demonstrate otherwise. Sampling also conserves judicial resources relative to a page‑by‑page review of 10,750 pages.

Fourth, requesters can improve the quality of their challenges. A requester who can show, by way of publicly available parallel records, prior disclosures from similar cases, or declaratory evidence, that certain categories of information have been disclosed in other contexts undermines blanket claims of interspersion. Even if the requester cannot access the sealed affidavit, demonstrating that certain kinds of content have previously been disclosed may pressure the agency to narrow its claims or provide more public detail.

Finally, appellate courts should provide guidance on how to evaluate the necessity and sufficiency of ex parte submissions in FOIA cases. The circuits have recognized the legitimacy of sealed affidavits when disclosure of reasoning would itself reveal sensitive information; but appellate precedent can and should sketch guardrails—insisting on public categorical detail, requiring in camera indexes, encouraging sampling, and, where feasible, requiring courts to articulate the bases for non‑segregability in a manner that does not reveal operational specifics but does permit public understanding and review.

## Conclusion

Farahi is not a radical recalibration of FOIA law. It aligns with longstanding precedents affording deference to the FBI in national‑security contexts and recognizing that nonexempt material can be non‑segregable where disclosure of small bits would reveal the substance of the exempt material. But the case crystallizes an enduring problem: when the government marshals public and sealed statements and invokes the mosaic concern, segregability obligations can become functionally thin, especially in large files tied to terrorism investigations.

Preserving FOIA’s promise of transparency will require creative procedural care from courts and more robust, targeted advocacy from requesters. Courts should push agencies to provide as much public, categorical detail as possible, employ sampling and in‑camera indexes to test claims, and explain their conclusions in ways that preserve secrecy without insulating agency assertions from meaningful review. Without such guardrails, “inextricably intertwined” risks becoming a broad invitation to withhold entire troves of material under the banner of national security—precisely the result the FOIA’s segregability requirement was meant to prevent.
