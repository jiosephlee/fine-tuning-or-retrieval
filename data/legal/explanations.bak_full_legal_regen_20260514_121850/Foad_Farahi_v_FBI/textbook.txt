Title: Casebook chapter about the opinion: Foad Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation

# FOIA Fundamentals: Structure, Purpose, and Litigation Posture

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is a disclosure statute. Its premise is simple but potent: government records are presumptively public, and information is to be released on request unless the statute expressly exempts disclosure. That presumption structures nearly every FOIA dispute—what an agency must do in response to a request, what it must prove when it withholds, how courts evaluate the agency’s proof, and how the balance between openness and other public interests is struck. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation, No. 23-5243 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025), illustrates many of those fundamentals in the context of a terrorism-related FOIA request and the often-difficult questions that arise when enforcement investigations and national-security concerns intersect with a requester’s interest in disclosure.

This chapter sets out the core FOIA structure and doctrine, explains the agency’s procedural and evidentiary obligations, and shows how courts resolve FOIA disputes—using Farahi to illuminate recurring issues such as Exemption 7(A), the agency declaration, updating stale affidavits, the use of ex parte submissions, segregability, and the summary-judgment posture in which most FOIA cases are decided.

## FOIA’s core mandate: disclosure as the rule, exemptions as the exception

FOIA’s starting point is a command of disclosure. The statute requires agencies to make records available to any person who requests them, subject to enumerated exemptions. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(3). That framework places on the requesting public the power to require government openness while recognizing that certain narrowly defined interests—such as personal privacy, law-enforcement effectiveness, and national-security secrets—may justify nondisclosure. The exemptions are therefore exceptions to the rule, and courts treat them accordingly.

The practical corollary is that FOIA disputes are necessarily protective of disclosure: an agency claiming an exemption bears the burden of demonstrating that the requested records fall squarely within an exemption. See 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B). This allocation incentivizes care on the agency side—an agency must not simply assert an exemption in conclusory terms but must provide sufficient factual and legal explanation to support the withholding. If the agency fails to carry its burden, the records are released.

Farahi exemplifies these principles. The requester sought his FBI file—tens of thousands of pages the FBI had compiled while investigating suspected terrorist ties. The FBI withheld most of the material and invoked Exemption 7(A), a law-enforcement exemption that permits withholding of records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). The D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court’s grant of summary judgment to the FBI because the agency met its burden with detailed declarations and other evidence showing the records were law-enforcement records and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with ongoing or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The case thus underscores FOIA’s disclosure default and the agency’s affirmative obligation to justify nondisclosure.

## Statutory framework: 5 U.S.C. § 552(a) (requests, timing, appeals) and § 552(b) (exemptions)

To litigate FOIA intelligently, one must keep the statute’s structure in mind. Section 552(a) governs the request–response process. It requires agencies to make records available on request, sets procedural deadlines for agency responses, and creates obligations for indexing, recordkeeping, and administrative review procedures (where an agency has an appeal process). Section 552(b) enumerates nine exemptions that permit agencies to withhold information categorically or conditionally—ranging from classified national-security information (Exemption 1) and internal personnel rules (Exemption 2), to trade secrets (Exemption 4) and law-enforcement records (Exemption 7).

Two practical points follow from this statutory architecture. First, statutory timing matters. The statute establishes strict response deadlines—agency responses must typically issue within twenty working days of receipt, with limited extensions—so failure to comply can produce early litigation and court-ordered timelines. Second, the exemptions are discrete and must be read together; a particular factual record may touch multiple exemptions, and the agency must justify withholding under whichever exemption(s) it invokes.

In Farahi the timeline feature mattered immediately: the FBI missed its statutory response deadline, prompting the requester’s suit. The litigation then unfolded under the familiar FOIA posture—agency review and redactions, the government’s invocation of exemptions, the submission of agency declarations in support, and ultimately, judicial review under the statute’s standards.

## Agency duties: search adequacy, processing deadlines, and administrative appeal

FOIA imposes a set of affirmative duties on agencies. Three are especially important in litigation:

1. Search adequacy. An agency must conduct a search reasonably calculated to locate records responsive to the request. What counts as “reasonable” is fact-specific and courts will usually accept a nonconclusory declaration describing the search methodology, search terms used, locations searched, and efforts to identify potentially responsive offices or systems—so long as the declaration is reasonably detailed and plausible. Declarations that are conclusory or that omit basic facts about the search can be rejected as inadequate.

2. Processing deadlines. Agencies must adhere to statutory response deadlines (and to any agency-specific administrative-appeal timetables). Failure to meet those deadlines often triggers litigation. Even after litigation begins, courts may require agencies to complete their processing within court-ordered schedules, and courts sometimes require agencies to update earlier statements if substantial time elapses between initial declaration and the court’s decision.

3. Administrative appeal. Many agencies provide an internal administrative appeal. Pursuing such an appeal can be required before judicial review in some contexts; in others, the statute allows suit after 20 working days if the agency fails to comply. In all events, administrative processing matters because it may narrow issues and produce a contemporaneous administrative record that the court will later evaluate.

Farahi illustrates each duty. The FBI identified over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs responsive to Farahi’s request and conducted an extended review. The agency initially missed the statutory deadline and the requester sued. Before the district court resolved the matter, the FBI filed a declaration in support of withholding and later was required by the district court to update that declaration because sufficient time had passed to raise doubt about whether the factual premise underlying the exemption—ongoing investigations—remained true. The update requirement demonstrates that agencies cannot rely on stale, unsupported assertions about the status of investigations indefinitely; courts will require contemporaneous verification when appropriate.

## Burden of proof on the government and the de novo standard of judicial review

FOIA law places the burden squarely on the government to justify withholding. Section 552(a)(4)(B) specifies that the agency bears the burden of establishing that a withheld document falls within an exemption. In practice, agencies discharge that burden by submitting declarations and supporting material that explain the nature of the records, the exemption(s) invoked, and why disclosure would harm the identified interests. Courts do not accept bare formulas; the agency must provide a factual predicate adequate for meaningful judicial review.

At the same time, FOIA cases are reviewed de novo. Courts, pursuant to precedent, do not merely rubber-stamp agency labels. They independently evaluate whether the statutory criteria for nondisclosure are met. That review, however, is exercised against particular backgrounds of deference. When an agency addresses national-security or law-enforcement judgments—areas in which the executive branch has special expertise—courts afford a reasonable measure of deference to the agency’s assessments of harm and factual predicates. The key tension is thus visible: the court must independently determine whether the exemption applies, yet it should respect the agency’s expertise and the practical realities of executing sensitive investigations.

Farahi demonstrates this dynamic. The D.C. Circuit reaffirmed that it reviews FOIA grants of summary judgment de novo. But the Court also explained that because the FBI specializes in law enforcement—and because national-security concerns were implicated—its invocation of Exemption 7(A) received “a fair measure of deference.” The net result is not a rubber stamp: the FBI still had to produce detailed declarations showing both that the records were law-enforcement records and that disclosure would reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.

## Summary judgment as the typical vehicle in FOIA cases

Most FOIA disputes are resolved on motions for summary judgment. The reason is practical: FOIA disputes typically turn on legal questions—does an exemption cover the material at issue?—and on adjudication of the agency’s written declarations and indices that describe the withheld records. There are often no disputed factual issues requiring an evidentiary trial; the agency’s declarations (and any competing proof the requester can offer) create a record on which the court can decide the dispute.

Procedurally, courts treat the agency’s declaration like an affidavit. To win summary judgment, the government must show that the declaration supplies material facts that, taken together with any other evidence, entitle the agency to judgment as a matter of law. The requestor may counter by demonstrating genuine factual disputes (for example, by challenging the adequacy of the search or the veracity and sufficiency of the agency’s explanations). If the record contains unresolved factual disputes material to the exemption claim, the court may deny summary judgment and require further proceedings. But many cases resolve at summary judgment because the agency’s record is sufficiently detailed, and requesters rarely can create enough factual counterproof to raise a triable issue.

Farahi followed this path. The FBI moved for summary judgment with agency declarations in support. The district court initially denied the motion without prejudice because it wanted updated information addressing the passage of time (both about the continued pendency of investigations and segregability). After the FBI supplied supplemental declarations (including an ex parte one), the district court granted summary judgment. The D.C. Circuit reviewed that grant de novo and affirmed.

## The role of agency declarations and Vaughn indices in building the record

When a requester sues, the agency must provide a record sufficient for meaningful judicial review. Two primary devices serve this function: the agency declaration (often called an “affidavit” although it is usually sworn), and the Vaughn index (a document-by-document or category-by-category index describing each withheld record and the exemption(s) claimed). Each performs complementary functions.

- Agency declarations. A well-crafted declaration explains the agency’s procedures, the nature of the records, the reasons for withholding, and the factual predicates for harm if disclosure occurred. The declaration should be nonconclusory: it should identify the kinds of investigations involved, the nature of the harm that disclosure would cause, and any factual basis (for example, ongoing investigative steps) that supports the claim of harm. Declarations may be public or, in sensitive cases, filed ex parte and under seal to provide additional detail without revealing the very information the agency seeks to protect. Courts may rely on sealed ex parte declarations where necessary, but they recognize the tension that such submissions create for the requester.

- Vaughn indices. A Vaughn index lists withheld documents or categories of documents and, for each, describes the document’s type, date (if possible), general subject matter, and the exemption(s) supporting nondisclosure. Where agencies rely on categorical descriptions instead of a line-by-line index (common when volumes are large), the categories must be sufficiently distinct and specific so the court can determine whether the exemptions apply to each category.

Both devices must provide “reasonable specificity.” The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly said that courts may rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents cannot be segregated and why exemptions apply. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54, 61 (D.C. Cir. 2008). But what amounts to sufficient specificity depends on the nature of the exemption and the case facts: national-security and law-enforcement contexts sometimes warrant more protection, but the agency still must identify factual grounds for its withholding.

Farahi is instructive on these points. The FBI’s initial submission included a declaration from the Section Chief of its Record/Information Dissemination Section (RIDS). Because significant time passed, the district court required updated confirmation that the investigations remained ongoing; the FBI supplied a public update and a sealed ex parte declaration containing more details. The court explained that supplemental sealed material could properly address concerns—particularly when national security and law-enforcement investigations were at stake—and that the agency’s material supplied the reasonable specificity needed for judicial review. The case thus shows how courts will use both public declarations and sealed ex parte submissions to balance openness with protection of sensitive investigative details.

## Where segregability fits in every FOIA case

Segregability is a central, often overlooked, doctrinal consequence of FOIA’s disclosure mandate. Although an agency may withhold records under an exemption, FOIA also requires disclosure of “[a]ny reasonably segregable portion of a record.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b); see also id. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii). That means the agency cannot hide behind a protective label and withhold an entire document if meaningful nonexempt information can be released after redaction. Courts therefore insist that agencies take “reasonable steps” to redact exempt information and disclose what remains.

How do courts analyze segregability? Several principles guide the inquiry:

1. The agency must demonstrate that it tried to segregate nonexempt material and explain why what remains is not meaningfully separable without revealing exempt material. This explanation typically appears in the agency declaration or the Vaughn index and should describe the redaction methodology and why further segmentation would be impracticable or would disclose sensitive information by inference.

2. Nonexempt fragments that are “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material need not be released. Where ostensibly benign words or phrases are embedded line-by-line in sensitive material—such that releasing them would still reveal the underlying exempt information—the agency may withhold entire passages or documents. The classic statement is Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. United States Dep’t of the Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 261 (D.C. Cir. 1977), where the court explained that the “focus of the FOIA is information, not simply words” and that random words scattered through a document are not necessarily “reasonably segregable” if their disclosure would communicate the protected information.

3. Courts may rely on agency affidavits that show with “reasonable specificity” why further segregation is not possible. Juarez, 518 F.3d at 61. But the agency’s explanation must be more than boilerplate; it must allow the court to evaluate whether the agency took the required steps.

Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. U.S. Dep’t of Veterans Affairs, 57 F.4th 1061, 1069 (D.C. Cir. 2023), and other recent D.C. Circuit cases illustrate that the inquiry is pragmatic: the court asks whether remaining nonexempt material would be “meaningful” and whether releasing it would, directly or by inference, disclose exempt information or hamper the government’s interests.

Farahi shows the segregability analysis in practice. Farahi argued that out of the tens of thousands of pages the FBI located, some nonexempt material must be segregable and releasable. The FBI acknowledged that some isolated words or phrases could, in theory, be released, but explained (in its ex parte submission) that such bits of information were interspersed line-by-line in clearly exempt investigative material and that revealing them would reveal or facilitate deduction of sensitive investigative facts. The district court reviewed the sealed explanations and concluded that the agency had provided a detailed justification for why no meaningful nonexempt material was reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit affirmed, noting that segregability obligations do not require agencies to release trivial fragments of information when doing so would compromise legitimate interests and that the agency’s sealed submission provided the necessary specificity.

The lesson is practical: when preparing to withhold, agencies should attempt reasonable redactions, explain their redaction methodology, and be prepared to show the court—if necessary, in camera or via sealed declaration—why the remaining material cannot be usefully separated from exempt material. Requesters challenging segregability should identify specific passages they believe are nonexempt and show why their disclosure would not reveal exempt information; generalized assertions that some nonexempt material must exist are often insufficient.

## Special issues illustrated by Farahi: Exemption 7(A), “pending or reasonably anticipated,” updating declarations, and ex parte submissions

Farahi centers on Exemption 7(A), which illustrates several recurring doctrinal points.

1. Elements of Exemption 7(A). The provision covers records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if their release “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Courts analyze Exemption 7(A) in two steps: first, whether the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes (the “nexus” requirement); and second, whether release could reasonably be expected to interfere with “enforcement proceedings.” For the latter, this court requires that the proceedings be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” See Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Wash. v. DOJ (CREW), 746 F.3d 1082, 1096 (D.C. Cir. 2014) (quoting Mapother v. DOJ, 3 F.3d 1533, 1540 (D.C. Cir. 1993)).

2. Proving “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The government may establish the existence of ongoing investigations and anticipated proceedings by attestation; an agency need not produce a charging document or detail every investigative step. CREW held that so long as the agency’s declaration is not merely conclusory and establishes an investigatory predicate, the agency may meet its burden by attesting that an investigation is ongoing and that certain harm—e.g., tipping off targets—would result from disclosure. Juarez confirmed that an investigation that continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case is a “proceeding” for these purposes. But CREW also warned that the “vague nature” of a bare assertion that an investigation is ongoing may not suffice; courts require enough factual content to evaluate the claim.

3. The need to update stale declarations. Time matters in Exemption 7(A) cases. If an agency submits a declaration stating that an investigation is ongoing, but significant time elapses, a court may require an updated declaration confirming that the investigation still exists. An initial declaration may “seem” to satisfy the prima facie need for withholding, but after a multi-year delay, a court is entitled to contemporaneous verification. Farahi’s litigation exemplifies this: after the FBI submitted its initial Hardy declaration in 2019, the district court in 2022 asked for an update, and the FBI supplied a supplemental public declaration and an ex parte declaration to confirm that investigations remained ongoing.

4. Use of ex parte and sealed submissions. Courts may permit agencies to file sealed ex parte declarations when public disclosure of the detail required to justify withholding would itself undermine the very interest the exemption protects. Ex parte submissions enable the court to see the factual predicate without disclosing the details to the requester. This is an imperfect solution—requesters understandably object to being deprived of the full record—but the D.C. Circuit recognizes that in national-security and sensitive law-enforcement contexts, ex parte material may be necessary and constitutionally permissible. In Farahi, the district court reviewed a sealed ex parte declaration and found it supplied the necessary factual specificity for both Exemption 7(A) and segregability. The D.C. Circuit affirmed the use of ex parte material where the requester had not objected to the procedure and where the sealed declaration legitimately filled gaps left by the public record.

5. Deference and limits. While courts defer to agency expertise, that deference is not limitless. Vague or conclusory assertions will not suffice; agencies must provide enough factual detail (in public or sealed submissions) to allow the court to determine whether the exemption applies. Farahi shows both sides: the court gave weight to the FBI’s expertise and the sensitive nature of the investigation, but it also required updated, specific attestations about the continued pendency of investigations before accepting the withholding.

## Practical guidance for applying FOIA doctrine to new fact patterns

A student confronting FOIA problems should take away the following practical rules of thumb:

- Treat disclosure as the norm. When drafting arguments for disclosure, emphasize the presumption of openness and press the agency for specific factual predicates for any exemption invoked.

- For agencies: prepare declarations with care. Declarations must provide a credible account of the nature of the records, the harm that disclosure would cause, and the specific statutory exemption(s) relied upon. Include factual bases that can be publicly stated without compromising sensitive information; for truly sensitive information, prepare a sealed ex parte declaration but avoid overreliance on secrecy to the point the public declaration reads as an empty label.

- Watch the clock. FOIA deadlines are strict; requests that are not timely answered may produce early litigation. If litigation is ongoing, remember that stale factual representations are vulnerable—courts often require contemporaneous confirmations, especially when attempts to withhold rest on the continued pendency of investigations.

- In litigating, press on segregability. Requesters should be specific when challenging an agency’s segregability claim. Point to particular pages or passages you believe are nonexempt and explain why their release would not reveal exempt information; general assertions that some nonexempt material must exist are less likely to prevail.

- Expect summary judgment. Build the record accordingly. If you represent a requester, shore up factual counterpoints to the agency’s declaration (e.g., show that the agency did not search a key location, that the agency’s timeline is inconsistent, or that information the agency claims is sensitive is already public). If you represent an agency, ensure your declaration explains not only that harm would occur but how—describe the nexus between the records and law-enforcement duties, and, where appropriate, explain why segregability is impracticable.

- Understand the doctrinal contours of specific exemptions. Exemption 7(A) is a good example: to succeed in withholding under 7(A) you must show the records are law-enforcement records and that release could reasonably be expected to interfere with proceedings that are pending or reasonably anticipated. “Pending or reasonably anticipated” is a distinct requirement that often hinges on whether an investigation is actively gathering evidence for a potential prosecution.

Farahi is a compact case study: the FBI carried its burden with detailed declarations (including sealed material), the district court required updates when reasonable doubts arose about stale assertions, and the segregability obligations were satisfied only after the court reviewed detailed sealed material explaining why nonexempt remnants were not meaningfully separable. The decision reflects the court’s dual role: enforcing FOIA’s mandate of openness while recognizing the executive’s expertise and the practical need to protect law-enforcement and national-security operations from disclosure that would compromise them.

In sum, FOIA litigation is a dance between transparency and protection. The statute presumes disclosure, places the burden of nondisclosure on the agency, and requires courts to undertake de novo review. But the statute also accommodates the realities of sensitive government functions: agencies may file detailed declarations and, when necessary, sealed submissions that provide courts with the information needed to determine whether an exemption truly applies. Understanding how these elements fit together is the key to analyzing and litigating FOIA disputes.

# The Law Enforcement Exemptions: Exemption 7 in Context

Exemption 7 of the Freedom of Information Act is the principal set of provisions that insulate law‑enforcement-related records from public disclosure when disclosure would harm specific law‑enforcement interests. Because law‑enforcement records often implicate national security, ongoing investigations, and protection of confidential sources, courts give the executive a measure of deference in applying Exemption 7. At the same time, the statute requires agencies to justify their withholdings and to disclose any reasonably segregable, nonexempt portions of records. This chapter uses Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation, No. 23‑5243 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025), together with the influential D.C. Circuit precedents that Farahi cites, to explain the doctrinal architecture of Exemption 7: its threshold, the separate harms protected by 7(A) through 7(F), the relationship between Exemption 7 and other FOIA exemptions in national‑security contexts, and when courts will accept categorical agency showings instead of line‑by‑line, document‑by‑document disclosures.

## Threshold requirement: records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes

Exemption 7 applies only to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7). That threshold is not a mere formality: it imposes a two‑part factual connection that the agency must establish as a predicate to invoking any of the six subparts. The D.C. Circuit has distilled the proof into a requirement that the agency show (1) a rational nexus between the records and one of the agency’s law‑enforcement duties and (2) a connection between the individual or incident described in the records and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. See Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982); Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998).

Farahi illustrates how those showings operate in practice. The FBI’s declarations in Farahi described an investigation into alleged ties between the requester and individuals and organizations associated with terrorism. The D.C. Circuit treated the FBI’s representations with the “fair measure of deference” typically accorded to a law‑enforcement agency and held that the declaration established the requisite nexus to the FBI’s investigative duties and the connection to national‑security and criminal concerns. That deference is not boundless: declarations that are conclusory, inconsistent, or lacking basic factual indicia may not suffice. But where, as in Farahi, the agency supplies factual context (even if some of that context is provided in ex parte filings) showing that records were compiled in the course of an investigation into violations of federal criminal statutes or threats to national security, the threshold is typically satisfied.

A student should therefore think of the threshold as a fact‑intensive inquiry. The court will ask whether the agency’s activity that produced the records is recognizably part of law enforcement (investigations, witness interviews, tips, evidence gathering, threat assessments), and whether the subject of the records is linked to potential violations or security threats. Merely being an agency document is not enough; conversely, for specialized law‑enforcement agencies (the FBI, DEA, ATF), courts will give substantial weight to the agency’s invocation of law‑enforcement purpose because those agencies’ core missions are enforcement‑oriented.

## Subparts overview: 7(A) through 7(F) and the distinct harms each targets

Once the records are shown to have been compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, Exemption 7 subdivides into six separate harms, each protecting a different interest:

- 7(A): records may be withheld if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The interest protected is the ability of investigators and prosecutors to build a case—evidence gathering, witness cooperation, strategy—without tipping off targets or undermining a prospective or pending proceeding. In Farahi the FBI invoked 7(A) to protect an ongoing counterterrorism investigation; the court accepted agency attestations that public disclosure could “tip off” targets and interfere with investigative strategy.

- 7(B): protects records that “would deprive a person of a right to a fair trial or an impartial adjudication.” This targets disclosure that would prejudice jurors, witnesses, or the tribunal.

- 7(C): protects “personal privacy” interests of third parties appearing in law‑enforcement records. This is often invoked to shield identifying information about confidential sources, witnesses, or persons mentioned in reports.

- 7(D): protects “confidential sources” and “informant” identities or information that would reveal a source. This subpart is commonly invoked where a source’s security or safety would be threatened by disclosure.

- 7(E): protects the “techniques and procedures” for law enforcement investigations and prosecutions—criminal‑investigative methods that, if revealed, would allow subjects to avoid detection. This is often used to withhold material about surveillance methods, forensic techniques, or covert tactics.

- 7(F): protects records that would “endanger the life or physical safety” of law‑enforcement personnel or other individuals. This is the most plainly protective of personal safety.

Understanding the distinct rationale behind each subpart is essential because the government must tie its withholding claim to the specific harm an identified subpart protects. 7(A) is functionally concerned with the prospective or ongoing character of proceedings and the real risk that disclosure would frustrate those proceedings. The D.C. Circuit’s recent decisions—Juarez, CREW, and Farahi—emphasize that the government must show that proceedings are pending or that they are reasonably anticipated; a stale investigation that has terminated and will not yield enforcement proceedings cannot support a 7(A) withholding. Conversely, 7(E) or 7(D) may justify withholding even where no prosecution is pending if revealing methods or sources would produce the protected harm.

Farahi is principally a 7(A) case. It shows how courts evaluate an agency’s evidentiary submissions to determine whether disclosure would “reasonably be expected” to interfere with enforcement. The court accepted the FBI’s explanation that disclosure might tip off targets as a conventional and legitimate basis for 7(A). But the opinion also demonstrates that, when significant time has elapsed, courts may demand updated or augmented declarations to ensure that the asserted enforcement proceedings are still pending or reasonably anticipated.

## Relationship between Exemption 7 and other exemptions (e.g., 1 and 3) in national security cases

National‑security cases frequently implicate multiple FOIA exemptions. Exemption 1 protects properly classified national security information (5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(1) and implementing Executive Orders); Exemption 3 covers information exempted by statute other than FOIA; and Exemption 7 protects law‑enforcement‑compiled records that implicate the six harms discussed above. The interactions among these exemptions are important to practical FOIA work and to judicial review.

First, the exemptions are cumulative. An agency may invoke multiple exemptions for the same document: for example, a classified investigative memorandum could be withheld under Exemption 1 (classified material) and under Exemption 7(E) (exposure of investigative techniques). Courts will assess each claimed exemption on its own terms, but they commonly afford deference to agency designations of classified status (Exemption 1) and to agency assertions about law‑enforcement harms (Exemption 7), particularly when national security interests are implicated. The D.C. Circuit has repeatedly emphasized deference in national‑security contexts; in Farahi the court reiterated that “[s]uch deference is particularly appropriate for records implicating national security” and cited Center for National Security Studies v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918 (D.C. Cir. 2003), among other authorities.

Second, the scope and provenance of Exemption 3 are different. Exemption 3 applies when Congress or another statute “requires” or “specifically exempts” certain information from disclosure. Where a statute applies, courts generally do not probe the specific harms that Exemption 7 asks the government to show; instead, the question is whether the statute operates to withhold certain categories of information. In national‑security investigations, Exemption 3 may apply through statutes that protect intelligence‑gathering materials, for example. When both Exemption 3 and 7 are invoked, a court may accept the statutory prohibition while still assessing whether the particular records fall within the statutory category.

Third, the practical consequence of overlapping exemptions is that courts will often review the agency’s submissions under a tiered standard of deference: Exemption 1 claims receive deference to the Executive’s classification determinations unless there is evidence of bad faith; Exemption 3 claims turn on statutory language and congressional intent; and Exemption 7 claims require that the agency demonstrate the particularized harm enumerated in the subpart invoked. Farahi underscores that even where national‑security concerns are at stake, courts expect agencies to offer declarations that, while often ex parte and in camera, provide enough factual specificity for a court to determine whether the exemption’s elements are satisfied.

Finally, the overlap also affects segregability arguments. A document that contains classified material (Exemption 1) may still contain reasonably segregable nonclassified information unless that information is so intertwined with classified material that segregable disclosure would itself harm the protected interests. The D.C. Circuit’s segregability jurisprudence (Mead Data, Juarez, Perioperative) and the Farahi decision together demonstrate that agencies can withhold entire documents when the nonexempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material or when nonexempt bits are dispersed line‑by‑line such that a meaningful disclosure is not possible without compromising protected information.

## Supreme Court validation of categorical approaches under 7(A): Robbins Tire & Rubber Co. v. NLRB

Courts frequently confront the question whether an agency may lawfully withhold records on a category‑by‑category basis rather than litigating exemption applicability document by document. The Supreme Court has recognized in other administrative contexts that agencies may adopt categorical approaches to administrative decisionmaking and remedies where the subject matter or remedial scheme justifies a rule‑like treatment. Robbins Tire & Rubber Co. v. NLRB, 437 U.S. 214 (1978), is one exemplar of the Court’s willingness to tolerate categorical agency action in the administrative sphere. Robbins Tire addressed the National Labor Relations Board’s remedial order and accepted that categorical relief, though broad, could be appropriate if authorized by statute and supported by the Board’s reasoning.

Although Robbins Tire is not a FOIA case, its doctrinal posture is useful for understanding FOIA practice because it communicates a general principle: where an agency’s mission and the need for administrable, predictable decisionmaking support a categorical approach, courts will not automatically insist on atomized, document‑by‑document inquiry. D.C. Circuit FOIA jurisprudence has imported this administrative sensibility into the FOIA context: the agency need not always produce page‑by‑page justification if it reasonably explains why groups of records share a common exempt quality and why disclosure of the group would cause the same statutory harm. The caveat—repeated in Gallant, CREW, and Farahi—is that such categories must be meaningfully distinct and the agency’s explanation for each category must supply sufficient factual detail for the court to assess whether the claimed exemption applies to the group as a whole.

Robbins Tire thus functions as an analogical endorsement: courts will permit categorical showings where they make sense, but FOIA’s transparency mandate controls. The government’s category‑based approach must still allow a court to determine whether the exemption is properly applied. Robbins Tire’s broader lesson—administrative agencies may adopt categorical treatments when justified—helps explain why the D.C. Circuit will accept category‑based withholdings when the agency’s declarations offer adequate specificity and the categories are not overbroad.

## When agencies may rely on categorical rather than document‑by‑document showings

The central question is practical: when is a category‑level justification adequate under FOIA? The D.C. Circuit has articulated guiding principles that the Farahi opinion applied.

First, the agency must make the categories “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” to each category. Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994); see also CREW v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082, 1088 (D.C. Cir. 2014). A category like “all documents” or “all investigative files” is too amorphous. Instead, the agency’s labels should identify the document types or subject matter that justify the particular exemption (e.g., “records discussing active sources or confidential informant identities,” or “records reflecting investigative techniques under 7(E),” or “reports of witness interviews concerning named suspects”).

Second, the agency must provide enough factual description—usually in an affidavit or declaration—so that the court can independently assess the claimed exemption for the category. In national‑security or sensitive law‑enforcement contexts, that factual description can occur in part ex parte. As Farahi emphasizes, courts can accept ex parte declarations that provide the necessary specificity when public disclosure of the details would itself defeat the purpose of the exemption. But there are limits: the agency cannot merely submit an unilluminating blanket statement; the declarations must explain why the documents fall into the category and why disclosure would cause the particular enumerated harm.

Third, the feasibility and efficiency considerations matter. Where an agency must process tens of thousands of pages (as in Farahi, where the FBI located more than 10,000 pages and numerous CDs), a document‑by‑document public justification would often force the agency to disclose sensitive information merely to explain why that information must remain secret. Courts recognize this dilemma and allow category‑level showings in appropriate circumstances. That allowance is most apparent when the withheld material is part of an ongoing investigation, when disclosure of even the fact of existence of certain subparts could harm the investigation, or when the documents are so heavily interspersed with exempt material that segregability is impracticable.

Fourth, after-the-fact vigilance: courts will be alert to stale or conclusory representations. CREW and Farahi both demonstrate that courts may require updated affidavits when significant time has elapsed between an agency’s initial declaration and the court’s decision. In Farahi the district court denied summary judgment without prejudice and required the FBI to update potentially stale declarations; the D.C. Circuit sustained that requirement. An agency relying on categorical assertions should therefore anticipate the need to update its explanations to show that the asserted harms remain real and imminent.

Fifth, segregability and “inextricably intertwined” material. Even where categories are accepted, the agency must still show that no meaningful, nonexempt information is reasonably segregable. Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. Department of the Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 260–61 (D.C. Cir. 1977), established that when nonexempt information is “interspersed line‑by‑line” with exempt material, segregation may not be feasible and the agency may withhold the whole record. The D.C. Circuit in Farahi accepted the FBI’s showing—primarily in ex parte submissions—that the nonexempt snippets were inextricably intertwined with the exempt substance and that any piecemeal release would either reveal or compromise the exempt material. Students should note that courts will scrutinize these claims: blanket or conclusory segregability assertions will fail, but detailed ex parte justifications that explain the intermixing of exempt and nonexempt material often succeed.

Finally, the plaintiff’s response matters. Farahi illustrates procedural dynamics: the appellant did not oppose the FBI’s motion to file ex parte submissions, and the D.C. Circuit observed that a failure to object to ex parte treatment undercuts later attacks to some degree. More generally, when an agency relies on categorical treatment, the challenger should identify with specificity why a category is overbroad or why segregable material exists; absent a concrete showing, courts may treat generalized objections as forfeited.

Conclusion

Exemption 7 is a nuanced set of protections tailored to multiple law‑enforcement interests. Its threshold—records compiled for law‑enforcement purposes—requires a factual nexus between the records and the agency’s investigative duties. Once the threshold is met, the six subparts point to distinct harms that the agency must justify. In practice, courts give agencies a fair degree of deference—particularly in national‑security investigations—but require adequate factual grounding for categorical showings, careful treatment of segregability, and timely updates where investigations span many years. Farahi v. FBI is a contemporary exemplar: it demonstrates both the court’s willingness to credit the FBI’s attestations about ongoing counterterrorism investigations and the court’s insistence that the agency refresh and detail its submissions so the litigated balance between secrecy and disclosure remains properly calibrated.

# “Compiled for Law Enforcement Purposes”: The Pratt–Campbell Standard

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) shields from disclosure a variety of records that were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” That threshold phrase—“compiled for law enforcement purposes”—is the gatekeeper for all Exemption 7 claims. If an agency cannot carry the threshold showing, the court need not consider downstream questions about interference with enforcement proceedings, disclosure harms, or segregability. The D.C. Circuit’s recent decision in Farahi v. FBI (2025) demonstrates how courts apply the familiar Pratt–Campbell line of authority to the modern facts of counterterrorism and long-running national-security investigations. This chapter explains the doctrinal test that flows from Pratt v. Webster and related cases, clarifies the deference accorded to specialized law-enforcement agencies such as the FBI, describes what kinds of investigations qualify as “compiled for law enforcement purposes” (with particular attention to counterterrorism and intelligence-driven inquiries), and offers practical guidance for how agencies can and courts should articulate the required nexus without unduly compromising ongoing investigations.

## Pratt v. Webster: the rational-nexus and possible-violation requirements

Pratt v. Webster established the basic structure of the “compiled for law enforcement purposes” inquiry that courts still apply today. The test is twofold. First, the agency must establish a rational nexus between the records sought and one of the agency’s law-enforcement duties. Second, there must be a connection between the individual or incident that is the subject of the records and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. The point of the test is straightforward: FOIA Exemption 7 was designed to protect information assembled in the service of governmental law-enforcement activity, not to shelter purely administrative materials or general policy work.

The “rational nexus” prong is not a demanding one in form. The agency need not prove criminality or a likelihood of prosecution; it must show only a rational relationship between the records and one of the agency’s law-enforcement responsibilities. This requirement prevents agencies from invoking Exemption 7 for materials that have only an attenuated connection to enforcement—personnel management files, policy memoranda, or routine regulatory work unconnected to a discrete investigatory function. But the showing is also not hyper-technical: documents created to investigate allegations, gather or analyze evidence, identify subjects, or otherwise advance an investigation typically satisfy the nexus requirement.

The “possible violation” prong ensures that the agency’s investigatory focus is on legal wrongdoing or security harms, not merely on general intelligence-gathering or administrative curiosity. Courts have required a showing of a connection between the subject (an individual or incident) and a plausible criminal or security risk. The agency need not demonstrate proof of a crime; a reasonable basis to suspect a violation—or a concern that the subject presents a security risk—is sufficient to place the materials within Exemption 7’s protective ambit. Thus Pratt contemplates a fact-sensitive review: the agency’s declarations and the surrounding circumstances must supply enough factual detail for a court to evaluate whether the requisite connection exists.

Pratt’s demands are deliberately balanced: they guard against casual invocation of Exemption 7 for materials unrelated to enforcement, while recognizing that an agency need not disclose the entire investigative theory or evidence to show that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes. Courts therefore look to the agency’s affidavits, the factual background in the public record, and any other non-sensitive contextual material to determine whether Pratt’s two elements are satisfied.

## Campbell v. DOJ and Clemente v. FBI: deference to specialized law-enforcement agencies

The D.C. Circuit has been clear that the degree of deference an agency receives in meeting the Pratt test depends in part on the agency’s character. In Campbell v. DOJ, the court emphasized that when a specialized law-enforcement agency like the FBI invokes Exemption 7, the agency’s determinations about whether records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes are entitled to a “fair measure of deference.” The reasoning is simple: specialized agencies possess institutional expertise about their own investigatory programs, their statutory missions, and the nature and significance of the records in their custody. Courts therefore accept proffers from those agencies with more latitude, so long as the agency’s declarations provide a sufficient factual predicate for judicial review.

Clemente v. FBI applies that principle to the modern FOIA context, affirming that the FBI can rely on its institutional knowledge, combined with factual declarations from agency personnel, to satisfy the Pratt threshold. But deference is not abdication. Campbell and Clemente reinforce that even the FBI must provide declarations that reasonably describe how the records relate to law-enforcement duties and the basis for believing the records concern a possible violation or security risk. Conclusory, boilerplate assertions are insufficient; the agency’s proffer must give the court some basis to evaluate the claim.

Farahi illustrates how the deference principle operates in practice. The court accepted that the FBI, as a specialized law-enforcement agency with counterterrorism responsibilities, is entitled to deference—and that deference supports the agency’s showing when the declarations, taken together, establish the nexus to law-enforcement duties and to possible violations. But the opinion also makes clear that the agency’s declarations must provide enough detail to permit judicial scrutiny: the FBI’s public declaration, together with a more detailed ex parte declaration, furnished the necessary factual foundation. Where an agency produces both public and classified or ex parte material that complements the public descriptions, the court will give the agency’s claims weight—especially when national-security concerns are implicated—but the agency still bears the burden of persuasion.

## What counts as “compiled for law enforcement purposes”: counterterrorism and intelligence-driven investigations

A perennial question is whether intelligence-driven investigations—particularly counterterrorism inquiries that borrow techniques from intelligence agencies—qualify as law-enforcement compilations under Exemption 7. The D.C. Circuit’s practice is to treat investigations led by law-enforcement agencies (especially the FBI) as law-enforcement compilations, even when they involve classified methods, foreign intelligence, or national-security considerations. The key inquiry is whether the records were assembled “in furtherance of” the agency’s law-enforcement duties.

Counterterrorism investigations, hybrid criminal-intelligence matters, and investigations that straddle national-security and criminal-law domains are routinely treated as falling within Exemption 7’s scope when a law-enforcement agency compiles the records and has a demonstrable law-enforcement purpose. The court’s decision in Farahi underscores this point: the FBI’s investigation into a person suspected of links to terrorist organizations, recruitment activity, and ties to convicted or detained terrorism suspects was squarely within the FBI’s law-enforcement remit. That the investigation engaged national-security concerns did not push the records outside Exemption 7; rather, it reinforced the importance of deference and of allowing the FBI to protect information assembled for investigative purposes.

What this means in practice is that documents generated by law-enforcement personnel, compiled to identify subjects, analyze networks, preserve evidence, or support prosecutions or other enforcement actions—regardless of whether the subject matter is conventional criminality or national-security related—will tend to qualify as “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” Conversely, materials compiled primarily for policy advice, administrative decisionmaking, or external diplomatic functions will not qualify unless the agency can point to a direct law-enforcement nexus.

Agencies should therefore frame their declarations to emphasize the investigatory function of the records: who compiled them, for what investigatory tasks (e.g., evidentiary collection, target analysis, case development), and how those tasks fall within the agency’s statutory law-enforcement duties. When investigations are hybrid in nature, stating that the record was compiled by a law-enforcement division, as part of an investigation intended to support possible enforcement proceedings, will typically be sufficient under Pratt—so long as the agency avoids pure boilerplate and supplies a factual predicate.

## Applying the threshold in practice: articulating the individual/incident connection to security risks

Applying Pratt’s second element—the connection between the individual or incident and a possible security risk or violation—requires concrete, fact-focused description. An agency cannot simply say, “these records concern a national-security investigation,” and rest on deference. Courts have repeatedly instructed agencies to provide a factual narrative—without disclosing sensitive intelligence—that ties the subject to a plausible law-enforcement interest.

The agency’s declaration should identify, in non-sensitive terms, the nature of the suspected wrongdoing or risk, the relationship between the subject and the alleged or reasonably suspected actors or incidents, and the role the records play in the investigation. For example, saying that the records “document the subject’s recruitment activities for an organization designated as a foreign terrorist organization” or “contain witness statements and field reports about the subject’s ties to indicted co-conspirators” furnishes a concrete link between the individual and a possible violation. Courts have accepted such descriptions when they avoid revealing the operational specifics that would themselves undermine an investigation.

Farahi provides a concrete model of acceptable practice. The FBI’s public declaration asserted that the records were compiled in connection with investigations into “national security and international terrorism,” and the ex parte declaration supplied additional detail about the nature and scope of the investigation. The district court and the D.C. Circuit judged the public declaration adequate when supplemented by the ex parte material, and concluded the agency had plausibly tied the records to a law-enforcement duty and to a possible security risk. The case also illustrates the practical interplay between public and classified materials: an agency can satisfy Pratt while withholding operational detail, so long as the public description, taken with the fact that the agency is a specialized law-enforcement body, gives the court a foundation to find the requisite connection.

Practitioners should note common pitfalls. Vague, conclusory assertions—“these records relate to potential wrongdoing”—are unlikely to satisfy a judicial reviewer, particularly in long-stale investigations. Declarations that merely recite the statutory language of Exemption 7 without factual support will not carry the agency’s burden. Likewise, assertions that the records were compiled for “national-security” reasons without any description of the subject’s alleged connection to that risk are susceptible to challenge. To withstand scrutiny, the agency must show that the records were used or intended to be used in furtherance of a law-enforcement function focused on a particular individual or incident that plausibly implicated federal law or security concerns.

## Using public and ex parte details to establish the threshold without compromising investigations

FOIA litigation involving national security often forces an uncomfortable tradeoff: the agency must give enough information to permit meaningful judicial review while not disclosing the very operational details that, if revealed, would endanger ongoing proceedings. The jurisprudence allows—indeed contemplates—the use of layered submissions: a public declaration that provides a non-sensitive factual framework, and a classified or ex parte declaration that supplies the sensitive particulars for the court’s in camera review.

Farahi demonstrates how that layered approach works. The FBI submitted a public declaration from a high-ranking records official that described the general purposes of the investigation and attested that the records were law-enforcement in nature. To supply the underlying factual detail without exposing it publicly, the FBI filed an ex parte sealed declaration that the district court reviewed in camera. The court relied on the aggregate record—public and ex parte—to conclude that the Pratt threshold was satisfied. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit affirmed that approach: an agency may rely on ex parte declarations to supply the detailed factual foundation required, and a court need not recite its in camera analysis on the public docket when doing so would disclose the sensitive material itself.

A few lessons flow from this practice. First, agencies should prepare public declarations that are as informative as possible without disclosing operational specifics: identify the agency component responsible for the records, describe the general nature of the investigation (e.g., counterterrorism, transnational criminal enterprise), and explain the law-enforcement function the records serve. Second, agencies should be ready to supplement those public proffers with classified or ex parte affidavits that the court may review in camera. Those sealed declarations should provide the factual particulars—who the targets are, what investigative steps have been taken, how the records fit into the case theory—so that the court can perform a meaningful review. Third, agencies must update stale declarations. Farahi required the FBI to confirm that the investigation remained ongoing; an agency that relies on older affidavits risks a finding that it has not carried its burden.

For litigants and courts, the practice raises difficult fairness questions. FOIA requesters may be handicapped when they cannot see the ex parte material, and they may have limited opportunity to rebut the agency’s classified assertions. The courts have acknowledged this tension, but they have also explained that there is nothing novel about ex parte submissions in national-security FOIA cases: when the only way to assess the government’s privilege claim is to examine the sensitive material itself, in camera review by the judge is the appropriate procedure. To balance the competing interests, courts will press agencies to make their public declarations as informative as they safely can and will scrutinize ex parte declarations to ensure they are not merely conclusory.

## Practical drafting and litigating guidance

For agency counsel:
- Draft public declarations that identify the compiling component, describe the nature of the investigation and the law-enforcement duties implicated, and provide non-sensitive factual predicates tying the records to suspected wrongdoing or security risks. Avoid boilerplate Exemption-7 language alone.
- Prepare thorough ex parte or classified declarations for in camera review that supply the sensitive factual details necessary to satisfy Pratt. Explain clearly why those details cannot appear on the public record.
- Update declarations when litigation is protracted. Courts expect agencies to account for the passage of time and to confirm whether investigations remain current.

For FOIA requesters:
- Press agencies on the adequacy of public declarations and on whether the public proffer, without the sealed material, is precise enough to permit judicial review.
- When possible, show why the public record undercuts the agency’s claimed nexus (e.g., evidence that an agency component did not compile the documents for enforcement purposes).
- Challenge conclusory or stale affidavits and demand renewal or supplementation, as Farahi did successfully in part at the district-court level.

For courts:
- Give appropriate deference to specialized law-enforcement agencies, particularly where national-security concerns are implicated, but insist on a sufficient factual showing under Pratt.
- Use in camera review of ex parte material where necessary to permit meaningful judicial review while minimizing public disclosure of sensitive information.
- Require updates when litigation has been lengthy and consider whether a narrowly tailored public description could be supplemented without revealing operational details.

## Conclusion

The Pratt–Campbell standard defines the FOIA Exemption 7 threshold as a pragmatic, fact-sensitive test. Agencies must show a rational nexus between the records and an agency law-enforcement duty, and a connection between the subject or incident and a possible security risk or violation of federal law. Courts give a “fair measure of deference” to specialized law-enforcement agencies like the FBI, especially in counterterrorism and national-security contexts, but that deference does not excuse conclusory assertions. Farahi v. FBI illustrates the modern application of the standard: a layered public/ex parte proffer, careful factual description (even if some details are reserved for in camera review), and timely updates can carry the agency’s burden under Pratt; failure to supply those elements may compel further scrutiny or supplementation. Understanding how to articulate the nexus without betraying investigative secrets is therefore the central practical challenge in Exemption 7 litigation—and the recurring theme of the Pratt–Campbell jurisprudence.

# Exemption 7(A): Interference with Pending or Reasonably Anticipated Enforcement Proceedings

Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) is a workhorse provision for law-enforcement agencies seeking to withhold records without disclosing details that could impede investigations or prosecutions. Its text is short—covering records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” whose disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings”—but the doctrine built around it is rich and factbound. This chapter develops the doctrinal framework courts use to determine when Exemption 7(A) shields records, explains the sorts of interference that count, and shows how courts treat the special problems of time (staleness), segregability, categorical withholding, and use of classified or ex parte submissions. The recent D.C. Circuit decision in Farahi v. FBI (2025) supplies a helpful modern example: the court sustained Exemption 7(A) withholding of an extensive FBI file after the agency refreshed and supplemented its declarations and explained why nonexempt material was not reasonably segregable.

## Text and burden: “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings”

Exemption 7(A) protects only records (1) compiled for law-enforcement purposes and (2) whose disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The statutory structure places the initial burden on the agency opposing disclosure. In litigation the government must show both elements by affidavit or declaration and, where possible, with appropriate supporting materials.

The phrase “could reasonably be expected” sets a probabilistic, predictive standard. The government need not prove that disclosure will in fact cause interference; it must show a reasonable expectation of interference based on the facts known to, and the expertise of, the agency. Courts give agencies, and particularly the FBI, “a fair measure of deference” in making that predictive judgment. That deference is especially pronounced when the records touch national-security or counterterrorism investigations; courts recognize that specialized expertise informs a reasonable prediction about investigative harm.

But deference is not carte blanche. The agency’s affidavits must be sufficiently detailed and contemporaneous to allow a court to evaluate whether the claimed harms are plausible and connected to the records at issue. Broad, conclusory assertions that release “could” interfere are insufficient if unsupported by facts or context. Once the agency carries its burden, the court resolves the FOIA claim on summary judgment; the court’s review is de novo, but the government’s factual showings, when reasonable and specific, are entitled to weight.

## Defining “enforcement proceedings”: criminal, civil, administrative; ongoing investigations as proceedings

A threshold interpretive question is what counts as an “enforcement proceeding.” The D.C. Circuit has treated that term expansively. Exemption 7(A) covers federal criminal prosecutions, and it also reaches civil and administrative enforcement actions where the government is pursuing regulatory or statutory remedies. More important for many FOIA disputes is that an “enforcement proceeding” can include an ongoing law-enforcement investigation that is gathering evidence for a possible criminal (or civil/administrative) case.

The practical consequence is significant: an investigation does not need to have ripened into an indictment, complaint, or formal administrative charge before the government may invoke 7(A). So long as the investigation is “pending or reasonably anticipated”—that is, the investigative activity is ongoing and concrete steps could lead to enforcement—the government can assert that premature disclosure would interfere. This principle reflects the reality that disclosing evidence, sources, targets, methods, timelines, or investigative scope at the investigative stage can thwart the very possibility of later enforcement.

Farahi v. FBI illustrates this principle. The FBI’s withheld material arose from counterterrorism-related work and investigations that had not necessarily produced charges, but the court upheld withholding because the agency demonstrated the investigations remained ongoing and were of the kind that could mature into enforcement proceedings. The case thus reinforces that investigations, when concretely tied to law-enforcement functions, are “proceedings” for purposes of Exemption 7(A).

## Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington and Juarez: proving “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings

Two D.C. Circuit precedents provide the bookends for how to demonstrate that an enforcement proceeding is “pending or reasonably anticipated.” In Juarez v. DOJ, the court held that an ongoing investigation is a “proceeding” when the investigation continues to gather evidence for a possible future criminal case and that premature release of evidence would jeopardize that future case. Juarez made clear that as long as the government is actively investigating and collecting evidence for potential prosecution, exemption 7(A) can apply.

CREW (Citizens for Responsibility & Ethics in Washington v. DOJ) refined the showing required to establish that a proceeding is pending or reasonably anticipated. CREW stressed that the government must do more than make a vague assertion that investigations are ongoing; the agency should identify a “concrete prospective law-enforcement proceeding” or provide specific factual bases for anticipating enforcement. In practice CREW requires more than an offhand or blanket statement: courts scrutinize the agency’s affidavits for adequate factual detail, a rational nexus between the records and the asserted enforcement interest, and contemporaneity.

From these two precedents derive a working rule. The government can show a “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceeding either by attesting to the existence of an ongoing investigation (as Juarez allows) or by pointing to concrete facts that make enforcement reasonably foreseeable (as CREW demands if the initial showing is thin or stale). The government’s affidavit need not provide trial-level detail, but it must explain why releasing the requested records would meaningfully risk interference with enforcement.

Farahi applies and reconciles these authorities. There the FBI initially filed a detailed declaration explaining the nature of investigations tied to the requested records, satisfying the Juarez/CREW principles. Because time had elapsed, the district court required an updated assessment. The FBI supplied a terse confirming declaration plus an ex parte submission with greater detail; the court credited the whole record and concluded the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement was met. The lesson is twofold: initial specificity matters; and when time passes the agency should refresh its factual assertions.

## Types of cognizable interference: tipping off targets, revealing scope and strategy, chilling witnesses

What does “interfere with enforcement proceedings” mean in practice? Courts have recognized several cognizable categories of interference, grounded in how information disclosure can affect investigative work:

- Tipping off targets: Public release that identifies targets, reveals the existence of surveillance or investigative focus, or discloses queries or markers used by investigators can enable targets to flee, destroy evidence, alter behavior, or take countermeasures. “Tipping off” is a classic and frequently cited justification for 7(A) withholding. If disclosure would alert suspects to the nature, timing, or focus of an investigation, courts often accept a reasonable expectation of interference.

- Revealing scope and strategy: Disclosure of who has been interviewed, what lines of inquiry have been pursued, what investigative techniques are used (e.g., electronic surveillance, source development), or what theories the investigators are pursuing can enable adversaries to predict and evade future steps. Revealing investigative priorities or legal theories can also impair the government’s tactical advantage in negotiations, searches, or plea discussions.

- Chilling witnesses and cooperation: Release of materials that identify or disclose the involvement of confidential sources, informants, or witnesses can chill cooperation and jeopardize ongoing witness development. Even if a particular individual’s name is not harmful, disclosure of the pattern of cooperation or the existence of relationships can deter future witnesses from assisting investigators.

- Compromising evidence: Disclosing raw intelligence, uncorroborated tips, or the agents’ analysis may expose the evidentiary fabric of an investigation and enable targets to tailor defenses or manipulate evidence before charges are brought.

Courts assess these categories in light of the specific record and the agency’s expertise. A broad, unexplained assertion that disclosure “might” tip off targets is weaker than a declaration that the requested records would reveal identifiable investigative techniques, the presence of ongoing surveillance, or the identity of cooperative sources. The D.C. Circuit has emphasized that the government need not prove which exact harm will occur, only that a reasonable expectation of interference exists; nevertheless, it must provide a plausible explanation that links the nature of the records to the kind of interference feared. Farahi relied principally on the tipping-off rationale, buttressed by detailed albeit ex parte descriptions of the investigation’s scope and subject matter.

## Time and staleness: how passage of time affects the showing; when updates are needed

Time matters in Exemption 7(A) cases. An affidavit that persuasively shows an investigation is ongoing at the time of filing can become stale as months or years pass. Courts therefore scrutinize whether the agency’s factual assertions remain true at the time of judicial decision. CREW and subsequent cases make clear that courts may order agencies to update declarations if a significant interval has elapsed between the original declaration and the court’s decision.

There are two related concerns about stale declarations. First, the factual predicate for the “pending or reasonably anticipated” showing may no longer exist: an investigation may have closed, enforcement decisions may have been made, or the passage of time may have rendered disclosed information innocuous. Second, even if an investigation remains technically open, its posture may have shifted in ways that undercut the predicted harms of disclosure.

What procedural steps do courts take? If a declaration is old, courts often allow the government to file a supplemental declaration that confirms or revises earlier factual representations. That supplemental showing can be public or ex parte (sealed) depending on whether disclosure of additional specifics would itself reveal sensitive information. The district court in Farahi exemplified the common approach: the court denied summary judgment without prejudice to give the FBI an opportunity to update its assessment; the FBI then filed both public and sealed supplemental declarations. The court accepted the update, credited the agency’s expertise, and granted summary judgment.

Two practical rules flow from these doctrines. Agencies should monitor older FOIA litigations and refresh declarations when material time has passed. Requesters should press for updated agency statements if the government relies on old affidavits. Courts weigh these competing interests against the reality that certain information—especially in counterterror investigations—cannot be put on the public record without defeating the agency’s asserted harms.

## Categorical withholding authorized and how to define defensible categories

Agencies facing large volumes of responsive records commonly withhold by category rather than litigate document-by-document. The practice is permissible so long as the categories are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied.” Courts have long recognized that categorical descriptions can be necessary and efficient when an agency must process thousands of pages and when much of the record is similar in nature.

Several features make categorical withholding defensible:

- Logical, document-based grouping: Categories should reflect objective similarities—e.g., investigative memoranda, witness interview summaries, intelligence reports, referral material from other agencies—rather than undifferentiated lumps. Grouping by function or form helps a court assess whether the exemption logically applies.

- Connection to asserted exemptions: The agency must explain why each category is subject to the claimed exemption. For Exemption 7(A), that explanation should show how disclosure of that class of material would produce the kinds of interference discussed above (tipping off, revealing methods, chilling witnesses).

- Sufficient specificity: The descriptions must be detailed enough for a court to evaluate the claim. Mere labels (“operational files,” “investigative reports”) are weak; an affidavit should describe what those things contain and why their contents create a reasonable expectation of interference.

- Opportunity for challenge: The requester should be given enough information to meaningfully challenge the categorical descriptions. A court will be skeptical if a category is so broadly defined as to foreclose any useful review.

Farahi demonstrates both the use and limits of categorical withholding. The FBI identified categories to explain its withholding decisions and the district court’s opinion notes that Farahi failed to explain why the categories were overbroad—an argument the court treated as forfeited because it was not developed. But the D.C. Circuit has repeatedly stated that when an agency uses categories, a challenger must make specific showings about why a category is not distinct or why its application is improper. Absent such developed argumentation, courts will defer to the agency if its declarations otherwise provide a reasoned justification.

You will also see the doctrine described in older precedents sometimes associated with “categorical” allowances (sometimes referred to in practice by case names in different contexts). Whatever the label, the essential inquiry remains the same: can the court, based on the agency’s description and any supporting material, determine whether the exemption was properly applied to the category in question?

## Segregability, inextricable intertwinement, and ex parte submissions

Even when a record is covered by an exemption, FOIA requires agencies to disclose “any reasonably segregable” nonexempt portions. Courts apply a practical standard: if the nonexempt material is merely isolated words or phrases scattered line-by-line through a document, and if separating them would itself disclose the exempt context or provide no meaningful information, they may be treated as not reasonably segregable. By contrast, where identifiable chunks of nonexempt substantive content can be released without disclosing exempt information, the agency must produce them.

Mead Data established that courts should focus on information, not on purely mechanical word counts; Perioperative Services and subsequent cases emphasize that courts may rely on government affidavits that explain, with reasonable specificity, why segregability is not possible. Where segregable information is intertwined line-by-line with exempt material—so that excising the exempt parts would render remaining fragments unintelligible or of no informational value—courts have permitted withholding in whole.

Ex parte and in camera submissions complicate segregability review. Agencies sometimes provide classified or sensitive affidavits under seal so that they can explain why disclosure would harm ongoing investigations. While such filings handicap the requester’s ability to rebut, courts have permitted their use when necessary to protect secrets; the alternative would be to force disclosure of the very information the government seeks to keep secret. Courts must balance these competing interests, and when a requester fails to object to ex parte filings or fails to press for a public accounting, courts are less likely to fault the government.

Farahi typifies these issues. After an initial public declaration, the FBI filed sealed ex parte declarations that provided the district court with the particulars needed to assess interference and segregability. The district court found those sealed submissions persuasive and concluded that meaningful segregable material did not exist. The D.C. Circuit upheld that conclusion, explaining that the burden of showing segregability was satisfied by the government’s detailed sealed submission and that the presence of ex parte material—while prejudicial to the requester to some degree—is sometimes unavoidable in cases involving sensitive investigations.

## Practical implications for litigants and for courts

For agencies:
- Prepare declarations that tie the requested records to concrete investigative functions and explain the precise harms that would reasonably follow from disclosure.
- If your original affidavit is many months old, file an updated declaration to address staleness; be prepared to provide classified or sealed ex parte detail when necessary to explain the asserted harms and segregability.
- When withholding by category, define categories by document type and content, give reasons why each category is covered by the asserted exemption, and describe what, if anything, is nonexempt within each category.

For requesters:
- Attack categorical descriptions specifically and develop arguments showing why the categories are overbroad or why particular subcategories should be released.
- Monitor time: seek updated declarations if the government relies on old affidavits; ask for specific information about the status and posture of investigations when appropriate.
- When possible, seek in camera review or targeted public disclosures to test the government’s claims without forcing full public release of sensitive information.

For courts:
- Insist on adequate factual specificity in government declarations, particularly where the claimed harm is speculative or where significant time has elapsed.
- Give appropriate deference on predictive judgments about interference, especially from specialized agencies, but require a rational nexus between the records and a plausible interference scenario.
- Use sealed ex parte submissions when necessary to preserve national-security or law-enforcement secrets, but make clear on the public record the bases on which the in camera material supports withholding to the extent possible without revealing protected information.

## Conclusion

Exemption 7(A) balances competing public interests: the public’s right to know and the public interest in effective enforcement of the law. The jurisprudence developed in Juarez, CREW, and subsequent cases—illustrated by Farahi v. FBI—reflects a pragmatic approach. Agencies are permitted substantial latitude to predict harms and to withhold investigatory records where release would reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or anticipated proceedings, but that latitude is bounded by the need for sufficiently detailed, timely, and document-linked factual showings; sensible categorical descriptions; and honest engagement with the segregability requirement. When those procedural and substantive requirements are met, courts will often defer to the agency’s predictive judgments; when they are not, courts will push agencies to justify or revise their positions before ordering broad disclosure.

# Proving and Policing 7(A): Affidavits, Vaughn Indices, and Ex Parte/In Camera Practice

The Freedom of Information Act’s Exemption 7(A) is one of the more consequential and litigated FOIA exemptions: it permits agencies to withhold records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Because Exemption 7(A) typically arises in the context of criminal or national-security investigations, courts must balance two competing institutional tasks—on the one hand, policing the government’s claim to secrecy and ensuring meaningful judicial review of exemption claims; on the other, deferring to law-enforcement expertise and protecting ongoing investigations from premature disclosure. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI (2025) illustrates how courts navigate that balance and highlights the principal evidentiary tools courts accept—and the limits of those tools—when agencies invoke Exemption 7(A): agency affidavits (often called “Vaughn” affidavits when used with an index), categorical descriptions of withheld records, and in camera or ex parte submissions to provide a judge with fuller factual context while avoiding public disclosure of sensitive details.

This chapter explains the doctrinal framework for assessing Exemption 7(A) claims, describes the evidentiary requirements that case law has developed for agency submissions, and shows how those requirements operated in Farahi. The goal is practical: to equip students with the standards courts apply, the reasons behind them, and the tactical implications for counsel representing either the requester or the agency.

## Vaughn v. Rosen requirements and the adequacy of categorical descriptions

Vaughn v. Rosen established the basic rule governing the informational content that agency submissions must provide when courts review exemptions: the agency must provide enough detail for the court to “perform a meaningful de novo review” of each withheld record or category. 484 F.2d 820 (D.C. Cir. 1973). The classic mechanism is the Vaughn index: an item-by-item index that identifies each withheld document, describes its nature and contents, and explains why the claimed exemption applies. The index must be specific enough to allow the court to determine whether the exemption was properly invoked without revealing the exempt information itself.

Yet the Vaughn index is not a mechanical, one-size-fits-all document. When an agency is dealing with thousands of pages of records or with information whose public disclosure would in itself reveal sensitive investigative details, courts permit variations: a limited number of descriptive affidavits, category-by-category justifications, and—where necessary—ex parte or in camera submissions. The key principle remains constant: whether presented page-by-page or category-by-category, the agency must supply sufficient specific factual material for the court to evaluate the exemption claim.

Farahi illustrates both the utility and the limits of categorical descriptions. The FBI did not simply file a generic statement that the records were exempt; instead, it provided a public declaration by David Hardy that explained the records were compiled in furtherance of investigations into national-security and terrorism violations. Hardy’s public declaration offered a nexus to law-enforcement duties and attested to the existence of ongoing investigations; an ex parte Hardy declaration (filed under seal) supplied greater detail necessary to justify withholding. When challenged on the breadth of withholding, the D.C. Circuit affirmed that an agency may use categories rather than line-by-line analysis so long as the categories are “sufficiently distinct to allow a court to determine whether the specific claimed exemptions are properly applied” (a standard the court has articulated in Gallant and applied in subsequent cases). See Gallant v. NLRB, 26 F.3d 168, 173 (D.C. Cir. 1994). In Farahi, the court found no persuasive showing that the FBI’s categories were overbroad and treated Farahi’s objections as forfeited because he failed to identify specific problems with the categories.

Practical guidance:
- For agencies: when voluminous, sensitive records are at issue, prepare a layered submission: a public declaration explaining the investigation and the categories of withheld materials, a Vaughn-style index where feasible, and an ex parte/in camera submission containing the detailed factual narrative that cannot be publicly disclosed.
- For requesters: be specific in challenging categories. A general complaint that a descriptor is “too broad” is likely to be deemed forfeited unless the requester identifies why the category prevents judicial review (for example, by showing different records with different exemption claims lumped together).

## Levels of specificity: sufficient detail without compromising the investigation

The courts have repeatedly emphasized that the agency’s affidavits must do more than parrot statutory language. They must (1) establish that the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes by showing a rational nexus between the records and the agency’s investigative duties, and (2) explain how disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere with” an identifiable or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceeding. See Pratt v. Webster, 673 F.2d 408, 420–21 (D.C. Cir. 1982); Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20, 32 (D.C. Cir. 1998).

At the same time, courts recognize that excessive public specificity can itself compromise an investigation. This tension produces a practical rule of thumb: an agency must provide in its public submissions enough factual context to allow a court reasonably to assess the claim (for example, the nature and status of the investigation, the type of proceedings at stake, and the kinds of harms disclosure would cause), and the agency may provide more detailed factual descriptions to the judge ex parte or in camera.

Farahi demonstrates the operation of this balancing rule. Hardy’s public declaration described the subjects and law-enforcement purpose and attested that the investigation related to national security/terrorism remained ongoing. Those public facts were sufficient, in light of the constrained public record, to establish the requisite nexus. The ex parte Hardy declaration then provided the more granular information—e.g., specifics about the investigative subject’s alleged ties to terrorism and the nature of the probes—that the court needed to be confident that disclosure would interfere with ongoing proceedings. The D.C. Circuit accepted the layered approach: while Bender’s later public declaration was terse, it served as an update rather than a standalone justification because the detailed factual foundation already existed in Hardy’s public and sealed materials.

Doctrinal pointers:
- Courts require “reasonably specific” factual representations, not a narrative that reveals every investigative fact. Reasonable specificity means the agency should identify (when possible without revealing secrets) the type of enforcement proceeding (criminal investigation, grand jury, detained enemy combatant inquiry, etc.), whether an investigation is ongoing, and the particular kinds of interference disclosure would cause (e.g., tipping targets, revealing lines of inquiry or non-public investigative techniques).
- If the agency’s public affidavit is sparse, the court may require additional explanation or permit an in camera review of more detailed material; a conclusory claim that an investigation is “ongoing” can be insufficient in isolation (see CREW).

## Use of ex parte and in camera submissions: necessity, limits, and fairness concerns

Ex parte (agency-only) and in camera (judge-only) submissions are an indispensable tool in FOIA litigation when public disclosure of the evidence necessary to sustain the exemption claim would itself reveal the very information the agency seeks to protect. But their use is not automatic or unbounded. Courts must balance the agency’s secrecy interest against the requester’s interest in adversarial testing of the agency’s claims and the public’s interest in openness.

When are ex parte or in camera submissions permissible?
- When the agency shows that the information necessary to support its exemption claim cannot be disclosed publicly without harming the asserted interest (e.g., revealing investigative targets or techniques).
- When the opposing party has the opportunity to contest the factual assertions that can be publicly disclosed and when the court can fairly evaluate the sealed submission without the requester’s participation. Courts sometimes consider whether the requester opposed the use of sealed submissions, but such non-opposition does not substitute for judicial scrutiny.

What limits apply?
- Ex parte or in camera submissions do not allow the agency to avoid all scrutiny. The judge must still independently review the sealed material and determine whether it justifies withholding under the statute. The court’s review should be sufficiently searching to “satisfy a judge of the validity of the reasons” offered, while not requiring public disclosure that would undermine the claimed interest.
- A court should not accept sealed submissions that are merely conclusory; the sealed material must supply the specific factual predicates missing from the public record.

Fairness concerns:
- Sealed submissions place the requester at a disadvantage because they preclude adversarial testing of the sealed factual claims. The D.C. Circuit has recognized that this disadvantage is “inherent” to FOIA cases where secrecy is at issue, see Perioperative Servs. & Logistics LLC v. VA, 57 F.4th 1061, 1067 (D.C. Cir. 2023), but courts should take steps to minimize that disadvantage by requiring adequate public disclosures, careful judicial review of sealed materials, and—where appropriate—narrow tailoring of withheld categories.

How Farahi applied these principles:
- The FBI filed both public and ex parte declarations. The district court initially denied summary judgment to allow the FBI to update declarations; after the FBI supplemented the public record and provided sealed materials addressing the court’s concerns (including segregability), the court granted summary judgment. The D.C. Circuit affirmed the use of the ex parte material for two reasons: (1) the sealed material was necessary to supply the detail the public declaration lacked; and (2) Farahi had not opposed the filing of sealed or ex parte declarations in the district court. The court also emphasized that ex parte submissions are many times “the only way” to resolve disputes about highly sensitive FOIA materials without disclosing the harmful information.

Practice guidance:
- Agencies should use sealed submissions sparingly and always ensure the public record contains as much non-sensitive justification as possible.
- Requesters should, when possible, contest the government’s motion to file ex parte materials and ask the court to identify which additional facts are necessary to support withholding so the requester can try to address them or propose alternative, less secretive means of proof (e.g., neutral or stipulated facts).
- Judges confronted with sealed materials should state, as much as the sealed nature permits, the reasons for accepting or rejecting the sealed material so that judicial review is transparent in principle even if not entirely public.

## Deference in national security contexts (CNSS) and how it affects affidavit sufficiency

A recurrent theme in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence is the deference afforded to national-security and law-enforcement agencies. The D.C. Circuit has emphasized that where national security concerns are implicated, courts should give greater leeway to agency assertions because the agencies possess specialized expertise and information. See Ctr. for Nat’l Sec. Stud. v. DOJ, 331 F.3d 918, 927–28 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS); Campbell, 164 F.3d at 32 (FBI’s decisions entitled to deference).

That deference has important doctrinal consequences:
- It lowers—but does not eliminate—the level of detail the agency must supply publicly. Courts will accept a measured degree of specificity short of revealing operational details that could harm national security.
- It influences the court’s assessment of harms: courts are more willing to accept that disclosure could “reasonably be expected to interfere” with specialized investigations where the agency explains the sensitive nature of the inquiry.

But deference is not carte blanche. The government still bears the burden of proof, must provide a factual basis for its claims, and cannot rely on conclusory or boilerplate language. Cases like CREW and Juarez remind agencies that courts will scrutinize bare assertions of ongoing investigations or generic statements about “interference” if the public record lacks meaningful factual support.

Farahi’s place in this balance:
- The D.C. Circuit’s opinion endorses deference to the FBI’s claim that its records were law-enforcement materials relating to terrorism investigations. The court emphasized the FBI’s institutional competence and the sensitive nature of counterterrorism investigations in accepting the agency’s layered affidavits and sealed submission.
- But the opinion also shows that deference interacts with the need for specificity: the Hardy and Bender submissions were not accepted merely because they came from the FBI; they were accepted because the public Hardy declaration and the sealed Hardy declaration together supplied a factual narrative that supported the claimed harms.

Practical consequences:
- Attorneys for agencies should marshal the agency’s institutional expertise in their affidavits but must also provide a factual narrative linking the withheld materials to specific investigative functions and anticipated harms.
- Attorneys for requesters should challenge any attempt to hide behind institutional deference when the public record lacks corroborating detail; however, they must recognize courts may sustain claims where revealing the necessary factual underpinnings would itself defeat the protective purpose of the exemption.

## Handling “vague” or conclusory submissions and the need for updates when delays occur

Courts will not sustain exemption claims based on conclusory or “vague” assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” without more context. CREW v. DOJ is a touchstone on this point: the D.C. Circuit warned that sparse, conclusory statements that an investigation is ongoing—or that disclosure “could interfere”—may be insufficient and that an agency must at least establish the “existence” of an investigation and articulate the ways in which disclosure would harm it. 746 F.3d at 1097–99. Juarez similarly held that Exemption 7(A) protects material where the agency shows that the investigation continues to gather evidence that would be jeopardized by premature release. 518 F.3d at 59.

Farahi shows a two-part lesson about vagueness and delays.
1. Vague public statements can be cured by more detailed sealed submissions. Bender’s terse public declaration would likely have been insufficient if considered alone; but the district court and the D.C. Circuit considered Bender’s declaration in the context of the earlier, more elaborate Hardy declarations (including the sealed Hardy submission). The FBI’s layered approach allowed the court to treat the later terse update as only an attestation that the status had not changed.
2. Time can render earlier affidavits stale. When a significant period has elapsed between an agency’s initial declaration and the court’s ruling—especially where no public litigation activity has been underway—the district court is entitled to require updated or supplemental declarations to show that alleged ongoing investigations remain ongoing and that the predicted harms from disclosure continue to apply. That is exactly what happened in Farahi: the district court denied summary judgment without prejudice and ordered an updated assessment; the FBI responded with both public and sealed updates, which the court accepted.

Practical tips:
- Agencies should anticipate staleness challenges in long-running FOIA cases. When a request involves an investigation that might continue for years, agencies should update their declarations at key litigation milestones (e.g., before summary judgment).
- Requesters should move for updated declarations when the agency’s submissions are old and the agency does not show continuity of investigative activity.
- Courts should be mindful that a short conclusory affidavit produced years earlier cannot substitute for a contemporaneous, fact-based account demonstrating the continuing potential for interference.

## Refreshing the record: addressing staleness with supplemental declarations

Farahi underscores an important remedial mechanism: courts can and do require agencies to “refresh” the record when the passage of time raises doubt about whether the circumstances justifying withholding still exist. This obligation serves both to prevent indefinite secrecy based on stale claims and to ensure that the court’s decision rests on current facts.

What counts as adequate supplementation?
- A supplemental declaration should identify what, if any, investigative activity has occurred since the prior affidavit, whether the proceeding remains active or was initiated, and why releasing the records would still cause the identified harms. It need not re-litigate every factual predicate of the initial claim if it credibly updates the material status.
- If the agency’s initial submission supplied the factual foundation and the supplement is simply a status update, a short attestation may suffice—as Bender’s “remain ongoing” attestation did in Farahi—so long as the court possesses the earlier factual foundation and can view the update as confirming continuity.

What if the agency’s supplement is brief?
- The court must evaluate the supplemental submission in context. CREW cautions against treating a brief update as adequate where the earlier record was thin; conversely, a brief update may be sufficient when a fuller foundational submission already exists. The district court in Farahi had already concluded the FBI “seemingly” met its burden in an earlier decision, which made the later brief update more plausibly sufficient.

Practice checklist:
- For agencies: file supplements when significant time has passed, and explain any changes in investigative posture. If the initial justification relied on sealed facts, indicate in the supplement that those sealed facts remain accurate and, if necessary, provide an updated sealed submission.
- For requesters: seek supplementation when the agency’s last supporting material is old; press the court to identify the specific gaps in the public record and, where appropriate, to require further public detail or to conduct an in camera review of sealed updates.

## Segregability and the “inextricably intertwined” rule

Even when Exemption 7(A) applies to most of a requested file, the FOIA statute requires the agency to disclose “any reasonably segregable portion of a record.” 5 U.S.C. § 552(b). The D.C. Circuit has explained that disclosure is not required when the non-exempt material is so interspersed with exempt material that meaningful disclosure would itself reveal the exempt information (the “inextricably intertwined” doctrine). See Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242, 261 (D.C. Cir. 1977).

The Mead standard requires courts to assess whether meaningful information can be disclosed without revealing exempt material. Where non-exempt words or phrases are randomly interspersed line-by-line through otherwise exempt documents, the court may accept an agency’s showing that reasonably segregable material does not exist. Perioperative Servs. reiterated that courts may defer to agency affidavits that explain why segregation is not feasible, but stressed that the affidavits must have “reasonable specificity.”

Farahi applied these principles: the district court, after reviewing Bender’s sealed submission, accepted the FBI’s explanation that non-exempt information was inextricably intertwined with exempt content and therefore not reasonably segregable. The D.C. Circuit affirmed. Importantly, the court rejected the argument that the sheer volume of records creates a presumption of some segregable, releasable information; volume alone does not displace the agency’s segregability showing where the agency explains why disclosure of small fragments would disclose substantive, sensitive content.

Practical rules:
- Agencies should explain, with as much specificity as safely possible, the forms of interspersion that frustrate segregation (e.g., “names in every paragraph that would reveal investigative targets,” “context-dependent summaries that cannot be redacted without revealing operational conclusions”).
- Requesters should press for a Vaughn index or at least representative samples so the court can assess segregability. Where the court receives sealed material, the requester should ask the court to indicate (in an unclassified summary when possible) why the sealed material justifies non-segregability.
- Courts should require tailored justifications; generic claims that “segregation is impracticable” will not suffice absent a credible explanation.

## Concluding guidance for litigation strategy and judicial management

Farahi v. FBI illustrates the doctrinal contours and practical mechanisms courts use to police Exemption 7(A) claims while respecting the government’s need for secrecy in sensitive investigations. The case teaches these practical lessons:

- Layered submissions work. When secrecy is at stake, agencies should provide a public declaration with core facts and a sealed submission for the court containing the full factual narrative. That layered approach preserves an adversarial record for the public parts of the case while enabling the court to treat sensitive details appropriately.

- Specificity is required—but calibrated. Courts will accept a degree of concision in public filings when an agency supplies more detailed sealed material. But conclusory, boilerplate assertions—especially about the existence of investigations—may be rejected. When in doubt, an agency should err on the side of providing a clear factual nexus and a reasoned explanation of how disclosure would harm the investigation.

- Be prepared to update. Time erodes the force of past affidavits. Courts will require updated declarations when significant time has elapsed between the supporting submissions and the decision date. Supplemental affidavits need not rehash previously supplied sealed facts in public, but they should plainly attest that the basis for withholding persists and identify any relevant intervening developments.

- Segregability cannot be ignored. Even in national-security contexts, agencies must explain why non-exempt material cannot be meaningfully segregated. A careful segregability analysis—again, layered where necessary—improves the chance of a court sustaining a withholding decision.

- Opponents must be specific in their challenges. Requesters should identify particular categories, documents, or claimed factual deficiencies rather than relying on general assertions that categories are “overbroad” or affidavits “conclusory.” Specific, document-level challenges increase the likelihood the court will order disclosure or an in camera review.

- Courts must balance transparency and protection. Judges must perform the independent review that FOIA demands, but judicial opinions need not and should not expose the sensitive details that the best submissions explain in sealed form. When judges accept sealed material, they should explain (to the extent they safely can) why the sealed material suffices—so that appellate courts and future litigants can discern the governing standards.

Farahi’s procedural arc—initial public declarations, a district-court order requiring updates, supplemental public and sealed declarations, and appellate affirmation—exemplifies how courts and agencies should litigate Exemption 7(A) disputes: with layered disclosures, careful factual justifications, responsiveness to staleness concerns, and judicial review that is searching in substance even when necessarily circumscribed in form. For students and practitioners, the case illustrates both the latitude available to national-security agencies and the practical limits of that latitude when courts insist on a record sufficient to ensure FOIA’s accountability function.

# Segregability: The Statutory Duty to Release Nonexempt Material

The Freedom of Information Act imposes a concrete duty on agencies not only to identify legitimately exempt records, but also to take affirmative and reasonable steps to separate and disclose whatever nonexempt information those records contain. That duty—often called the segregability requirement—is an indispensable part of FOIA’s design: the statute presumes disclosure as the norm and treats exemptions as limited exceptions. This chapter explains the statutory text and the D.C. Circuit’s doctrinal architecture for evaluating segregability claims. It uses the recent decision in Farahi v. FBI to illustrate how courts apply those principles in the law‑enforcement and national‑security context, and it surveys the principal doctrinal tools courts use—Vaughn indices, in camera review, sampling, category analysis, and ex parte submissions—to test whether an agency has satisfied its duty.

## Statutory language: 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(8)(A)(ii) and § 552(b) mandates

FOIA’s segregability obligation appears in two complementary places in the statute as cited by the D.C. Circuit. The statute’s exemptions clause closes with an unequivocal command: “Any reasonably segregable portion of a record shall be provided to any person requesting such record after deletion of the portions which are exempt.” That sentence—found in 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)—expresses the fundamental rule: exemption is not a license to withhold whole documents when portions could lawfully be disclosed. Related provisions (as cited in current practice) require agencies to take “reasonable steps” to segregate and release nonexempt information. These parallel provisions together place the burden on the agency to show both that a claimed exemption applies and that it has complied with the duty to separate and disclose nonexempt content.

The text is categorical in form but requires judgment in operation. What counts as “reasonably segregable”? When must an agency redact rather than withhold an entire document? How precise must the agency’s justification be? The courts have developed a body of doctrine to answer these questions, recognizing two competing principles embedded in the statute: a strong presumption of disclosure, on the one hand, and practical deference to agency expertise and operational constraints, especially in law‑enforcement and national‑security contexts, on the other.

## Meaningful information versus isolated words: Mead Data Center’s “inextricably intertwined” concept

The seminal D.C. Circuit decision that framed the modern segregability inquiry is Mead Data Central, Inc. v. U.S. Department of the Air Force. Mead confronted the practical problem that some documents contain a mixture of exempt and nonexempt information so tightly interwoven that any attempt at disclosure would reveal exempt material or would leave only a handful of meaningless words. Mead articulated two key propositions that continue to govern courts’ analysis.

First, agencies are required to release any “meaningful” nonexempt information. The aim of FOIA is the dissemination of information, not the mere disclosure of words without informative content. Mead therefore rejects a mechanical rule that would treat the presence of any nonexempt token as requiring disclosure regardless of whether disclosure yields useful information. Conversely, the court explained that isolated words, or fragments whose separable disclosure would reveal no substantive information, are not the kind of “portion” that the statute requires agencies to reveal.

Second, Mead stated that an agency may withhold an entire document where the nonexempt and exempt information are “inextricably intertwined.” If separating the nonexempt content would itself expose exempt material, or if redaction would destroy the informative context making the remaining words meaningless, then the agency may withhold the whole record. Mead thus recognizes that segregability is a functional inquiry into whether redaction can preserve the utility of the released content without compromising the exemption interests.

The Mead standard requires courts to go beyond a literal word‑count and to consider the informational value of any portion that might be released. That is why courts insist on a showing—usually by detailed affidavit or index—explaining not simply that an exemption applies, but also why redaction would either fail to protect the exempt material or would reduce the remainder to trivia.

## When categorical withholdings defeat segregability and when they don’t

Agencies often confront large document sets and respond by withholding records in categories rather than itemizing every page. The D.C. Circuit permits categorical withholdings, but it imposes limits to ensure that categories are sufficiently concrete to permit judicial review. The controlling idea is procedural: a court must be able to determine whether the claimed exemptions properly apply to each category. Two lines of authority illuminate the permissible bounds.

First, categorical withholdings are acceptable where the agency defines categories that are “sufficiently distinct” and provides a logical explanation—often in a declaration or Vaughn index—showing why each category is exempt and why the records in that category are or are not reasonably segregable. When an agency files a declaration that describes the category, the nature of the withheld records, and the harm that disclosure would cause, a court can test the claim without door‑by‑door review. This approach recognizes practical realities: with thousands of pages, itemized indexing may be disproportionate or impossible.

Second, categorical withholding is defective when categories are overbroad, conclusory, or lack the factual detail necessary for a court to assess the exemption claim. In Gallant and subsequent cases, the court required categories to be described with enough specificity to permit meaningful judicial review. The plaintiff must be given sufficient information to challenge the agency’s assertions; the court must be able to determine whether the exemption fits the record and whether segregable information exists. A category label without explanation—merely saying “investigatory records” or “internal deliberations”—is often insufficient.

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi provides a concrete example of how courts balance these concerns. The FBI initially presented a large, complex corpus and relied on declarations from officials to describe the nature and risks of disclosure. The district court required updated information because of the passage of time; after the agency supplemented its showing with additional public and ex parte declarations, the court accepted the categorical descriptions and concluded that the agency had justified nondisclosure and nonsegregability. The Farahi court stressed that a categorical withholding can be adequate when declarations explain the categories with “reasonable specificity” and when the agency shows why nonexempt material is not meaningfully separable.

## Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. VA: reliance on ex parte submissions to decide segregability

Perioperative Services & Logistics LLC v. Department of Veterans Affairs is an important recent decision that clarifies the role of sealed or ex parte submissions in the segregability inquiry. The D.C. Circuit recognized that in some FOIA cases—especially those touching on law enforcement or national security—an agency’s only practicable means of explaining the full nature of its harm assessment may be a sealed, ex parte declaration. Revealing the content of that declaration on the public docket could itself disclose sensitive information and defeat the agency’s asserted exemption interests.

Perioperative held that courts may consider ex parte submissions so long as doing so is necessary to resolve the dispute without publicly exposing the exempt material, and so long as the agency has otherwise borne its burden of showing that the exemption applies. The decision acknowledged that ex parte evidence handicaps the requester, but concluded that such handicaps are sometimes inherent in FOIA litigation concerning classified or sensitive investigations. Perioperative therefore accepts sealed submissions as a permissible evidentiary device, while also reminding courts that agency affidavits—even when ex parte—must provide “reasonable specificity” and factual grounding sufficient to allow a reviewing court to evaluate the claimed exemptions.

Farahi illustrates this principle in practice. The FBI supplemented public declarations with a sealed, ex parte Hardy declaration describing investigatory details and explaining why meaningful segregable information could not be released. The district court relied on the sealed material to test the FBI’s segregability claim, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed that reliance. The opinion emphasized that Farahi did not oppose the use of ex parte declarations and that the sealed submissions were the only practical way to decide the dispute without revealing the very material the government sought to protect.

Even under Perioperative and Farahi, sealed submissions are not an automatic win for agencies. Courts still insist that the agency’s sealed affidavit provide enough information-even if only to the judge‑to satisfy the statutory command. Where sealed affidavits are conclusory or evasive, courts have required further public detail, in camera testing, or other remedial measures.

## Practical indicators of non-segregability in law enforcement/national security files

Certain features of law‑enforcement and national‑security files commonly predict—and the courts commonly accept—claims that nonexempt content is not reasonably segregable. These practical indicators guide both agencies and courts in applying the Mead standard.

One frequent indicator is the intermixing of exempt operational details and nonexempt factual data on a line‑by‑line basis. Investigative memoranda, interview reports, and intelligence analyses often embed contextual descriptions, source identifiers, investigative methods, and analytic judgments in the same paragraphs. Redacting the operational details may leave isolated factual fragments that—although nonexempt when standing alone—would, in the agency’s assessment, reveal the existence of sources, tactics, or the scope of an investigation if disclosed in context. Courts have accepted that where exempt and nonexempt information are so intertwined, segregation is not reasonable.

A second indicator is the presence of material whose disclosure would produce “mosaic” effects—where many innocuous facts, when disclosed together or in sequence, would allow a motivated adversary to reconstruct operational capabilities, witness identities, or investigative scope. In national‑security and counterterrorism contexts, the government often argues (and courts often accept) that even seemingly trivial dataset elements can be fused with other public information to produce a harmful picture. Mead’s insistence on meaningful information, rather than isolated words, dovetails with this concern: releasing isolated fragments that facilitate reconstruction is not a legitimate exercise of FOIA.

A third practical factor is volume coupled with the nature of the information. Large file collections assembled during long‑running investigations commonly contain repeated references to techniques, witness statements, and analytic inferences. Where the agency demonstrates by affidavit that these repeated references are pervasive and interlaced with nonexempt items, the court may conclude that segregable information is de minimis—or at least not “reasonably” segregable in light of the harms that would follow from piecemeal release.

Farahi embodies these indicators. The FBI located over 10,000 pages and 80 CDs potentially responsive to the request. The agency’s sealed declarations explained that the files were part of ongoing national‑security and terrorism investigations involving named terrorist actors and organizations—circumstances in which the agency plausibly showed that descriptions, context, and connective tissue among documents would reveal sensitive operational facts if released. The court accepted that the nonexempt material was not meaningfully separable without revealing exempt details or producing harms the exemptions were designed to prevent.

## Judicial tools: in camera review, sampling, and requiring granular justifications

To test an agency’s segregability claim, courts have developed several procedural tools that reconcile FOIA’s disclosure objective with agencies’ legitimate need to protect sensitive information. Three of the most important are in camera review, sampling, and requirements for granular justifications (Vaughn indices and detailed affidavits).

In camera review allows a judge alone to inspect the contested records. It is particularly valuable when the public record cannot fully reflect the issues because of the secrecy of the exempt material. A judge can decide whether an exemption applies and whether segregability is practical without compounding the problem by placing sensitive information on the public docket. In camera review, however, the court bears the risk of substituting its own assessment of operational harms without the agency’s context; nevertheless, the option exists as a potent remedial device when declarations are thin.

Sampling is a practical device in cases involving voluminous records. Rather than requiring an agency to index every page, the court may review a representative sample to test whether the agency’s descriptions are accurate and whether nonexempt content tends to be segregable. Sampling places a practical burden on the agency to demonstrate that its practice in the sample reflects its treatment of the larger corpus. Courts use sampling to avoid formulaic lawyering or overly generalized categorical descriptions.

Granular justifications take the form of Vaughn indices and detailed agency affidavits. A Vaughn index is an item-by-item description of withheld records showing the document type, the exemption(s) claimed, and the justification for withholding. The D.C. Circuit has emphasized that these indices or supporting declarations must supply information adequate for meaningful judicial review—sufficient specificity to connect the exemption to the content and to explain why segregability is not feasible. Declarations that merely parrot statutory language or offer conclusory assertions of “investigative harm” fail the test; those that provide a narrative of the investigation, explain the investigative significance of the withheld material, and describe why redaction would not protect the exempt content, fare better.

Farahi demonstrates how these tools interplay. The district court initially denied summary judgment and asked the FBI to update potentially stale declarations. The court’s request reflected the obligation to ensure that declarations remain current and to subject the agency’s claimed harms to scrutiny. The FBI supplemented its public affidavits with sealed, ex parte declarations and a more detailed showing on segregability. The district court applied those submissions (in camera) and concluded that the nonexempt material was not reasonably segregable; the D.C. Circuit accepted that approach and affirmed. The decision confirms that courts may, and sometimes must, demand refreshed and granular evidence before accepting an agency’s categorical or holistic segregability claim.

## Practical advice for counsel and courts

For agencies: When asserting nonsegregability, prepare a declaration that does more than identify the exemption. Describe the document types, explain the nature and stage of the investigation, identify the particular harms disclosure would cause, and explain why redaction is impractical or would leave only non‑meaningful fragments. If the agency relies on categories, describe the categories with concrete examples and explain the methodology used to assign records to each category. For national‑security and law‑enforcement records, describe how context and connections among documents would reveal sensitive information and explain why even small disclosures would be harmful.

For FOIA requesters: Challenge overbroad categories by demanding specificity. Seek in camera review or sampling where practicable. Insist on updated declarations when significant time passes, because assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” may lose force without current backing. If an agency uses sealed submissions, argue for at least a public summary that identifies the legal and factual bases for withholding without revealing sensitive facts, and press the court to sample or to inspect a representative set of documents in camera.

For courts: Balance deference to agency expertise with the statutory presumption of disclosure. Require reasonable specificity in declarations; where the agency’s public showing is insufficient, use in camera review or sampling to test the agency’s representations. Accept ex parte submissions when necessary to avoid revealing the very information the agency seeks to protect, but demand that sealed affidavits be probative and explain why segregability is not feasible. Remember Mead’s touchstone: the focus is on meaningful information, not on a substratum of isolated words that discloses nothing of substance.

## Conclusion

The segregability requirement is both doctrinally principled and pragmatically flexible. Mead’s “inextricably intertwined” concept gives substance to the statutory command to release “any reasonably segregable portion” by focusing inquiry on whether redaction would preserve meaningful information without disclosing exempt materials. The D.C. Circuit’s later decisions—CREW, Perioperative, Juarez, and Farahi among them—balance that principle against practical realities in law‑enforcement and national‑security contexts. Those cases teach that courts will demand reasonable specificity from agencies, will permit categorical withholdings where categories are sensibly drawn and justified, and will employ tools such as in camera review and sampling when the public record cannot safely reveal the sensitive details necessary to adjudicate the dispute. For students and practitioners alike, the lesson is straightforward: the duty to segregate is real, but its application must be attentive to the informational value of what would be released and to the legitimate operational harms that disclosure might produce.

# National Security, Counterterrorism, and Deference under FOIA

This chapter examines how national security and counterterrorism concerns shape Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation, with particular emphasis on Exemption 7(A). Using Farahi v. Federal Bureau of Investigation, 23-5243 (D.C. Cir. Sept. 30, 2025), as the focal example, the discussion explains the doctrinal requirements for Exemption 7(A), the scope of judicial deference when national-security or counterterrorism investigations are implicated, the “mosaic” risks that justify withholding piecemeal information, strategic choices among FOIA exemptions, the role of parallel or collateral proceedings (including immigration removal), and the significance of public-source information versus official confirmation. Throughout, the chapter aims to teach the tests courts apply and the practical implications for both agencies and requesters.

## Deference to the Executive When National Security Is Implicated (CNSS and related authority)

The FOIA’s exemptions create a balance between disclosure and the legitimate government interest in secrecy. When national security or counterterrorism matters are involved, courts traditionally afford the Executive a pronounced measure of deference. The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Center for National Security Studies v. Department of Justice, 331 F.3d 918 (D.C. Cir. 2003) (CNSS), is a touchstone: it recognized that national security concerns often present factual and predictive questions for which courts should defer to the specialized expertise of agencies. Farahi reiterates that principle. The court emphasized that “deference to the executive in the FOIA context” is especially appropriate when Exemption 7(A) is invoked for counterterrorism-related records.

This deference manifests in a few distinct doctrinal ways. First, courts will generally accept an agency’s characterization that a record was “compiled for law enforcement purposes” when the agency is a law-enforcement body like the FBI, so long as a rational nexus between the investigation and law-enforcement duties is shown. Cases such as Campbell v. DOJ, 164 F.3d 20 (D.C. Cir. 1998), and Clemente v. FBI, 867 F.3d 111 (D.C. Cir. 2017), recognize that the FBI’s professional judgment about the investigatory character of its files merits weight. In Farahi, the court accepted the FBI’s showing — including public and ex parte declarations — that the records were compiled in furtherance of investigations into national-security and terrorism-related conduct.

Second, deference affects the level of detail a court requires in agency declarations. Agencies must meet the burden of proof, but the D.C. Circuit allows agency affidavits to supply the requisite showings with “reasonable specificity” without requiring disclosure that would defeat the national-security interests at issue. Juarez v. DOJ, 518 F.3d 54 (D.C. Cir. 2008), illustrates this approach: courts may “rely on government affidavits that show with reasonable specificity why documents withheld pursuant to a valid exemption cannot be further segregated.” Farahi is illustrative: the FBI provided a public declaration that attested to the existence and purpose of the investigations and supplemented it with a sealed ex parte declaration that supplied detail the public declaration could not safely disclose. The court accepted that combination as adequate.

That said, deference is not an unbounded license. The D.C. Circuit has required enough factual specificity to permit a meaningful judicial assessment. CREW v. DOJ, 746 F.3d 1082 (D.C. Cir. 2014), established the “pending or reasonably anticipated” standard for Exemption 7(A) and warned against conclusory, vague assertions that an investigation is ongoing. Farahi demonstrates how courts approach this tension: the district court pressed the FBI for updated, non-stale attestations before accepting the Exemption 7(A) claim, and the D.C. Circuit endorsed that approach. Thus, while courts defer, agencies must still produce competent, timely declarations that provide a factual predicate for the claimed harm.

## The Mosaic Concern: How Seemingly Innocuous Details Can Reveal Sensitive Operations

One of the most important rationales for withholding information in national-security FOIA cases is what is often called the “mosaic” theory: discrete pieces of information, innocuous in isolation, may combine with other information (public or private) to reveal sensitive operational details or the scope of an investigation. Courts have repeatedly recognized that FOIA’s focus on “information” (not merely words) means that the context-sensitive risk of aggregation must be considered. Mead Data Cent., Inc. v. U.S. Dep’t of Air Force, 566 F.2d 242 (D.C. Cir. 1977), first articulated concerns about inextricably intertwined disclosures and the practical impossibility of redacting every harmless-looking data point when such redactions would still leave a usable mosaic.

The mosaic concern justifies both the withholding decision under Exemption 7(A) and the use of sealed ex parte declarations: public exposure of the agency’s reasoning, if it requires explaining the sensitivity of particular facts, may itself reveal the very operational contours the agency seeks to protect. In Farahi, the FBI’s decision to submit ex parte material was not just a litigation convenience; it was necessary to avoid producing an explanation that would tip off investigative targets. The D.C. Circuit accepted this approach, noting that ex parte submissions “handicap FOIA plaintiffs” but are sometimes “inherent” in cases where secrecy must be preserved. The court’s reasoning demonstrates the practical mechanics of the mosaic theory: a public declaration can state the general risk (e.g., “releasing information would tip off targets”), while sealed supporting material can demonstrate how even apparently trivial details could do so.

For students, the mosaic idea requires attention to context. An agency’s assertion that redaction or segregation is impracticable will be more persuasive when the record is a comprehensive, interwoven investigative file that includes timelines, corroborating identifiers, and operational planning. When relevant facts are “interspersed line-by-line throughout the document,” Mead Data and subsequent cases instruct that the small nonexempt fragments are not “reasonably segregable.” But the agency still must describe, with sufficient specificity, the nature of the interconnections so a court can assess whether the mosaic risk is real and substantial rather than speculative.

## Choice of Exemptions: Why Agencies May Rely on 7(A) Rather Than 1 or 3

Agencies have a menu of FOIA exemptions to choose from, and the selection often reflects the nature of the harm they seek to avert. Exemption 1 protects properly classified national-security information under an executive order; Exemption 3 covers matters specifically exempted by statute; Exemption 7(A) protects law-enforcement records whose release could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings. Each exemption has distinct prerequisites and evidentiary paths.

Exemption 1 hinges on classification decisions: the information must be properly classified under an executive order governing classified national security information (e.g., EO 13526). In contrast, Exemption 3 requires a separate statute that either forbids disclosure or sets a particular standard of non-disclosure. Exemption 7(A) requires that the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are pending or reasonably anticipated. Because Exemption 7(A) turns on the functional context of the records and the reasonable expectation of interference—rather than a formal classification decision or a narrowly tailored statutory prohibition—agencies frequently rely on 7(A) when the core concern is the interference that disclosure would create for investigations or prosecutions.

Farahi exemplifies this strategic choice. Although the case involves national-security and counterterrorism themes, the FBI relied principally on Exemption 7(A). Several reasons make that decision understandable. For one, the FBI’s files were investigatory and law-enforcement in nature: they were “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” a predicate that is easy for a law-enforcement agency to satisfy. For another, Exemption 7(A) directly addresses the harm of disclosure to investigative continuity and to the integrity of pending or prospective enforcement proceedings—harms that classification alone (Exemption 1) or a separate statutory bar (Exemption 3) may not precisely capture. Moreover, prosecutorial and investigative sensitivity often arises from procedural dynamics (e.g., tipping off targets) rather than simply classification, making 7(A)’s predictive interference analysis particularly apt.

That said, agencies sometimes invoke multiple exemptions in tandem. When information is formally classified, Exemption 1 provides an independent and often stronger basis for withholding because it rests on a distinct statutory–regulatory classification system. Conversely, agencies may prefer 7(A) when the central concern is operational interference or when the relevant information has not been classified but remains sensitive because of investigative implications. Students should appreciate that exemption choice is strategic and fact-dependent; courts will evaluate each claimed exemption on its own terms and will not permit agencies to rely on conclusory assertions. Farahi shows that the agency’s demonstration under 7(A) must include both the law-enforcement nexus and a plausible prediction that disclosure would interfere with “pending or reasonably anticipated” proceedings.

## Parallel Proceedings and Collateral Forums: Immigration, Criminal Investigations, and FOIA Justifications

FOIA disputes do not occur in a vacuum; the underlying records often connect to active or past proceedings in other fora. An especially salient example is immigration removal proceedings, which frequently draw on investigative files compiled by law-enforcement agencies. The “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement in Exemption 7(A) is therefore frequently litigated in contexts where some form of enforcement activity—criminal investigation, immigration removal, administrative sanctions, or other proceedings—exists or could be anticipated.

CREW held that Exemption 7(A) requires the government to demonstrate that enforcement proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Subsequent cases, including Juarez and Farahi, have clarified that ongoing investigations that are actively gathering evidence for potential prosecution qualify as “proceedings.” Farahi shows the interplay between immigration proceedings and ongoing FBI investigations. Although Farahi’s immigration case is civil in nature, the FBI’s counterterrorism investigation was criminal and investigative. The court accepted that the disclosure of FBI materials could interfere with law-enforcement proceedings (criminal or prospective criminal ones), even though the requester’s most immediately relevant litigation context was immigration removal. Put differently, the existence of collateral proceedings does not prohibit the government from asserting 7(A) where an independent, ongoing law-enforcement investigation would be harmed by disclosure.

There are two practical points for students. First, a FOIA requester who is a party in a collateral proceeding should not assume that the needs of that forum (e.g., discovery rights in immigration proceedings) will trump general FOIA exemptions. The government can argue that disclosure in response to a FOIA request would differ in scale or publicity from targeted discovery and could undermine an investigation in ways that in-court disclosure under protective order might not. Second, an agency’s claim that disclosure would interfere with a law-enforcement proceeding must demonstrate a nexus between the records and possible enforcement activity. In Farahi, the government supplied both public and sealed evidence that the FBI was investigating ties to designated terrorist organizations and to convicted terrorists; the court accepted that nexus as sufficient to meet the Exemption 7(A) standard.

The doctrinal lesson is that “proceedings” under 7(A) are not limited to formal prosecutions and can include pending or reasonably anticipated criminal investigations and other enforcement activities. Collateral civil proceedings, like immigration removal, can increase the sensitivity of a file (if they reveal alleged associations or allegations publicized in different contexts), but they do not eliminate the government’s right to claim interference when criminal investigative interests are at stake.

## Public-Source Information versus Official Confirmation: Risks of “Confirmation” and the Value of Context

A common FOIA battleground concerns the relationship between information already in the public domain and the government’s assertion that disclosure would still be harmful. Requesters often argue that if facts are already public—through media reports, court filings, or other disclosures—then releasing agency records containing the same facts cannot harm national security or an investigation. Courts have recognized that public-source information can blunt an agency’s argument of harm, but they have also cautioned that official confirmation of facts or the aggregation of those facts in a government file can create distinct risks.

Farahi illustrates the nuance. The record shows that many allegations about the requester’s ties to terrorist organizations appeared in immigration proceedings and in public court records. Farahi argued that because some allegations were in the public record, the FBI could not reasonably claim that producing its file would interfere with an investigation. The D.C. Circuit rejected that argument. Two separate harms support that outcome.

First, official confirmation: when an agency produces its own records, it does more than repeat publicly available facts; it confirms the premises of those facts, sometimes in detail, and that confirmation can change the calculus for targets or co-conspirators who may not have previously known that the government is actively investigating. An official investigative dossier, even if its individual elements mirror public sources, signals investigatory attention and often reveals lines of inquiry. The court in Farahi accepted that even confirmation of public facts could tip off targets about the scope and focus of an investigation, thereby frustrating evidence-gathering.

Second, aggregation and context: public materials might be scattered, partial, or dated. The agency’s file aggregates, organizes, and potentially connects disparate items—timestamps, interview summaries, internal assessments, and linkages among individuals. That organization and the focus of internal analysis can reveal what investigators believe to be important and where they are placing their efforts. That is the mosaic risk in another guise: the same public pieces, when rearranged and contextualized, can become exponentially more informative.

For practitioners, these principles mean that requesters cannot rely solely on the existence of public allegations; they must show either that the information the agency seeks to withhold does not add materially to what is already public or that the agency’s asserted risk of harm is speculative. Conversely, agencies should explain why their records add value beyond public sources—whether by confirming an active focus, revealing temporal sequencing, or identifying unpublicized connections—and why that added value creates a realistic interference risk.

## Procedural Mechanics: Declarations, Updates, Ex Parte Submissions, Segregability, and Categorical Descriptions

Farahi also provides a useful lesson in the procedural mechanics that govern FOIA litigation. Agencies bear the burden of proving that an exemption applies, but courts allow certain practical tools to make that showing without compromising security. The following points summarize the key procedural doctrines demonstrated by the case.

- Declarations and Reasonable Specificity. Agency declarations must supply enough factual detail for a court to engage in meaningful review. The D.C. Circuit accepts affidavits that demonstrate with “reasonable specificity” why withholding is appropriate, but it will reject mere ipse dixit. In Farahi the FBI’s initial Hardy declaration and later ex parte supplementation were adequate when considered together; the court stressed, however, that the district court properly required an updated attestation because time had passed and factual circumstances could change.

- Updating Declarations. Courts expect agencies to update stale declarations. The district court’s denial without prejudice in Farahi was premised on the reasonable concern that facts and investigative status evolve over time, and that a declaration from years earlier may no longer be probative of whether a proceeding remains “pending or reasonably anticipated.” Agencies should therefore anticipate the need to refresh their factual showings if long delays occur. The Bender declaration in Farahi performed exactly that function: it confirmed that nothing had changed, thereby curing the district court’s concern about staleness.

- Ex Parte and Sealed Submissions. Courts may accept sealed, ex parte declarations when disclosure of the agency’s reasoning on the public record would itself cause harm. There is a tradeoff: the requester cannot test the sealed declaration publicly, but the court can review it in camera to ensure that the agency’s claims are genuine. Farahi recognizes this necessity for national-security FOIA disputes and validates the approach where ex parte materials are the only practicable way to resolve the case without further disclosure.

- Segregability and Inextricable Intertwining. FOIA requires agencies to disclose any “reasonably segregable” nonexempt portions of records. But courts accept that, in some investigative files, nonexempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material so that segregation would either be futile or would itself reveal sensitive linkages. Mead Data set the pattern for this analysis; later cases refine it. In Farahi, the court accepted the FBI’s explanation that meaningful, substantive nonexempt information was not segregable because it was scattered and interwoven throughout the record. The agency’s sealed explanation persuaded the court that a line-by-line parsing would not produce a releasable product that avoided operational risk.

- Categorical Descriptions and Specificity. When agencies withhold records category-by-category, they must describe categories with enough precision that a court can judge the applicability of claimed exemptions. CREW and Gallant require categories be “sufficiently distinct.” Farahi shows the limits of the requester’s challenge: because Farahi did not provide specific arguments explaining why the categories were overbroad, the court treated the objection as forfeited. Practitioners should note that a successful attack on categorical descriptions requires concrete examples and analytic argumentation.

## Practical Takeaways for Litigants and Counsel

For FOIA requesters challenging national-security-related withholdings, Farahi teaches several tactical lessons. First, attack agency declarations with particularity: identify where the agency’s factual assertions are stale, conclusory, or inconsistent, and ask the court to require updated, timely attestations. Second, when the agency relies on public-source material to justify non-disclosure, show precisely why the agency’s records do not add materially to what is already public or demonstrate that the agency’s confirmation would not meaningfully increase the risk of interference. Third, challenge categorical descriptions with concrete examples and explain how disclosure of representative records would not cause the predicted harm.

For agencies, the judgment is different. Provide a public declaration that sets out the law-enforcement nexus and the general harm of disclosure, and where necessary supplement with a sealed ex parte declaration that demonstrates, with greater specificity, the nature and risks of disclosure. Update declarations if litigation is protracted. When asserting non-segregability, explain why ostensibly nonexempt fragments are materially intertwined with exempt content such that partial release would itself be harmful or meaningless. And select exemptions strategically: use Exemption 7(A) when the predominant harm is investigatory interference, Exemption 1 when information is properly classified, and Exemption 3 when a specific statute applies.

## Conclusion

Farahi v. FBI exemplifies how courts apply Exemption 7(A) in the national-security and counterterrorism context: agencies receive a measure of deference, but courts still require factual showings that investigations are “pending or reasonably anticipated,” and they insist on reasonable specificity and timely attestations. The mosaic theory justifies withholding even when pieces of information are publicly available because official confirmation and contextual aggregation can meaningfully increase the risk to investigations. Procedurally, ex parte declarations and sealed submissions play a central role where openness would defeat secrecy, but agencies must use those tools prudently and be prepared to update their proofs. For students and practitioners, the case distills the principal doctrinal tests and practical strategies that govern FOIA disputes at the intersection of law enforcement and national security.

# Procedural Architecture of FOIA Litigation

Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) litigation is, in many respects, litigation of a distinctive procedural species. The statute creates a highly regimented administrative process, allocates burdens of proof, and invites courts to resolve disputes largely on the paper record assembled by agencies. This chapter examines the procedural building blocks that govern most FOIA suits: when a requester may sue; how courts resolve disputes on summary judgment; the scope and limits of appellate review and judicial deference; how litigants preserve and forfeit claims; the role of sealed and ex parte filings in protecting sensitive materials while enabling review; and the remedies and remands courts employ when agency submissions are insufficient. Each section draws on the Farahi v. FBI litigation to illustrate how these procedural doctrines play out in practice.

## Exhaustion and timing: when a requester may sue

FOIA requires agencies to respond to requests within a prescribed period and gives requesters a cause of action if an agency fails to comply. Under the statute, an agency must determine whether to comply “within 20 working days” after receipt of a request, subject to tolling and certain statutory extensions (for consultations, for “unusual circumstances,” and for administrative appeals). If the agency does not make a timely determination, the requestor may treat the delay as a constructive denial and bring suit in federal court. The statutory text and regulations thus create a firm procedural trigger: a plaintiff may sue after the statutory period lapses without an adequate response, or after an adverse determination has issued.

Practically, FOIA litigation often begins after an agency misses the statutory deadline. Farahi’s suit is a canonical example: he sued after the FBI missed the statutory deadline for responding to his request for his FBI file. Long after suit commenced, the agency completed its document review and moved for summary judgment. That procedural posture — suit filed based on a missed deadline, followed by successive agency disclosures and withholdings — is common. It underscores a key lesson: FOIA ripeness is mechanical in the sense that the requester may initiate litigation once the statutory deadlines or administrative steps have been exhausted, but the merits will usually be litigated only after the agency has produced the record it intends to rely on.

Two corollaries are worth stressing. First, administrative exhaustion in FOIA differs from exhaustion doctrines in other statutory schemes (for example, administrative law requiring exhaustion of agency rulemaking processes): FOIA contemplates prompt judicial review once the statutory response period lapses or an adverse determination is issued. Second, requesters should preserve and document administrative steps (the date of the request, any tracking numbers, communications about extensions) because courts will focus on whether the requester diligently pursued his administrative remedy or simply rushed to court without giving the agency a chance to comply within the statutory framework.

## Summary judgment in FOIA: record-based resolution via declarations

FOIA is predominantly a paper-intensive statute; the typical case is resolved on motions for summary judgment. The reason is simple: the controlling question is almost always whether the agency’s withholding was authorized by a statutory exemption, and that question can usually be resolved by comparing the withheld documents (or accurate descriptions thereof) to the statutory exemptions. Pursuant to Rule 56, courts routinely decide FOIA cases on summary judgment, with the agency bearing the burden of proving that an exemption applies.

Because exculpatory or incriminating facts are rarely disputed in FOIA litigation, the evidentiary device for resolving the exemption question is the agency affidavit or declaration. These declarations (sometimes called Vaughn affidavits when they supply item-by-item descriptions) must provide sufficient factual and logical detail to permit meaningful judicial review. The agency’s declaration should identify the records at issue, the exemption(s) claimed, and the specific reasons why each exemption applies. It should also address segregability: whether any nonexempt material can reasonably be released and, if so, whether the agency has produced it.

Farahi illustrates both the centrality of agency declarations and the pitfalls that arise when time passes. The FBI initially supported its summary-judgment motion with a declaration that described the law enforcement nature of the records and asserted that disclosure could interfere with pending enforcement proceedings. The district court, mindful that years had elapsed since that declaration, denied summary judgment without prejudice and ordered updated declarations. The court’s approach reflects a recurring practice: courts will accept agency affidavits as the primary record for review, but they insist on contemporaneous, specific explanations and will require supplementation when the passage of time or lack of detail raises legitimate concerns.

A few doctrinal points about declarations are particularly important for students and practitioners. First, specificity matters. General, conclusory assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” or that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere” will not always suffice; courts ask for a rational connection between the asserted harms and the specific information withheld. Second, courts will often accept in camera or sealed submissions when disclosures would reveal the very secrets the agency seeks to protect — but agencies cannot rely exclusively on unreviewable assertions. Public summaries that provide enough detail for the adversary and the court, accompanied by sealed elaborations for the court’s eyes only, are the norm in cases implicating law enforcement or national security. Third, segregability is not a ritual; agencies must explain why redactions, rather than wholesale withholding, would still permit meaningful disclosure without undermining protected interests. In Farahi the FBI argued that meaningful segregable material was not reasonably separable because nonexempt material was interspersed with exempt material line-by-line, an argument the district court accepted after reviewing the agency’s sealed submissions.

## Appellate review: de novo standard and deference to agency expertise where appropriate

When a district court disposes of a FOIA case on summary judgment, appellate courts review the ruling de novo. That de novo review means that the appellate court reexamines whether the agency satisfied its statutory burden to justify withholding under a claimed exemption. The appellate court independently evaluates the sufficiency of the record, including agency declarations and any in camera materials, to determine whether the statutory criteria are met.

But de novo review does not mean a blank slate. In FOIA cases, especially ones implicating law enforcement or national security, courts recognize a constrained form of deference to agency expertise. The D.C. Circuit has long explained that the FBI and other law-enforcement agencies are entitled to “a fair measure of deference” in invoking exemptions that bear on ongoing investigations or security matters. That deference manifests in two ways. First, courts accept that agencies — by virtue of their mission — have superior access to facts about ongoing investigations, the significance of particular records, and the potential for adverse effects from disclosure. Second, courts are often willing to accept agency factual assertions (for example, that an investigation exists or that a particular disclosure would “tip off” targets) so long as the declarations are reasonably specific and nonconclusory.

Farahi exemplifies this balancing act. The D.C. Circuit applied de novo review to the district court’s grant of summary judgment, but it also emphasized that the FBI’s invocation of Exemption 7(A) merited a “fair measure of deference.” The court relied on the agency’s public and sealed declarations to conclude that the withheld materials were compiled for law enforcement purposes and that their disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings. The opinion shows how appellate courts will independently review whether the statutory elements are satisfied while simultaneously acknowledging that agencies have institutional competence that justifies relying on their specialized assessments — particularly where national security or counterterrorism are involved.

Students should internalize the practical implication: an appellate court will scrutinize whether agency declarations are detailed and timely, but it will not substitute its own judgment for the agency’s where the agency has explained the connection between the information withheld and the asserted harm. The appropriate posture is de novo review with an appreciation for agency expertise, not blind deference.

## Forfeiture and issue preservation: raising challenges to categories and segregability

A separate but recurrent procedural theme in FOIA practice concerns the preservation of issues and the consequences of forfeiture. Courts will not entertain on appeal arguments a party failed to press sufficiently in the district court. This rule applies with special force in FOIA cases, where agencies often justify withholding on multiple grounds and where the district court may resolve the dispute after in camera review or after issuing detailed rulings based on the entire record.

Two instructive decisions illuminate the point. Gallant v. NLRB articulated that when an agency withholds files in categories (rather than item-by-item), those categories must be “sufficiently distinct” to allow a court to assess the propriety of each claimed exemption. If the agency provides distinct categories, the challenger must identify how and why those categories are inadequate; a generalized attack on the categorization will often fail. In Khine v. DHS, the D.C. Circuit reiterated that parties who fail to raise specific challenges to the partitions or descriptions used by the agency forfeit those issues on appeal.

The Farahi opinion underscores the practical bite of these rules. Farahi objected on appeal to the way the FBI defined categories of withheld records, arguing they were overbroad. The court refused to entertain that argument because Farahi did not specify why the FBI’s categories were insufficient or how the agency’s designations were legally defective — he had forfeited the point by not developing it adequately below. The lesson is clear: litigants must press specific, developed arguments in the district court about the sufficiency of categories, the content of the Vaughn index, and segregability; merely asserting in general terms that categories are broad or that segregable material must exist is unlikely to preserve an appellate claim.

For practitioners, the takeaway is tactical: early in the district-court litigation, force the agency to explain categories and to produce a detailed Vaughn index or to allow in camera review. If the agency resists, crystallize objections on the record. On appeal, courts will look to whether the challenger presented a meaningful, focused challenge below.

## Protective orders, sealed filings, and structuring the record to permit meaningful review

FOIA’s twin goals — transparency and protection of legitimately secret information — often collide. The procedural response is a menu of tools: public declarations that summarize key facts, Vaughn indices that describe records with enough specificity to permit review, sealed or ex parte declarations that provide the detailed factual foundation the agency cannot disclose publicly, and in camera review as a safety valve. Courts have developed practices intended to balance the requester’s right to a fair adversarial process against the agency’s interest in preventing disclosure.

Farahi presents a prototypical posture. The FBI filed both public and ex parte sealed declarations. The district court allowed the ex parte filings; Farahi did not oppose the agency’s motion for sealed and ex parte submissions. The district court relied on the sealed declarations in concluding that the records were not reasonably segregable and that Exemption 7(A) applied. On appeal the D.C. Circuit stressed that while ex parte filings impose a handicap on requesters, they are sometimes necessary to permit the court to decide the dispute without disclosing the very material sought to be kept secret. The court also noted that the requester’s failure to object to ex parte submissions undermined his later challenge to the court’s reliance on them.

Several practical rules emerge. First, when an agency moves to file ex parte or sealed declarations, a requester should carefully consider whether to consent; if the requester objects, the district court must decide whether the public interests favor an in camera or public resolution. Second, agencies should, when feasible, provide a public declaration that summarizes the basis for withholding without compromising security; the public declaration facilitates meaningful adversarial testing and appellate review. Third, Vaughn indices or categorical descriptions should be sufficiently detailed and organized to allow the court to perform its function; courts will sometimes demand better indexing or invite in camera review when the public record is inadequate. Finally, when national security or ongoing investigations are implicated, sealed submissions and in camera review are routine; but the agency still bears the burden to justify secrecy, and courts will scrutinize the sealed materials themselves.

## Remedies and remands: when courts order refreshed or more detailed submissions

When agency submissions are stale, conclusory, or otherwise insufficient, courts frequently do not proceed to summary judgment in the agency’s favor; instead they deny the motion without prejudice or remand for further agency action or supplementation. Remedies available to courts include ordering the agency to produce refreshed declarations, to provide a more detailed Vaughn index, to disclose segregable material, or to submit documents for in camera review. Courts sometimes enter partial orders — permitting disclosure of redacted records while preserving certain materials — or they may order further fact-finding about whether proceedings are “pending or reasonably anticipated” for purposes of Exemption 7(A).

Farahi offers a good map of the remedial landscape. The district court initially denied the FBI’s summary-judgment motion without prejudice, explicitly because the prior declarations were old and the court needed updated information to determine whether enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. The FBI supplied supplemental public and sealed declarations, and the district court then granted summary judgment in the agency’s favor. On appeal the D.C. Circuit affirmed, explaining that courts commonly require updated declarations when significant time has passed and that a short, contemporaneous declaration can be understood as an update to an earlier detailed affidavit rather than as a new, stand-alone justification.

A few practical rules flow from these outcomes. First, staleness is a real procedural defect; agencies must be prepared to update declarations that are several years old. Second, a district court’s remedial posture often depends on the litigation history: a court may grant an agency leave to supplement its record and then reassess; it may also order disclosure of segregable portions if the agency cannot carry its burden. Third, the scope of a remand or order for supplementation can vary widely; sometimes courts seek only a brief confirmation that nothing has changed, while at other times they demand a fuller, contemporaneous exposition of the factual bases for the claimed exemptions. Finally, the prospect of remand counsels practitioners to think ahead: agencies should assemble contemporaneous records about the status of investigations, and requesters should consider asking for a narrow set of declaratory rulings or targeted discovery if an agency’s justifications appear facially inadequate.

Conclusion

FOIA litigation is governed by a distinctive procedural architecture that channels disputes into summary-judgment proceedings, sharpens the debate around agency declarations and segregability, and requires litigants to preserve and press specific arguments at the district court level. Farahi v. FBI illustrates many of these features: the typical motion-practice arc (initial agency declarations, a district court’s demand for updated information, submission of sealed and public declarations, and appellate de novo review tempered by deference) and the practical consequences of failing to preserve particular arguments. Mastery of FOIA procedure therefore requires attention to timing and exhaustion rules, careful construction and contestation of agency declarations, strategic choices about sealed filings, and vigilant issue preservation to avoid forfeiture on appeal.

# Case Study: Farahi v. FBI as a Vehicle for Exemption 7(A)

This case study uses Farahi v. FBI to teach how courts evaluate Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), 5 U.S.C. § 552(b)(7)(A). Exemption 7(A) permits an agency to withhold records “compiled for law enforcement purposes” if disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings.” The opinion illustrates: (1) the evidentiary showings an agency must make to satisfy the “compiled for law enforcement” and “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirements; (2) how courts treat public and ex parte (sealed) declarations, including the risks and benefits of ex parte submissions; (3) the scope and limits of the “tipping off” interference rationale; and (4) the practice and doctrine governing segregability and categorical withholdings. The case also shows how procedural posture—district court requests for updated declarations and appellate de novo review—shapes the litigation. The following sections walk through these topics, tie them to governing precedent, and explain how to apply the principles to new fact patterns.

## Factual background: FOIA request for an FBI file, immigration context, and counterterrorism allegations

Farahi is an Iranian national who, after years of immigration litigation, sought production of his FBI file by FOIA. The factual backdrop matters: DHS relied on evidence and testimony from an FBI agent in immigration removal proceedings that implicated Farahi in ties to individuals and organizations associated with terrorism. The FBI located roughly 10,750 pages and many CDs as potentially responsive. Over a multi-year review, the FBI released some material but withheld the bulk of the records under FOIA exemptions, principally Exemption 7(A).

These facts are important because Exemption 7(A) only applies to records “compiled for law enforcement purposes,” and the FBI’s asserted national-security and counterterrorism investigation creates the type of law-enforcement context in which courts traditionally afford the FBI deference. The immigration context also demonstrates how records compiled for one law-enforcement purpose (immigration enforcement) may be intertwined with broader counterterrorism investigations. For a student applying the law, the key lesson is that the underlying factual link between the subject and a suspected or investigated violation of law supplies the “nexus” courts require under Pratt and Campbell to treat the records as law-enforcement materials.

## The record: initial Hardy declarations (public and ex parte), district court’s staleness concern, and Bender’s updates

The agency supported its initial summary-judgment motion with a public declaration from David Hardy (RIDS Section Chief) and an ex parte, sealed Hardy declaration that provided fuller detail. The district court denied the FBI’s initial motion without prejudice, not because Exemption 7(A) was unavailable in principle, but because the court—sensitive to the passage of time—required the FBI to update its assessment that enforcement proceedings remained pending or reasonably anticipated. The court also said it needed more information to resolve segregability but could not state the reasons on the public record. The government then furnished updated public and sealed declarations from Joseph Bender (Acting RIDS Section Chief) to confirm that investigations remained “ongoing” and to elaborate on segregability. The district court accepted those submissions and granted summary judgment; the D.C. Circuit affirmed.

Two lessons emerge from this posture. First, courts may treat older agency declarations as stale if a long interval has elapsed between the declarations and judicial decision or appeal; agencies should refresh declarations. Second, agencies commonly use a two-tiered approach: a publicly filed declaration that gives general grounds for withholding, and a sealed ex parte declaration that supplies sensitive specifics the agency cannot disclose publicly. When a plaintiff does not oppose use of ex parte declarations, courts may rely heavily on them—particularly in national-security or sensitive law-enforcement contexts—although that reliance can limit the plaintiff’s ability to rebut the agency on the public record.

For practitioners, these facts teach a tactical rule: when the underlying investigations are protracted, agencies should anticipate judicial orders to update declarations; FOIA requesters should press for specificity and resist secrecy only when they can identify public indicators of staleness or inadequacy.

## Threshold met: compiled for law enforcement purposes under Pratt/Campbell

Exemption 7(A) is available only for records “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” The D.C. Circuit’s cases (Pratt and Campbell) require a demonstrable nexus between the records and the agency’s law-enforcement duties—i.e., that the records were created “in furtherance of” a law-enforcement investigation into suspected violations of law or threats to security. In Campbell, the court emphasized deference to the FBI’s determination because the FBI is a law-enforcement agency; but that deference is not limitless. The agency must supply a rational explanation tying the documents to investigatory duties and to a person or incident that plausibly implicates a law-enforcement interest.

In Farahi the FBI met this threshold: the public declarations described the records as compiled “in furtherance of investigations of violations of national security and international terrorism,” and the sealed declaration supplied substantive detail about the investigation. The court also relied on the broader record—Farahi’s own admissions in immigration proceedings that he had been investigated for ties to designated terrorist organizations and individuals convicted of terrorism-related offenses. Together, those facts establish the required nexus: the documents were not mundane administrative materials but part of an investigation into potential criminal or national-security violations.

To apply this standard in new fact patterns, ask whether the document’s purpose or provenance is investigatory (e.g., created by agents to gather evidence, reports about suspicious conduct, or intelligence summaries), and whether the agency explains the connection to its enforcement responsibilities. If the documents are plainly administrative (contract, personnel records unrelated to investigation), Exemption 7(A) will likely fail. Conversely, records generated as part of an agent’s investigatory activity will usually meet the threshold, especially when the agency furnishes concrete factual connectors.

## Pending or reasonably anticipated proceedings: combining public and ex parte detail; addressing CREW’s caution on vagueness

Exemption 7(A) applies only where disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated.” CREW clarified that the government may establish this by showing the existence of an ongoing investigation, but cautioned against sustained, vague assertions that an investigation is “ongoing” without factual support. CREW warned that a bare or conclusory claim of an ongoing investigation may be insufficient.

Farahi shows how courts reconcile CREW’s caution with the practical limits of public disclosure. The FBI’s public Hardy declaration attested to an ongoing investigation and gave general reasons why disclosure would interfere; the sealed Hardy declaration provided the explanatory detail the public declaration lacked. When the district court later asked for an update because of the passage of time, the FBI’s Bender declaration confirmed the continuing pendency. The D.C. Circuit recognized the danger of treating a terse, standalone statement that an investigation “remains ongoing” as adequate under CREW, but it held that the Bender declaration need not be evaluated in a vacuum: if earlier (and contemporaneous sealed) submissions already established the scope and existence of an ongoing investigation, an update showing no change can be relatively concise.

For readers, the practical rule is this: (1) CREW requires more than a conclusory formula; (2) agencies can meet CREW by combining public attestations with ex parte disclosures that give the court the needed factual predicate; and (3) when a court requires an update, a short confirmatory declaration may suffice if the earlier record already provided the substantive detail. When applying these principles to new facts, consider the temporal gap between the agency’s showing and the court’s decision—long gaps will attract closer scrutiny—and evaluate whether the administrative record includes facts (even in sealed form) establishing a realistic prospect of enforcement proceedings.

## Interference rationale: tipping off targets and revealing scope of ongoing investigations

One paradigmatic reason why disclosure of law-enforcement records can interfere with proceedings is that it would “tip off” targets, allowing them to evade law enforcement, destroy evidence, or adapt their behavior. Courts frequently accept “tipping off” as a legitimate interference rationale in Exemption 7(A) cases, and Farahi reiterates that point. The public Hardy declaration explained that disclosure could reveal the scope of the investigation and tip off subjects; the sealed declarations provided further context making that explanation credible.

To apply this rationale to new situations, evaluate the content of the records and the nature of the investigation. Disclosure is most likely to interfere when the records reveal investigatory targets, lines of inquiry, investigative techniques, timing, or any details that would enable suspects to modify their conduct. Conversely, disclosure of purely historical summaries of concluded investigations, where no follow-on enforcement action is reasonably anticipated, is less likely to raise valid “tipping off” concerns. The court in Farahi accepted the FBI’s claim that the investigation remained broad and prospective in nature (including possible further prosecutions or intelligence actions), and so “tipping off” remained a realistic harm.

Importantly, the government must anchor the “tipping off” assertion in facts—either public facts or sealed explanatory material—so that the court can determine whether interference is reasonably expected. CREW warns against rubber-stamp claims; Farahi shows how sufficiently detailed sealed declarations can satisfy the court.

## Categorical withholdings and the requester’s forfeiture of category-specific challenges

When agencies withhold records by category (e.g., “investigative reports,” “interview memoranda,” “attachments”), courts require the categories to be “sufficiently distinct” so that the court can determine whether the claimed exemptions properly apply to each category. Cases such as Gallant and CREW articulate that principle. In Farahi, the FBI identified categories and justified withholding across them. Farahi challenged the breadth of those categories on appeal but did not articulate specific arguments about particular categories in a way that allowed the court to evaluate overbreadth. The D.C. Circuit therefore treated those arguments as forfeited under Khine.

The doctrinal lesson is double: (1) agencies should draft categories with clarity—each category should be intelligible and tied to a plausible exemption rationale—so the court can test the withholding; and (2) requesters must preserve and press specific category-by-category challenges with record evidence or reasoned legal argument. A generalized complaint that categories are “overbroad” is unlikely to prevail if the requester never shows why a specific category includes non-exempt material or how the agency’s rationale fails for a subset of documents. For students and litigators, the practical takeaway is to be granular: if you represent a FOIA plaintiff, identify specific records or categories that appear non-exempt and explain why; if you represent the agency, be prepared to show how each category maps to exemption criteria.

## Segregability: reliance on ex parte submissions and the “meaningful information” standard

Even when Exemption 7(A) covers a record, FOIA requires disclosure of any “reasonably segregable” non-exempt portions. The governing standard (Mead Data and later cases) is pragmatic: courts require that agencies disclose “meaningful information,” not isolated words or trivial items, and they may accept agency affidavits that explain why the non-exempt material is inextricably intertwined with exempt material. Farahi reiterates that if non-exempt material is scattered line-by-line throughout documents, removing it may not be “reasonably segregable” because the resulting redactions would not yield meaningful information and could themselves risk disclosure of sensitive context.

In Farahi the district court relied on Bender’s sealed ex parte declaration to conclude that the agency had satisfied its segregability obligation. The court was permitted to do so: a sealed declaration can describe, to the judge, why any non-exempt text is either trivial or so interwoven with exempt material that redaction would either be infeasible or would itself disclose protected information. The D.C. Circuit emphasized that a party who does not oppose ex parte filings cannot complain on appeal that the district court relied on sealed material. The court also explained that ex parte submissions are sometimes the only way to evaluate claims without revealing the very information the agency seeks to protect.

For students, the key principles for segregability analysis are: (1) the agency bears the burden to show what is reasonably segregable; (2) it may meet that burden with particularized affidavits, including sealed material where necessary; (3) courts assess whether the disclosed portion(s) would be “meaningful” to the requester; and (4) where non-exempt bits are intimately bound up with exempt content, courts may accept that segregability is not practicable. Practically, litigants should identify specific examples of allegedly non-exempt material, and agencies should provide concrete explanations—preferably examples in camera if publicly disclosable descriptions would compromise interests—showing why redactions would not meaningfully inform the public or would risk harm.

## Appellate posture: de novo review, deference in national security, and affirmance

FOIA summary-judgment decisions are reviewed de novo on appeal. The agency bears the burden of proving that the exemptions apply. Nonetheless, the D.C. Circuit has explained that courts should afford a “fair measure of deference” to the FBI in evaluating Exemption 7 claims because the FBI specializes in law enforcement; that deference is especially pronounced in national-security cases. Cases such as Campbell and CNSS reflect that doctrine: the court will not substitute its evaluation of investigative priorities for the FBI’s, but the agency must still supply sufficiently detailed factual predicates in its declarations.

Farahi typifies this balance. The D.C. Circuit reviewed de novo but accepted the district court’s in camera reliance on sealed declarations and credited the agency’s public and ex parte attestations as establishing both the law-enforcement nexus and the pendency of enforcement proceedings. The court also noted that the district court, after reconsidering due to time lapse, had required and received updated declarations. The appellate court affirmed, emphasizing both the agency’s burden and the permissible deference to agency expertise—especially where national-security implications justify limited public disclosure.

For litigation strategy, understand that appellate review will examine the sufficiency of the agency’s factual assertions and the district court’s explanation for relying on sealed material, but the court will not require the agency to publicly produce the very details that the agency says are sensitive. Practitioners should prepare record-level support (including in camera material if necessary) to withstand de novo appellate scrutiny.

## Practical lessons and application to new fact patterns

Farahi offers several practice-oriented rules students and practitioners should internalize:

- Timeliness and updates matter: courts may treat older declarations as stale and require updates. When a FOIA case lingers, agencies should refresh factual attestations; requesters should press for updates if they suspect a change in enforcement posture.

- Use of sealed declarations is permissible and often necessary in national-security or ongoing-investigation contexts, but reliance on sealed material reduces the plaintiff’s opportunities to rebut claims. Parties should anticipate that and plan accordingly.

- Specificity is required: although courts afford deference to the FBI, that deference is not blanket. Agencies should supply a factual nexus to law enforcement and an explanation of how disclosure would interfere with specific or plausible future proceedings.

- Segregability requires meaningful disclosure: agencies must disclose non-exempt information that is reasonably segregable in a way that yields meaningful content; trivial excision of single words is not sufficient.

- Categorical withholdings must be intelligible: an agency’s categories should be discrete and explained; requesters should press specific, targeted challenges instead of general assertions of overbreadth.

When applying these lessons to new facts, structure your analysis around the statutory elements and the relevant precedents: first determine whether the records were compiled for law-enforcement purposes (Pratt/Campbell); then ask whether the government has shown the existence of pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings (CREW/Juarez); next examine the asserted interference rationale (e.g., tipping off), looking for factual support; then test the agency’s segregability showing (Mead Data and offspring); and finally evaluate whether the agency’s categorical descriptions permit meaningful judicial review. If an agency relies on sealed material, courts will often review that material in camera, but the party challenging the withholding should be prepared to point to public facts that undermine the agency’s claims or to identify discrete categories or documents that appear non-exempt.

Conclusion

Farahi v. FBI illustrates how Exemption 7(A) operates in the context of counterterrorism investigations: courts are willing to withhold large volumes of investigative material where the agency supplies a factual nexus to law enforcement, establishes the existence of ongoing or reasonably anticipated proceedings, and explains why non-exempt material is not reasonably segregable. The case also exemplifies the procedural dynamics of FOIA litigation—staleness concerns, updated declarations, and the judicious use of sealed ex parte filings—and confirms the practical and doctrinal boundary between judicial review and deference to national-security and law-enforcement expertise.

# Comparative Doctrine: Positioning Farahi Among Leading Cases

This chapter places the D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI (Sept. 30, 2025) in conversation with the leading precedents that shape Exemption 7(A) practice. Farahi presents a useful syntheses of several distinct doctrinal threads: the requirement that withholdings protect “pending or reasonably anticipated” enforcement proceedings; the degree of factual particularity courts expect from agency affidavits; the deference afforded to the FBI (especially in national-security contexts); the scope of what counts as a law‑enforcement “proceeding” and an investigation “compiled for law enforcement purposes”; and the practical limits of segregation and the permissibility of ex parte submissions. Each of the following subsections analyzes a doctrinal strand, explains the governing test or rule, shows how Farahi applied it, and draws practical lessons for litigants and courts.

## Robbins Tire: Supreme Court’s approval of categorical 7(A) withholdings during active enforcement

A recurrent theme in Exemption 7(A) jurisprudence is that when enforcement proceedings are active, courts will often accept agency claims that disclosure would interfere with the government’s efforts. Farahi fits squarely within that posture: the D.C. Circuit affirmed the district court’s conclusion that the FBI had shown ongoing investigations and that disclosure could “tip off” targets and thereby interfere with law‑enforcement efforts. The opinion treats the FBI’s categorical withholding (with thousands of pages withheld) as consistent with the statute once the agency establishes the nexus to a pending or reasonably anticipated proceeding and the harm that disclosure could cause.

Although the modern Exemption 7(A) regime is shaped mostly by D.C. Circuit precedents, the Supreme Court’s decisions over time have provided general acceptance of deference to executive assertions of interference when enforcement is active. The practical consequence is that categorical withholding of investigative material is permissible when the court is satisfied that a hostile disclosure would prejudice an ongoing or imminent enforcement effort. Farahi illustrates this point: the court relied on the agency’s showings — both public and ex parte — that an investigation remained ongoing and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to reveal investigative tactics and targets.

For practitioners, the immediate lesson is strategic: when enforcement is genuinely active, agencies should make a clear, specific, and contemporaneous factual showing tying the records to ongoing investigative work and describing the sort of interference release would cause. Plaintiffs facing categorical withholdings during active enforcement must press the district court to require updated, non‑conclusory factual statements and should seek arguments that particular records are demonstrably unrelated to enforcement activity.

## CREW v. DOJ: “Pending or reasonably anticipated” and policing conclusory affidavits

CREW v. DOJ articulated a core constraint on Exemption 7(A): the enforcement proceedings that an agency invokes must be “pending or reasonably anticipated.” That condition prevents agencies from invoking the exemption to protect investigations that are merely hypothetical or long dead. Importantly, CREW also emphasized that affirmative statements that an investigation is “ongoing” cannot be purely conclusory; the agency’s affidavits must supply enough concrete fact to permit the court to assess whether the investigation is truly pending or reasonably anticipated.

Farahi applies CREW’s dual commands in a layered way. First, the D.C. Circuit reiterates the CREW gloss that 7(A) requires a pending or reasonably anticipated proceeding. Second, Farahi confronts the common posture identified in CREW: with the passage of time, an agency’s prior declaration that an investigation exists may no longer suffice. That is exactly what the district court recognized when it denied the FBI’s initial summary‑judgment motion and asked for updated declarations. The appellate court upheld that approach: it accepted that an earlier affidavit could show the existence of a proceeding, but it also endorsed the district court’s insistence on contemporaneous confirmation when significant time has passed. Farahi thus demonstrates CREW’s practical operation: courts will not blindly accept stale boilerplate; they will require supplementation where needed to bridge the temporal gap.

This is important for litigants. Plaintiffs should invoke CREW to demand timely and specific affidavits when the agency’s original declarations are old; courts should demand more than ritualistic phrases. Agencies, in turn, should anticipate CREW by calibrating their declarations to show contemporaneity — for example, by confirming that the investigation continues, identifying the investigative unit or agent responsible, and, where possible without compromising operations, describing the current investigative posture or likely enforcement avenues.

## Juarez v. DOJ: Ongoing investigations as “proceedings” and the harm of premature release

Juarez clarifies another doctrinal point foundational to Farahi: an ongoing criminal investigation itself counts as an Exemption 7(A) “proceeding.” The logic is pragmatic — an investigation that is actively gathering evidence for a potential criminal case can be prejudiced by premature disclosure of either the fact of the investigation or details that would reveal its scope and methods. Juarez emphasized that Exemption 7(A) protects investigations so long as they are in the phase of collecting evidence that could fuel a future prosecution.

Farahi repeatedly leans on this Juarez principle. The D.C. Circuit there and in Farahi recognizes that an agency need not wait until charges are filed; it need only show that the investigative work remains ongoing and that disclosure would meaningfully impair that work — for instance, by revealing investigative targets, methods, or lines of inquiry and thereby giving subjects the opportunity to evade detection, destroy evidence, or coordinate false stories.

The doctrinal takeaway is straightforward: courts will treat active evidence‑gathering as a “proceeding” that can be reasonably anticipated to lead to enforcement, and an agency’s showing need only connect disclosure to the reasonable probability of interference, not prove that disclosure will end a prosecution. Practically, agencies should explain how particular categories of information (e.g., contact lists, interview notes, contemporaneous summaries, or technical collection methods) would permit targets to neutralize investigative steps. Plaintiffs should try to demonstrate — through temporal facts, public record, or other evidence — that the relevant investigative activity has ceased or that specific withheld documents are tangential to any ongoing inquiry.

## Pratt, Campbell, Clemente: Law‑enforcement purpose and judicial deference to the FBI

The first gate in Exemption 7(A) analysis is whether the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” Pratt established the connective requirement: there must be a rational nexus between the records and an agency’s law‑enforcement duties, and a link between the individual or incident and a potential law‑enforcement risk. Campbell and Clemente reaffirm this principle and articulate the proper level of judicial deference, particularly to the FBI. Because the FBI is manifestly a law‑enforcement agency and especially because of its national‑security role, courts generally afford its declarations a “fair measure of deference” in determining the law‑enforcement purpose of records.

Farahi demonstrates the practical reach of these precedents. The court accepted the FBI’s initial public declaration (Hardy) that tied the records to investigations of national security and terrorism, and it treated Farahi’s own concessions about past investigative allegations as corroborative. The D.C. Circuit therefore concludes comfortably that the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes, appropriately deferring to the FBI’s institutional role and the particular content of its supporting declarations.

For law students, the doctrinal rule is twofold: (1) agencies must show a rational nexus between the records and law‑enforcement duties, but (2) courts will give weight to an agency’s representations — especially to the FBI’s. In practice, an effective agency declaration will describe the investigative context (who is investigating, whether it is tied to a terrorism or national‑security matter, whether it supports removal, criminal, or intelligence aims) and show how the records serve investigative functions. Plaintiffs can try to rebut the presumed nexus by pointing to records that are administrative, historical, or unrelated to any enforcement aim.

## Mead Data and Perioperative Services: Segregability’s limits and ex parte reliance

Mead Data long ago established that an agency must release “any reasonably segregable portion” of a withheld record; but it also recognized practical limits. When meaningful nonexempt information is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material — for example, when nonexempt material is interspersed line‑by‑line — segregation may be impracticable and the agency may permissibly withhold entire documents. Perioperative Services (a recent D.C. Circuit decision) reiterates Mead Data’s core point while applying it to modern, voluminous records that, if redacted line‑by‑line, would still reveal the substance of an investigation.

Farahi applies these cases to the facts at hand. The district court — and the D.C. Circuit on review — found that the FBI’s ex parte supplemental declarations adequately explained why the withheld material was not reasonably segregable. The court cited Mead Data’s observation that FOIA is about substantive information and not merely words on a page: the presence of an occasional nonexempt word or phrase scattered through the record does not require public disclosure if revealing those words would itself disclose the exempt information in context. Perioperative reinforced that ex parte submissions may be the only practical way to show the context and interconnection of sensitive material without revealing that material in open court.

The practical lesson is two‑part. First, agencies should be prepared to show with specificity why redaction cannot strip out exempt portions without destroying the informative content of the records. That often requires tailored, detailed affidavits that describe the documents’ structure and how the redaction of discrete bits would disclose the underlying exempt information. Second, courts may accept ex parte, in camera submissions to evaluate segregability; plaintiffs who do not oppose the filing of sealed affidavits risk forfeiting the ability to complain on appeal about courts’ reliance on those materials. Farahi underscores both points: the FBI’s sealed declarations supplied necessary detail, and Farahi’s failure to oppose ex parte filings limited his appellate arguments.

## Gallant and category specificity: crafting categories that survive judicial scrutiny

When an agency withholds records on a category‑by‑category basis, Gallant requires the categories to be “sufficiently distinct” so that a court can determine whether each category’s asserted exemption properly applies. Vague, overbroad, or ill‑defined categories make meaningful judicial review impossible and risk reversal. The requirement is not merely formal; it serves the core FOIA objective of accountability by enabling judicial testing of an agency’s exemption claims.

Farahi highlights how courts police category specificity — but also how plaintiffs can lose the point in practice. The D.C. Circuit noted Farahi’s objection that the FBI had not adequately justified its categories, but held that Farahi forfeited the claim by failing to develop a concrete showing of overbreadth in the district court. The appellate court therefore treated the FBI’s categorical descriptions as adequate in the absence of a specific challenge. In short, Gallant remains an important constraint, but plaintiffs must press it concretely and early, or they may be held to have waived it.

For practitioners: agencies should label categories precisely (e.g., “raw investigative leads from confidential human sources,” “agent interview notes re: individual X,” “communications intercept metadata,” etc.) and explain why each category is exempt and why nonexempt material is not reasonably segregable within that category. Plaintiffs should challenge category definitions in the district court with particularity — pointing to sample pages, describing why categories sweep too broadly, or showing why specific records do not fit the asserted category — or else risk forfeiture on appeal, as Farahi demonstrates.

Conclusion: what Farahi teaches about doctrinal synthesis

Farahi is in many respects a paradigmatic Exemption 7(A) decision for our era. It synthesizes longstanding D.C. Circuit tests (Pratt/Campbell/Clemente on law‑enforcement purpose; Juarez and CREW on what counts as a pending or reasonably anticipated proceeding and what level of specificity an agency must provide) with Mead Data’s and Perioperative’s pragmatic commonsense on segregability and Gallant’s demand for categorical clarity. It also reiterates the pragmatic reality that, in national‑security and counterterrorism contexts, courts give “a fair measure of deference” to FBI affidavits while insisting that that deference be exercised on the basis of non‑conclusory, contemporaneous facts when time has passed.

As a doctrinal guide for future litigants and courts, Farahi encourages three habits: (1) agencies should produce contemporaneous, specific declarations that tie withheld records to active investigative tasks and explain the concrete harms of disclosure; (2) courts should require updating of stale affidavits and use ex parte submissions carefully to evaluate both harm and segregability without exposing the very materials that would defeat the exemption; and (3) plaintiffs should press early and specifically on category definitions and segregability, and should object to ex parte procedures where appropriate, because failure to do so can foreclose appellate relief.

# Practitioner’s Toolkit: Litigating and Resisting 7(A) Claims

This chapter uses Farahi v. FBI (D.C. Cir. 2025) as a teaching vehicle to show how Exemption 7(A) disputes are litigated in practice and to give concrete strategies for both requesters and agencies. The opinion shows recurring themes in 7(A) litigation: the government’s need to show records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” that are “pending or reasonably anticipated,” the court’s expectation that agencies update stale declarations, the role of ex parte/sealed submissions, and how courts evaluate segregability. Below are doctrinal rules framed as practical guidance, with examples and tactics grounded in Farahi and leading D.C. Circuit precedent (CREW, Juarez, CNSS, Campbell, Mead, Gallant, Khine).

## Requester strategies: demanding specificity, attacking overbroad categories, and leveraging staleness for updates

The requester’s fundamental task in a 7(A) case is to force the government to carry its burden with tangible specificity and to exploit any gaps—particularly gaps created by stale or conclusory declarations.

- Demand a particularized administrative record and a Vaughn-style disclosure. Do not let the government proceed with only a single conclusory affidavit. Ask for a Vaughn index (or an equivalent itemized log) describing withheld records by date, type, custodian, and exemption(s) claimed. CREW and Gallant require categories to be “sufficiently distinct” so a court can properly review the claimed exemptions; insist on that granularity early. In Farahi, the FBI supplied multiple declarations and ex parte material; Farahi’s failure to press specific category-based objections led to forfeiture. To avoid that, make concrete objections: identify particular categories you contend are overbroad and explain why.

- Attack conclusory or stale declarations. When a government declaration says an investigation is “ongoing” but provides no details, argue it is conclusory under CREW. Use the passage of time to demand updated declarations: move the district court to order refreshed attestations if significant time elapses between the declaration and the court’s decision. Farahi shows that courts often require updated assessments when there is delay—seek that remedy routinely. If the agency declines to update voluntarily, move to compel. Your motion should explain why the original declaration cannot answer whether enforcement proceedings remain “pending or reasonably anticipated” given intervening events or delay.

- Force detail about “interference.” The government must explain how disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” — examples include tipping off targets to investigative scope, revealing techniques or sources, or compromising cooperation. Ask for examples from the agency. Where public declarations are thin, contend they are insufficient unless supplemented by specific factual statements about investigative steps that would be compromised. In Farahi, the Hardy and ex parte declarations together supplied such detail; a requester should press to break that combination apart and force as much public specificity as possible.

- Narrow your request strategically. If the responsive set is enormous (e.g., 10,750 pages and 80 CDs in Farahi), propose narrower, staged requests targeted at categories less likely to implicate 7(A) (personal identifying records, immigration adjudicative documents, internal administrative communications unrelated to investigative strategy). Show the court that the agency could reasonably segregate and produce those narrower categories. This both reduces the government’s ability to hide behind voluminous records and focuses the litigation on discrete issues.

- Use procedural levers. Move promptly for summary judgment after the administrative record is closed; insist on a Vaughn index before summary judgment; file a motion to compel more adequate affidavits; and, if warranted, seek in camera review of withheld records or the ex parte declaration (discussed below). A requester should also propose protective orders or counsel-only review (including clearance-based access) as a way to let the court balance secrecy and adversarial testing.

- Preserve objections. If you intend to challenge the agency’s categorization or segregability, raise those objections specifically in the district court and on summary judgment. The D.C. Circuit enforces forfeiture rules (Khine), so ambiguous or belated objections are likely to be rejected. Spell out with citations to the record why a category is overbroad or why segregability is feasible; when possible supply redaction proposals to show practicality.

In short: press for specificity; use delays to force updates; narrow requests if practicable; and make specific, preserved arguments on categories and segregability.

## Agency strategies: drafting detailed but non‑prejudicial declarations and using categorical Vaughn submissions

Agencies must simultaneously be candid enough to meet the court’s burden‑of‑proof requirements while protecting information that would itself create the harm FOIA exemptions are meant to prevent.

- Structure declarations to establish the three core elements. For Exemption 7(A) the agency should: (1) show the records were “compiled for law enforcement purposes” (establish nexus to agency duty and to a possible violation); (2) explain how disclosure “could reasonably be expected to interfere with enforcement proceedings” (identify the nature of harm — e.g., tipping off targets, compromising sources); and (3) if the original declarations are old, provide contemporaneous updates confirming that the risk persists. The D.C. Circuit gives the FBI particular deference in law‑enforcement contexts (Campbell), but the agency still has the evidentiary burden. Farahi illustrates a successful approach: an initial Hardy declaration establishing the investigative nexus and an ex parte supplement providing detail, followed by an update to address staleness concerns.

- Use ex parte sealed submissions when necessary — but prepare to justify them. An agency may file ex parte declarations that give a court more detail than the public record can safely bear; Farahi’s outcome shows such filings are often decisive. However, filing ex parte material invites criticism that the plaintiff cannot test the claim. To reduce that risk, the agency should: (a) provide a thorough public declaration that establishes the basic elements; (b) make the ex parte declaration a carefully confined supplement that the court can use to resolve doubts; and (c) explain in the public record why the more detailed content must remain sealed. Courts will accept ex parte submissions when disclosure of their contents would itself cause the harm asserted (Perioperative Servs.; Farahi).

- Draft the public declaration with enough specificity to survive initial scrutiny. Even if the agency intends to rely on sealed material, the public declaration should: identify the investigation’s general subject matter (without revealing sources or techniques), assert with factual support that the investigation is ongoing or reasonably anticipated, describe in general terms the types of records at issue, and explain why disclosure would prejudice enforcement (e.g., by alerting targets, revealing investigative scope, or compromising cooperation). Saying only “ongoing” may not be enough if the declaration is old or unsupported.

- Use categorical Vaughn indices prudently. When there are thousands of pages, item‑by‑item descriptions are often impractical. Courts accept categorical descriptions when categories are “sufficiently distinct” to permit meaningful judicial review (CREW, Gallant). Choose categories that map neatly to exemptions (e.g., “correspondence from overseas source regarding fundraising” vs. “agent reports reflecting investigative steps and operational assessments”), provide representative examples (redacted or in camera), and explain why segregability of categories is infeasible (e.g., exempt material is interspersed line-by-line). The agency’s categorical approach is most credible if accompanied by a sworn statement that catalogues search and review methods and confirms the impossibility or impracticality of piecemeal redactions.

- Anticipate the court’s concern about staleness. If significant time may elapse between declaration and decision, the agency should plan to provide a short update (a paragraph or two) confirming that the factual predicate (e.g., ongoing investigation, pending grand jury) remains unchanged. Farahi demonstrates that an update like Bender’s — short but tied to an earlier, fuller declaration — can be sufficient when the earlier declaration contains the details.

- Show segregability analysis. The agency must explain with reasonable specificity why nonexempt portions are not reasonably segregable. Practical points to include: where exempt and nonexempt material are line-interspersed, why electronic redaction would reveal context and thereby harm the investigation; the technical infeasibility of redacting (especially in audio/video or scanned images); and why release of discrete excerpts would itself create the harm. The Mead rule that words “interspersed line-by-line” are often not reasonably segregable is a useful doctrinal anchor; but agencies should demonstrate that conclusion with examples, not merely assertion.

In short: prepare layered submissions — a robust public declaration, carefully tailored ex parte supplement if needed, and categorical Vaughn tied to clear, evidence-backed explanations of segregability.

## When and how to seek or oppose ex parte/in camera review

Ex parte and in camera review are powerful but sensitive tools. Courts allow sealed in camera review of sensitive records and sealed declarations when necessary to resolve FOIA disputes without public disclosure. But counsel must use these devices thoughtfully.

- When requesters should ask for in camera review. If an agency submits an ex parte declaration and the public record is inadequate, requesters should move for limited in camera review of either (a) the withheld records themselves or (b) the sealed declaration. This is most appropriate when the public declarations are conclusory and “update” mechanisms would be insufficient, or when the requester has offered a protective order that would allow limited disclosure to cleared counsel. Request an in camera review as narrowly as possible: ask the court to inspect a sampling of withheld records or to review the sealed declaration to determine whether the agency’s public justifications are pretextual. Be prepared to justify the request: explain the insufficiency of the public showing and propose precise bounds for the in camera inquiry.

- When requesters should oppose ex parte sealed filings. If the government seeks to rely on sealed, ex parte declarations to defeat disclosure, challengers should argue that the public record must before the court contain enough information to permit adversarial testing of 7(A) claims. Ask the court to require the government to provide a public summary of the sealed material that is sufficiently detailed to allow meaningful review. If the government resists, press for in camera review. But beware: courts often accept ex parte filings where public disclosure would itself cause the harms invoked — Farahi accepted ex parte material when it was necessary and appropriately restricted.

- When agencies should seek sealed submissions. Agencies should use sealed submissions when public descriptions would reveal operational details, identities of confidential sources, or investigative techniques that would themselves undermine enforcement. If such a submission is necessary, craft a public baseline showing the general contours of the investigation, and then explain why the sealed submission is essential to resolve the dispute. Anticipate the judge will inspect the sealed submission to confirm the public account; therefore the sealed submission must contain factual specifics that justify the claimed exemptions.

- How courts tend to allocate risk. Courts in this circuit accept that ex parte submissions handicap a plaintiff, but they have sanctioned their use where there is no workable alternative to resolving the dispute without disclosure of sensitive material (Perioperative Servs., Farahi). Requesters can mitigate the handicap by offering protective orders or counsel-only review, but courts will weigh the competing policy interests.

Practical tip: propose a staged approach — require the government to provide as much public detail as it can, demand a sworn public declaration explaining why disclosure would cause harm, and only then seek sealed ex parte review if that public baseline is inadequate.

## Framing segregability: demonstrating “inextricably intertwined” records versus proposing practical redaction regimes

Segregability is where many FOIA disputes live or die. Courts require agencies to release “any reasonably segregable portion” of a record. But the definition of “reasonable” depends on facts: the degree of interspersion, technical feasibility of redaction, and whether isolated disclosures would themselves cause harm.

- Establish the legal baseline. The statutory duty is unqualified: an agency must disclose “any reasonably segregable portion” of a record after deleting exempt portions. Courts will accept agency affidavits showing with “reasonable specificity” why segregability is impracticable; they will not accept mere boilerplate. Mead affords agencies room to argue that where nonexempt material is “inextricably intertwined” with exempt material, it is not reasonably segregable. Perioperative and Mead explain that random words or phrases scattered throughout thousands of pages are not necessarily “information” that FOIA protects; what matters is the meaningful information conveyed when fragments are combined.

- For requesters: propose redaction schemes and sampling. If the agency asserts that exempt and nonexempt information are line-interspersed and therefore inseparable, insist on a sampling approach. Ask the court to order production of a representative sample of redacted pages to test the agency’s claims. Offer feasible redaction regimes (for electronic documents: machine redaction + review; for scanned images: OCR + contextual redaction; for audio/video: transcript redaction). Demonstrate the practicality of redaction by proposing a timeline and methodology and identify whether technical limitations exist (e.g., if the records are on legacy media). Show that release of redacted samples would not compromise the investigation.

- For agencies: document the interspersion and technical impediments. If you contend that nonexempt material cannot be meaningfully segregated, provide concrete examples showing how redaction would reveal context that would itself harm ongoing proceedings. If redaction is technically infeasible (e.g., because scanned images or video cannot be redacted without revealing context), explain that with technical detail. Where the record contains small fragments of nonexempt material “interspersed line-by-line,” use Mead and Perioperative to explain why disclosure would not yield usable information and might reveal more than the fragments alone. Courts will accept an agency’s segregability analysis if it shows reasonable specificity; blanket claims will not suffice.

- Tactical compromise: offer to disclose narrow, depersonalized summaries. As a way to resolve disputes without full release, offer to produce summaries that do not reveal sensitive details (e.g., dates of interviews, general topics discussed without names or operational tactics). Agencies should consider whether such summaries would adequately serve FOIA’s transparency goals without undermining investigations. Requesters should be prepared to accept carefully worded summaries if they reveal meaningful information.

Bottom line: litigants should treat segregability as a technical exercise: the requester must show redactions are practical and would not cause harm; the agency must show why they are not.

## Preserving issues: avoiding forfeiture of category‑based objections and segregability challenges

Procedural vigilance is essential. The D.C. Circuit enforces forfeiture rules in FOIA litigation: arguments not raised with specificity in the district court may be lost on appeal.

- Make timely, specific challenges. If you are the requester and believe the agency’s categorical descriptions are overbroad, raise that objection with specificity—name the category, explain why it is overbroad, and suggest a remedial narrowing. In Farahi the court cited forfeiture (Khine) when the requester failed to explain why categories were objectionable. A passing or general objection is not enough.

- Use the summary‑judgment record to develop the record. FOIA is typically decided on summary judgment based on the agency’s affidavits and the administrative record. If you discover insufficiencies in the declaration, move to supplement the record (for example, by requesting a Vaughn index, sample documents, or court-ordered in camera review). Preserve any dispute about ex parte submissions at the district court level—challenge their adequacy and request an opportunity to respond under seal or by means of in camera inspection.

- Raise segregability issues early. Make a segregability argument in your opposition to summary judgment and request a specific remedy (production of redacted samples, in camera inspection to test the agency’s claims, or a required methodology for redaction). If you accept the district court’s reliance on sealed declarations without protesting, you have likely forfeited later appellate claims.

- Preserve alternative theories. If you believe some categories may be protected under 7(A) but other parts must be released under different exemptions or the FOIA’s request structure, present alternative proposals (e.g., partial releases, narrowed requests) to the district court. This gives the appellate court a record to review.

- Use protective orders strategically. If your inability to see sealed material is the principal obstacle to framing objections, propose an appropriate protective order allowing cleared counsel or counsel with need-to-know to review the material. If the court declines, preserve the record by asking for an explanation and asserting that the denial hampered your ability to challenge the government’s claims.

In short: be precise, timely, and proactive—procedural missteps can be fatal.

## Anticipating collateral proceedings (criminal, administrative, immigration) that bear on 7(A)

The “proceedings” protected by Exemption 7(A) are not limited to federal criminal charges; they include ongoing investigations that could reasonably lead to enforcement actions (criminal, civil, or administrative) and, as Farahi demonstrates, proceedings in contexts that cross agency lines (e.g., immigration) where the FBI’s records may be sought or used.

- Identify the relevant proceedings and show their nexus to the records. Agencies should describe what “proceeding” is at issue and how the requested records relate to it. In Farahi the FBI connected its investigative records to terrorism-related enforcement proceedings and to known prosecutions and detentions; that nexus made the 7(A) claim credible. When the proceeding is not a criminal prosecution (e.g., immigration proceedings), the agency must explain how the records relate to law enforcement duties and prospective enforcement steps.

- For requesters: attempt to show the absence of pending or anticipated proceedings. You can petition the court to require evidence that a proceeding is reasonably anticipated. Look for indications that an investigation is dormant (no recent investigative steps, closed files, no probable cause). Use public sources (dockets, press releases, declinations) to show no active enforcement step exists. If the agency points to another agency’s use of the records (e.g., ICE in immigration cases), seek clarification on whether the FBI is coordinating with that agency and whether prosecutions or other actions are reasonably anticipated.

- Anticipate cross‑agency coordination. Where records have been shared with other federal actors (e.g., DHS, prosecutors), the government’s ability to show anticipation of enforcement actions may be strengthened. Requesters should probe whether documents were used in actual enforcement proceedings (e.g., testimony at immigration hearings, evidence in trials). If some records have already been used in open proceedings, those records may no longer be covered by 7(A) with respect to the same proceedings—ask for specifics.

- Consider timing and predicate events. Investigations that have moved to grand jury presentation, charging decisions, or forensic analysis are easier for the agency to show as “pending” or “reasonably anticipated.” Conversely, long lags with no investigative steps strengthen a requester’s motion for updated declarations and disclosure. Farahi highlights that courts are sensitive to the passage of time, and a short confirming update (Bender) tied to an earlier substantive affidavit (Hardy) can bridge that gap for the agency.

- Use collateral proceedings as evidence. If you represent a requester and know the records were used in a tribunal that is public (e.g., immigration hearing), use that to argue waiver or public domain status for the portions used in those proceedings. But recognize that use in one forum does not necessarily waive all exemptions for the full file.

Practical posture: map the universe of potential proceedings early, and tailor litigation strategy (narrow requests, targeted demands for updates, requests for production of records already used in public proceedings) to exploit or blunt the government’s 7(A) claim.

## Checklists: Practical moves for each side

Requesters — checklist
- Serve a narrowly tailored or staged FOIA request if feasible; otherwise ask for a Vaughn index early.
- In FOIA litigation, demand a detailed public declaration that establishes: (i) law‑enforcement nexus; (ii) nature of the proceeding; (iii) how disclosure would interfere.
- If the declaration is old, move to compel an updated declaration; explain why the time lapse matters.
- Propose redaction regimes and request redacted samples or in camera review of a representative subset.
- Offer protective orders, counsel‑only review, or clearance‑based review to increase chances of accessing sealed material.
- Make timely, specific objections to categories and segregability; preserve these at the district court level.
- If the agency relies on ex parte submissions, move for an in camera inspection of those submissions or the records themselves.

Agencies — checklist
- Draft multi‑layered submissions: a detailed public declaration, a sealed ex parte supplement when necessary, and a categorical Vaughn with distinct categories.
- Explain the nexus between records and law‑enforcement duties; provide examples showing investigations are ongoing or reasonably anticipated.
- Anticipate staleness: plan to file short updates confirming that the investigative predicate persists.
- Provide practical segregability explanations showing why redaction is infeasible or would reveal context that defeats the exemption.
- When using categorical Vaughn, ensure categories are sufficiently distinct and link each category to specific exemption claims.
- If seeking to file sealed material, justify why public disclosure would itself cause harm and propose a confined sealed submission rather than broad secrecy.

Conclusion

Farahi v. FBI is a paradigmatic 7(A) opinion: it reaffirms the government’s burden to show the records were compiled for law‑enforcement purposes and that disclosure could reasonably be expected to interfere with pending or reasonably anticipated enforcement proceedings; it underscores the risk of stale declarations and the remedial need for updates; it recognizes a legitimate role for ex parte sealed submissions where the public record cannot safely contain necessary detail; and it enforces the segregability obligation while accepting the Mead principle when nonexempt material is inextricably intertwined with exempt material.

Successful practice in this area requires both sides to be technical and procedural: the requester must insist on specificity, propose feasible redaction regimes and preserve objections; the agency must supply a factual, layered record and be prepared to justify sealed submissions and categorical withholding. Litigation tactics that leverage the timing of declarations, the availability of protective orders, and the feasibility of redaction will often determine whether sensitive records remain secret or are partially released.

# Significance and Implications of Farahi

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in Farahi v. FBI is an important instructional decision about how Exemption 7(A) of the Freedom of Information Act operates in long-running national-security investigations. The opinion is not a novel reworking of the law; rather, it clarifies how existing doctrines—particularly deference to the FBI in counterterrorism contexts, the “pending or reasonably anticipated” requirement from CREW and Mapother, the segregability obligations under Mead, and the role of ex parte filings—interact when an agency seeks to withhold large volumes of records assembled over many years. Below we analyze the ruling’s doctrinal holdings and practical lessons for agencies, litigants, and courts, and highlight areas the decision leaves unsettled.

## Reaffirmation of robust deference to the FBI in counterterrorism contexts

Farahi reaffirmed the longstanding principle that courts give the FBI (and similar law-enforcement agencies) a substantial measure of deference when those agencies invoke FOIA exemptions in the counterterrorism and national-security arena. That deference has two distinct components.

First, there is deference to the agency’s identification of the records as “compiled for law enforcement purposes.” The statute’s Exemption 7(A) applies only to records compiled for law-enforcement purposes, and the D.C. Circuit has required that an agency show a rational nexus between the records and a law-enforcement duty. But courts give the FBI a “fair measure” of deference because the bureau is itself principally a law-enforcement body. Farahi accepted the FBI’s showing—supported by a public declaration and a more detailed ex parte declaration—that the files were gathered in the course of investigations into alleged terrorist connections.

Second, there is deference to the agency’s prediction about the adverse effects of disclosure. When national-security or counterterrorism investigations are implicated, courts have repeatedly stressed that executive judgments about danger, jeopardy to investigations, and the risk of tipping off suspects deserve weight. Farahi applied that principle: the public Hardy declaration described the risk that revealing investigative scope would alert targets, and that attestation—supplemented by ex parte material—was enough to establish interference with ongoing or reasonably anticipated proceedings. The court cited the line of D.C. Circuit precedent that treats agency affidavits in this space as entitled to particular respect.

This deference is not limitless. The agency must still provide declarations that establish the relevant facts with “reasonable specificity,” and the court must conduct a meaningful, if deferential, review. But Farahi demonstrates that in counterterrorism cases a well-supported agency showing—even where much of the proof is presented ex parte—will typically survive judicial scrutiny.

## Operational lesson: updating declarations can cure staleness without re-litigating the entire record

A recurring problem in FOIA litigation is “staleness”: an affidavit prepared years before decision may no longer reflect the current state of investigations. Farahi shows a pragmatic path for agencies and courts: rather than re-litigating the initial merits from scratch, a targeted update to an earlier detailed declaration can suffice to cure concerns about passage of time.

In Farahi the FBI initially submitted detailed public and classified (ex parte) declarations explaining the investigative context and the interference risk. The district court denied summary judgment without prejudice because the initial filings were several years old; it required an updated assessment of whether enforcement proceedings were still “pending or reasonably anticipated.” The FBI responded with a brief public update from a different RIDS official confirming that the investigations remained ongoing, together with an ex parte update. The D.C. Circuit accepted this approach: the later, succinct update was not treated in isolation but as a temporal gloss on substantial earlier statements. The court emphasized context—particularly that the district court had earlier concluded the agency had “seemingly” met its burden and that the ex parte filings supplied detailed support that could not safely be placed on the public record.

For practitioners, the lesson is clear. If a court expresses concern that a declaration is stale, the agency should provide a focused update that (1) confirms whether investigations remain active, (2) ties that confirmation to previously-established facts (e.g., the scope of the investigation, types of targets, and connections to other matters), and (3) supplies any fresh facts needed to show why disclosure would still interfere. The agency need not repeat every element of the original declaration, so long as the update, considered in context, permits the court to find that the factual predicate continues to exist.

For requesters, the lesson is correspondingly tactical: a stale initial declaration is a viable basis for targeting discovery or pressuring the agency for a meaningful update. But challengers should recognize that a narrow update that tracks and reaffirms earlier, detailed affidavits will often be adequate to rebut staleness objections.

## Validation of ex parte submissions for both interference and segregability showings

One of Farahi’s central practical holdings is that ex parte, sealed declarations may be used not only to justify the assertion that disclosure would interfere with enforcement proceedings but also to support the agency’s position on segregability. The case reiterates that ex parte submissions are sometimes the only way to adjudicate the dispute without undermining the very interests the agency seeks to protect.

Ex parte filings present obvious concerns for FOIA plaintiffs: they deprive the requester of the opportunity to contest factual assertions contained in the sealed material. The court recognized those concerns but stressed that they are inherent when the materials necessary to evaluate the agency’s claims are themselves sensitive. Farahi follows prior D.C. Circuit precedent—e.g., CNSS and Perioperative Services—in permitting courts to rely on classified or sealed affidavits when necessary, so long as the public record contains enough to permit meaningful review and the sealed material itself establishes the asserted facts.

Farahi also confirms that ex parte material can be decisive in segregability analyses. Under FOIA the agency must disclose “any reasonably segregable portion” of a record. When non-exempt words or phrases are sprinkled throughout otherwise highly sensitive documents, the agency can demonstrate—by ex parte affidavit—that those fragments are inextricably intertwined with exempt material, and that attempting to release them would in effect disclose the very protected information. The court found precisely that: where non-exempt content is “interspersed line-by-line” and disclosing it would reveal the substance of the investigation, it is not reasonably segregable. The ex parte declaration provided the necessary specificity for the court to conclude segregability would not produce a meaningful public disclosure.

That said, ex parte use is a balancing act. Courts will be more comfortable when ex parte submissions are used to supplement, not supplant, public explanations; when the sealed material is narrowly tailored; and when the agency’s public declarations provide a sufficient roadmap so that the court’s reliance on sealed material is measured and constrained. Absent such context, reliance on sealed material is more likely to be viewed skeptically.

## Boundaries of CREW’s vagueness concerns: contextual readings of terse updates

The D.C. Circuit’s CREW decision warned that conclusory or vaguely worded affidavits—especially those asserting that an investigation remains “ongoing”—may not carry the agency’s burden. CREW is an important guardrail: it prevents agencies from relying on boilerplate declarations without factual particulars about what the investigation entails and why disclosure would interfere.

Farahi clarifies the scope of CREW’s concern without displacing it. The court acknowledged that the later Bender declaration in Farahi looked terse if considered in isolation. But the appellate court read that brief update in the context of the earlier, detailed Hardy declarations and the district court’s prior “seemingly” finding that the agency had carried its burden. In that light, a short confirmation that “the relevant proceedings remain ongoing” functioned as a permissible update, rather than a fresh, conclusory assertion intended to substitute for supporting facts.

The resulting rule is therefore contextual: a court evaluating whether an agency’s later, abbreviated affidavit is sufficient must consider (at least) these factors:

- the existence of an earlier, detailed affidavit that provided the factual foundation for the agency’s assertion;
- whether the later affidavit is an update, not a standalone claim; and
- the nature and scope of the underlying investigation (e.g., counterterrorism matters are more likely to justify even terse updates because of the recognized sensitivity).

Farahi does not authorize agencies to be persistently laconic. If the record contains only a short, conclusory statement with no historical, contextual, or ex parte support, CREW remains a viable ground for rejecting the agency’s showing. But when a concise update is explicitly tied to earlier, detailed public and sealed proofs, courts will often accept it.

## Practical consequence: sustained viability of categorical 7(A) withholdings in complex, multi-decade investigations

Farahi has significant practical implications for FOIA litigation over large, complex, and long-lived investigations. It upholds the viability of broad, categorical withholdings under Exemption 7(A) when justified by the investigation’s scope and sensitivity.

First, the decision endorses the withholding of large document sets compiled during extensive counterterrorism investigations—even if those compilations span thousands of pages and many years—so long as the government can show that disclosure would interfere with enforcement proceedings. The court accepted that the FBI’s file of some 10,750 pages and 80 CDs could be withheld in bulk following agency and ex parte justification.

Second, the opinion validates agency reliance on category-by-category justifications, subject to the standard articulated in CREW and Gallant: categories must be sufficiently distinct to permit judicial review. The court disposed of Farahi’s challenge to the FBI’s categorization in part because the appellant failed to identify concrete ways in which the categories were overbroad. That outcome signals that categorical withholdings can be defensible, especially when an agency’s declarations explain why disclosure of any item in a category would harm an ongoing investigation.

Third, the segregability standard remains demanding in theory but deferential in practice when the agency shows that any non-exempt bits are inextricably intertwined with exempt material. In huge files where releasable words are scattered across documents and their release would expose investigative content, the agency can prevail on a non-segregability theory—particularly when the proof for that theory can be shown ex parte.

Taken together, Farahi makes clear that courts will not lightly second-guess agency judgments about ongoing national-security investigations. Requesters will win more often when they can identify facts showing the investigation is genuinely closed, substantially altered, or otherwise no longer threatened by disclosure.

## Open questions: interplay with the FOIA Improvement Act’s foreseeable-harm provision and the threshold for requiring more public detail

Farahi leaves two important questions unresolved—both important for future FOIA practice.

First, how does Exemption 7(A) interact with the FOIA Improvement Act of 2016’s “foreseeable harm” provision? The 2016 Act requires agencies to take account of foreseeable harm when exercising discretion to release information that does not fall under a mandatory exemption; it also encourages release “unless” foreseeable harm outweighs the public interest in disclosure. That provision primarily governs discretionary releases where an exemption does not mandate withholding. In Farahi, the FBI invoked a statutory exemption—Exemption 7(A)—which, if properly applied, provides a mandatory basis to withhold. The opinion did not analyze the foreseeability standard, and it did not suggest that the Act changes the underlying analytical framework for determining whether Exemption 7(A) applies.

But practical tension exists. An agency that argues a document is exempt need not make a separate foreseeable-harm balancing; yet many records contain mixed content, and agencies sometimes release segregable material as a matter of discretion even if they could have withheld it under an exemption. Future courts will need to decide whether, and to what extent, an affirmative “foreseeable harm” consideration should influence the agency’s presentation of non-exemption justifications in cases where exempt material abounds but some non-exempt content arguably could be disclosed harmlessly.

Second, Farahi leaves open what level of public detail courts must require before accepting an agency’s ex parte submissions as a sufficient basis for judicial review. Farahi approves reading a terse update in light of a prior detailed sealed declaration, but it does not set a bright-line rule for how much public exposition is minimally necessary. That question matters for public accountability: an agency could, in theory, rely on sealed affidavits and only cursory public statements to justify withholding large volumes of records, thereby shrinking the scope of informed public review.

Reasonable future constraints will likely include (at least) these elements:

- Public declarations should describe, in non-sensitive terms, the kinds of investigations at issue, the general nexus between the requester and the investigation, and the particular harm that disclosure would cause (e.g., tipping off targets).
- When an agency relies exclusively on ex parte submissions for critical facts, courts should be able to point to some public indicators—such as earlier judicial findings, procedural history in related matters, or non-sensitive summaries—that permit meaningful appellate review of the court’s reliance on sealed material.
- Where an initial public declaration is conclusory, a later terse update should be insufficient unless the sealed material supplies the missing detail and the district court makes explicit findings based on that sealed material (and explains why greater public disclosure would itself be harmful).

Farahi’s pragmatic approach suggests that courts will continue to craft flexible remedies: requiring limited public summaries when possible, while still allowing sealed submissions when necessary to adjudicate the dispute without disclosing the very harms under contention.

Conclusion

Farahi does not change the basic law of Exemption 7(A); instead, it confirms how that law operates when national-security investigations are long-lived and heavily classified. The decision reiterates the court’s deference to the FBI in counterterrorism contexts, accepts focused updates to earlier declarations as a remedy for staleness, and validates the controlled use of ex parte submissions both to show interference with proceedings and to demonstrate non-segregability. At the same time, Farahi preserves the CREW caution against boilerplate assertions, underscores the continuing (though sometimes circumscribed) obligation to segregate and disclose non-exempt information, and leaves for future courts the delicate task of balancing secrecy and public accountability—especially in light of the FOIA Improvement Act’s focus on foreseeable harm. For practitioners, the takeaways are concrete: agencies should maintain a paper trail of detailed initial declarations and targeted updates; litigants should challenge conclusory updates and press for public detail when the sealed record is sparse; and courts will continue to police the margins, ensuring that deference does not become an unreviewable blank check.
