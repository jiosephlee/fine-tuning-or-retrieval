Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer

### Why did the court avoid deciding whether § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits OSC’s authority under § 1216?
Question:
The district court decided that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin § 1216 investigations, but the D.C. Circuit expressly did not reach that merits question. Is this purely Steel Co. sequencing (no jurisdiction, no merits)? Or did the unresolved statutory-interpretation dispute bear on standing (e.g., entitlement to information) in a way the court thought unnecessary to decide?
Answer:
Short answer: It was a jurisdictional (Steel Co.) decision. The D.C. Circuit declined to reach the statutory question because it concluded AFL lacked Article III standing, and that defect made the merits unnecessary to decide — in particular, even assuming §1216 required OSC to investigate, AFL could not show the requisite injury, causation, or redressability (and had no statutory entitlement to the information it sought).

Why the court said so (with pointers to the opinion)

- The court opened the standing analysis by framing it as jurisdictional: “Before reaching the merits, we must consider whether AFL satisfied the requirements for Article III standing, a question bearing on whether the district court had subject‑matter jurisdiction. Steel Co. v. Citizens for a Better Env’t, 523 U.S. 83, 101–02 (1998).” (Op. § II.) That signals the controlling sequence: resolve jurisdiction first.

- After briefing standing, the court applied the settled standing tests and the relaxed standard for alleged procedural injuries, but concluded AFL failed even that relaxed showing: “Because AFL cannot satisfy even the more relaxed standing requirements for procedural injuries, we need not decide whether these injuries are procedural or substantive.” (Op. § II.)

- The court explained two independent standing failures that made any ruling on the §552(a)(4)(F)(i) / §1216 relationship unnecessary:
  - Causation/redressability problem as to DOJ production of records: any informational injury from DOJ’s withholding “is plainly traceable to DOJ—not OSC,” and any chain tying an OSC investigation to DOJ’s production would be “speculation about the unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts.” (Op. § II, paras on first standing theory.)
  - No entitlement to the investigatory information: even if §1216 required OSC to investigate, AFL had not shown a statutory entitlement to receive the investigative notices or information (it conceded OSC’s decision to apply §1214 procedures in §1216 matters is discretionary). Thus AFL lacked the informational right that would give it concrete injury/redressability. (Op. § II, paras on second standing theory; cites Carson and related discussion.)

- Because those jurisdictional defects would persist even if the court accepted AFL’s contention about §1216 and §552(a)(4)(F)(i), the court properly declined to reach the merits: “we need not decide” whether the injuries were procedural or whether §552(a)(4)(F)(i) limits §1216. (Op. § II.)

Bottom line: This is not an avoidance by choice on a close statutory issue; it is classic Steel Co. sequencing. The panel resolved that it lacked Article III jurisdiction (standing) and therefore had no power to decide the statutory merits; moreover, the standing defects were independent of (and would not be cured by) any particular resolution of the §552/§1216 question.
