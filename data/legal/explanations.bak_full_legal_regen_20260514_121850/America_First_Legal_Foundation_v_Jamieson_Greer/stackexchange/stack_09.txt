Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer

### What does the court mean by relaxing imminence/redressability for procedural injuries but 'not injury in fact or causation'?
Question:
The opinion cites Center for Law & Education and Florida Audubon to say procedural-injury cases relax some elements but not injury in fact or causation. Yet it then finds both causation and redressability lacking and also no concrete informational entitlement. How, exactly, are the 'relaxed' requirements supposed to operate here, and why could AFL not satisfy them even under that more forgiving framework?
Answer:
Short answer
“Relaxing imminence and redressability” means the court will be more forgiving about how imminent the threatened harm must be and how directly a favorable ruling must redress that harm when the plaintiff alleges denial of a statute-created procedural right that is designed to protect a concrete interest. What the court will not relax are the basic Article III requirements that there be a concrete injury-in-fact and that the injury be caused by the defendant (traceability). In this case AFL failed on those non‑relaxed requirements (and on redressability for one theory), so even the more forgiving procedural‑injury framework did not save its standing.

Explanation and how the opinion applies that framework
1) The doctrinal framework (as the opinion states)
- The opinion quotes the familiar formulation: where plaintiffs allege a violation of a procedural right “designed to protect” a threatened concrete interest, courts “relax—while not wholly eliminating—the issues of imminence and redressability, but not the issues of injury in fact or causation.” Ctr. for Law & Educ., 396 F.3d at 1157; Fla. Audubon, 94 F.3d at 664–65 (opinion, ¶ beginning “Through this framing…”). Put differently:
  - Imminence and redressability: may be less demanding because the statute’s procedures exist to protect future or informational harms.
  - Injury-in-fact and causation: remain strict—plaintiffs still must show a concrete, particularized injury and a causal connection to the defendant’s actions.

2) How that plays out factually here (two theories AFL advanced)
- Theory A: OSC’s refusal to investigate caused DOJ not to produce FOIA records.
  - The opinion rejects causation and redressability as too speculative. It explains the required causal chain: OSC might investigate → OSC might pursue corrective/disciplinary action → MSPB might sanction DOJ employees → DOJ might produce records. That chain depends on independent actors (MSPB, DOJ) and discretionary choices by those entities and by OSC. The court says courts cannot ground standing on such speculation about the “unfettered choices made by independent actors not before the courts” and about how an agency would exercise enforcement discretion. See FDA v. All. for Hippocratic Med., Heckler v. Chaney, and the opinion’s discussion of the speculative chain (¶ beginning “This theory suffers from fatal causation and redressability problems.”). Because causation and redressability fail here, AFL’s informational injury vis‑à‑vis DOJ production cannot support standing even under the relaxed imminence/redressability approach.
- Theory B: AFL was denied information that OSC would have had to provide as part of an investigation (an “informational injury” from OSC’s own process).
  - The opinion explains that informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to receive the information (citations: Feld Ent., Akins, Pub. Citizen). Even assuming §1216(a)(3) requires OSC to conduct an investigation, the statute does not plainly create an entitlement to the notices/information that OSC provides under §1214. The opinion stresses that OSC’s decision whether to follow §1214’s procedural requirements in a §1216 investigation is discretionary (citing Carson), and AFL does not allege a statutory right to the §1214 notices. Thus there is no concrete informational entitlement and no injury-in-fact on this theory (¶ beginning “AFL’s second standing theory… fares no better.”). Because the alleged entitlement is absent, a court order forcing OSC to investigate would not be likely to produce the informational relief AFL seeks—so redressability fails as well.

3) Why the “relaxed” approach did not help AFL
- The “relaxed” part of the test only eases imminence and how directly relief must remedy the claimed harm; it does not eliminate the need for a concrete injury or for traceable causation. The opinion applies that limitation directly: it says AFL “satisfies neither those requirements nor even a relaxed standard of redressability” (opinion ¶ beginning “In such cases…”). For Theory A, the chain from OSC to DOJ production is too attenuated—causation and redressability fail. For Theory B, there is no statutory informational entitlement—no concrete injury-in-fact—and so redressability fails as well. Because these non‑relaxed elements are not met, the fact that the court would be more lenient on imminence/redressability does not rescue AFL’s standing.

Bottom line
The court embraced the “procedural‑injury” framework only to the point of acknowledging the limited relaxation of imminence/redressability. It then held that AFL could not clear the still‑required hurdles: it had no concrete entitlement to information from OSC (no injury‑in‑fact on that theory), and its claim that OSC’s inaction caused DOJ’s continued nondisclosure was too speculative (causation and redressability fail). Those defects independently doom standing.
