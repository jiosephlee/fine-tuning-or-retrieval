Title: Stack Exchange about the opinion: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer

### Why isn’t AFL’s asserted informational injury satisfied by § 1216 investigations given Akins and Public Citizen?
Question:
The panel says informational standing requires denial of information that a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive. AFL pointed to the notices and updates available in § 1214 processes and argued § 1216(c) allows similar treatment. The court dismisses this because OSC need not import § 1214’s notice regime into § 1216 matters. Can someone unpack why § 1216(a)(3)’s mandatory investigation language is not itself an entitlement to specific information (as in Akins/Public Citizen)? Is the key that § 1216 lacks a disclosure mandate to the complainant, so no 'entitlement' exists?
Answer:
Short answer: Yes — the panel’s holding turns on the difference between a statutory duty to investigate and a statutory entitlement to receive information. Under Akins and Public Citizen, a plaintiff suffers an “informational” injury only when a statute creates a right to receive specific information; § 1216(a)(3) commands OSC to investigate alleged FOIA withholdings but does not entitle a complainant to notices or investigation reports. Because § 1216 does not impose a disclosure mandate to the complainant (and OSC need not import § 1214’s notice rules into § 1216 matters), AFL has no concrete informational injury under Akins/Public Citizen.

Why the opinion says so (pointing to the opinion’s reasoning)
- Akins/Public Citizen standard: The court repeats the settled rule that an informational injury exists only where the plaintiff is “denied information that a statute entitles the plaintiff to receive” (citing Akins, Pub. Citizen, and Feld Ent.). The panel therefore asks whether § 1216 creates any such entitlement. (Op. § II, para 2–3.)
- What § 1216 actually does: § 1216(a)(3) provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of alleged FOIA withholdings; subsection (c) says OSC “may investigate and seek corrective action under section 1214 … in the same way as if a prohibited personnel practice were involved.” The panel treats those provisions as authorizing OSC to act, not as creating a separate duty to disclose investigation-related information to the complainant. (Op. I.A.)
- No statutory entitlement to information: The court concludes explicitly that “even if section 1216(a)(3) requires OSC to investigate FOIA violations, it creates no entitlement to receive information.” The opinion emphasizes that AFL did not (and could not) argue that § 1216 itself contains notice or disclosure obligations to complainants equivalent to § 1214’s. (Op. § II, para 3.)
- Discretion whether to apply § 1214’s notice regime: The panel notes AFL conceded that OSC’s decision whether to follow § 1214’s procedural requirements in a § 1216 investigation is discretionary, and it cites Carson for the proposition that importing § 1214 procedures into § 1216 is not mandatory. Because OSC need not “engraft section 1214 notice requirements” into § 1216 matters, AFL cannot point to a statutory right to the notices it seeks. (Op. § II, para 3; citing Carson language discussed in the opinion.)
- Consequence for standing: Without a statutory entitlement to information, AFL’s claimed informational injuries fail the Akins/Public Citizen test and thus lack the concrete injury required for Article III standing. The court therefore rejects AFL’s second standing theory. (Op. § II, para 3–4.)

Putting it succinctly for doctrinal memory
- Akins/Public Citizen: informational standing requires a statutory entitlement to the information.
- § 1216: imposes an investigatory duty on OSC, but does not require OSC to provide investigation-related notices/reports to the complainant.
- Because there is no disclosure mandate to AFL under § 1216 (and OSC may, but need not, apply § 1214’s notice scheme under § 1216(c)), AFL cannot say it was denied information that the statute entitles it to receive — so no informational injury under Akins/Public Citizen.

That is precisely the point the opinion makes when it says, “it creates no entitlement to receive information,” and rejects AFL’s attempt to rely on speculative possibility that OSC “may choose to give AFL notices and information” by importing § 1214. (Op. § II, para 3.)
