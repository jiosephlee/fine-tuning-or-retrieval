Title: Blog about the opinion: America First Legal Foundation v. Jamieson Greer

# Heckler by Another Name: Using Standing to Police Separation of Powers in Enforcement

## Why this case matters

The D.C. Circuit’s decision in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer is the latest installment in a growing body of cases in which courts decline to resolve disputes over executive nonenforcement — not by resolving the underlying statutory question, but by holding plaintiffs lack Article III standing. At first glance the opinion may look like garden-variety standing law: the plaintiff, America First Legal Foundation (AFL), sued the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to force an investigation into an alleged Department of Justice FOIA policy, and the court concluded AFL had not plausibly pleaded the causation and redressability elements necessary for Article III jurisdiction. But read more closely, and the decision is a tethered reply to Heckler v. Chaney and its progeny: the D.C. Circuit uses standing doctrine to avoid prescribing remedies that would effectively compel administrative enforcement decisions and second-guess discretionary prosecutorial choices. Because OSC’s statutory duty to “investigate” FOIA withholding under 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) sounds mandatory, the court’s route to dismissal is doctrinally significant — and illustrative of how courts are channeling separation-of-powers concerns through standing’s causation and redressability prongs.

## The court’s reasoning in brief

AFL alleged that DOJ had a policy of not searching employee emails without consent, and it asked OSC to investigate under § 1216(a)(3), which provides that OSC “shall … conduct an investigation” of certain FOIA violations. OSC declined, pointing to a different FOIA provision, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), which imposes OSC enforcement duties only after three predicates (a court order for production, attorneys’ fees against the agency, and a court question whether the agency acted arbitrarily). The district court found OSC erred as a matter of law but nevertheless remanded because it thought § 1216(c) left remedial choices to OSC’s discretion. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit did not resolve the statutory preemption/tension question. Instead it concluded AFL lacked Article III standing.

The court rested its decision on two independent failures. First, AFL’s claim that OSC’s refusal to investigate had prevented DOJ from producing FOIA records was too attenuated: any informational injury flowed directly from DOJ’s actions, not OSC’s decision, and the chain by which an OSC investigation might lead to MSPB action and then to DOJ production required speculation about the discretionary choices of multiple independent actors. Second, AFL’s alternative theory — that it suffered an informational injury by being denied the notices and process that would accompany an OSC investigation under § 1214 — failed because AFL could not show an entitlement to receive such information. The court emphasized the foundational principle from Summers and its D.C. Circuit progeny: a procedural right alone, without some concrete, particularized interest likely to be redressed, does not give rise to Article III standing.

## Heckler in the background — and the standing twist

Heckler v. Chaney is the canonical restraint: courts generally will not compel the Executive to prosecute or enforce. Chaney’s central point is that nonenforcement decisions are presumptively committed to agency discretion and thus not judicially reviewable. The D.C. Circuit in Greer did not rest on nonreviewability per se; instead, it used standing’s causation and redressability requirements to achieve much the same practical result. That is a familiar judicial approach: rather than confronting statutory text that says “shall” and parsing whether Congress has displaced prosecutorial discretion, courts often say the plaintiff lacks the concrete injury necessary to invoke federal jurisdiction.

There’s a doctrinal synergy here. Chaney bars courts from directly compelling agency enforcement in many situations; standing doctrine prevents plaintiffs from getting to the merits of those claims by requiring a near-certain chain of causation between an enforcement decision and a concrete injury. The D.C. Circuit explicitly leans on this synergy: it reasons that mandamus-like relief compelling an OSC investigation would not be “very likely” to redress AFL’s FOIA injury because it would require OSC to pursue discipline, the MSPB to sanction, and DOJ to change its disclosure practices. And courts are reluctant to rest redressability on the supposed persuasive power of a judgment on agencies or on the speculative choices of independent actors.

## Assessing the court’s approach

There is merit in the caution the D.C. Circuit displays. Where plaintiffs seek to weaponize procedural statutes to force enforcement actions, courts must be careful not to convert ordinary adjudication into continual oversight of executive priorities. Standing doctrine is an appropriate gatekeeper against precisely the sort of federal-court micromanagement of enforcement the separation of powers forbids. By demanding a concrete injury traceable to the defendant and likely redressed by judicial relief, the court avoids resolving knotty questions about whether Congress intended to cabin prosecutorial discretion when it used the word “shall” in certain enforcement provisions.

But this approach also raises serious doctrinal and practical questions. First, it risks permitting the Executive to insulate potentially unlawful nonenforcement from judicial scrutiny by ensuring that any plaintiff’s injuries are a few steps removed and hence nonjusticiable. Congress sometimes does create private rights and mandatory duties intended to constrain executive discretion; channeling all challenges through standing’s attenuated causation analysis risks ignoring those statutory commands. Second, it places a premium on the precise identity of the party sued. Here AFL sued OSC alleging an informational injury traceable to DOJ’s FOIA responses; had AFL sued DOJ directly and obtained a merits ruling that DOJ wrongfully withheld records, the FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) provision would have obliged OSC to initiate proceedings. The D.C. Circuit’s framing therefore privileges procedural posture and may encourage plaintiffs to pursue direct suits against the agency that controls the records rather than suits targeting enforcement agencies.

Third, the decision underscores the limits of “procedural injury” cases. The court correctly applied the principle that not all statutory procedures create justiciable informational interests; a plaintiff must show the procedural right was intended to protect a concrete interest (and that the right’s deprivation caused a concrete harm likely to be redressed). But lower courts have at times been inconsistent in applying Summers/Spokeo-derived guardrails, and Greer will be another data point that litigants and courts cite on both sides of that divide.

## Downstream effects and where challenges will turn

Practically, Greer is likely to steer plaintiffs who seek to challenge executive nonenforcement toward two routes. First, they will sue the agency whose noncompliance allegedly causes the discrete informational or economic harm (here, DOJ), hoping to produce the judicial predicate that triggers statutory enforcement obligations tied to an agency like OSC. Second, they will search for plaintiffs who can show direct, particularized injuries from the nonenforcement (e.g., competitive or economic harms) rather than relying on more attenuated informational injuries.

For enforcement agencies and for Congress, Greer signals that statutory “shall” language alone may not be enough to drag enforcement choices before a court unless plaintiffs can identify concrete injuries that flow directly from the agency’s nonenforcement and are likely to be redressed by judicial relief. That outcome may be defensible as a separation-of-powers accommodation, but it will leave unresolved questions about how to vindicate congressionally created procedural rights against the Executive when the actor who could redress the harm (another agency or the political branches) stands outside the litigation.

## Conclusion

America First Legal Foundation v. Greer is a clarifying, if conservative, example of how modern courts use standing’s causation and redressability requirements to manage separation-of-powers concerns implicated by enforcement litigation. The D.C. Circuit did not say OSC could never be compelled to investigate; it simply held that AFL’s chosen legal theory did not present an Article III case or controversy. The decision thus preserves judicial modesty and Chaney’s core lesson, but it also highlights the tension between statutory mandates that read “shall” and the judiciary’s prudential reluctance to forecast or compel executive enforcement actions. That tension is unlikely to be resolved soon, and litigants and Congress will continue to grapple with how to make mandatory-sounding obligations meaningful in a world where standing doctrine and prosecutorial discretion intersect.



## FOIA Discipline Reality Check: Why § 552(a)(4)(F) Remains the True Gatekeeper

Why this matters

America First Legal Foundation v. Greer (D.C. Cir. Oct. 3, 2025) is a reminder that, in the world of FOIA enforcement, statutory detail matters. For requesters who want not merely documents but also accountability—discipline or corrective proceedings against agency officials—this decision reinforces the practical reality that the FOIA provision that expressly triggers Office of Special Counsel (OSC) discipline, 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i), is the reliable route. Attempts to compel OSC to investigate under the broader Civil Service Reform Act provision, 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3), without first obtaining the three explicit FOIA prerequisites—(1) a court order compelling disclosure, (2) an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and (3) a judicial finding that the withholding was arbitrary or capricious—face both doctrinal and pragmatic obstacles, including fundamental Article III standing problems illustrated in this case.

What the court held

The D.C. Circuit did not reach a merits ruling about whether § 1216(a)(3) authorizes OSC to investigate FOIA withholdings independently of § 552(a)(4)(F)(i). Instead, the court disposed of AFL’s suit on threshold Article III grounds: AFL lacked standing to force OSC to investigate DOJ’s alleged email-search policy. The opinion rests on two core points.

First, AFL’s attempt to characterize OSC’s refusal to investigate as a violation of a procedural right did not transform that alleged procedural wrong into an Article III injury in fact. Procedural-right injuries must still be tied to a concrete, particularized interest; a procedural right in the abstract is insufficient. The court noted that AFL’s two suggested concretizations—(a) that OSC’s refusal prevented AFL from receiving FOIA records, and (b) that AFL was entitled to notices or other information that would follow from an OSC investigation—fell short.

Second, both of those concretizations failed on causation and redressability. Any informational injury from DOJ’s initial FOIA stance is traceable to DOJ, not OSC; showing that an OSC investigation would have set in motion a chain of enforcement events—OSC invoking § 1214 or § 1215, MSPB sanctions, DOJ then producing records—is too speculative to satisfy Article III. Likewise, AFL could not identify a statutory entitlement to OSC-produced information: while § 1216 requires OSC to investigate alleged arbitrary FOIA withholdings, AFL did not—indeed could not—point to a statutory rule that would have forced OSC to provide the sort of investigatory notices § 1214 contemplates. And to the extent OSC has enforcement discretion, courts cannot reliably predict how OSC or independent actors like the MSPB or DOJ would act in response to a compelled investigation.

What the opinion means doctrinally

Two doctrinal lessons stand out.

First, enforcement-by-proxy claims against an independent enforcement agency are difficult to anchor in Article III when redress depends on the discretionary choices of third-party actors. The court emphasized principles from standing doctrine: causation cannot rest on speculative chains involving the choices of agencies not before the court, nor on unfettered prosecutorial discretion. That reasoning draws on (and applies) familiar precedents—Heckler v. Chaney for prosecutorial discretion and cases like FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine for limits on causal chains involving independent actors.

Second, the opinion sharpens the practical boundary between a statutory duty to investigate and an actionable right to relief. Even if § 1216(a)(3) imposes a mandatory duty on OSC to investigate FOIA arbitrariness, that duty does not automatically confer on FOIA requesters an independent informational entitlement that courts can vindicate by ordering OSC to investigate. Procedural-right standing remains constrained: plaintiffs still must show a concrete interest and a realistic link to judicial relief.

Analytical perspective and limits

The D.C. Circuit’s disposition is careful and narrow. It leaves open questions about the merits—whether § 1216 by its terms independently requires OSC to initiate investigations of alleged FOIA withholdings even where the § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) prerequisites have not been satisfied. The district court had thought § 1216 was independent, and the D.C. Circuit did not conclusively reject that statutory reading; it resolved the case on Article III grounds because AFL couldn’t show any cognizable, redressable injury.

That procedural posture matters for two reasons. First, it means the statutory debate about the interplay between § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) remains live for future litigation in an appropriate case where a plaintiff can establish standing. Second, by focusing on standing, the D.C. Circuit reinforces a pragmatic rule of litigation: suits seeking to compel an enforcement agency to act must begin from a position of strong, concrete injury, not a hope that judicial pronouncements will trigger a favorable cascade of administrative enforcement decisions.

Tactical takeaways for FOIA plaintiffs who want OSC discipline

For practitioners and advocacy groups seeking not only records but also disciplinary scrutiny of agency officials, this decision points to practical steps to maximize the chance that OSC discipline will follow.

1) Push for the § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) trifecta in your FOIA litigation. The statutory mechanism that expressly obligates OSC to “promptly initiate” discipline requires three things: a court order compelling production, an award of attorneys’ fees against the agency, and a judicial statement that the withholding was arbitrary or capricious. If you seek enforcement rather than merely disclosure, litigating for language that satisfies all three prerequisites is a direct route to forcing OSC to act.

2) Seek explicit judicial findings. Courts sometimes grant production without elaborating on whether the withholding was arbitrary or capricious. If your goal is to trigger OSC involvement, ask the court to explain its ruling and expressly state whether, in the court’s view, the agency acted arbitrarily or capriciously in withholding records. Ambiguous or boilerplate language may not carry the statutory trigger.

3) Pursue a fee award when merited. Even where disclosure is obtained, plaintiffs often negotiate fees; a clear attorneys’-fee award tied to a finding of arbitrary withholding completes the statutory trio that compels OSC to investigate under § 552(a)(4)(F)(i).

4) Preserve a clear record of alleged agency misconduct. If OSC must later assess whether discipline is warranted, a detailed, well-documented record of alleged arbitrariness will help both in provoking OSC action and in any subsequent judicial review.

5) Recognize the limits of suing OSC directly. America First Legal Foundation shows that suing OSC to compel an investigation can founder on standing unless the plaintiff can point to a concrete, redressable injury traceable to OSC’s refusal. Litigation strategies that aim to obtain judicial language satisfying § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) are more reliable than litigation aimed primarily at forcing OSC to act absent those statutory triggers.

Potential downstream effects

This decision will likely produce several predictable tactical shifts. FOIA litigants who want accountability will increasingly litigate not just for disclosure but for the precise judicial findings and fee awards that Congress chose as the mechanism for invoking OSC’s investigatory duty. Advocacy groups mindful of administrative enforcement will structure litigation positions around securing explicit findings of arbitrariness and fee awards.

At the same time, the D.C. Circuit’s narrow holding leaves unresolved statutory interpretive disputes that will likely reappear in future cases—for example, whether § 1216(a)(3) independently obliges OSC to investigate and what process rights, if any, flow to FOIA requesters from a § 1216 inquiry. Because standing is a recurring gatekeeper, potential plaintiffs who can demonstrate a more direct, concrete injury from OSC’s refusal (for example, a statutory entitlement to information that OSC denied) may be able to litigate the merits later and obtain a definitive resolution on the interplay between the two statutes.

Conclusion

America First Legal Foundation v. Greer is a practical reality check for FOIA litigants: if your goal is to produce documents and to trigger OSC’s disciplinary machinery, litigating to obtain the specific statutory triggers in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) is the surest path. Suits aimed at compelling OSC to investigate via § 1216 without those prerequisites will face steep standing and redressability hurdles, and courts are unlikely to compel enforcement agencies to act based on speculative chains of administrative decision-making. For now, § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) remains the true gatekeeper to OSC discipline.



## Organizational Litigation After Hecate: The Perils of Speculative Causal Chains

Why this decision matters

The D.C. Circuit’s ruling in America First Legal Foundation v. Greer is a short, crisp lesson in modern standing doctrine for public-interest and watchdog groups. At stake is a familiar problem: when an advocacy organization challenges one agency’s refusal to act, but the relief it wants depends on a chain of discretionary decisions by other, independent actors, federal courts are increasingly skeptical that the plaintiff has shown the “very likely” causal link necessary for Article III redressability. The court here relied on Hecate Energy’s reasoning about speculative causal chains to dismiss AFL’s suit against the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) for lack of standing. For organizations pressing structural or policy claims that require multiple agencies, this opinion sharpens the boundary between litigable grievances and judicially nonjusticiable policy hopes.

What the court did

AFL sued OSC after OSC declined to investigate whether the Department of Justice had a policy of not searching employee emails in response to FOIA requests. AFL alleged that OSC was required by 5 U.S.C. § 1216(a)(3) to investigate arbitrary FOIA withholdings, and that OSC’s refusal deprived AFL of both the information it had sought from DOJ and the notices that would attend an OSC investigation under § 1214. The district court initially found OSC’s interpretation erroneous but held that OSC had discretion over remedial procedures and remanded; after OSC again declined to investigate, the case was dismissed. On appeal, the D.C. Circuit did not reach the statutory interpretation dispute. Instead it held that AFL lacked Article III standing.

The court applied familiar elements of standing law. It treated AFL’s claims as alleged procedural injuries and explained that procedural-right claims still require a concrete injury that is caused by the defendant and likely to be redressed by a favorable decision. The court rejected both of AFL’s “informational” theories.

First, AFL argued that OSC’s failure to investigate prevented DOJ from producing records in response to AFL’s FOIA request. The court found that any FOIA-related injury was traceable to DOJ, not OSC, and that the causal chain AFL asserted—OSC investigates → OSC brings corrective or disciplinary action → MSPB sanctions employees → DOJ produces records—was too speculative. The chain depended on the discretionary choices of multiple independent actors (OSC, the Merit Systems Protection Board, and DOJ), so a court order against OSC would not “very likely spur the next steps necessary to redress” AFL’s FOIA injury. The court invoked Hecate Energy and FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine to make clear that speculation about the unilateral decisions of nonparties (and about how an agency might exercise enforcement discretion) cannot bootstrap standing.

Second, AFL argued that it had a right to information flowing from an OSC investigation itself (e.g., notices and updates under § 1214). The court held that § 1216(a)(3), even if it requires OSC to investigate FOIA-related conduct, does not itself create an entitlement to receive investigative information in the way § 1214 discloses when OSC investigates “prohibited personnel practices.” AFL could not point to a statutory entitlement to the investigatory reports or notices it wanted, and it conceded that OSC’s decision whether to apply § 1214 procedures in § 1216 investigations is discretionary. Thus, there was no concrete informational injury that a judicial order requiring an OSC investigation would be likely to cure.

The upshot: because AFL could not show that a favorable ruling would very likely redress its asserted injuries, it lacked Article III standing. The D.C. Circuit vacated and instructed the district court to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction.

Analytical perspective: doctrine meeting litigation strategy

This decision is doctrinally unsurprising but strategically important. Hecate Energy (and other recent cases) have pushed courts to police plausibility not only at the merits stage but at the jurisdictional threshold: if redressability hinges on acts of third parties or on discretionary enforcement choices, plaintiffs must show a non-speculative path from the requested relief to the concrete benefit sought.

Two features of AFL’s posture made its case especially vulnerable. First, AFL was litigating against OSC—an enforcement agency that, even if ordered to investigate, would have discretionary authority over whether to pursue corrective or disciplinary proceedings. Courts are traditionally reluctant to command prosecutorial or enforcement discretion; when relief would depend on that discretion producing a particular outcome, redressability becomes doubtful. Second, AFL’s FOIA suit against DOJ was ongoing and separately producing records, which further undermined the argument that an OSC investigation was the necessary key to obtaining information. If the information sought is more directly controlled by another defendant (or by nonparty agencies), suing OSC to pull the levers that would make DOJ produce records is a weak theory of standing.

Doctrinal implications and downstream effects

This opinion reinforces several existing trends that litigators should heed:

- Tightening redressability scrutiny. The court reiterates that “relief must be likely to redress the plaintiff’s injury,” and not rest on speculative chains of causation involving independent decisionmakers. Hecate remains the touchstone: courts will parse whether a decision against the defendant will tangibly bind the actors whose actions actually affect the plaintiff’s interest.

- Limits on suing enforcement agencies to force third-party compliance. Where a plaintiff’s real gripe is with the policy or refusal of a separate agency, suing a supervisory or oversight body (like OSC) is risky if compliance depends on the independent enforcement choices of the agency being complained about or of adjudicatory bodies (like MSPB).

- Constraining “informational injury” theories that piggyback on other suits. Courts will insist that an informational injury be grounded in a statutory entitlement to the information the plaintiff seeks. Absent that entitlement, a speculative possibility that an investigation might produce favorable disclosures is insufficient.

- Continued deference to enforcement discretion. Although OSC’s statutory duties are complex, the court emphasized the practical limits on judicial compulsion of enforcement outcomes. Even if a statute says an agency “shall” investigate, courts will still require that the plaintiff demonstrate a concrete and redressable interest tied to the defendant’s capacity to remedy the injury.

Practical strategies for litigators and advocates

For organizations contemplating litigation to address structural policy concerns that implicate multiple agencies, this decision suggests a few practical adjustments:

- Target the actor who controls the specific relief you seek. If the primary injury is nonproduction of records, the FOIA target (the records-holding agency) is the obvious defendant. If the injury is an agency’s policy, bring a claim that would directly compel or review final agency action by that same agency. Relying on an oversight agency to indirectly produce the relief is vulnerable to the speculative-causation problem.

- Tie relief to obligations that bind the defendant. Redressability is strongest when the court can order the defendant to do the very thing that cures the plaintiff’s injury—e.g., process a FOIA request, disclose specific records, or change a policy through adjudicatory or rulemaking procedures that the defendant must follow. If the plaintiff’s theory depends on persuading other actors to do something, explain why those actors are likely or compelled to follow.

- If you must sue a supervisory or enforcement agency, plead non-speculative chains. Show that the defendant’s ordered action would, by force of law or binding procedure, produce the remedial step you need. For example, if an OSC investigation automatically triggers particular MSPB procedures or notifications that create a statutory entitlement to information, plead that statutory sequence and any mandatory steps tightly and factually. Absent automatic or mandatory downstream effects, courts will view the chain as conjectural.

- Build statutory entitlements to information into your theory of injury. Informational standing works when the plaintiff is denied information the statute entitles it to receive. If possible, frame claims around statutory notice or disclosure provisions that create a clear entitlement to receive certain investigatory materials or notices. Mere hope that an investigation “may” produce useful information is not enough.

- Consider alternative causes of action or relief that bind the nonparty. If your desired relief realistically depends on an independent agency’s action, consider suing that agency directly (if feasible) or pursuing forms of relief that are more likely to bind it—final agency action review under the APA, declaratory relief that clarifies legal obligations, or seeking injunctions that address concrete actions rather than broad investigatory processes.

- Beware of overlapping suits and messy causal narratives. When you have an ongoing FOIA suit against an agency that is producing records, think carefully before litigating against a different agency in the hope that the latter’s enforcement action will accelerate or expand production. Courts will treat the direct FOIA defendant as the more proximate source of any informational injury.

A closing caution

The impulse behind AFL’s suit—using an oversight agency’s investigatory power to remedy perceived FOIA gamesmanship—is understandable and, in the right case, could be a sound strategy. But after Hecate and today’s D.C. Circuit opinion, advocacy groups must be prepared to demonstrate a non-speculative causal path from judicial relief to the specific concrete benefit they seek. Litigation strategies that rest on persuasive power over third parties, or on sequences of discretionary enforcement steps, will invite dismissal at the threshold for lack of standing. In practice, that means focusing efforts where the law gives you a direct right to the relief or where an adverse decision will bind the actor who controls the benefit you seek—not on hopes that a court order will prod multiple independent actors to act in precisely the way you want.



## Vacatur and the Vanishing Merits: Jurisdictional Clean‑Up in AFL v. Greer

Why this matters: America First Legal Foundation’s suit asked the Office of Special Counsel (OSC) to investigate what AFL characterized as a Justice Department policy of refusing to search employee emails for FOIA requests. The district court concluded that OSC had misread the statute—holding that FOIA’s enforcement trigger in 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not cabin OSC’s independent investigatory power under 5 U.S.C. § 1216. That looked like a tidy statutory holding that could have clarified the interplay between FOIA’s enforcement provision and OSC’s broader authorities. The D.C. Circuit, however, never reached the merits. Instead it held that AFL lacked Article III standing and, on that basis, vacated the district court’s merits ruling and remanded with instructions to dismiss for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction.

The court’s disposition is consequential in two related ways. First, it limits the preclusive and precedential force of the district court opinion: vacatur removes the district court’s statutory interpretation from the body of controlling decisions. Second, it leaves the core interpretive question unresolved—whether and when OSC must investigate alleged FOIA withholdings under § 1216 as distinct from FOIA’s own enforcement clause in § 552(a)(4)(F)(i)—while signaling the sorts of procedural and doctrinal hurdles future litigants must clear to get the question definitively resolved.

Why the D.C. Circuit cleaned up the docket

At the heart of the appellate opinion is a familiar Article III gatekeeping analysis. The court held that AFL’s asserted injuries were too speculative and too attenuated to satisfy the injury‑in‑fact, causation, and redressability requirements. AFL advanced two theories of informational injury: (1) that OSC’s refusal to investigate caused DOJ to withhold records in response to AFL’s FOIA request, and (2) that AFL was harmed by being denied the procedural notifications and information that accompany an OSC investigation (as provided in § 1214) if OSC were to treat an alleged FOIA withholding as a “prohibited personnel practice” under § 1216(c).

The court rejected both theories. The chain linking OSC inaction to DOJ production decisions, the court said, relies on multiple speculative steps: that OSC would initiate corrective or disciplinary proceedings, that MSPB or other bodies would sanction responsible employees, and that DOJ then would produce the records AFL sought. Courts cannot rest standing on such conjecture about independent actors and unfettered agency discretion. Nor did AFL show a statutory entitlement to the investigatory disclosures it sought; § 1216(a)(3) may create an investigatory duty, but it does not itself create an informational entitlement to particular notices that would give rise to informational standing. In short, the court concluded AFL lacked a cognizable, concrete, and redressable injury, and so the district court lacked Article III jurisdiction.

What vacatur does — and does not do

Because the appellate court found no jurisdiction, it vacated the district court’s merits decision and instructed dismissal for lack of subject‑matter jurisdiction. That procedural posture has predictable—yet important—consequences.

First, vacatur means the district court’s statutory holding does not stand as binding precedent. A merits judgment is ordinarily the foundation for issue preclusion or claim preclusion and provides a precedent thread other courts may follow; a vacated merits ruling removes those effects. The D.C. Circuit’s vacatur both erases the district court’s interpretive conclusion that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) does not limit OSC’s § 1216 authority and clears the path for relitigation of the same statutory question in a proper vehicle. Parties seeking to rely on the district court’s reasoning cannot claim that it has preclusive effect. Likewise, the opinion has less persuasive value—while other courts are free to cite the district court’s reasoning, they should do so with the caveat that it was vacated for want of jurisdiction.

Second, vacatur is not merely a semantic erasure. It matters for practical litigation strategy. A vacated ruling cannot be used to collaterally estop an agency or bind non‑party agencies like DOJ or the Merit Systems Protection Board. That tightening is especially material here because AFL’s theory depended on compelling downstream action by separate administrative actors (OSC → MSPB → DOJ). Without a surviving merits judgment, future plaintiffs cannot lean on a judicial declaration that OSC must investigate to bootstrap such downstream effects.

The court’s approach is doctrinally orthodox, but leaves open interpretive terrain

The D.C. Circuit’s ruling is doctrinally conservative: it insists on the same concrete injury, causation, and redressability standards the Supreme Court and this circuit have applied whenever private parties seek to judicially compel official action that allegedly would benefit them indirectly. The opinion reaffirms two key limits on private enforcement of administrative duties. First, standing cannot be premised on a bare procedural entitlement divorced from a concrete interest affected by that procedural deprivation. Second, standing cannot rest on speculation about how multiple, independent public actors might act in the future.

That said, the court explicitly leaves the statutory question unresolved. The district court thought FOIA’s § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) did not displace § 1216’s independent investigatory duty; the D.C. Circuit took no position on the merits because it found the case non‑justiciable. Practically, that means the relationship between § 1216 and the FOIA enforcement clause remains an open question in this circuit. Other circuits have treated the issue in different ways—Carson v. U.S. Office of Special Counsel (6th Cir.) was relied on in the record here to show that OSC’s application of § 1214 procedures to § 1216 investigations is discretionary—but a controlling interpretation by the D.C. Circuit or the Supreme Court is still absent.

Implications for future litigants and agencies

What should future litigants take from AFL v. Greer?

First, for plaintiffs seeking to compel investigatory action from OSC (or similar agencies), the opinion is a cautionary note to build standing around a concrete, particularized stake that is directly traceable to the defendant agency’s conduct and likely redressed by judicial relief. Reliance on long causal chains that require favorable exercise of discretion by multiple non‑party agencies will likely fail. Put differently, the safest route is to identify a statutory entitlement to specific information or to concrete, non‑speculative relief that a court can effectuate against the defendant.

Second, litigants who want to resolve the statutory question about § 1216 versus § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) will need a better vehicle—one where an actual, concrete injury traceable to OSC’s decision can be shown. That might mean bringing a FOIA case against the agency believed to have withheld records (DOJ here), securing a merits determination under FOIA (including the specific findings that § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) contemplates), and then invoking § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) to trigger OSC’s duty under FOIA’s own enforcement clause. Alternatively, a plaintiff with a distinct, non‑speculative personal harm flowing from an OSC decision not to investigate might get over the standing hurdle and thus allow courts to confront the statutory interpretation directly.

Third, OSC and agencies like DOJ gain breathing room. Because the D.C. Circuit did not reach the merits, OSC’s current practices remain unexamined in a binding appellate holding here. That means agencies may continue treating § 1216 and § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) as distinct—so long as their practices don’t otherwise draw a plaintiff with standing into court.

A final thought on persuasive force

Although vacatur deprives the district court’s decision of preclusive power, it does not erase it from the docket book entirely. Future litigants and courts may still cite the district court’s reasoning as persuasive authority, particularly if its statutory reading is well‑argued. But they must do so with two caveats: (1) the reasoning was not sustained on appeal and was vacated for lack of jurisdiction; and (2) the D.C. Circuit expressly declined to endorse the district court’s statutory interpretation. In practice, that relegates the opinion to the status of an interesting but non‑binding argument, useful for shaping briefs but insufficient by itself to settle the issue.

Conclusion

AFL v. Greer is a jurisdictional tidy‑up that eliminates a district court’s attempt to decide a knotty statutory question about OSC’s investigatory role in FOIA enforcement. The D.C. Circuit’s vacatur reinforces familiar standing constraints—particularly the aversion to speculative chains of causation involving multiple independent actors—and makes clear that litigants seeking reviewable agency obligations must present concrete injuries fairly directly traceable to the defendant and likely redressable by the court. The upshot: the § 1216 versus § 552(a)(4)(F)(i) debate remains live, but any party hoping to press it in court will have to pick a vehicle that clears Article III’s gate.
