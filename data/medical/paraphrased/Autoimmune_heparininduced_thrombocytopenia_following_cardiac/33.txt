Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received cardiac surgery utilizing heparin anticoagulation; following the operation, his platelet count progressively declined, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Imaging of the brain demonstrated a parietal infarct. HIT antibody screening via immunoassay was strongly positive, and the serotonin-release assay for platelet activation displayed an unusual response—marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, which was only partially inhibited by a monoclonal antibody targeting the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulation was transitioned to argatroban in conjunction with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing hypercoagulability necessitated a further switch to fondaparinux, after which platelets increased steadily (155×10⁹/L by POD62); the patient’s neurologic deficits resolved. Further laboratory testing confirmed HIT antibodies that could activate platelets independently of heparin, signifying a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to marked thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count 13×10⁹/L), distinguishing features of this aHIT case involved a postoperative onset before POD5 attributed to earlier heparin exposure and a distinctive aHIT antibody pattern partially resistant to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

Throughout the 70-year era of heart surgery utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the primary intraoperative anticoagulant, owing to numerous benefits such as suppression of haemostatic activity triggered by contact with artificial surfaces, elimination independent of renal and hepatic mechanisms, availability of real-time anticoagulation monitoring via activated clotting time, and affordability. Nevertheless, UFH carries a significant immune-mediated complication—heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Following cardiac procedures, HIT arises relatively often, with an estimated incidence ranging from 0.5% to 1.0%, contingent upon the regimen and type of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). Importantly, HIT demonstrates a pronounced tendency toward thrombosis, presenting a relative risk approximating 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been characterized by intense platelet activation stemming from clustering of platelet Fc receptors, which results from the formation of immune complexes comprising heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies that target platelet factor 4 (PF4), a cationic protein, when associated with polyanionic heparin. In the last several years, increasing attention has been paid to the subset of patients who experience atypical and often severe clinical manifestations due to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin; this group, termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT), has been recently described in the literature (for review). Here, we present a case of aHIT marked by an unusually profound and prolonged thrombocytopenia, which was further complicated by thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. This case offers new perspectives regarding the range of aHIT presentations, illustrating a highly severe serological profile that displayed partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. Additionally, the report underscores the complexity of diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the remarkably rapid onset of aHIT in this patient was attributed to preoperative UFH exposure. In the end, the patient achieved a positive outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had experienced exertional shortness of breath for two years and had a history of chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was incidentally noted to have a new systolic murmur. Diagnostic cardiac catheterization was performed; however, apixaban had not been discontinued prior to the procedure. Consequently, the cardiologist opted for a transradial arterial approach and reduced the intraprocedural anticoagulant dose, administering only 2000 units of UFH. Angiography demonstrated 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and enlargement of the ascending aorta. The patient was scheduled for cardiac surgery the following week.

The cardiac operation was conducted with cardiopulmonary bypass, using 45,000 units of UFH for anticoagulation. Procedures performed included aortic valve replacement utilizing a #27 bioprosthetic valve, replacement of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, a single saphenous vein bypass graft to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The surgery was uncomplicated, with minimal blood loss and no need for transfusion, and the patient was weaned from bypass without difficulty, demonstrating stable hemodynamic parameters.

Immediately prior to surgery, the patient's platelet count was measured at 210×10^9/L (reference range [RR] 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the platelet count dropped to 155×10^9/L, which constituted a mild early decrease relative to typical post-cardiac surgery trends. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis was initiated on POD1 using low molecular weight heparin (LMWH), specifically 5000 U dalteparin administered subcutaneously; doses were increased to 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, the platelet count continued to fall steadily from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). The patient otherwise exhibited good postoperative recovery, with the exception of developing postoperative delirium. Dalteparin was omitted on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was introduced, with the first dose given at 18:00 on POD5.

Though no focal neurological deficits were present, the aggravation of delirium and the continued platelet decline by POD6 (26×10^9/L; repeat value 24×10^9/L) prompted a CT scan of the brain. Imaging demonstrated an acute infarct characterized by a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm within the left parietal lobe, extending to the cortical surface.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, with hemoglobin measured at 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), consistent with values seen over the prior three days and reflective of anticipated early postoperative blood loss in the absence of continued bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. The reticulocyte count was within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was increased at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no active hemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 concentration normalized.

Assessment of coagulation parameters showed a normal prothrombin time and a corresponding international normalized ratio of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1). The partial thromboplastin time (PTT) was 34 seconds (RR 25–37 seconds), and fibrinogen was elevated at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), in keeping with the acute phase response often seen in the early postoperative period. A blood sample collected on POD5 was forwarded to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody testing.

Additional laboratory investigations revealed normal serum chemistries, including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Both blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest X-ray did not demonstrate significant abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear from POD7 supported the presence of thrombocytopenia without platelet clumping and no evidence of red cell fragmentation (schistocytes) or abnormal/toxic changes in white blood cells. A screening assay for HIT (PF4/polyanion ELISA) was markedly positive at 2.2 optical density units (RR <0.4 OD units); however, the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) produced an 'indeterminate' result, which is addressed in further detail later.

Differential diagnosis:

Common causes of pronounced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock or severe illness and infection, were considered unlikely due to clinical findings of stable haemodynamics and negative results from blood and urine cultures. Less frequent etiologies for thrombocytopenia post-cardiac surgery were also deemed improbable: post-transfusion purpura was excluded as the patient had not received any transfusions; post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura was unlikely in light of normal haptoglobin levels and the initial absence of schistocytes; and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was ruled out, given that the decline in platelet count occurred earlier than expected following exposure to perioperative drugs—both those administered solely on the operative day (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) and those introduced postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

While the drop in platelet count occurred earlier than would typically be anticipated from UFH administered intraoperatively during CPB, further review revealed that the patient had received intraprocedural UFH during a preoperative cardiac catheterization. The occurrence of a thrombotic stroke alongside a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA led to the conclusion that HIT was the most likely diagnosis, probably triggered by the preoperative catheterization UFH exposure. Additional clarification about the 'indeterminate' SRA was solicited from the reporting facility, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

A thorough assessment of the SRA findings was conducted. Indicative of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum from postoperative day 5 induced marked serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20% release), while no serotonin release occurred at a supratherapeutic UFH dose (100 U/mL; 0%). The lack of platelet activation at elevated heparin concentrations typifies HIT, attributable to the failure of PF4-containing immune complexes to form at such high UFH levels. Nevertheless, two additional aspects of the SRA were observed. Firstly, robust serotonin release was triggered by the patient's serum even in the absence of UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; reference range <20%), a finding that is characteristic of aHIT, as opposed to traditional heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) was added, complete inhibition of serotonin release did not occur in the presence of the patient's serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH; serotonin release remained at 76% with IV.3, whereas typical HIT antibodies would result in inhibition below 20%. This led to the initial classification of the SRA as ‘indeterminate’. Ultimately, the underlying explanation was that the patient did have aHIT, with HIT antibodies exhibiting atypically high reactivity, thereby limiting the usual inhibitory effect of IV.3. As far as we are aware, this unusual SRA response pattern has not been documented before.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and the patient was started on argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min. The goal was to maintain PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is considered a valid off-label anticoagulant option for HIT, the haematologist in charge chose argatroban because it has a shorter half-life, which could be beneficial in the event of bleeding, especially in light of the recent stroke and marked thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was administered, beginning with 100 g on each of two consecutive days and then a third dose of 100 g, due to the absence of an initial rise in platelet count after the first two days of IVIG therapy. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were also undertaken as surrogate markers to monitor anticoagulant activity; increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer levels would indicate effective anticoagulation and control of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of treatment, the patient experienced a gradual reduction in fibrinogen levels between postoperative day 6 (POD6) and postoperative day 17 (POD17), with the lowest value recorded at 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked near POD12, and peripheral blood films revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16. Together, these findings indicated the presence of significant, progressively worsening HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. Additionally, after the administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (Ig), the patient's platelet count increased only modestly—from 13×10^9/L at POD7 to a maximum of 54×10^9/L by POD14—representing a substantially lower increase in platelet count than is typically observed following high-dose intravenous Ig treatment in cases of aHIT (see review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulant regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. The rationale for selecting fondaparinux included its simpler dosing schedule—once-daily subcutaneous administration—compared to the need for intravenous dosing adjusted according to PTT for argatroban. Additionally, there was concern that argatroban might not be adequately controlling HIT-related hypercoagulable states, as evidenced by previously noted declining fibrinogen, rising d-dimer levels, and the development of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Because fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, supplemental antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) was administered before initiation; the antithrombin activity prior to infusion was slightly reduced at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count steadily returned to normal levels, reaching 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87, and he achieved full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was maintained up to POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dosage of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Successive SRA assays remained strongly positive for several months, with later SRA results displaying a profile more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The most recent blood sample, drawn at POD124 after normalization of the platelet count, showed findings compatible with predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, demonstrated by 15% serotonin release without UFH and 100% release when UFH was present at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

This patient exhibited several uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were ultimately ascribed to a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Firstly, the thrombocytopenia related to HIT manifested prior to the typical time frame, which for individuals undergoing cardiac surgery is generally between postoperative day 5 and day 10. Among those with cardiac surgery who develop HIT, only approximately 10% experience a HIT-associated decline in platelet count before postoperative day 5, a pattern associated with recent heparin administration before surgery, as observed in this case.

Secondly, the patient’s platelet count dropped to an unusually low minimum of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen concentrations, elevated d-dimer measurements, and the presence of schistocytes during argatroban anticoagulant therapy. These findings, indicative of pronounced HIT-related hypercoagulability, are consistent with aHIT.

Thirdly, our patient did not demonstrate the rapid improvement in platelet count typically seen when patients with aHIT are treated with high-dose intravenous Ig; this suggests the presence of an especially severe form of aHIT. In fact, the only serological indication of a particularly severe aHIT subtype was the partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present. To our knowledge, this unique serological pattern has not previously been linked with aHIT, and recognizing it may help prevent missed diagnoses of aHIT, which could otherwise result in negative clinical outcomes. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT in our patient was ultimately established based on at least three lines of evidence. First, analysis of later blood samples taken during the extended period of platelet count normalization revealed SRA reactivity patterns that are more in line with aHIT. Second, the most recent specimen assessed exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA profile that is characteristic of classic HIT, indicating that the aHIT antibodies were diminishing. Third, there was no alternative explanation for the patient’s atypical disease progression. Encouragingly, the patient went on to achieve a full clinical recovery, with no lingering neurological deficits.
