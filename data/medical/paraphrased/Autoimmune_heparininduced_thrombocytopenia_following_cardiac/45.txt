Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An elderly male patient underwent cardiac surgery with intraoperative heparin anticoagulation. Following surgery, his platelet count declined steadily, reaching 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Neuroimaging identified a parietal stroke. HIT antibody screening by immunoassay was strongly positive, and the serotonin-release assay revealed an unusual pattern, with robust serotonin release observed at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin and only partial inhibition upon addition of a monoclonal antibody against the platelet Fc receptor. Argatroban was initiated for anticoagulation, along with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to continued hypercoagulability, fondaparinux was substituted, after which the platelet count gradually recovered (155×10⁹/L, POD62), and neurological deficits resolved. Laboratory studies demonstrated HIT antibodies that activated platelets independently of heparin, confirming autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Noteworthy features of this aHIT case, beyond marked thrombocytopenia (nadir, 13×10⁹/L), include its unusually early onset (prior to POD5), attributable to preoperative heparin exposure, and a distinctive antibody profile characterized by partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the principal intraoperative anticoagulant in cardiac procedures involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to several benefits such as the inhibition of haemostatic processes induced by artificial surfaces, clearance independent of renal and hepatic systems, the capacity for intraoperative anticoagulation monitoring through activated clotting time, and its relatively low expense. Despite these advantages, UFH carries a significant risk for an immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). After cardiac surgery, HIT occurs with a frequency estimated at 0.5%–1.0%, influenced by the choice and duration of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (comparing UFH with low-molecular-weight heparin [LMWH]), and is notable for its pronounced link to thrombotic events, presenting a relative risk of approximately 15 for thrombosis.

Traditionally, HIT is characterized as a condition driven by marked platelet activation via clustering of platelet Fc receptors, which is triggered by immune complexes formed with immunoglobulin G (IgG) class antibodies that recognize platelet factor 4 (PF4), a cationic protein, once it has bound to polyanionic heparin. More recently, it has been recognized that certain patients exhibit atypical, frequently severe clinical manifestations due to HIT antibodies that activate platelets independently of heparin; this variant has been designated ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). Here, we present a case of aHIT distinguished by profound and sustained thrombocytopenia and further complicated by thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. This case expands understanding of aHIT by illustrating an exceptionally severe serologic profile, with partial insensitivity to Fc receptor blockade. Additionally, it emphasizes the complexities inherent in diagnosing thrombocytopenia occurring early after surgery, as the patient's rapid development of aHIT was attributable to preoperative UFH exposure. The patient ultimately experienced a positive outcome.

Case presentation:

A male in his seventies, who had experienced exertional shortness of breath over the past two years and had a history of chronic atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was newly noted to have a systolic heart murmur. He underwent cardiac catheterisation for diagnosis; however, he had not discontinued apixaban prior to the procedure. As a result, the cardiologist selected a transradial artery access and administered a reduced dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (2000 units of unfractionated heparin). Angiography revealed a 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal branch of the coronary artery, moderate aortic valve stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Cardiac surgery was scheduled one week later.

The patient underwent open-heart surgery using cardiopulmonary bypass, with anticoagulation accomplished via 45,000 units of unfractionated heparin. The operative interventions included aortic valve replacement with a 27-mm bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta with a 30-mm Gelweave graft, a single saphenous vein bypass to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The procedure was completed without complications, significant bleeding, or requirement for blood transfusion, and the patient was weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass without difficulty and demonstrated stable haemodynamics.

The patient's platelet count immediately prior to surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the count had decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicating a modest initial reduction, which is commonly observed following cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1; dalteparin at a dose of 5000 U was delivered via subcutaneous injection, and the dose was increased to 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, the platelet count continued to fall from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Apart from postoperative delirium, the patient was otherwise stable clinically. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5; in its place, fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose of 2.5 mg was started (first dose given at 18:00 on POD5).

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, a worsening delirium and an additional drop in platelet count on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (with repeat measurement at 24×10^9/L) led to the decision to obtain a CT scan of the brain. The imaging revealed an acute infarct, characterized by a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal region, extending to involve the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

The complete blood count performed on postoperative day 5 revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, with a corresponding haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), comparable to the haemoglobin levels observed over the previous three days, indicating anticipated early postoperative blood loss without evidence of continued bleeding. The white blood cell count was measured at 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), and the differential was within normal limits. Reticulocyte count was noted to be unremarkable at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin levels were elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting the absence of significant haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 was within the normal range.

Coagulation assessment demonstrated a prothrombin time within normal parameters, resulting in an international normalised ratio (INR) of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), a normal partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (RR 25–37 s), and an elevated fibrinogen level at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L). This fibrinogen elevation aligns with the acute phase response commonly seen during the early postoperative period. On POD5, a sample was sent to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody analysis.

Additional investigations showed normal results for renal function and liver enzymes (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin). Both blood and urine cultures were obtained. Imaging with a chest X-ray identified no significant abnormalities.

A peripheral blood smear evaluated on postoperative day 7 confirmed thrombocytopenia with no platelet clumping and absence of red cell fragments (schistocytes) or abnormal white cell morphology suggestive of toxicity. The HIT screening immunoassay (PF4/polyanion ELISA) was significantly elevated at 2.2 optical density units (RR <0.4 OD units). However, the confirmatory functional test, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (detailed further below).

Differential diagnosis:

Common causes for pronounced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock, critical illness, and infection, appeared improbable based on both clinical presentation and laboratory findings (hemodynamic parameters were stable; blood and urine cultures were negative). In addition, less frequent causes such as post-transfusion purpura (as the patient did not receive blood products), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (in light of normal haptoglobin and no schistocytes on initial smear), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia were considered unlikely—the reduction in platelet count occurred too soon to be attributed to either newly introduced postoperative medications (administered only on the day of operation: vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid) or those started during the postoperative course (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

Even though the timing of platelet count decrease seemed too early to be due to unfractionated heparin (UFH) received intraoperatively on cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), additional review revealed that UFH was given during preoperative cardiac catheterization. Taking into account the thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was considered the most likely diagnosis, probably initiated by UFH at the time of preoperative cardiac catheterization. The reporting laboratory—the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory—was contacted for more information regarding the ‘indeterminate’ serotonin release assay (SRA).

A detailed assessment of the SRA findings was conducted. The patient’s POD5 serum exhibited robust serotonin release in the presence of both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), consistent with platelet-activating HIT antibodies. Additionally, no serotonin release (0%) was observed at the supratherapeutic UFH concentration (100 U/mL), which is indicative of HIT due to the inability to form PF4-immune complexes at high levels of UFH. Two further aspects of the SRA data were observed. Firstly, in the absence of UFH (0 U/mL), the patient’s serum induced significant serotonin release (96%; RR<20%), a finding commonly associated with aHIT rather than classical heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, introducing the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully inhibit the serotonin release observed with patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH; serotonin release remained elevated (76%) despite IV.3, whereas typical inhibition to below 20% would be expected with HIT antibodies. This led to an initial classification of the SRA outcome as ‘indeterminate.’ The interpretation was that the patient truly had aHIT, but their HIT antibodies displayed atypically high activity, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3. To date, such a distinctive SRA response pattern has not been documented in prior reports.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and the patient was started on therapeutic-dose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at a rate of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Although therapeutic dosing of fondaparinux is an effective ‘off-label’ option for HIT, the haematologist chose argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which was deemed advantageous should bleeding occur, particularly considering the patient’s recent stroke and pronounced thrombocytopenia. Furthermore, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at high doses—100 g daily for two days, followed by an additional 100 g dose—because there was no notable rise in platelet count after the first two days of IVIG. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were collected to indirectly evaluate the effectiveness of anticoagulation, since increasing fibrinogen levels and decreasing d-dimer concentrations would indicate effective management of HIT-associated hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of treatment, the patient experienced a gradual reduction in fibrinogen levels, reaching a low point of 2.49 g/L between POD6 and POD17. The d-dimer concentration peaked around POD12, and schistocytes appeared on the peripheral blood smear from POD9 through POD16. Collectively, these findings indicated marked, advancing HIT-related consumptive coagulopathy. Additionally, the platelet count showed only a slight rise after the initiation of high-dose intravenous Ig, increasing from 13×10^9/L on POD7 before intravenous Ig administration to a maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This increase in platelet count was considerably less than what is typically observed following high-dose intravenous Ig in patients with aHIT (see review).

On postoperative day 14, the anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg subcutaneously once per day. Factors influencing this switch included the practical benefits of fondaparinux, which can be administered once daily via subcutaneous injection compared to the requirement for intravenous dosing and PTT adjustment with argatroban, as well as concerns that argatroban may not have adequately managed HIT-related hypercoagulability. These concerns were based on decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, an AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to starting fondaparinux due to a slightly reduced antithrombin level of 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL) before infusion.

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count progressively returned to within normal range (155×10^9/L at POD62; 202×10^9/L at POD87), accompanied by the patient’s full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dose of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial SRA tests remained persistently positive over several months, with subsequent profiles developing features more typical of aHIT (figure 2). The final blood sample collected, obtained at POD124 after platelet normalization, demonstrated findings indicative of primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, showing only 15% serotonin release in the absence of UFH and 100% release with UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited multiple unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. To begin with, the patient's thrombocytopenia associated with HIT began earlier than the typical timeframe, which is generally between postoperative days 5 and 10 (POD5–POD10) in individuals who have undergone cardiac surgery. Only approximately 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a HIT-related decrease in platelet count prior to POD5; this is frequently related to recent heparin exposure before surgery, which was also observed in this case.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count declined to an unusually low nadir of 13×10^9/L, along with decreasing fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer, and the presence of schistocytes while receiving argatroban anticoagulation. These manifestations of pronounced HIT-associated hypercoagulability are indicative of aHIT.

Third, unlike the typical rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia observed with high-dose intravenous Ig administration in aHIT, our patient showed no prompt platelet count correction, suggesting an especially severe presentation of this condition. Supporting this, serological testing indicated an unusually severe aHIT subtype, as demonstrated by only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro with monoclonal antibody IV.3. To our knowledge, this distinctive serological finding has not previously been described in aHIT and increased recognition could prevent diagnostic delays that might lead to negative clinical outcomes. Despite these atypical features, our patient’s diagnosis of aHIT was eventually substantiated by several factors. First, serum samples collected later, during the gradual platelet recovery phase, exhibited SRA reactivity profiles more consistent with aHIT. Second, the final serum tested displayed a classic heparin-dependent SRA pattern, typical of standard HIT, corresponding with the decline of aHIT antibodies. Third, alternative explanations for the patient's unusual clinical course were lacking. Encouragingly, the patient achieved a full recovery without ongoing neurological deficits.
