Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An elderly male patient received cardiac surgery with heparin as anticoagulation. After the operation, his platelet count progressively declined, falling to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody testing. Imaging of the brain revealed a parietal stroke. The immunoassay used to screen for HIT antibodies showed a markedly positive result, while the serotonin-release assay—designed to assess platelet activation—exhibited an atypical pattern: pronounced serotonin release at heparin concentrations of 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL, with only incomplete inhibition by an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Anticoagulation therapy was changed to argatroban along with administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Continued hypercoagulable status led to a subsequent switch to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count gradually improved (155×10⁹/L by POD62), and neurological deficits resolved. Laboratory assessment confirmed the presence of HIT antibodies capable of heparin-independent platelet activation, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count 13×10⁹/L), this aHIT case was distinguished by unusually early postoperative onset (prior to POD5) due to heparin exposure before surgery, as well as an unprecedented laboratory antibody signature characterized by partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been the predominant anticoagulant used intraoperatively in cardiac procedures involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). This long-standing preference is attributed to several benefits, such as effective inhibition of haemostatic processes associated with artificial surfaces, clearance mechanisms that do not rely on renal or hepatic pathways, the feasibility of monitoring anticoagulant effect via activated clotting time, and economical pricing. Nevertheless, a significant drawback of UFH is the potential to induce heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-mediated complication. After cardiac surgery, the incidence of HIT ranges from 0.5% to 1.0%, influenced by the duration and type of heparin used for postoperative thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). HIT is particularly notable for its strong link to thrombotic events, conferring a relative risk for thrombosis of approximately 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been characterised by pronounced platelet activation mediated through clustering of platelet Fc receptors, initiated by immune complexes formed from IgG-class, heparin-dependent antibodies targeting the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) when complexed with polyanionic heparin. More recently, it has become apparent that a subset of patients develops atypical—and often more severe—clinical manifestations due to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets even in the absence of heparin. This variant, termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review), represents a distinct clinical entity. Herein, we present a case of aHIT marked by exceptionally profound and persistent thrombocytopenia, further complicated by the occurrence of thrombotic stroke in the post-cardiac surgery context. This case expands the current understanding of aHIT by revealing a particularly severe serological phenotype, with only incomplete inhibition by Fc receptor blockade. Furthermore, it illustrates the complexities involved in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, with the early onset of aHIT in this patient attributable to prior exposure to UFH before surgery. Ultimately, the clinical outcome was favourable.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had experienced two years of exertional dyspnoea and had a history of chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was identified as having a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. Diagnostic cardiac catheterisation was indicated; however, the patient did not discontinue apixaban prior to the procedure. As a result, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery access and administered a reduced dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (only 2000 U of UFH). Coronary angiography revealed a 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Based on these findings, cardiac surgery was planned to take place one week subsequently.

During surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was established with anticoagulation accomplished using 45 000 U of UFH. The operative procedures included replacement of the aortic valve with a #27 bioprosthetic valve, replacement of the ascending aorta utilizing a #30 Gelweave graft, coronary artery bypass grafting with a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal branch, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The surgery was carried out smoothly, with no significant hemorrhagic events or requirement for transfusion, and the patient was successfully weaned from CPB with stable haemodynamic parameters.

The platelet count immediately before surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range (RR) 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the count decreased to 155×10^9/L, which constitutes a relatively modest reduction, typical in the setting of cardiac surgery. Standard thromboprophylaxis using LMWH was initiated on POD1, administering 5000 U dalteparin subcutaneously, followed by increased doses of 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. However, there was a continuous decline in platelet count from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (figure 1). Aside from the onset of postoperative delirium, the patient remained clinically stable. On POD5, dalteparin was withheld and replaced with fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose of 2.5 mg, given at 18:00.

Despite an absence of localized neurological deficits, a worsening postoperative delirium and a further drop in platelet count to 26×10^9/L (repeat measurement, 24×10^9/L) on POD6 prompted brain imaging with CT. This scan revealed an acute cerebral infarction, characterized by a region of hypodensity measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm within the left parietal lobe and involving the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count showed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L. The haemoglobin concentration measured 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), similar to haemoglobin levels recorded over the previous three days, aligning with anticipated early postsurgical blood loss and not indicating persistent hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), accompanied by a normal differential. Reticulocyte levels were within typical limits at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no clear evidence of haemolysis. Additionally, serum vitamin B12 levels were restored to normal.

Assessment of coagulation parameters revealed a prothrombin time within normal range, corresponding to an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1). The partial thromboplastin time (PTT) was also normal at 34 s (RR 25–37 s). Fibrinogen levels were markedly raised at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), a finding in keeping with the acute phase response commonly observed shortly after surgery. A blood specimen obtained on POD5 was submitted to a reference lab for evaluation of HIT antibodies.

Further laboratory investigations demonstrated normal results for renal and liver function tests, including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Both blood and urine cultures were collected. No significant findings were observed on chest X-ray.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on POD7 reaffirmed thrombocytopenia, in the absence of platelet clumping, schistocytes, or signs of dysplastic or ‘toxic’ changes in white blood cells. Testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) by PF4/polyanion ELISA yielded a markedly positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units). However, the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) returned an ‘indeterminate’ outcome, as discussed in detail elsewhere.

Differential diagnosis:

Common causes of severe thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infection, appeared improbable in light of both clinical presentation and laboratory data (hemodynamics were stable; blood and urine cultures were negative). Additionally, less common etiologies—post-transfusion purpura (in the absence of blood transfusions), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (with normal haptoglobin and initially no schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia—were also deemed unlikely. This was because the decrease in platelet count happened too soon for drugs introduced postoperatively, including both those administered intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) and those given during the postoperative period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine), to be responsible.

While the temporal pattern of thrombocytopenia also did not correspond with UFH exposure during intraoperative cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), further review revealed that UFH was given during a preoperative heart catheterisation procedure. Considering the co-occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, the most likely diagnosis was HIT, presumed to have been precipitated by the UFH exposure during preoperative heart catheterisation. To clarify the findings related to the ‘indeterminate’ SRA, additional information was solicited from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which had performed the assay.

A comprehensive analysis of the SRA findings was performed. In line with the existence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s postoperative day 5 (POD5) serum induced marked serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20% release), and showed no serotonin release (0%) at a supratherapeutic UFH concentration (100 U/mL). This absence of platelet activation at extremely high heparin levels is emblematic of HIT and is explained by the inability of PF4-immune complexes to form when UFH levels are excessive. Nonetheless, two noteworthy aspects emerged in the SRA data. First, the patient’s serum provoked substantial serotonin release even without added UFH (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; reference range <20%), a typical finding in aHIT but not seen in conventional, heparin-dependent HIT. Second, introducing the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 did not fully suppress serotonin release triggered by the patient’s serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH; instead of reducing it below 20% (as is expected with standard HIT antibodies), release remained at 76% with IV.3. This partial inhibition led to the initial categorization of the SRA as ‘indeterminate.’ The underlying cause appears to be that, while the diagnosis of aHIT was correct, the patient’s HIT antibodies were unusually potent, resulting in incomplete inhibition by IV.3 relative to typical cases. According to our review, such an uncommon SRA response pattern has not been documented previously.

Treatment:

Prophylactic fondaparinux was halted, and therapy was switched to argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at a therapeutic dosage of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT of 55–85 s. Although therapeutic-dose fondaparinux, used off-label, is recognized as a valid anticoagulant for HIT, the haematologist managing the case opted for argatroban due to its shorter elimination half-life, thereby reducing the risk should hemorrhagic complications arise—an important consideration given the patient’s recent cerebrovascular accident and marked thrombocytopenia. High-dose intravenous Ig was also administered: 100 g daily for two consecutive days, with a subsequent 100 g dose administered after observing that platelet counts had not improved during the initial two days of treatment. In parallel, serial assays of fibrinogen and d-dimer were conducted to monitor the indirect effects of anticoagulation; an increase in fibrinogen and decrease in d-dimer would align with successful suppression of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of therapy, there was a gradual reduction in fibrinogen concentrations observed between POD6 and POD17, with the lowest point at 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and schistocytes were identified on the peripheral blood smear from POD9 through POD16. Collectively, these laboratory abnormalities indicated severe and progressive HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. In addition, after the initiation of high-dose intravenous Ig, the patient’s platelet count demonstrated only a slight rise—from 13×10^9/L prior to intravenous Ig (POD7) to a transient maximum of 54×10^9/L (POD14). This increment is notably lower than the platelet response typically documented in cases of aHIT treated with high-dose intravenous Ig (as summarized in the literature).

On POD14, the patient’s anticoagulation regimen was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered once daily via subcutaneous injection. The rationale for selecting fondaparinux included its simpler method of administration—requiring only a once-daily subcutaneous injection—compared to the continuous intravenous infusion and PTT monitoring necessitated by argatroban. Additionally, concerns arose that argatroban might not have been adequately managing the HIT-associated hypercoagulable state, as reflected by decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the development of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin (AT)-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, administration was preceded by supplementation with AT concentrate (1000 U), due to the patient's mildly reduced baseline AT level of 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet count steadily returned to the normal range, reaching 155×10^9/L by POD62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87. Neurological function was fully restored. Fondaparinux therapy continued until POD65, after which long-term anticoagulation was initiated with apixaban, administered orally at 5 mg twice daily. Serial SRA testing remained persistently and strongly positive over several months, and subsequent SRA assays demonstrated a profile more typical of aHIT, as illustrated in figure 2. The final blood specimen, taken at POD124 following normalization of the platelet count, indicated mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, with 15% serotonin release detected in the absence of UFH, and 100% release in the presence of UFH at a concentration of 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, the thrombocytopenia associated with HIT developed prior to the typical time frame observed after cardiac surgery, which generally occurs between POD5 and POD10. Notably, only approximately 10% of patients undergoing cardiac surgery who subsequently develop HIT experience a decline in platelet count prior to POD5, a pattern correlated with recent heparin administration before surgery—an association that was present in this case.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count dropped to an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreased fibrinogen levels, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and schistocytosis, even while receiving argatroban anticoagulation. These laboratory findings—indicative of marked hypercoagulability related to HIT—are characteristic of aHIT.

Third, unlike the rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia that is typically observed in aHIT patients following administration of high-dose intravenous Ig, our patient’s platelet count corrected more slowly, suggesting an exceptionally severe manifestation of aHIT. Supporting this, serological testing revealed a distinctive and unusually severe subtype of aHIT: in vitro platelet activation was only partially suppressed in the presence of monoclonal antibody IV.3. To our knowledge, this specific serological pattern has not previously been linked with aHIT, and recognition of it could be crucial to prevent misdiagnosis of aHIT, thereby reducing the risk of negative clinical outcomes. Despite these atypical features, we confirmed the diagnosis of aHIT in our patient based on several key observations. First, later serum samples collected during the protracted period of platelet count recovery exhibited SRA reactivity profiles that are more commonly seen in aHIT. Second, the final serum sample demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA profile typical of conventional HIT, consistent with decreasing titers of aHIT antibodies. Third, no alternative diagnosis adequately accounted for the patient’s unusual clinical trajectory. Importantly, the patient experienced full clinical recovery, without any lingering neurological deficits.
