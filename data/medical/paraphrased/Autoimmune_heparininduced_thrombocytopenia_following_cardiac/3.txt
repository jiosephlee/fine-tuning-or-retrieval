Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received cardiac surgery during which he was given heparin for anticoagulation. Following the procedure, his platelet levels progressively declined, falling to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led clinicians to test for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Imaging of the brain revealed a parietal lobe stroke. The initial immunoassay for HIT antibodies showed a strong positive, and the serotonin-release assay for platelet activation demonstrated an unusual pattern—marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin with only partial inhibition when exposed to a monoclonal antibody that blocks the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulation was switched to argatroban along with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to ongoing hypercoagulability, therapy was later changed to fondaparinux, which was associated with a gradual rise in platelet count (reaching 155×10⁹/L on POD62), alongside resolution of neurological symptoms. Subsequent laboratory testing verified the presence of HIT antibodies that were capable of activating platelets independent of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Distinctive aspects of this aHIT case—beyond severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count 13×10⁹/L)—included an onset shortly after surgery (prior to POD5), attributable to prior exposure to heparin before surgery, and a distinctive aHIT antibody profile showing only partial susceptibility to Fc receptor blockade in laboratory analysis.

Background:

Over the past seven decades of cardiac surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), unfractionated heparin (UFH) has remained the predominant intraoperative anticoagulant. Its continued preference is attributed to several benefits: it effectively inhibits haemostatic activation on artificial surfaces, is cleared independently of renal and hepatic pathways, allows real-time anticoagulant monitoring via activated clotting time, and is relatively inexpensive. Nonetheless, UFH poses a significant risk of inducing heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-mediated complication. The incidence of HIT following cardiac procedures is estimated at 0.5%–1.0%, varying based on the nature and duration of postoperative heparin prophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). HIT is particularly concerning due to its pronounced link to thrombotic events, carrying a relative thrombosis risk of approximately 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been understood as a disorder typified by potent platelet activation. This process is driven by the cross-linking of platelet Fc receptors, mediated by immune complexes formed between IgG-class heparin-dependent antibodies and the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4), when PF4 is complexed with polyanionic heparin. Recent research, however, has revealed that a subset of patients develop atypical—and sometimes markedly severe—manifestations owing to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets without the presence of heparin. This variant, termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT), has been the subject of recent reviews. The present case study details an instance of aHIT characterized by profound and persistent thrombocytopenia, complicated further by thrombotic stroke, occurring post-cardiac surgery. This case sheds light on the heterogeneity of aHIT by illustrating a distinct serological presentation, notably demonstrating only partial responsiveness to Fc receptor blockade. Moreover, the case underscores the diagnostic complexities associated with early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the particularly prompt onset of aHIT in this patient was attributed to preoperative UFH exposure. Notably, despite these complications, the patient ultimately recovered well.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had been experiencing exertional dyspnoea for two years and had a long-standing diagnosis of atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a newly detected systolic murmur. He proceeded to diagnostic heart catheterisation without interrupting his apixaban regimen prior to the procedure. Given this, the cardiologist opted for a transradial arterial approach and administered a reduced dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (only 2000 U of UFH). Coronary angiography revealed a 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Consequently, the patient was planned for cardiac surgery in one week.

Cardiac surgery was conducted under cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), with anticoagulation provided by 45 000 U of UFH. The operative procedures included replacement of the aortic valve using a #27 bioprosthesis, substitution of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, single-vessel coronary artery bypass using a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The surgical course was uncomplicated, with minimal blood loss, no need for transfusion, and the patient was weaned from CPB smoothly, demonstrating stable haemodynamic parameters.

The preoperative platelet count was recorded at 210×10^9/L (reference range (RR): 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the platelet count had decreased to 155×10^9/L, a modest reduction that is not uncommon following cardiac surgery. Standard thromboprophylaxis with LMWH was initiated on POD1, involving a subcutaneous injection of 5000 U dalteparin. For POD2 through POD4, the dalteparin dose was increased to 7500 U daily. Unexpectedly, the patient's platelet count continued to decline each day from POD1 to POD5, reaching a nadir of 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Apart from postoperative delirium, the patient remained clinically stable. On POD5, dalteparin was withheld, and fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose of 2.5 mg was introduced (first administration at 18:00 on POD5).

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the worsening delirium in conjunction with a further reduction in platelet count on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (confirmed at 24×10^9/L on repeat testing) led to the decision to obtain a CT scan of the brain. Imaging demonstrated an acute cerebral infarction, characterized by a hypodense lesion measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending to involve the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count showed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L. At the same time, the haemoglobin measured 97 g/L (reference range [RR]: 135–180 g/L), a value comparable to those obtained over the previous three days, indicating early, anticipated postoperative blood loss without signs of persistent bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), and the differential remained within normal parameters. The reticulocyte count was measured at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L), with no notable abnormalities. Haptoglobin was elevated to 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), aligning with a lack of significant haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 concentration had normalized.

Coagulation parameters included a normal prothrombin time, corresponding to an international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (RR 25–37 s), and a raised fibrinogen level of 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L). The elevation in fibrinogen is consistent with the expected acute-phase response in the early postoperative period, when it often becomes significantly increased. A blood sample taken on POD5 was submitted to a reference center for testing for HIT antibodies.

Additional laboratory tests demonstrated normal results for kidney and liver function tests, including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Blood and urine cultures were obtained. The chest radiograph did not reveal any significant pathology.

Examination of the peripheral blood film on POD7 confirmed thrombocytopenia (with no platelet clumping) and no evidence of red cell fragmentation (schistocytes) or morphological abnormalities in white blood cells suggestive of toxicity. The initial screening for HIT using the PF4/polyanion ELISA yielded a strongly positive result, with an optical density (OD) of 2.2 (RR <0.4 OD units), whereas the serotonin-release assay (SRA), a functional confirmatory test, gave an indeterminate outcome (as discussed further below).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery—such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, or infection—were considered improbable given the patient’s stable haemodynamics and negative results from blood and urine cultures. Similarly, infrequent etiologies like post-transfusion purpura (which was discounted as no transfusions had taken place), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (evidenced by normal haptoglobin levels and the initial absence of schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (since the decline in platelet count occurred earlier than would be expected for any perioperative medications—either those administered only intraoperatively, including vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid, or those introduced postoperatively, such as furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine) were also deemed unlikely.

Even though the timing of thrombocytopenia appeared inconsistent with intraoperative UFH exposure during CPB, a more comprehensive review revealed that UFH had, in fact, been administered earlier during the preoperative heart catheterisation. In light of the patient’s thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, a diagnosis of HIT was considered most plausible, likely induced by the preoperative UFH exposure. The reporting center, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, was approached to provide additional clarification on the ‘indeterminate’ SRA result.

A detailed analysis was performed on the SRA findings. In line with the expected behavior of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum on postoperative day 5 (POD5) induced marked serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL unfractionated heparin (UFH) concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range [RR] <20%), while no serotonin release (0%) was observed when supratherapeutic levels of UFH (100 U/mL) were applied. The absence of platelet activation at very high heparin concentrations is typical of HIT, signifying that PF4-containing immune complexes fail to form under these conditions. Nevertheless, two other noteworthy aspects emerged from the SRA data. First, in the absence of UFH, the patient’s serum still induced robust serotonin release (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a phenomenon commonly observed in aHIT, but not in the classical, heparin-dependent form of HIT. Second, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) was introduced, it did not fully suppress serotonin release elicited by patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release remained at 76%, whereas typically, HIT antibodies are inhibited to below 20% under these conditions. This unusual finding led to the preliminary categorization of the SRA result as ‘indeterminate.’ Upon further consideration, the findings are attributable to aHIT, wherein the patient’s HIT antibodies exhibited an exceptional degree of reactivity, such that IV.3 conferred only partial inhibition relative to the usual response. As far as we are aware, this distinctive SRA reaction pattern has not been documented previously.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and the patient was initiated on therapeutic-dose argatroban—an inhibitor of thrombin—administered at 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55–85 s. Although therapeutic dosing of fondaparinux is recognized as an effective ‘off-label’ treatment option for HIT, the clinical team chose to employ argatroban due to its comparatively short half-life, a precaution considered important given the patient’s recent stroke and marked thrombocytopenia, should bleeding complications arise. Additionally, high-dose intravenous Ig was administered, beginning with 100 g on two successive days, and a further 100 g dose on a third day, due to the absence of a platelet count response after the initial 2-day treatment period. To indirectly monitor the success of anticoagulation, serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were obtained, as an increase in fibrinogen and a decrease in d-dimer would indicate effective suppression of HIT-related hypercoagulable activity.

During the initial phase of treatment, there was a marked and progressive decline in fibrinogen concentrations between POD6 and POD17, reaching a minimum of 2.49 g/L. Concurrently, d-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 through POD16. Collectively, these three laboratory abnormalities indicated the development of severe and advancing HIT-related consumptive coagulopathy. In addition, the patient experienced only a limited elevation in platelet count after receiving high-dose intravenous Ig: from a baseline value of 13×10^9/L on POD7 to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L by POD14. This platelet response is substantially less than what is typically expected following high-dose intravenous Ig administration in cases of aHIT (see review).

On POD14, the patient's anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux, administered at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg once daily via subcutaneous injection. This adjustment was made partly due to the simplicity of fondaparinux administration, as its once-daily subcutaneous dosing is less complex compared to argatroban, which requires intravenous delivery adjusted by PTT. Additionally, there was concern that argatroban might not be adequately mitigating the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, as suggested by declining fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer levels, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux operates as an indirect factor Xa inhibitor dependent on antithrombin (AT), a 1000 U dose of AT concentrate was provided prior to initiation; the patient's AT level before infusion was mildly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count steadily returned to normal values (155×10^9/L on POD62; 202×10^9/L on POD87), coinciding with full neurological restoration. Fondaparinux treatment was maintained until POD65, after which the patient was transitioned to apixaban at an oral dose of 5 mg twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial SRA assessments continued to yield strong positive results over several months, with later tests exhibiting an SRA pattern more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The most recent sample, collected at POD124 after normalization of platelet levels, demonstrated findings indicative of primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release was measured at only 15% in the absence of UFH, but reached 100% with UFH present at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited multiple unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, all of which could be ascribed to a diagnosis of severe aHIT. To begin with, the decline in platelet count associated with HIT appeared earlier than typically expected; the standard time frame for HIT following cardiac surgery is generally between POD5 and POD10. Fewer than 10% of post-cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT demonstrate a HIT-related reduction in platelet count before POD5—a phenomenon correlated with recent heparin exposure prior to surgery, which was also present in our patient.

Additionally, the patient's platelet count dropped to an exceptionally low nadir of $13 \times 10^9/\mathrm{L}$, accompanied by decreased fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer, and schistocytosis observed while receiving argatroban anticoagulation. These findings, which reflect pronounced hypercoagulability associated with severe HIT, are characteristic markers of aHIT.

Third, our patient did not demonstrate the rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia that is commonly seen in cases of aHIT treated with high-dose intravenous Ig; this suggests the presence of an especially severe manifestation of the condition. In support of this, serological testing revealed only partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro when exposed to monoclonal antibody IV.3, indicating a particularly severe subtype of aHIT. To our knowledge, this distinctive serological pattern has not been documented previously in the context of aHIT, and recognizing it may be crucial to prevent diagnostic oversight and its attendant clinical risks. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT was substantiated in this patient based on multiple factors. Firstly, analysis of serum samples obtained later during the extended period of platelet recovery exhibited SRA reactivity patterns that are more typically associated with aHIT. Secondly, the final sample analyzed showed a heparin-dependent SRA response profile consistent with conventional HIT, corresponding to a decline in aHIT antibody levels. Thirdly, no alternative diagnosis could adequately account for the patient's unusual clinical presentation. Importantly, the patient ultimately experienced a favorable recovery, without any lasting neurological impairments.
