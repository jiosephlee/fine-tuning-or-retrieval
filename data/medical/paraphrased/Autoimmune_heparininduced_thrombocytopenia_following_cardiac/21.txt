Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient underwent cardiac surgery utilizing heparin anticoagulation. Following the procedure, his platelet count progressively decreased, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to an evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Imaging of the brain revealed a parietal region stroke. The immunoassay for HIT antibodies returned a strong-positive result, while the platelet activation test (serotonin-release assay) demonstrated an unusual pattern: robust serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with only partial inhibition when using a monoclonal antibody that blocks the platelet Fc receptor. As a result, anticoagulation was transitioned to argatroban and high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to ongoing hypercoagulability, fondaparinux was introduced, after which platelet counts rose gradually (155×10⁹/L by POD62); neurological deficits resolved. Laboratory analysis verified the presence of HIT antibodies with platelet-activating capacity that was not dependent on heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Notably, in addition to severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), this case of aHIT exhibited early postoperative onset (prior to POD5), attributable to prior heparin exposure, as well as an uncommon antibody profile that demonstrated partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

Over the past seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been almost exclusively utilized as the intraoperative anticoagulant during heart surgeries employing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). This preference stems from several benefits such as its ability to suppress haemostasis triggered by artificial surfaces, elimination pathways that do not involve renal or hepatic clearance, availability of intraoperative monitoring through the activated clotting time, and its affordability. Despite these advantages, UFH is associated with a notable immune-mediated complication, known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). After cardiac surgery, HIT occurs with a frequency estimated between 0.5% and 1.0%, depending upon the specifics of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (UFH or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and is significant for its strong link to thrombosis, reflected in a relative thrombosis risk of approximately 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been considered a condition marked by pronounced platelet activation via Fc receptor aggregation, prompted by immune complexes formed between heparin and immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies that target the positively charged platelet factor 4 (PF4) when it binds to heparin, a negatively charged molecule. More recently, it has been observed that a subset of patients display atypical—and often severe—clinical presentations due to HIT antibodies that activate platelets independently of heparin; this variant has been termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (for review). In this paper, we present a case of aHIT characterized by exceptionally severe and persistent thrombocytopenia, further complicated by a thrombotic stroke, after cardiac surgery. This case reveals new perspectives on the range of aHIT manifestations, including a uniquely severe serological profile with only partial responsiveness to Fc receptor blockade. It also underscores the difficulties encountered in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the patient’s unusually prompt development of aHIT was attributed to preoperative UFH exposure. The patient ultimately experienced a positive outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his 70s, with a two-year history of exertional dyspnea and persistent atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He was referred for diagnostic cardiac catheterization, during which apixaban therapy was not interrupted beforehand. As a result, the procedure was conducted via the transradial artery, utilizing a reduced dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (just 2000 U of UFH). Coronary angiography revealed a 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. He was subsequently scheduled for cardiac surgery one week later.

Cardiac surgery was carried out under cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), with anticoagulation accomplished using 45,000 U of UFH. The operation included aortic valve replacement with a #27 bioprosthetic valve, replacement of the ascending aorta using a #30 Gelweave graft, single saphenous vein bypass grafting to the obtuse marginal artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The procedure was completed without significant bleeding or the requirement for blood transfusion, and the patient was weaned from CPB with stable hemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately before surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range [RR] 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), it had decreased to 155×10^9/L, which constituted a mild reduction expected in the setting of cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with LMWH was initiated on POD1, starting with a subcutaneous injection of dalteparin 5000 U, followed by increased doses of 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, the platelet count continued to drop from POD1 through POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Apart from postoperative delirium, the patient remained clinically stable. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started instead, with the initial dose administered at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite an absence of focal neurological deficits, the combination of aggravated delirium postoperatively and a further reduction in platelet count to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (with a repeat value of 24×10^9/L) led to the decision to perform a CT scan of the brain. Imaging identified an acute cerebral infarction characterized by a region of hypodensity measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm within the left parietal lobe, extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

The complete blood count obtained on postoperative day 5 revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, accompanied by a haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), which was in line with haemoglobin measurements from the previous three days, indicating anticipated early postoperative blood loss without evidence of continued bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count measured 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L), showing no remarkable findings. Haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting the absence of significant haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 concentrations had returned to within normal limits.

Coagulation testing revealed a normal prothrombin time with a corresponding international normalised ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), a normal partial thromboplastin time (PTT) at 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds), and an increased fibrinogen level of 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), findings typical for the immediate postoperative phase when fibrinogen, as an acute-phase reactant, often rises significantly. A blood specimen collected on postoperative day 5 was submitted to a reference lab for HIT antibody analysis.

Additional laboratory studies demonstrated normal chemistry values (including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography did not show any significant abnormalities.

Review of the peripheral blood smear from postoperative day 7 confirmed thrombocytopenia (with no platelet clumping) and showed no evidence of red blood cell fragmentation (schistocytes) or abnormal ‘toxic’ white blood cell forms. HIT screening via the PF4/polyanion ELISA immunoassay was markedly positive at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units); however, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), a confirmatory platelet activation test, gave an ‘indeterminate’ result (to be discussed further).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes for marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infection, appeared improbable based on both clinical assessment and laboratory results (hemodynamic parameters were normal; blood and urine cultures were negative). Uncommon etiologies such as post-transfusion purpura (patient had not received blood transfusions), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura post-surgery (haptoglobin levels were normal, no schistocytes were seen initially), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia were also deemed unlikely. This was because the decline in platelet count occurred too early to be attributed to any postoperative medications—those administered on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or those introduced later during recovery (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

Even though the timing of the platelet decrease was too soon to be accounted for by the UFH used intraoperatively during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), further review uncovered that the patient had received UFH during preoperative cardiac catheterisation. Considering this, along with the thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was deemed the most likely diagnosis, presumably prompted by UFH exposure during heart catheterisation. Additional clarification on the ‘indeterminate’ SRA result was sought from the reporting institution, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings underwent careful evaluation. The patient’s POD5 serum demonstrated substantial serotonin release at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), which aligns with the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies. Additionally, there was no serotonin release (0%) at high UFH levels (100 U/mL), which is a hallmark of HIT, signifying that PF4-immune complexes do not form at elevated UFH concentrations. Despite this, two notable observations were made. Firstly, the serum induced robust serotonin release without UFH present (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), which is typical of aHIT, but not classic heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, the use of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress the serotonin release produced by patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: 76% release occurred even with IV.3, rather than the expected reduction to below 20%, which is standard in HIT antibody-mediated reactions. As a result, the SRA result was initially considered ‘indeterminate’. Ultimately, the conclusion was that the patient indeed had aHIT, with unusually reactive HIT antibodies, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3 rather than the usual suppression. To our knowledge, this uncommon SRA pattern has not been previously documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosage was stopped, and therapy with argatroban—a direct thrombin inhibitor—was initiated at a dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, targeting a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While therapeutic fondaparinux can serve as an effective 'off-label' anticoagulant for HIT, the hematologist in charge opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, considering the recent stroke and particularly profound thrombocytopenia, which posed heightened bleeding risk. Moreover, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was administered, beginning with 100 g on each of two consecutive days, and then a third dose of 100 g was added because platelet counts had not increased after the first two days of IV Ig therapy. Fibrinogen and d-dimer levels were repeatedly measured to provide indirect assessment of anticoagulation efficacy, as increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer concentrations would indicate that anticoagulation was successfully suppressing HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of treatment, fibrinogen concentrations showed a marked decline between POD6 and POD17, reaching a low point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and the peripheral blood smear revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 through POD16. Collectively, these laboratory findings indicated severe, worsening consumptive coagulopathy related to HIT. In addition, after the patient received high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, only a slight increase in platelet count was observed—rising from 13×10^9/L on POD7 prior to IVIg administration to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This increment was notably less than the platelet response typically reported in patients with aHIT following high-dose IVIg therapy (for review).

On postoperative day 14, anticoagulation was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg once daily administered subcutaneously. The switch was made primarily due to the simplicity of fondaparinux dosing, which involves once-daily subcutaneous injections, in contrast to the continuous intravenous infusion of argatroban that requires monitoring and adjustment based on PTT. Additionally, concerns arose about argatroban potentially inadequately managing the hypercoagulability associated with HIT, as evidenced by decreasing fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban treatment. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, 1000 U of AT concentrate was administered before commencing fondaparinux due to a mildly reduced antithrombin level, measured at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL) prior to infusion.

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count steadily returned to within normal limits, reaching 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87. The patient experienced full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux was continued until POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dosage of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial SRA testing remained strongly positive for several months, with subsequent SRA results displaying a profile more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The final blood sample, collected at POD124 following restoration of platelet count, demonstrated results indicative of predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release measured at 15% in the absence of UFH and 100% in the presence of UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited multiple atypical findings across clinical presentation, laboratory data, and serological testing, all of which were ultimately explained by the diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, the drop in platelet count associated with HIT appeared earlier than the typical window for HIT onset, which is between postoperative days 5 and 10 (inclusive) among individuals undergoing cardiac surgery. Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT present with thrombocytopenia attributable to HIT before postoperative day 5, a pattern that is usually related to recent preoperative administration of heparin, as occurred in this case.

Additionally, this patient’s platelet count decreased to an unusually profound nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by reductions in fibrinogen, elevations in d-dimer, and evidence of schistocytes while on argatroban therapy. Such manifestations of pronounced hypercoagulability in association with HIT are suggestive of aHIT.

Thirdly, our patient did not demonstrate rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia, which is commonly observed when patients with aHIT receive high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin; this suggests that the patient had a particularly severe form of this condition. In fact, serologic findings indicated a notably severe variant of aHIT, as evidenced by only partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro when tested with the monoclonal antibody IV.3. To our knowledge, this distinct serological pattern has not previously been described in association with aHIT. Recognizing this presentation may help prevent the oversight of an aHIT diagnosis, thereby avoiding potentially serious clinical outcomes. Despite this, our patient’s diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately verified based on three factors. Firstly, subsequent serum samples collected during the extended recovery of platelet counts exhibited SRA reactivity profiles more indicative of aHIT. Secondly, the most recent sample tested displayed a heparin-dependent SRA reaction consistent with classic HIT, indicating a decline in aHIT antibody levels. Thirdly, no alternate diagnosis could account for the patient’s atypical clinical trajectory. Fortunately, the patient experienced a remarkable recovery, with no lingering neurological deficits.
