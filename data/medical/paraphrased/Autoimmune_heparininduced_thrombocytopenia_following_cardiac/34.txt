Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received cardiac surgery with heparin as anticoagulation. Following the operation, his platelet count progressively declined, reaching 50×10⁹/L on postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Neuroimaging revealed a parietal stroke. A screening immunoassay for HIT antibodies was strongly positive, while the serotonin-release assay exhibited an unusual response: marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with only partial suppression by a monoclonal antibody that blocks the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulant therapy was transitioned to argatroban in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing hypercoagulability resulted in a subsequent change to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count steadily increased (reaching 155×10⁹/L by POD62); neurological deficits resolved. Further laboratory testing verified the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets without heparin, consistent with a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (minimum platelet count 13×10⁹/L), notable features in this aHIT case included a presentation shortly after surgery (before POD5) due to preoperative heparin administration, and a distinctive laboratory antibody profile characterized by partial insensitivity to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

Over the past seventy years, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been the nearly exclusive choice for intraoperative anticoagulation in heart surgery performed with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), largely due to its numerous benefits. These include effective inhibition of haemostatic activation on artificial surfaces, clearance independent of renal or hepatic function, the capability for intraoperative anticoagulant monitoring through activated clotting time, and affordability. Nevertheless, UFH carries a significant immune-mediated risk known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Following cardiac surgery, HIT is observed with a frequency estimated at 0.5%–1.0%, which depends upon the length and nature of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin [LMWH]), and is significant for its pronounced association with thrombotic events, the relative risk for thrombosis being approximately fifteenfold higher.

Traditionally, HIT has been considered a condition characterized by intense platelet activation driven by clustering of platelet Fc receptors. This occurs when heparin-dependent antibodies of the immunoglobulin G (IgG) type form immune complexes by binding to platelet factor 4 (PF4)—a cationic protein—once it associates with polyanionic heparin. However, recent studies have revealed that certain individuals experience atypical and often severe manifestations due to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets even in the absence of heparin; this variant, termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT), is reviewed in the literature. Here, we present a case of aHIT that involved profound and persistent thrombocytopenia, subsequently complicated by a thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. This case contributes new perspectives to the range of aHIT by illustrating a remarkably severe serological presentation, marked by incomplete response to Fc receptor blockade. The case further underscores the complexity of diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia; the patient’s exceptionally prompt onset of aHIT was attributable to preoperative UFH exposure. The patient ultimately achieved a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male in his seventies with a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (maintained on oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily) was noted to have a newly appreciated systolic murmur. He underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization, but had not paused his apixaban prior to the procedure; therefore, the cardiologist opted for a transradial arterial access and used a reduced dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (only 2000 units of UFH). Angiography demonstrated 60% narrowing in the obtuse marginal artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and dilation of the ascending aorta. He was subsequently scheduled for cardiac surgery one week later.

The cardiac surgery was carried out using cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), with anticoagulation established using 45,000 units of UFH. The operative interventions consisted of replacement of the aortic valve with a size 27 bioprosthesis, substitution of the ascending aorta with a size 30 Gelweave graft, single-vessel coronary artery bypass with a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal branch, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The procedure was completed without complications, significant bleeding, or blood transfusion requirements, and the patient was weaned from CPB smoothly with excellent haemodynamic stability.

The platelet count immediately before surgery was measured at 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), it decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicating a modest reduction often seen after cardiac procedures. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis using LMWH was initiated on POD1, with dalteparin 5000 U administered subcutaneously, followed by increased doses of 7500 U given on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, the platelet count continued to drop steadily from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient showed no other issues. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux at a dose of 2.5 mg was introduced, with the first dose given at 18:00 on POD5.

In the absence of localized neurological deficits, the patient’s worsening postoperative delirium and further decrease in platelet count on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (confirmed by repeat measurement of 24×10^9/L) prompted a CT scan of the brain. Imaging identified an acute cerebral infarction, characterized by a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm within the left parietal lobe and extending into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L and a haemoglobin measurement of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), consistent with values observed over the previous three days and suggestive of typical early post-surgical blood loss without evidence of continued hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count measured 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L), which was not noteworthy. Haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting the absence of clinical haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 concentration had normalized.

Coagulation parameters demonstrated a normal prothrombin time, corresponding to an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), and a normal partial thromboplastin time of 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds). Fibrinogen was markedly raised at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), which is typical in the immediate postoperative period due to its role as an acute phase reactant. On postoperative day 5, a sample was sent to an external laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody assessment.

Additional laboratory investigations revealed normal results for renal and liver chemistries, including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Blood and urine cultures were collected. Chest radiography did not identify significant pathology.

Examination of a peripheral blood film on postoperative day 7 verified thrombocytopenia (no platelet clumping) in the absence of schistocytes or abnormal (‘toxic’) white blood cells. The immunoassay for HIT—PF4/polyanion ELISA—was strongly positive with an optical density reading of 2.2 (reference range <0.4 OD units); however, the confirmatory platelet activation assay, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), returned an ‘indeterminate’ result, to be discussed later.

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery—such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, or infection—were considered improbable based on clinical presentation and laboratory data (hemodynamics remained stable; both blood and urine cultures were negative). Other less frequent etiologies, including post-transfusion purpura (patient did not receive transfusions), post-operative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (haptoglobin was within normal limits; schistocytes were initially absent), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also unlikely since the reduction in platelet count occurred earlier than would be expected for recently introduced perioperative medications—these included vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid given intraoperatively and furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine administered postoperatively.

While the timing of the thrombocytopenia’s onset was similarly not compatible with unfractionated heparin (UFH) exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), further history revealed that UFH had been administered intraprocedurally at the time of a preoperative cardiac catheterization. When this was considered in conjunction with the occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was regarded as the most likely diagnosis, precipitated by the UFH exposure during preoperative catheterization. Additional information about the ‘indeterminate’ serotonin-release assay (SRA) was solicited from the testing facility, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings underwent detailed evaluation. Supporting the existence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s postoperative day 5 serum induced marked serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20%), while there was no serotonin release (0%) when tested with a supratherapeutic concentration (100 U/mL) of UFH; the absence of platelet activation at this elevated heparin level is typical for HIT, indicating that PF4-containing immune complexes cannot form at such high concentrations of UFH. Nonetheless, two further aspects of the SRA findings warrant attention. Firstly, the patient’s serum triggered robust serotonin release even in the absence of UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; reference range <20%), which is indicative of autoimmune HIT (aHIT), rather than the classic heparin-dependent variant. Secondly, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 was added, it did not entirely suppress serotonin release caused by the patient’s serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH; instead, serotonin release persisted at 76%, unlike typical HIT antibody responses, which would be inhibited to below 20%. This led to the initial classification as an ‘indeterminate’ SRA result. The rationale, however, was that the patient had aHIT, but their HIT antibodies displayed unusually strong activity, resulting in incomplete inhibition by IV.3. To our knowledge, this atypical pattern of SRA reactivity has not been previously described in the literature.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic doses was stopped, and argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was initiated at a therapeutic dosage of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Although therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is a recognized ‘off-label’ anticoagulation option for HIT, the hematologist managing the case opted for argatroban, which has a shorter half-life, to allow for more rapid reversal should hemorrhage develop, considering the patient’s recent stroke and marked thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous Ig was administered, starting with 100 g over two days and an additional 100 g on a third day, due to the absence of platelet count improvement after the initial two days of treatment. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were obtained to provide indirect assessment of anticoagulation effectiveness; specifically, an increase in fibrinogen and decrease in d-dimer would indicate control of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of therapy, the patient experienced a gradual reduction in fibrinogen levels between postoperative day (POD) 6 and POD17, reaching a lowest concentration of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood films revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16. Collectively, these findings indicated a pronounced, ongoing HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. Additionally, after receiving high-dose intravenous Ig, the platelet count demonstrated only a minimal improvement, rising from 13×10^9/L on POD7 to a temporary high of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This increase was considerably less than what is typically observed following high-dose intravenous Ig therapy for patients with acute HIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient's anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. Factors influencing this decision included the convenience of fondaparinux dosing—once daily via subcutaneous injection—compared to argatroban, which necessitates continuous intravenous administration adjusted by PTT. Additionally, there was concern that argatroban might not sufficiently manage HIT-associated hypercoagulability, as suggested by declining fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer levels, and the emergence of schistocytes observed during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered before initiating fondaparinux; the antithrombin level prior to infusion measured slightly below normal at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet levels gradually returned to normal, measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87; neurological function was also fully restored. Fondaparinux therapy continued through POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dose of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation management. Consecutive SRA tests remained strongly positive over several months, with later SRA results displaying patterns more typical for aHIT as depicted in figure 2. The most recent blood specimen analyzed on POD124, after normalization of platelet counts, yielded findings consistent with primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: only 15% serotonin release occurred without UFH, whereas 100% release was observed in the presence of UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which could be explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Firstly, his HIT-associated thrombocytopenia developed earlier than is typically seen; in cardiac surgery patients, the expected window for HIT onset is between postoperative day 5 and 10. Only approximately 10% of individuals who undergo cardiac surgery and subsequently develop HIT demonstrate a platelet count decline prior to postoperative day 5—a pattern associated with recent preoperative heparin administration, as was observed in this case.

Secondly, the patient’s platelet count reached an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, with concurrent decreases in fibrinogen, elevations in d-dimer concentrations, and presence of schistocytes while on argatroban therapy. These findings, indicative of severe HIT-related hypercoagulability, are characteristic of aHIT.

Third, our patient did not show the rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia typically seen following administration of high-dose intravenous Ig in cases of aHIT, indicating a particularly severe presentation of this disorder. Serological findings further supported this, as only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro was observed with monoclonal antibody IV.3; as far as we are aware, this unique serological response has not previously been described in the context of aHIT, and recognition of it may be important to prevent overlooking the diagnosis of aHIT and its potential for serious outcomes. In spite of this, aHIT was ultimately established in this patient based on at least three factors. First, sera obtained later in the extended course of platelet count recovery demonstrated SRA reactivity patterns more aligned with aHIT. Second, the most recent specimen tested displayed a heparin-dependent SRA response characteristic of conventional HIT, indicating a decline in aHIT antibodies. Third, no alternative explanation was found for the patient’s atypical clinical course. The patient fortunately had a remarkable recovery, without any lingering neurological deficits.
