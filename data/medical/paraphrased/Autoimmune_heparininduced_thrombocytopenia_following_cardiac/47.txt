Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin for anticoagulation during cardiac surgery; following the procedure, his platelet count progressively decreased, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led clinicians to order testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Imaging of the brain detected a stroke in the parietal region. A screening immunoassay detected a strongly positive result for HIT antibodies, while a serotonin-release assay displayed an unusual pattern: there was robust serotonin release at heparin concentrations of 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL, with only incomplete inhibition observed when using a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. The patient's anticoagulant regimen was changed to argatroban combined with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Because the patient remained hypercoagulable, therapy was further modified to fondaparinux, which was associated with a slow increase in platelet count to 155×10⁹/L by POD62; his neurological symptoms improved. Laboratory analysis confirmed the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets without heparin, consistent with a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Notable features of this aHIT case included profound thrombocytopenia (platelet count nadir 13×10⁹/L), an unusually early onset shortly after surgery—attributed to heparin received before the operation—and a distinct laboratory profile marked by partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

Throughout the 70-year era of heart surgery utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant intraoperative anticoagulant, owing to several benefits. These include the suppression of artificial surface-triggered haemostasis, elimination via pathways independent of renal and hepatic systems, the ability to monitor intraoperative anticoagulation through activated clotting time, and affordability. Despite these advantages, UFH carries a significant immunological risk—heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). The incidence of HIT after cardiac surgery is fairly high, estimated between 0.5% and 1.0% depending on both the type and duration of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (UFH compared with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and is notable for its pronounced association with thrombotic complications, exhibiting a relative risk for thrombosis of approximately 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been understood as a disease marked by pronounced platelet activation via platelet Fc receptor cross-linking, which stems from heparin-dependent antibody immune complexes. These immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies target the positively charged platelet factor 4 (PF4) when it is bound to negatively charged heparin molecules. More recently, it has become apparent that certain patients exhibit atypical, often severe clinical courses because their HIT antibodies possess platelet-activating activity independent of heparin. This variant is referred to as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). This report presents a case of aHIT characterized by unusually profound and prolonged thrombocytopenia, further complicated by thrombotic stroke subsequent to cardiac surgery. The case illustrates new aspects of aHIT, documenting a distinctly severe serological presentation with only partial response to Fc receptor blockade. It also underscores the complexities of diagnosing thrombocytopenia early in the postoperative period, since the patient’s unusually rapid onset of aHIT was attributed to UFH exposure prior to surgery. The patient eventually achieved a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male in his seventies who had experienced exertional dyspnoea for two years and had chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily) was noted to have a newly identified systolic murmur. Prior to a diagnostic heart catheterization, his apixaban was not discontinued; therefore, the cardiologist selected a transradial artery approach for catheterization and used a reduced dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (administering only 2000 U of UFH). Angiography demonstrated a 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta; cardiac surgery was planned for the following week.

Cardiac surgery was conducted using cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and anticoagulation was established with 45,000 U of UFH. The operative procedures included aortic valve replacement utilizing a #27 bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, single coronary artery bypass grafting using a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal artery, and left atrial appendage removal. The operation was completed without complications, there was no significant blood loss or requirement for transfusions, and the patient was weaned from CPB smoothly with stable haemodynamics.

Immediately prior to surgery, the patient’s platelet count was 210×10^9/L (reference range: 150–400×10^9/L). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), this dropped to 155×10^9/L, a modest decrease typically seen following cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis using LMWH began on POD1, starting with a 5000 U subcutaneous injection of dalteparin, followed by increased dosing to 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, the platelet count steadily decreased from POD1 through POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient’s clinical status was otherwise stable. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic-dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was initiated at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the patient’s delirium worsened and platelet counts fell further to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (retested at 24×10^9/L), prompting CT imaging of the brain. The scan demonstrated an acute cerebral infarction, with a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, accompanied by a haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), which remained consistent with the haemoglobin measurements from the previous three days—suggesting expected early postoperative blood loss without continued hemorrhage. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocyte levels were within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was found to be elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting the absence of significant haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 level was restored to normal.

Evaluation of coagulation parameters showed a normal prothrombin time, with an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1). Partial thromboplastin time (PTT) was recorded as normal at 34 s (RR 25–37 s), whereas fibrinogen was markedly increased at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), reflecting an acute phase response commonly observed in the immediate postoperative period. On POD5, a blood specimen was sent to an external laboratory to assess for HIT antibodies.

Additional laboratory assessments—including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin—showed values within normal ranges. Both blood and urine cultures were collected. Chest radiography did not reveal any significant abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear obtained on POD7 corroborated the thrombocytopenia (without the presence of platelet clumping), with no evidence of red blood cell fragments (schistocytes) and no signs of toxic changes in the white cell line. Screening for HIT using the PF4/polyanion ELISA was distinctly positive, yielding 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units), while the definitive serotonin-release assay (SRA) was reported as ‘indeterminate’ (details discussed below).

Differential diagnosis:

Frequent causes of significant thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock, severe illness, and infection, appeared unlikely in view of both clinical presentation and laboratory findings (hemodynamics were normal; blood and urine cultures yielded negative results). Less common etiologies, such as post-transfusion purpura (the patient had not received any blood transfusions), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (evidenced by normal haptoglobin and absence of schistocytes initially), and immune thrombocytopenia from medications, were also deemed improbable as the decrease in platelet count occurred earlier than would be expected with exposure to new postoperative drugs (those administered on the operative day: vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid; and those prescribed postoperatively: furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

The timing of platelet reduction similarly preceded what would be anticipated with intraoperative UFH exposure during CPB; however, further review revealed UFH had been administered during a preoperative heart catheterization. Given the occurrence of a thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, HIT emerged as the most likely diagnosis, presumably initiated by the preoperative UFH exposure at heart catheterization. The reporting laboratory, McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, was contacted to obtain more information regarding the ‘indeterminate’ SRA result.

The SRA findings underwent a thorough assessment. Indicative of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum collected on POD5 induced marked serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), along with a complete absence of serotonin release (0%) when exposed to supratherapeutic UFH levels (100 U/mL). Notably, failure to elicit platelet activation at such elevated heparin concentrations typifies HIT, attributable to the inability to form PF4-containing immune complexes under these conditions. Yet, two additional observations regarding the SRA emerged. Firstly, robust serotonin release was triggered by the patient’s serum even in the absence of UFH (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a characteristic finding associated with aHIT and distinct from conventional heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, introduction of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 did not fully suppress the serotonin release caused by the patient’s serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: 76% serotonin release persisted in the presence of IV.3 rather than reduction below 20%, which is typically expected with HIT antibodies. This led to the initial categorization of the SRA outcome as ‘indeterminate.’ Ultimately, it was determined that the patient indeed had aHIT, but the HIT antibodies displayed unusually high reactivity, limiting the inhibitory effect of IV.3 to only partial suppression. According to our records, this abnormal SRA pattern has not been previously described.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was initiated at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Despite the fact that therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is an effective off-label treatment for HIT, the hematologist managing the case opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, allowing for more rapid reversal if hemorrhage should develop, particularly in light of the patient's recent stroke and pronounced thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (Ig) was administered, beginning with 100 g on two consecutive days, followed by a third 100 g dose, prompted by the absence of an increase in platelet count after the first two days of IV Ig. To evaluate the effectiveness of anticoagulation indirectly, serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were conducted, as an increase in fibrinogen and a decrease in d-dimer would indicate anticoagulant suppression of HIT-related hypercoagulable states.

During the initial phase of treatment, fibrinogen concentrations declined steadily from postoperative day 6 (POD6) to postoperative day 17 (POD17), reaching a low point at 2.49 g/L. D-dimer values peaked around POD12, and the presence of schistocytes was observed on peripheral blood smears from POD9 to POD16. Collectively, these three parameters indicated severe, worsening consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. Additionally, after administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), the patient's platelet count demonstrated only a moderate rise—from 13×10^9/L on POD7 (prior to IVIg) to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14—which is notably less than the typical increase expected after high-dose IVIg in patients with aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. The rationale for selecting fondaparinux included the convenience of once-daily subcutaneous injections, in contrast to argatroban, which requires intravenous infusion and frequent adjustments based on PTT. Additional motivation for transitioning arose from concerns that argatroban might not adequately control the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, as suggested by declining fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, administration of AT concentrate (1000 U) was performed prior to initiating fondaparinux due to a slightly decreased baseline antithrombin level of 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count progressively returned to within the normal range, measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and increasing to 202×10^9/L by POD87, accompanied by a full restoration of neurological function. Fondaparinux therapy was continued through POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dose of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation management. Over several months, repeated SRA assays consistently yielded strong positive results, with subsequent analyses demonstrating a pattern more indicative of aHIT, as illustrated in figure 2. The final blood sample, collected on POD124 after the platelet count had normalized, revealed findings compatible with primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: 15% serotonin release without UFH and 100% release when exposed to UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

This patient displayed several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, all of which were ultimately ascribed to a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, his development of thrombocytopenia related to HIT occurred outside the typical window, which for cardiac surgery patients generally falls between postoperative days (POD) 5 and 10. Fewer than 10% of individuals with postoperative HIT exhibit a platelet count decline before POD5, a pattern associated with recent preoperative heparin exposure—an exposure our patient also experienced.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count reached an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L. Concurrently, his fibrinogen levels decreased, d-dimer concentrations rose, and schistocytosis was observed while on argatroban therapy. These manifestations of marked hypercoagulability in the context of HIT are suggestive of aHIT.

Third, the patient failed to show a rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia, which is typically observed when high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin is administered to individuals with aHIT; this suggests an unusually severe manifestation of the disease. Notably, serological findings indicated a rare and severe aHIT variant, demonstrated by only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro using monoclonal antibody IV.3. To the best of our knowledge, this unique serological pattern has not been previously described in relation to aHIT; recognizing this could help prevent missed diagnoses and associated negative outcomes. Despite this, aHIT was eventually diagnosed in the patient based on three main factors. First, subsequent serum samples collected during the extended phase of platelet recovery revealed some SRA reactivity patterns more indicative of aHIT. Second, the final sample analyzed exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA response profile typical of classic HIT, signifying diminishing aHIT antibody levels. Third, no alternative diagnosis could account for the patient’s atypical clinical presentation. The patient ultimately experienced a favorable outcome, recovering fully without any lasting neurological deficits.
