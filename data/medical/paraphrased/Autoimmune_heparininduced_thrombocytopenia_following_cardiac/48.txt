Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin for anticoagulation during cardiac surgery; subsequently, his platelet levels progressively declined, falling to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), leading to an evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Imaging of the brain revealed a parietal lobe infarct. The HIT antibody immunoassay returned a strong positive, and the serotonin-release assay showed an unusual pattern: marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with platelet activation only partially suppressed by a monoclonal antibody blocking the Fc receptor. Anticoagulant therapy was transitioned to argatroban and high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing hypercoagulable state necessitated changing to fondaparinux, after which platelet counts gradually rose (155×10⁹/L by POD62), and neurological symptoms resolved. Further laboratory analysis detected HIT antibodies with platelet-activating effects that did not require heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to marked thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), notable features of this aHIT case included an early appearance after surgery (prior to POD5), likely due to heparin given preoperatively, and an atypical laboratory HIT antibody pattern that showed partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been almost exclusively utilized as the intraoperative anticoagulant during cardiac operations involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), attributable to several benefits. These include its capacity to suppress haemostasis triggered by artificial surfaces, elimination mechanisms that do not depend on renal or hepatic function, feasibility of real-time monitoring using activated clotting time, and affordability. Nonetheless, a notable complication linked to UFH is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-driven adverse reaction. After cardiac surgery, HIT occurs with a frequency ranging between 0.5% and 1.0%, contingent upon the postoperative heparin regimen (whether UFH or low-molecular-weight heparin [LMWH] is used) and the length of prophylaxis. Notably, HIT is closely associated with thrombotic events, carrying an approximate fifteenfold increased risk.

Historically, HIT has been characterized by potent platelet activation mediated via platelet Fc receptor aggregation, triggered by the formation of heparin-dependent immune complexes. These involve immunoglobulin G (IgG) class antibodies directed against platelet factor 4 (PF4)—a cationic protein—complexed with the polyanionic heparin. More recently, recognition has grown concerning certain patients whose clinical presentations are both atypical and often severe due to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets even without heparin. This variant is termed 'autoimmune HIT' (aHIT) (for review). This article presents a case of aHIT that manifested with extraordinary, persistent thrombocytopenia and was further complicated by a thrombotic stroke post-cardiac surgery. The case adds to the understanding of aHIT by detailing a highly unusual serological profile, notable for its partial insensitivity to Fc receptor inhibition. Additionally, the case underscores the complexities in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the very rapid onset of aHIT was attributed to preoperative UFH exposure. Ultimately, the patient's prognosis was positive.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his 70s with a two-year duration of exertional shortness of breath and a long-standing history of atrial fibrillation (managed with apixaban 5 mg orally twice daily) was found to have a previously unrecognized systolic heart murmur. He underwent a diagnostic cardiac catheterization without discontinuation of his apixaban beforehand; therefore, the cardiologist selected a transradial artery access and administered a lower than typical intraprocedural anticoagulant dose (2000 U of UFH). Angiography demonstrated 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal branch of the coronary artery, moderate severity aortic stenosis, and enlargement of the ascending aorta; as a result, he was scheduled for open cardiac surgery a week later.

The operative procedure utilized cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) with anticoagulation provided by 45,000 U of UFH. The interventions consisted of replacing the aortic valve with a #27 bioprosthetic device, substituting the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, performing a single coronary artery bypass with a saphenous vein conduit to the obtuse marginal branch, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The surgical course was uneventful, without significant hemorrhage or the necessity for blood transfusion, and separation from CPB was straightforward with stable hemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400×10^9/L). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the platelet count had decreased to 155×10^9/L, signifying a modest drop as is often observed after cardiac surgery. Routine postoperative thromboprophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1; dalteparin at 5000 units was given subcutaneously, followed by increased dosing of 7500 units on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, the platelet count continued to fall from POD1 through POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Apart from postoperative delirium, the patient's condition was otherwise stable. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux at a dose of 2.5 mg was started, with the first administration at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite the absence of localized neurological deficits, worsening postoperative delirium and a further platelet drop to 26×10^9/L (repeat measurement: 24×10^9/L) on POD6 led to head CT imaging. The scan detected an acute infarction in the brain, identified by an area of hypodensity measuring 3.2×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count showed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, accompanied by a haemoglobin value of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L). This haemoglobin level was consistent with readings from the previous three days, reflecting expected early postoperative blood loss without evidence of continued hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), and the differential was within normal limits. Reticulocyte count measured 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L), showing no abnormalities. Haptoglobin was elevated to 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), which aligns with the lack of apparent haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 level had normalized.

Coagulation testing indicated a normal prothrombin time and an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), as well as a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds). Fibrinogen was raised at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), reflecting a typical postoperative rise due to fibrinogen’s status as an acute phase reactant that commonly increases significantly in the early period following surgery. Blood was drawn on POD5 and submitted to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody evaluation.

Additional laboratory assessments demonstrated normal renal and liver chemistry values (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were obtained, and chest radiography revealed no significant findings.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on POD7 corroborated thrombocytopenia, showing no platelet aggregates and no schistocytes or abnormal ‘toxic’ features in white blood cells. A PF4/polyanion ELISA screening immunoassay for HIT was markedly positive at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units), whereas the serotonin-release assay (SRA), used for confirmation, produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (discussed later).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of significant thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, or infection, were considered unlikely due to both normal haemodynamic parameters and negative results for blood and urine cultures. Infrequent etiologies, including post-transfusion purpura (the patient had not received transfusions), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (evidenced by normal haptoglobin and initially absent schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also discounted; this was due to the timing of the thrombocytopenia, which developed too soon after any new postoperative medications, including agents administered only intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) and those initiated postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

While the decrease in platelet count appeared too early to be attributed to intraoperative UFH exposure during CPB, it was subsequently discovered that the patient had also received UFH intraprocedurally during preoperative cardiac catheterisation. Considering the development of thrombotic stroke and a strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was deemed the most likely explanation, potentially initiated by UFH exposure at the time of preoperative catheterisation. Additional clarification regarding the 'indeterminate' SRA result was sought from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which had provided the assay.

A thorough analysis of the SRA findings was performed. The patient's POD5 serum triggered marked serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% is normal), which aligns with the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies. Additionally, no serotonin release occurred when a supratherapeutic UFH concentration (100 U/mL) was used (0%), a typical HIT feature indicating that PF4-containing immune complexes cannot form at such high levels of UFH. Yet, two unusual aspects emerged from the SRA data. Firstly, strong serotonin release was elicited by the patient's serum even without any UFH added (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a pattern characteristic of aHIT rather than classical, heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) was introduced, it did not fully suppress the serotonin release from patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release remained high at 76%, instead of dropping below 20% as typically expected for HIT antibodies. This led to the classification of the SRA as 'indeterminate.' The underlying explanation was that the patient indeed had aHIT, but possessed HIT antibodies with unusually high reactivity, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3 compared to typical cases. This distinctive SRA response profile, to the best of our knowledge, has not previously been documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosing was stopped, and argatroban—a direct thrombin inhibitor—was started at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Despite therapeutic-dose fondaparinux being a suitable 'off-label' choice for HIT, the hematologist opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which would allow for faster reversal in the event of bleeding, considering the patient’s recent stroke and marked thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (Ig) was administered, beginning with 100 g on two consecutive days, and a third 100 g dose was given after the platelet count failed to rise following the first two days of Ig therapy. To assess anticoagulant effectiveness indirectly, serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were performed, since increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer results would indicate successful suppression of HIT-associated hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of management, fibrinogen concentrations steadily declined between postoperative days 6 and 17, with a lowest measurement of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around postoperative day 12, and peripheral blood smear demonstrated schistocytes between days 9 and 16. Collectively, these three abnormalities indicated a severe and progressively worsening consumptive coagulopathy related to HIT. In addition, the patient’s platelet count increased only minimally after high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was given, rising from 13×10^9/L prior to IVIg administration (POD7) to a transient peak of 54×10^9/L (POD14). This represents a significantly smaller platelet response compared to what is typically observed following high-dose IVIg therapy in patients with acute HIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient's anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg once daily administered subcutaneously. The decision to make this switch took into account the simpler dosing schedule of fondaparinux, which is given once daily subcutaneously, in contrast to the requirement for continuous intravenous infusion of argatroban titrated to PTT, as well as concerns that argatroban might not have been sufficiently controlling the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, suggested by declining fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer levels, and the presence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. As fondaparinux acts as an indirect factor Xa inhibitor dependent on antithrombin (AT), AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to initiation of fondaparinux due to a mildly decreased baseline AT activity of 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet count steadily returned to normal levels, measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87, and he achieved full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux therapy continued through POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dose of 5 mg twice daily by mouth for ongoing anticoagulation. Repeated SRA tests continued to yield strongly positive results for several months, with some follow-up profiles displaying findings more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The most recent blood sample, drawn on POD124 after normalization of platelet counts, demonstrated predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release was 15% without UFH and 100% when UFH was present at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

This patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serologic characteristics, all of which were ultimately consistent with a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Firstly, thrombocytopenia associated with HIT developed prior to the typical post-operative time frame for HIT, which typically ranges from POD5 to POD10 in individuals undergoing cardiac surgery. Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who experience HIT demonstrate a platelet count drop prior to POD5; this early onset is related to recent preoperative heparin exposure, a finding also present in this case.

Secondly, the patient’s platelet count declined to a notably low nadir of 13×10^9/L, alongside decreased fibrinogen concentrations, elevated d-dimer values, and the presence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. These manifestations of pronounced hypercoagulability secondary to HIT are characteristic of aHIT.

Third, our patient did not demonstrate the rapid improvement in platelet count that is typically observed after administration of high-dose intravenous Ig to individuals with aHIT. This suggests that the patient experienced an especially severe variant of this condition. Supporting this, serological testing revealed only partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present—a finding that, to our knowledge, represents a previously unreported serological pattern in aHIT. Recognizing this novel profile may help prevent missed diagnoses of aHIT and the resulting negative clinical outcomes. Despite this, a diagnosis of aHIT was eventually established based on three key factors. First, additional sera collected during the patient's protracted recovery period exhibited SRA reactivity patterns more typical of aHIT. Second, the final serum sample tested demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA activation profile consistent with conventional HIT, likely reflecting a decrease in aHIT antibody levels. Third, no alternative explanation could account for the patient's unusual clinical trajectory. Ultimately, the patient experienced a full clinical recovery without any persistent neurological deficits.
