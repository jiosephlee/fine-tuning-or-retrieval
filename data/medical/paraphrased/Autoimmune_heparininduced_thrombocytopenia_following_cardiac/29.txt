Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient underwent heart surgery with heparin administered for anticoagulation; afterward, his platelet count gradually decreased, reaching 50×10⁹/L on postoperative day 5 (POD5), leading to an evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging revealed a stroke in the parietal region. A HIT antibody screening immunoassay returned a strongly positive result, while the platelet activation assay (serotonin-release assay) demonstrated an unusual pattern—marked serotonin release at heparin concentrations of 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL, which was only partially suppressed by a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. The patient’s anticoagulation therapy was switched to argatroban along with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to ongoing hypercoagulability, therapy was subsequently changed to fondaparinux, which was followed by a gradual increase in platelet count (to 155×10⁹/L by POD62) and resolution of neurological deficits. Laboratory studies established the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets without heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Unique aspects of this aHIT case, in addition to marked thrombocytopenia (platelet count nadir of 13×10⁹/L), were its early onset (preceding POD5) attributed to prior heparin exposure, and a distinct laboratory antibody pattern partially resistant to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For over seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant anticoagulant during heart operations performed with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to advantages such as effective suppression of haemostatic responses to artificial surfaces, elimination that does not rely on renal or hepatic mechanisms, intraoperative anticoagulant monitoring using activated clotting time, and affordability. Nevertheless, UFH is associated with a significant immunologically mediated adverse event known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Following cardiac procedures, HIT occurs in an estimated 0.5%–1.0% of cases, contingent upon the postoperative regimen and the type of heparin used—whether UFH or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)—and is distinguished by a markedly increased risk of thrombosis, with a relative risk approximating 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been characterised as a disorder marked by robust platelet activation via clustering of platelet Fc receptors, prompted by the formation of immune complexes with heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies targeting the positively charged protein, platelet factor 4 (PF4), when it is bound to polyanionic heparin. More recently, recognition has grown regarding certain patients who experience atypical and often severe courses stemming from HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin; this variant has been termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (for review). Herein, we report a case of aHIT with exceptionally prolonged and profound thrombocytopenia, further complicated by thrombotic stroke in the aftermath of cardiac surgery. This case offers new understanding of the aHIT spectrum by illustrating a distinctive and severe serological phenotype, showing partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. The situation also emphasizes diagnostic difficulties presented by early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the unusually rapid onset of aHIT was attributed to UFH exposure prior to surgery. Ultimately, the patient’s course resulted in a positive outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his 70s, who had experienced exertional dyspnoea for two years and had a known history of chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was newly noted to have a systolic murmur. For further evaluation, he underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterisation; however, his apixaban was not discontinued prior to the procedure. Therefore, the cardiologist opted for a transradial arterial access and utilized a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulant dose (administering only 2000 U of UFH). Coronary angiography revealed a 60% narrowing in the obtuse marginal branch, moderate stenosis of the aortic valve, and dilation of the ascending aorta. He was subsequently listed for cardiac surgery in one week.

The patient underwent cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), using a total of 45,000 U UFH for anticoagulation. The operative interventions included replacement of the aortic valve with a #27 bioprosthesis, substitution of the ascending aorta using a #30 Gelweave graft, single saphenous vein bypass grafting to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and resection of the left atrial appendage. The procedure progressed smoothly, with no significant bleeding, no requirement for blood transfusions, and the patient was weaned off CPB without difficulty, demonstrating stable haemodynamics throughout.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the count decreased to 155×10^9/L, representing a modest early decline, which is typical following cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with LMWH began on POD1, using 5000 U dalteparin administered subcutaneously. On POD2, POD3, and POD4, the dalteparin dose was increased to 7500 U each day. Unexpectedly, from POD1 through POD5, the platelet count continued to fall, reaching 50×10^9/L by POD5 (see figure 1). Apart from postoperative delirium, the patient remained stable. Dalteparin was omitted on POD5, and instead, prophylactic fondaparinux at 2.5 mg was initiated (first administration on POD5 at 18:00).

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the patient experienced worsening postoperative delirium and further reduction in platelet count to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (confirmed at 24×10^9/L upon repeat testing), leading to CT imaging of the brain. The scan identified an acute infarct, marked by a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L. The haemoglobin concentration measured 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), consistent with prior values obtained over the previous three days—this pattern aligns with anticipated early postoperative blood loss without evidence of active bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential observed. Reticulocyte count was within the expected range at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin levels were elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), a finding in keeping with the absence of significant haemolysis. The vitamin B12 concentration had returned to normal.

Coagulation testing demonstrated a normal prothrombin time and an international normalised ratio (INR) of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), as well as a normal partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (RR 25–37 seconds). Fibrinogen levels were found to be increased at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), a typical feature in the early postoperative phase as fibrinogen is an acute phase reactant that often rises substantially following surgery. On POD5, a blood specimen was forwarded to a reference laboratory for evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies.

Additional laboratory analyses, including renal and hepatic chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, bilirubin), were unremarkable. Blood and urine cultures were obtained, and chest radiography revealed no significant abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood film from POD7 verified ongoing thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet clumping, schistocytes, or atypical/toxic white cell changes. HIT screening by PF4/polyanion ELISA produced a markedly positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units); however, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), a confirmatory test for platelet activation, returned an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (details discussed below).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, or infection, were considered improbable due to the patient's stable haemodynamics and negative results from blood and urine cultures. Less frequent etiologies, like post-transfusion purpura (no history of transfusion), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (normal haptoglobin level and no initial schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also considered unlikely, as the onset of thrombocytopenia occurred too soon after exposure to any new medications—either those administered solely on the operative day (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or in the postoperative period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

While the temporal pattern of platelet decline seemed too rapid to attribute to unfractionated heparin (UFH) provided intraoperatively during cardiopulmonary bypass, additional investigation revealed that UFH had been administered during preoperative cardiac catheterisation. Given the presence of thrombotic stroke and a strong-positive PF4/polyanion ELISA result, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was considered the most likely diagnosis, presumably initiated by UFH exposure during the preoperative catheterisation procedure. To clarify the findings from the 'indeterminate' SRA, the authors sought further information from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

A critical evaluation of the SRA findings was conducted. The patient’s serum on POD5 induced significant serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% considered normal), which supports the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets. Additionally, no serotonin release was detected at supratherapeutic UFH levels (100 U/mL), a classic HIT hallmark representing the inability to form PF4-containing immune complexes at very high heparin concentrations. Two further SRA observations were made: First, the patient’s serum elicited robust serotonin release without any added UFH (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a pattern typically seen in aHIT but not in conventional heparin-dependent HIT. Second, introducing the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress serotonin release in the presence of 0.1 U/mL UFH and patient serum; instead, release remained elevated at 76%, instead of dropping below 20% as is usual for HIT antibodies, resulting in an initial classification of the SRA as ‘indeterminate.’ The underlying rationale was that the patient had aHIT, but their HIT antibodies were exceptionally reactive, resulting in only partial inhibition with IV.3. As far as we are aware, such an unusual SRA reaction profile has not been documented previously.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosing was stopped, and argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was initiated at a therapeutic dosage of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Although therapeutic doses of fondaparinux are considered an effective 'off-label' option for HIT management, the attending haematologist chose argatroban, which has a shorter half-life, to allow for rapid reversal in the event of hemorrhage, especially given the patient’s recent stroke and markedly low platelet count. Additionally, intravenous immunoglobulin was administered at high doses, with 100 g on two consecutive days and a subsequent 100 g dose, as there was no increase in platelet count after the initial two days of therapy. Fibrinogen and d-dimer levels were monitored serially in order to assess the effectiveness of anticoagulation indirectly; increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer would indicate successful management of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of therapy, fibrinogen concentrations progressively declined from postoperative day 6 through day 17, reaching a low point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around postoperative day 12, and peripheral blood smears showed the presence of schistocytes between postoperative days 9 and 16. Together, these findings were indicative of a severe, evolving consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. Additionally, after initiation of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, the patient's platelet count demonstrated only a modest rise—from 13×10^9/L prior to treatment on postoperative day 7 to a temporary peak of 54×10^9/L on postoperative day 14—representing a considerably smaller increase compared to the typical platelet recovery observed with high-dose intravenous Ig therapy in cases of autoimmune HIT (see review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient's anticoagulant was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. The rationale behind selecting fondaparinux included its convenient once-daily subcutaneous administration compared to argatroban, which requires intravenous infusion and dosage adjustment based on PTT. Additional reasons for the switch were concerns that argatroban may not have sufficiently controlled the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, as evidenced by declining fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the emergence of schistocytes noted during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux functions as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, 1000 units of antithrombin concentrate were administered before starting fondaparinux; the antithrombin level prior to infusion measured slightly below the reference range at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet count progressively returned to normal levels, measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87, along with full restoration of neurological function. Fondaparinux therapy continued through POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at an oral dose of 5 mg twice daily for extended anticoagulation management. Repeated SRA testing remained markedly positive over several months, with later assessments displaying a typical SRA pattern for aHIT, as illustrated in figure 2. The final sample analyzed, drawn at POD124 following normalization of the platelet count, indicated mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, demonstrating 15% serotonin release without UFH and 100% release when UFH was present at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient presented with several uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, all of which were ultimately explained by the diagnosis of severe atypical HIT (aHIT). To begin with, his HIT-related thrombocytopenia developed earlier than the typical timeframe for HIT, which normally occurs between postoperative days (POD) 5 and 10 in individuals undergoing cardiac surgery. HIT-related declines in platelet count before POD5 are observed in only approximately 10% of post-cardiac surgery cases, a pattern associated with recent exposure to heparin prior to the operation; this was also confirmed in our case.

Additionally, this patient experienced an unusually low lowest platelet count of 13×10^9/L, alongside decreasing fibrinogen concentrations, increasing d-dimer values, and the presence of schistocytes during treatment with argatroban anticoagulation. Such findings, indicative of marked HIT-associated hypercoagulability, are characteristic of aHIT.

Thirdly, our patient failed to demonstrate the rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia that is typically observed when patients with aHIT receive high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin; this indicates that the patient was experiencing an exceptionally severe variant of aHIT. In fact, serological findings pointed toward a severe aHIT subtype, as only partial inhibition of platelet activation was observed in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present; to our knowledge, this distinct serological pattern has not previously been associated with aHIT, and recognizing it may be important to prevent missed diagnoses and subsequent adverse clinical events. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately established in our patient, supported by three lines of evidence. Firstly, analysis of serum samples collected later during the prolonged phase of platelet count recovery revealed SRA reactivity profiles consistent with aHIT. Secondly, the final tested sample displayed a heparin-dependent SRA response typical of conventional HIT, suggesting diminishing aHIT antibodies. Thirdly, there was no alternative plausible explanation for the patient’s atypical clinical progression. The patient ultimately experienced a favorable clinical outcome, with full resolution of neurological symptoms.
