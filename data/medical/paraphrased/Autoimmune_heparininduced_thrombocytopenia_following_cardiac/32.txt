Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient underwent cardiac surgery with heparin anticoagulation, after which his platelet count consistently declined, dropping to 50×10⁹/L on postoperative day 5 (POD5), leading to assessment for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging revealed a parietal lobe infarct. An initial screening immunoassay for HIT antibodies was strongly positive, and the serotonin-release assay—a functional platelet activation test—demonstrated an unusual pattern (marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin that was only partially reduced with the addition of a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody). Anticoagulant therapy was switched to argatroban in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing signs of hypercoagulability led to a subsequent switch to fondaparinux, and the platelet count slowly increased, reaching 155×10⁹/L by POD62; neurological symptoms resolved. Laboratory analysis confirmed the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets without heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count of 13×10⁹/L), notable features of this aHIT case included the unusually early postoperative onset (prior to POD5) due to prior heparin exposure and a distinctive aHIT antibody laboratory profile showing partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been the predominant anticoagulant used during heart surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), attributed to its multiple benefits such as suppression of haemostasis linked to artificial surfaces, elimination independent of renal or hepatic pathways, the capability to monitor intraoperative anticoagulation through activated clotting time, and affordability. Nonetheless, UFH carries a significant risk of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-mediated reaction. After cardiac surgery, HIT occurs relatively frequently, affecting approximately 0.5%–1.0% of patients, depending on the duration and nature of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (whether UFH or low-molecular-weight heparin [LMWH] is used), and is distinguished by its strong correlation with thrombotic events, reflected in a relative risk for thrombosis around 15.

Traditionally, HIT is characterized as a condition involving intense activation of platelets via clustering of Fc receptors on the platelet surface, facilitated by immune complexes formed when IgG class heparin-dependent antibodies recognize the basic protein, platelet factor 4 (PF4), after it binds with polyanionic heparin. More recently, it has been identified that certain patients exhibit atypical and frequently severe manifestations, due to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin; this subgroup is designated ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). Here, we present a case of aHIT distinguished by profoundly severe and sustained thrombocytopenia, complicated by thrombotic stroke, in the context of cardiac surgery. This case expands our understanding of aHIT by revealing a distinctive serological profile featuring partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. Furthermore, it illustrates the complexity of diagnosing thrombocytopenia in the immediate postoperative period, as the unusually rapid onset of aHIT in this patient was attributed to preoperative UFH administration. The patient ultimately experienced a positive outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his 70s, who had experienced exertional dyspnoea for two years and had chronic atrial fibrillation managed with apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He underwent a diagnostic cardiac catheterisation without discontinuing apixaban beforehand; therefore, the cardiologist elected to use a transradial artery approach and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulation dose (only 2000 U of UFH). Angiography revealed 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Cardiac surgery was then planned for the following week.

The operative procedure was conducted utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), with anticoagulation maintained via administration of 45,000 U of UFH. The interventions performed included aortic valve replacement with a #27 bioprosthetic valve, replacement of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary artery bypass graft using the saphenous vein to the obtuse marginal branch, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The surgery was completed without complications, significant hemorrhage, or the necessity for transfusion, and the patient was readily weaned from CPB, displaying optimal haemodynamic stability.

The platelet count immediately before surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the count decreased to 155×10^9/L, which constituted a modest reduction commonly seen after cardiac procedures. Standard thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, starting with 5000 U of dalteparin administered subcutaneously. Higher doses of 7500 U were given on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, from POD1 to POD5, the platelet count continued to drop, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient's postoperative course was unremarkable. On POD5, dalteparin was discontinued, and prophylactic fondaparinux at a dose of 2.5 mg was initiated, with the first dose administered at 18:00.

Despite the absence of localized neurological deficits, the patient experienced an aggravation of postoperative delirium, accompanied by a further decrease in platelet count to 26×10^9/L (confirmed on repeat at 24×10^9/L) on POD6. These findings led to a CT scan of the brain, which identified a recent cerebral infarction. The hypodense region measured 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm within the left parietal lobe and extended into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count revealed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, accompanied by a haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), consistent with prior values from the preceding three days and indicative of anticipated early surgical blood loss without evidence of ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was measured at 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count was within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was found to be elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), which is in keeping with the absence of significant haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 returned to normal.

Coagulation parameters demonstrated a normal prothrombin time, yielding a normal international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), and a normal partial thromboplastin time (PTT) at 34 seconds (RR 25–37 s). Fibrinogen was elevated at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), a finding compatible with the early postoperative phase, given the acute phase reactant nature of fibrinogen which often peaks during this period. On POD5, a blood specimen was forwarded to a reference laboratory for HIT antibody analysis.

Additional laboratory assessment showed normal chemistry values (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography demonstrated no significant abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on POD7 confirmed thrombocytopenia (with no platelet clumping), and did not identify red cell fragments (schistocytes) or signs of ‘toxic’ changes in white blood cells. Testing for HIT with the PF4/polyanion ELISA screening immunoassay was markedly positive at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units), whereas the confirmatory platelet activation assay—the serotonin-release assay (SRA)—provided an ‘indeterminate’ result, which is addressed in further detail later.

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of profound thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery—such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, and infection—were considered improbable, given stable haemodynamic parameters and negative results from blood and urine cultures. Likewise, less common etiologies for postoperative thrombocytopenia appeared unlikely: post-transfusion purpura was excluded due to the absence of any transfusions; post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura was discounted because of normal haptoglobin and no initial schistocytes; and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was improbable given the timeline, since the platelet decrease began too early for either perioperative medications (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or drugs administered postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine) to be the cause.

While the temporal relationship between intraoperative UFH exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass and thrombocytopenia was too brief to account for the fall in platelet count, further review revealed the patient had received intraprocedural UFH at the time of preoperative cardiac catheterization. Coupled with the occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was considered the most likely diagnosis, probably precipitated by UFH used during the preoperative catheterization. Additional information regarding the 'indeterminate' SRA result was subsequently requested from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which performed the test.

The SRA findings underwent thorough evaluation. Indicative of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum on postoperative day 5 induced significant serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL unfractionated heparin (UFH), measuring 97% and 99%, respectively (reference range [RR]<20% release). At supratherapeutic UFH concentrations (100 U/mL), serotonin release was absent (0%), a pattern typical of HIT where PF4-containing immune complexes do not form at high UFH levels. Nonetheless, two notable aspects of the SRA data were observed. Firstly, the patient’s serum triggered robust serotonin release even in the absence of heparin (96% at 0 U/mL; RR<20%), which aligns with the features of aHIT but not classic, heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, the use of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 failed to fully suppress serotonin release with patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH; instead of decreasing to below 20%, 76% serotonin release persisted. This contrasts with typical HIT antibody responses, which are generally inhibited to less than 20% serotonin release by IV.3, and led to an initial classification as ‘indeterminate’ for the SRA. The underlying explanation is that the diagnosis was aHIT, yet the patient's HIT antibodies demonstrated unusual reactivity, resulting in only partial inhibition with IV.3, rather than the expected complete suppression. As far as we are aware, this atypical SRA response profile has not been documented previously.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was initiated at a therapeutic dosage of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT range of 55–85 s. Although therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is recognized as an effective off-label option for HIT management, the attending haematologist chose argatroban due to its shorter half-life, providing a safety margin for potential bleeding, particularly considering the patient’s recent stroke and unusually pronounced thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was administered—starting with 100 g on two consecutive days, then a further 100 g dose—after the initial two days of IVIg did not yield a rise in platelet count. Serial fibrinogen and d-dimer measurements were obtained to indirectly assess the effectiveness of anticoagulation; increases in fibrinogen and decreases in d-dimer would support anticoagulant control of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of therapy, the patient's fibrinogen levels showed a gradual decline between POD6 and POD17, reaching a minimum of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer values peaked near POD12, while peripheral blood films revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16. Together, these findings indicated a significant and worsening HIT-related consumptive coagulopathy. In addition, after receiving high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, the patient's platelet count demonstrated only a slight increase, rising from 13×10^9/L on POD7 before IV Ig to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This increment is considerably less pronounced than the typical platelet response observed following high-dose IV Ig therapy in patients with aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulation regimen was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux, administered subcutaneously at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg once daily. Factors influencing this switch included the simpler administration of fondaparinux, requiring only daily subcutaneous injection compared to argatroban’s need for intravenous infusion with PTT monitoring, and concerns that argatroban might not have been adequately addressing the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, as suggested by decreasing fibrinogen levels, increasing d-dimer values, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect factor Xa inhibitor dependent on antithrombin, the patient received a dose of 1000 U AT concentrate prior to fondaparinux initiation; the antithrombin level before infusion was modestly diminished at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count progressively returned to normal values (155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87), and full neurological recovery was achieved. Fondaparinux therapy was maintained up to POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at an oral dosage of 5 mg twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial serotonin-release assay (SRA) testing remained strongly positive over several months, with subsequent profiles displaying features more typical of autoimmune HIT (see figure 2). The final sample tested, collected at POD124 after the platelet count normalized, demonstrated findings indicative of predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release was 15% without UFH but reached 100% with UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited several uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Firstly, his HIT-associated thrombocytopenia developed prior to the typical time frame seen in HIT, which generally occurs between postoperative day 5 (POD5) and POD10 for cardiac surgery patients. In only about 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT does the platelet count decrease before POD5; this earlier onset is associated with perioperative heparin exposure, a pattern that was also observed in this case.

Secondly, the patient's platelet count dropped to an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by declining fibrinogen concentrations, elevated d-dimer measurements, and presence of schistocytes while on argatroban therapy. These manifestations of pronounced HIT-related hypercoagulability are characteristic of aHIT.

Third, our patient did not demonstrate a rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia, which is commonly seen when high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin is administered in cases of aHIT; this suggests that the patient experienced an exceptionally severe variant of this condition. Notably, serological testing indicated a rare form of aHIT, as platelet activation in vitro showed only partial inhibition when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present—a serological pattern not previously documented in aHIT to our knowledge. Awareness of this novel profile may help clinicians avoid overlooking aHIT, which could lead to significant adverse outcomes. Ultimately, the diagnosis of aHIT in our patient was supported by at least three key points. First, sera obtained later, during the extended period of platelet count recovery, displayed SRA reactivity patterns more consistent with aHIT. Second, the final sample analyzed revealed a heparin-dependent SRA reaction profile typical of standard HIT, indicating a decline in aHIT antibody levels. Third, no alternative diagnosis sufficiently accounted for the patient's atypical clinical presentation. Thankfully, the patient experienced a full recovery with no lasting neurological deficits.
