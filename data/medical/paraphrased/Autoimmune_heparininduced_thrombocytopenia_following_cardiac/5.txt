Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin as anticoagulation during cardiac surgery. In the postoperative period, his platelet levels progressively declined, falling to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging identified a parietal lobe stroke. HIT antibody screening through immunoassay produced a strong positive, while the platelet activation (serotonin-release) assay revealed an unusual pattern: robust serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with only partial inhibition achieved using a monoclonal antibody that blocks the platelet Fc receptor. The patient's anticoagulant therapy was modified to argatroban combined with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to continued hypercoagulability, treatment transitioned to fondaparinux, resulting in gradual normalization of the platelet count (reaching 155×10⁹/L by POD62) and resolution of neurologic symptoms. Subsequent laboratory analysis confirmed HIT antibodies with heparin-independent capacity to activate platelets, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to marked thrombocytopenia (nadir of 13×10⁹/L), distinctive features of this aHIT case involved early onset following preoperative heparin exposure and an atypical laboratory profile characterized by only partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been the predominant anticoagulant used during heart surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), primarily due to its multiple benefits. These include effective inhibition of haemostasis induced by artificial surfaces, elimination independent of renal or hepatic pathways, the capability for real-time intraoperative monitoring using activated clotting time, and affordability. Nevertheless, UFH is associated with a significant complication: an immune-mediated reaction known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). HIT is relatively prevalent following cardiac operations, with an incidence estimated between 0.5% and 1.0%, varying according to the nature and length of postoperative heparin prophylaxis (comparing UFH with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). Importantly, HIT demonstrates a pronounced propensity for provoking thrombosis, with an approximate 15-fold increase in relative risk.

Traditionally, HIT has been conceptualized as a condition marked by robust platelet activation via clustering of the platelet Fc receptor, precipitated by immune complexes formed between heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies and the positively charged platelet factor 4 (PF4) when complexed with the negatively charged heparin molecule. However, it has become increasingly apparent in recent literature that some patients exhibit atypical, often severe disease trajectories due to HIT antibodies capable of inducing platelet activation independently of heparin; this particular form is referred to as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). Here, we present a case of aHIT that was characterized by unusually persistent and severe thrombocytopenia complicated by thrombotic stroke following heart surgery. This report sheds light on the broader clinical and serological manifestations of aHIT by illustrating a remarkably severe antibody profile with only partial susceptibility to inhibition via Fc receptor blockade. Moreover, the case underscores the diagnostic complexity posed by thrombocytopenia appearing early after surgery, with the rapid emergence of aHIT in this patient attributed to preoperative UFH exposure. In the end, the clinical outcome was favorable.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, with a two-year history of exertional dyspnoea and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was recently found to have an undiagnosed systolic murmur. He underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterisation; however, he had not discontinued apixaban before the procedure. Therefore, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery approach for the catheterisation, administering a lower than standard dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (only 2000 U of UFH). Angiography revealed a 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Cardiac surgery was planned for the following week.

The patient subsequently underwent cardiac surgery utilizing CPB, with anticoagulation maintained using 45 000 U of UFH. The operative procedure included replacement of the aortic valve with a #27 bioprosthetic valve, substitution of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary artery bypass to the obtuse marginal artery using a saphenous vein graft, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation progressed without complication, with no significant hemorrhage or need for transfusion, and the patient was successfully weaned from CPB demonstrating stable haemodynamics.

The patient's platelet count immediately before surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range (RR) 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the count decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicating only a modest initial drop, which is not unusual following cardiac surgery. Standard thromboprophylaxis using LMWH was started on POD1, administering 5000 U dalteparin subcutaneously, followed by an increased dose of 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, the platelet count continued to decline steadily from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Apart from experiencing postoperative delirium, the patient showed no significant clinical deterioration. On POD5, dalteparin was discontinued and substituted with prophylactic-dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg, first dose at 18:00 on POD5).

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the combination of worsening delirium and a further drop in platelet count to 26×10^9/L (and on repeat, 24×10^9/L) on POD6 warranted a CT scan of the brain. Imaging identified a recent cerebral infarct, with a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm within the left parietal lobe and extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10$^9$/L and a haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range [RR]: 135–180 g/L). This haemoglobin measurement was consistent with those recorded over the prior three days, indicating anticipated early postoperative blood loss without ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10$^9$/L (RR: 4.0–11.0×10$^9$/L), and the differential was within normal limits. The reticulocyte count, at 73×10$^9$/L (RR: 10–100×10$^9$/L), was also within expected parameters. Haptoglobin was notably increased at 3.19 g/L (RR: 0.32–1.97 g/L), which is in line with the absence of active haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 concentration had normalized.

Coagulation testing revealed a prothrombin time and international normalised ratio of 1.1 (RR: 0.9–1.1), both within normal parameters. The partial thromboplastin time (PTT) registered at 34 seconds (RR: 25–37 s), which was also normal. Fibrinogen was markedly increased at 6.59 g/L (RR: 2.00–3.93 g/L), a finding consistent with the acute-phase response typical of the early postoperative period, given that fibrinogen frequently surges during this time. A blood sample collected on POD5 was forwarded to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody evaluation.

Additional laboratory investigations, including serum chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin), were within normal limits. Blood and urine cultures were performed, and chest radiography did not demonstrate any significant abnormalities.

Microscopic examination of a peripheral blood smear on POD7 confirmed thrombocytopenia, without evidence of platelet clumping or the presence of red cell fragments (schistocytes), and there were no 'toxic' morphological changes observed in white blood cells. The PF4/polyanion ELISA, used as a screening immunoassay for HIT, was strongly positive with an optical density (OD) value of 2.2 (RR <0.4 OD units). In contrast, the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA), designed to detect functional HIT antibodies, produced an 'indeterminate' outcome (as detailed later).

Differential diagnosis:

Conventional causes of significant thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery—such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, and infection—appeared improbable, given both the unremarkable hemodynamic profile and negative results from blood and urine cultures. Other uncommon etiologies for post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia, including post-transfusion purpura (not applicable as no blood transfusions were performed), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (with normal haptoglobin levels and initially absent schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were similarly unlikely. The reduction in platelet count transpired too early to be attributable to any newly started postoperative medications, whether administered on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or during the postoperative period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

Although the timing of thrombocytopenia also preceded any potential effects of UFH provided intraoperatively during CPB, further investigation revealed that intraprocedural UFH had been given at the time of preoperative heart catheterization. Combined with the clinical finding of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, HIT was determined to be the most likely diagnosis, most plausibly provoked by the UFH used during the preoperative catheterization. Additional clarification regarding the ‘indeterminate’ SRA was subsequently solicited from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, the entity that performed the test.

A thorough evaluation of the SRA findings was performed. In line with the expected response for platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum collected on POD5 led to pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), and no serotonin release (0%) was detected at a supratherapeutic UFH concentration (100 U/mL). This absence of platelet activation at elevated heparin levels is emblematic of HIT and arises because PF4-containing immune complexes cannot form when UFH concentrations are extremely high. Despite this, the SRA results also revealed two notable atypical findings. Firstly, robust serotonin release was induced by the patient’s serum even in the absence of UFH (96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a response characteristic of aHIT, in contrast to classic heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, applying the monoclonal antibody IV.3, which blocks the Fc receptor, did not entirely suppress serotonin release in the presence of 0.1 U/mL UFH and patient serum: serotonin release was still 76%, whereas typical HIT antibodies would generally reduce this to below 20%. This initially led to an ‘indeterminate’ SRA interpretation. On further analysis, it was determined that the patient did, in fact, have aHIT, but possessed HIT antibodies with atypical reactivity, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3 rather than complete suppression typically observed. To the best of our knowledge, such an unusual SRA reaction pattern has not been described before.

Treatment:

Prophylactic fondaparinux administration was halted, and the patient was initiated on argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, targeting a PTT between 55 and 85 s. While therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is recognized as an effective ‘off-label’ therapy for HIT, the consulting haematologist selected argatroban instead, citing its shorter half-life as advantageous in the context of heightened bleeding risk, considering the patient’s recent stroke and pronounced thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous Ig was administered: an initial 100 g dose on each of two consecutive days, with a third 100 g dose subsequently provided because platelet counts did not rise after the first two days of treatment. To indirectly assess the efficacy of anticoagulation, serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were conducted; trends of increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer were interpreted as indicative of adequate anticoagulant management of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of treatment, there was a steady decline in fibrinogen concentrations between POD6 and POD17, reaching a lowest recorded level of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, while peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of schistocytes between POD9 and POD16. Collectively, these three laboratory findings indicated a severe, worsening HIT-related consumptive coagulopathy. In addition, following high-dose intravenous Ig therapy, the patient's platelet count increased only marginally from 13×10^9/L on POD7 to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This modest increment is considerably less than the typical platelet response observed in patients with aHIT treated with high-dose intravenous Ig, as described in the literature.

On POD14, the patient's anticoagulation regimen was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. Factors influencing this change included the more convenient once-daily subcutaneous delivery of fondaparinux compared to the continuous intravenous infusion of argatroban that requires aPTT monitoring. Another key consideration was the concern that argatroban might not have been adequately managing HIT-related hypercoagulability, as suggested by declining fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Because fondaparinux exerts its effect as an indirect, antithrombin (AT)-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, 1000 U of AT concentrate was administered prior to initiating fondaparinux. The patient’s AT level prior to this infusion was slightly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count progressively returned to the normal range (155×109/L on POD62 and 202×109/L on POD87), and the patient achieved full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was continued until POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dose of 5 mg twice daily by mouth for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial SRA testing remained highly positive over several months, and subsequent assessments revealed an SRA pattern more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The most recent blood sample, collected at POD124 after platelet normalization, demonstrated findings indicative of primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, with serotonin release of 15% in the absence of UFH, compared to 100% in the presence of UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited multiple unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. To begin with, his HIT-associated thrombocytopenia emerged earlier than typically observed; the expected timeframe for HIT onset in patients undergoing cardiac surgery is between POD5 and POD10 (inclusive). Only approximately 10% of individuals with cardiac surgery who develop HIT demonstrate a decline in platelet count prior to POD5—a pattern associated with recent exposure to heparin before surgery, as was evident in this case.

Furthermore, the patient’s platelet count dropped to an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen concentrations, increasing d-dimer values, and evidence of schistocytosis during ongoing argatroban anticoagulation. These manifestations of severe HIT-induced hypercoagulability are hallmarks of aHIT.

Third, unlike the typical rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia seen in aHIT patients treated with high-dose intravenous Ig, our patient experienced a notably slow response, suggesting a particularly aggressive manifestation of aHIT. The serological findings further supported this interpretation: only partial suppression of platelet activation was observed in vitro in the presence of the monoclonal antibody IV.3, indicating an atypically severe aHIT variant. This distinctive serological pattern has not, to our knowledge, been previously described in the context of aHIT and may be important for clinicians to recognize in order to prevent missed diagnoses and their associated risks. Despite this unusual presentation, the diagnosis of aHIT was substantiated in our patient for several reasons. First, subsequent testing of sera collected during the extended phase of platelet count normalization revealed SRA reactivity patterns aligning more closely with classical aHIT. Second, the final serum sample demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA reaction consistent with typical HIT, suggestive of diminishing aHIT antibody levels. Third, alternative diagnoses were systematically excluded given the unique clinical features observed. Ultimately, the patient achieved a full recovery without persistent neurological deficits.
