Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An elderly male patient underwent cardiac surgery with intraoperative heparin anticoagulation. In the postoperative period, his platelet count progressively decreased, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to assessment for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging identified a parietal stroke. The HIT antibody screening immunoassay produced a strongly positive result, and the serotonin-release assay demonstrated an unusual pattern—marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin concentrations with only partial suppression by a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Anticoagulation was transitioned to argatroban in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Continued hypercoagulability led to initiation of fondaparinux, resulting in a gradual platelet count increase to 155×10⁹/L by POD62, alongside resolution of neurological symptoms. Further laboratory analysis verified HIT antibodies capable of platelet activation independent of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Distinctive features of this aHIT case—beyond severe thrombocytopenia (platelet nadir, 13×10⁹/L)—included an early postoperative presentation (prior to POD5) due to preoperative heparin exposure and a distinctive antibody profile displaying partial insensitivity to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

Over the past seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been the principal intraoperative anticoagulant utilized in heart surgeries requiring cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Its widespread use is attributable to several benefits: prevention of haemostatic activation on synthetic surfaces, elimination that does not depend on renal or hepatic mechanisms, real-time monitoring through activated clotting time, and affordability. Nevertheless, UFH is associated with a significant immune-driven adverse event known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). HIT occurs with relative frequency following cardiac procedures, estimated at 0.5%–1.0% incidence, influenced by both the duration and choice of postoperative thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). This condition is particularly concerning due to its marked predisposition to thrombotic complications, as evidenced by a relative risk for thrombosis approaching 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been characterized as a disorder driven by vigorous platelet activation via Fc receptor clustering, facilitated by immune complexes containing IgG antibodies directed against the platelet factor 4 (PF4) when complexed with heparin. In recent years, it has become apparent that certain individuals exhibit atypical and often severe presentations, linked to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independent of heparin. This distinct form of HIT has been termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (for review). This paper discusses a case of aHIT presenting with pronounced and prolonged thrombocytopenia, further complicated by a thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. The case underscores the diversity of aHIT presentations by revealing an unusually severe serological profile, which showed partial insensitivity to Fc receptor inhibition. Furthermore, it illustrates the complexities involved in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the patient’s expedited onset of aHIT was attributed to prior UFH exposure before surgery. The patient ultimately experienced a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A man in his seventies, who had experienced exertional shortness of breath for two years and had longstanding atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was found to have a previously unrecognized systolic heart murmur. For further evaluation, he underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization; however, he did not discontinue his apixaban prior to the procedure. As a result, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery access and utilized a reduced dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (UFH, 2000 units). Angiography revealed 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Plans were made for him to undergo cardiac surgery one week later.

Cardiac surgery was performed using cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and anticoagulation was maintained with 45,000 units of UFH. The operation entailed aortic valve replacement with a size 27 bioprosthetic valve, replacement of the ascending aorta with a size 30 Gelweave graft, single-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting using a saphenous vein to the obtuse marginal artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The procedure proceeded without complications, with minimal blood loss, no need for transfusions, and the patient separated from bypass smoothly, demonstrating excellent hemodynamic stability.

Immediately prior to surgery, the patient’s platelet count measured 210×10⁹/L (reference range 150–400×10⁹/L). On the first postoperative day (POD1), this value had decreased to 155×10⁹/L, a modest early reduction often seen in cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) began on POD1, consisting of 5000 units of dalteparin delivered subcutaneously. This was increased to 7500 units on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, platelet levels continued to fall steadily from POD1 through POD5, reaching 50×10⁹/L (see figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient showed no other notable issues. On POD5, dalteparin was withheld, and prophylactic fondaparinux at a dose of 2.5 mg was started (first dose administered at 18:00 on POD5).

There were no specific neurological deficits, but the intensifying postoperative delirium and a further drop in platelets on POD6 to 26×10⁹/L (repeat value 24×10⁹/L) led to a brain CT scan. Imaging identified an acute infarct within the left parietal lobe, demonstrating a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm that extended to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, accompanied by a haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), which was comparable to haemoglobin measurements recorded over the prior three days—findings in line with anticipated early postoperative blood loss and no indication of continued hemorrhage. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count was within reference parameters at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was found to be elevated at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), suggesting no evidence of acute haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 concentration normalized.

Assessment of coagulation revealed prothrombin time within normal limits, reflected by an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1). Partial thromboplastin time was 34 s (reference range 25–37 s), also within normal boundaries. Fibrinogen concentration was elevated at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), typical of an early postoperative phase in which fibrinogen—a marker of acute phase response—frequently increases substantially. A blood specimen obtained on postoperative day 5 was forwarded to a reference center for HIT antibody assessment.

Other laboratory evaluations, including tests of urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin, were unremarkable. Cultures of blood and urine were collected. Chest radiography did not reveal significant abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on postoperative day 7 supported thrombocytopenia (with no platelet clumping) and showed an absence of schistocytes or abnormal, ‘toxic’ morphology in white blood cells. The PF4/polyanion ELISA, utilized as an initial immunoassay for HIT, demonstrated a pronounced positivity at 2.2 optical density units (reference range <0.4 OD units); however, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), serving as a confirmatory platelet activation test, provided an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (further discussed below).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of significant thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery—such as cardiogenic shock, severe illness, or infection—were considered improbable due to unremarkable clinical findings and laboratory results (stable haemodynamics; negative blood and urine cultures). Unusual causes, including post-transfusion purpura (as no transfusions were administered), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (demonstrated by normal haptoglobin and an initial absence of schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also deemed unlikely since the drop in platelet count occurred sooner than would be expected after exposure to any newly initiated postoperative medications; these included drugs given solely on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) and those introduced postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

While the rapid decline in platelets was also premature relative to the intraoperative UFH administered during cardiopulmonary bypass, further investigation revealed the patient had received intra-arterial UFH during a preoperative cardiac catheterisation. When combined with the occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) emerged as the leading diagnosis, most probably incited by UFH at the time of preoperative catheterisation. Additional clarification regarding the 'indeterminate' serotonin release assay (SRA) was sought from the reporting center, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings underwent thorough evaluation. The patient’s serum collected on POD5 induced pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL unfractionated heparin (UFH) (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range [RR]<20%), indicating the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies. There was no serotonin release (0%) when tested at a supratherapeutic concentration (100 U/mL UFH); this absence of platelet activation at elevated heparin levels is distinctive of HIT and results from an inability to generate PF4-containing immune complexes under such conditions. Additional noteworthy findings emerged from the SRA. First, the patient’s serum stimulated robust serotonin release without heparin (96% release at 0 U/mL; RR<20%), a hallmark of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) rather than traditional heparin-dependent HIT. Second, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 was added, inhibition of serotonin release was incomplete in the setting of patient serum with 0.1 U/mL UFH—76% release persisted, instead of the expected reduction below 20% seen with conventional HIT antibodies; thus, the SRA was initially characterized as ‘indeterminate.’ The conclusion reached was that the patient indeed had aHIT, with antibodies exhibiting unusually strong activity and only partial inhibition by IV.3 compared to typical cases. To date, such an unusual SRA response pattern has not previously been documented.

Treatment:

Prophylactic fondaparinux was stopped, and the patient began receiving therapeutic argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at a dosage of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, with a PTT goal of 55–85 s. While therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is considered an effective off-label anticoagulant option for HIT, the consulting haematologist selected argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which allowed for a quicker reversal in the event of hemorrhage, particularly given the recent stroke and marked thrombocytopenia. Furthermore, high-dose intravenous Ig was administered—100 g for two consecutive days initially, then an additional 100 g dose—because platelet counts did not increase after the first two days of intravenous Ig. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were obtained to indirectly monitor anticoagulant effectiveness; a pattern of increased fibrinogen and decreased d-dimer would suggest the anticoagulant was effectively controlling the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT.

During the initial phase of management, there was a marked decline in fibrinogen levels from postoperative day 6 through postoperative day 17, reaching a lowest value of 2.49 g/L. Peak d-dimer concentrations were observed around postoperative day 12, and the peripheral blood smear demonstrated red cell fragments (schistocytes) between postoperative days 9 and 16. Collectively, these laboratory findings indicated severe and worsening HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. In addition, the patient's platelet count demonstrated only a slight improvement after high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was given, rising from 13×10^9/L prior to IV Ig administration (POD7) to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This increase was substantially less than the typical platelet response observed following high-dose intravenous Ig therapy in cases of aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulation regimen was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg subcutaneously once per day. The decision was influenced by the simpler administration schedule of fondaparinux, which permits once-daily subcutaneous injection compared to argatroban’s requirement for intravenous delivery with PTT-based dose adjustments. There was also apprehension that argatroban may not have adequately managed HIT-induced hypercoagulability, suggested by the decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer levels, and emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, the patient received AT concentrate (1000 U) immediately before starting fondaparinux; at that time, the antithrombin level measured 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL), indicating a slight reduction.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet levels gradually returned to within normal range (155×10^9/L on POD62; 202×10^9/L on POD87), accompanied by complete neurological restoration. Fondaparinux therapy continued until POD65, after which apixaban was reinstated at 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation management. Multiple sequential SRA assessments stayed strongly positive over several months, with some later SRA readings exhibiting a profile more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The final blood specimen evaluated on POD124, post normalization of platelet counts, indicated primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies—serotonin release was 15% without UFH and 100% when UFH was present at a concentration of 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited multiple unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were eventually explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. To begin with, his HIT-associated thrombocytopenia developed earlier than the typical timeframe for HIT, which commonly appears between postoperative days 5 and 10 in individuals after cardiac surgery. Only around 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a platelet decline before postoperative day 5; this early onset is associated with recent preoperative exposure to heparin, as was evident in this case.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count reached an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10⁹/L, accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer values, and the presence of schistocytes on smear while receiving argatroban. These findings, characteristic of pronounced HIT-related hypercoagulability, are suggestive of aHIT.

Third, our patient did not show the rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia typically observed when patients with aHIT are treated with high-dose intravenous Ig; this suggests that the case represented an unusually severe manifestation of aHIT. In fact, serological assessment revealed only partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro in the presence of monoclonal antibody IV.3—a unique finding that, to our knowledge, has not been previously linked to aHIT. Recognizing this new serological pattern could aid in preventing misdiagnosis of aHIT, thereby avoiding potentially negative clinical outcomes. Nevertheless, the diagnosis of aHIT in this patient was ultimately established on at least three grounds. Firstly, analysis of serum samples obtained during the prolonged recovery of platelet counts demonstrated SRA activity patterns that are more typical of aHIT. Secondly, the final specimen exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA result consistent with conventional HIT, indicative of declining aHIT antibodies. Thirdly, no alternative diagnosis adequately accounted for the patient’s unusual disease progression. Notably, the patient achieved a full clinical recovery, with no persistent neurological deficits.
