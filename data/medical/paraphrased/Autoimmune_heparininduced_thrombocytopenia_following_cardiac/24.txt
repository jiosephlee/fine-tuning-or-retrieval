Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

An older male patient received cardiac surgery during which he was anticoagulated with heparin. After the operation, his platelet count progressively decreased, dropping to 50×10⁹/L on postoperative day 5 (POD5). This reduction prompted assessment for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies, and a brain scan identified a parietal stroke. Initial screening with an immunoassay detected HIT antibodies at a high level; subsequent platelet activation testing using the serotonin-release assay revealed an unusual response—marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin concentrations, with only partial suppression observed when a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody was applied. Anticoagulation therapy was modified to argatroban and high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to continued hypercoagulability, the regimen was switched to fondaparinux, leading to a gradual recovery in platelet numbers (155×10⁹/L by POD62), alongside improvement of neurological symptoms. Laboratory analysis corroborated the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin, supporting a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (nadir platelet count of 13×10⁹/L), notable features of this aHIT case involved its early manifestation after surgery (before POD5), attributable to prior heparin exposure, and an atypical antibody profile with partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant anticoagulant during cardiac operations involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to advantages such as inhibition of haemostatic processes induced by contact with artificial surfaces, clearance independent of renal and hepatic function, intraoperative monitoring with activated clotting time, and affordability. Nevertheless, a significant complication associated with UFH is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-mediated adverse effect. HIT occurs relatively frequently after cardiac procedures, with reported rates between 0.5% and 1.0%, contingent on the type and duration of postoperative heparin prophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and is notable for its substantial risk of thrombosis, displaying a relative thrombosis risk around 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been regarded as a condition marked by robust platelet activation through Fc receptor clustering, precipitated by the formation of immune complexes containing heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies targeting platelet factor 4 (PF4), which binds to polyanionic heparin. More recently, recognition has increased of a subset of patients exhibiting atypical and often severe manifestations caused by HIT antibodies capable of heparin-independent platelet activation; this variant has been termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). Here, we present a case of aHIT with strikingly severe and prolonged thrombocytopenia that was further complicated by thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. The case affords new perspectives on aHIT, demonstrating an unusually severe serological profile with only partial susceptibility to Fc receptor inhibition. Additionally, it underscores the complexities of diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the remarkably prompt onset of aHIT in this patient was attributable to preoperative UFH exposure. In the end, the patient experienced a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had experienced exertional dyspnea for two years and had chronic atrial fibrillation managed with apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. Diagnostic cardiac catheterization was performed without prior cessation of apixaban; therefore, the cardiologist selected a transradial artery approach and administered a reduced intraprocedural dose of anticoagulation (UFH 2000 U only). Angiography revealed a 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and enlargement of the ascending aorta. The patient was scheduled for cardiac surgery one week afterwards.

Cardiac surgery was carried out using cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) with anticoagulation achieved via 45,000 U of UFH. The operative procedures involved aortic valve replacement utilizing a #27 bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary artery bypass with a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal branch, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was completed without complication, with minimal blood loss, no blood product requirements, and successful separation from CPB, demonstrating stable haemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately before surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the count dropped to 155×10^9/L, indicating a mild early decrease in platelets commonly observed after cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis using low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, with a 5000 U subcutaneous injection of dalteparin. Higher doses of 7500 U were given on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, the platelet count continued to fall from POD1 through POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient was otherwise stable. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started, with the first dose administered at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite the absence of localized neurological deficits, the combination of worsening postoperative delirium and an additional decrease in platelets to 26×10^9/L (confirmed at 24×10^9/L) on POD6 led to the decision to perform a CT scan of the brain. Imaging identified an acute cerebral infarction, characterized by an area of hypodensity measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm within the left parietal lobe and extending into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

The complete blood count performed on postoperative day 5 (POD5), which showed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, had an associated haemoglobin of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), comparable to haemoglobin levels measured over the prior 3 days and consistent with anticipated early postsurgical blood loss in the absence of ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count at that time was 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count measured 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L), which was unremarkable. Haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting a lack of active haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 level was within normal limits.

Coagulation parameters included a normal prothrombin time and an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), along with a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 s (RR 25–37 s) and a raised fibrinogen concentration at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L). The elevated fibrinogen is indicative of the acute phase response characteristic of the immediate postoperative period. On POD5, a blood sample was forwarded to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody testing.

Additional laboratory assessments revealed normal findings for urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Blood and urine cultures were obtained, and chest radiography demonstrated no significant abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear from POD7 corroborated the thrombocytopenia, demonstrating the absence of platelet clumping and lacking evidence of red cell fragmentation (schistocytes) or any “toxic” changes in white blood cells. The initial HIT screening immunoassay (PF4/polyanion ELISA) showed strong positivity with a value of 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units), though the subsequent confirmatory platelet activation assay (serotonin-release assay [SRA]) produced an indeterminate result (further details provided below).

Differential diagnosis:

Standard causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock, critical illness, and infection, were considered improbable based on the patient's clinical stability and normal hemodynamic parameters, as well as negative results from blood and urine cultures. Similarly, uncommon etiologies such as post-transfusion purpura (no transfusions were administered), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (evidenced by normal haptoglobin levels and absence of schistocytes initially), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (the decline in platelet count occurred too early for both perioperative and postoperative medications—vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid on surgery day; furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine after surgery) were deemed unlikely.

Despite the temporal relationship between the onset of thrombocytopenia and intraoperative UFH during cardiopulmonary bypass appearing inconsistent, further review revealed administration of UFH during preoperative cardiac catheterization. In conjunction with the occurrence of a thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, a diagnosis of HIT was considered most likely, probably precipitated by the preoperative UFH exposure. Additional clarification regarding the 'indeterminate' serotonin release assay (SRA) result was sought from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings underwent thorough evaluation. Supporting the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s postoperative day 5 serum produced robust serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20% release), while serotonin release was absent (0%) at the supra-therapeutic concentration of UFH (100 U/mL). This lack of platelet activation at elevated UFH levels is indicative of HIT, attributable to the inability to form PF4-immune complexes under such conditions. Nonetheless, two additional characteristics in the SRA results were observed. First, the patient’s serum elicited significant serotonin release without UFH present (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; RR <20%); this pattern is seen in aHIT but not in traditional heparin-dependent HIT. Second, use of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress serotonin release when the patient’s serum was tested at 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release was 76% with IV.3, rather than the expected reduction to below 20% associated with HIT antibodies, which prompted the initial classification of the SRA as ‘indeterminate’. The rationale provided was that the patient indeed had aHIT, but the HIT antibodies demonstrated unusual reactivity, with only partial inhibition by IV.3 instead of the typical degree. To the best of our knowledge, this atypical SRA profile has not previously been documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and the patient was started on therapeutic-dose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at 0.75 mcg/kg/min, targeting a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Although therapeutic doses of fondaparinux are known to be an effective off-label option for managing HIT, the attending hematologist opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, allowing for easier management in the event of bleeding, particularly given the patient’s recent stroke and pronounced thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was administered—100 g on each of two consecutive days, and a third dose of 100 g was provided as there was no significant rise in platelet count after the initial two days of IVIg treatment. Serial monitoring with fibrinogen and d-dimer assays was conducted to assess the efficacy of anticoagulation, since increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer levels would suggest that the anticoagulation was effectively controlling the hypercoagulability associated with HIT.

During the initial treatment phase, fibrinogen levels showed a gradual decline between POD6 and POD17, reaching a minimum of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer concentrations peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood films revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16. Together, these observations indicated the development of severe, progressive consumptive coagulopathy related to HIT. Additionally, the patient's platelet count experienced only a slight elevation following high-dose intravenous Ig therapy, increasing from 13×10^9/L on POD7 to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14—an increment notably smaller than what is typically reported after administration of high-dose intravenous Ig for aHIT patients (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulant was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. The rationale for selecting fondaparinux included its simpler dosing regimen, requiring only once-daily subcutaneous injection compared to the PTT-adjusted intravenous infusion with argatroban, and apprehension regarding argatroban's potentially inadequate management of HIT-related hypercoagulability, as evidenced by decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer levels, and the presence of schistocytes observed while on argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect antithrombin (AT)-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, AT concentrate (1000 U) was given before initiating fondaparinux; the AT level prior to infusion was slightly below normal at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

Platelet levels progressively returned to the normal range (155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87), and the patient achieved full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was maintained through POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial SRA assessments remained markedly positive for several months, and several later SRA results exhibited an SRA pattern typical of aHIT (figure 2). The most recent blood sample analyzed, taken at POD124 after normalization of platelet count, revealed findings indicative of mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release was 15% without UFH, but rose to 100% in the presence of UFH at a concentration of 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

This patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were ultimately ascribed to a diagnosis of severe aHIT. To begin with, the development of his HIT-associated thrombocytopenia occurred earlier than typically observed, which for cardiac surgery patients is most often noted between postoperative day 5 and day 10. Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients with HIT show a decline in platelet counts prior to postoperative day 5, a pattern that is associated with perioperative heparin exposure—an exposure present in our case.

In addition, the patient experienced a marked reduction in platelet count to a nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by declining fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and schistocytes while on argatroban. These findings of pronounced HIT-related hypercoagulability are suggestive of aHIT.

Third, our patient failed to demonstrate a rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia, which is commonly observed in individuals with aHIT treated with high-dose intravenous Ig; this suggests that the patient was affected by an exceptionally severe variant of aHIT. In fact, serological findings indicative of a particularly severe form of aHIT included only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present; to our knowledge, this distinct serological pattern has not previously been described in the context of aHIT, and recognizing it may be important in preventing missed diagnoses and subsequent negative outcomes. Nonetheless, the diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately established in this case, based on several lines of evidence. First, sera obtained later, during the extended phase of platelet count recovery, displayed SRA reactivity profiles that were more aligned with typical aHIT. Second, the final serum sample analyzed exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA response characteristic of conventional HIT, corresponding to a decline in aHIT antibody levels. Third, alternative diagnoses were not sufficient to account for the patient’s atypical clinical progression. The patient, fortunately, achieved a full recovery with no persistent neurological deficits.
