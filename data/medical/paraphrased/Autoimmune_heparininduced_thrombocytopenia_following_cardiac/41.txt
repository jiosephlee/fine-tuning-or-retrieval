Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient underwent cardiac surgery during which heparin was used for anticoagulation; after the procedure, his platelet count progressively declined, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), leading clinicians to test for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging detected a stroke in the parietal region. HIT antibody screening via immunoassay was strongly positive, while the serotonin-release assay, used to assess platelet activation, demonstrated an unusual pattern—significant serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin that was only partially inhibited by a monoclonal antibody targeting the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulation therapy was changed to argatroban together with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Because hypercoagulability persisted, fondaparinux was initiated, resulting in gradual improvement of platelet counts to 155×10⁹/L by POD62 and resolution of neurological symptoms. Laboratory findings identified HIT antibodies with heparin-independent, platelet-activating effects, supporting a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Distinguishing features in this aHIT case, aside from severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count of 13×10⁹/L), include an onset early after surgery (before POD5) likely related to heparin exposure prior to the operation, as well as an uncommon antibody laboratory profile demonstrating partial resistance to blockade of the Fc receptor.

Background:

For almost seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been the primary anticoagulant used during heart surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to its numerous benefits such as effective suppression of haemostatic reactions induced by artificial surfaces, elimination pathways not reliant on the kidneys or liver, real-time intraoperative monitoring via activated clotting time, and economical pricing. Despite these advantages, UFH is associated with a notable immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). The incidence of HIT after cardiac surgery is approximately 0.5%–1.0%, influenced by the choice and duration of postoperative heparin prophylaxis—whether UFH or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)—and is characterized by a pronounced tendency towards thrombosis, with a thrombosis relative risk nearing 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been described as a condition driven by marked platelet activation due to clustering of platelet Fc receptors; this process is triggered by immune complexes formed from immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies that target platelet factor 4 (PF4) when complexed with heparin. More recently, it has become apparent that certain patients experience distinctive and often severe manifestations related to HIT antibodies possessing platelet-activating capability independent of heparin; this variant is termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). The following report details a case of aHIT with notably profound and prolonged thrombocytopenia, further complicated by thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. This case expands understanding of aHIT, demonstrating an exceptionally severe serological presentation with only partial response to Fc receptor blockade. In addition, it illustrates the complexities in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as unusually rapid onset of aHIT was attributed to preoperative UFH exposure. Despite these challenges, the patient achieved a successful outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was noted to have a newly identified systolic murmur. Despite not discontinuing apixaban prior to diagnostic heart catheterisation, the procedure was performed using a transradial artery technique and a reduced intraoperative anticoagulation dose (2000 U UFH). Angiography revealed 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and enlargement of the ascending aorta; cardiac surgery was consequently planned for the following week.

The patient underwent cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, using 45,000 U UFH for anticoagulation. The operation consisted of aortic valve replacement with a #27 bioprosthesis, ascending aorta replacement with a #30 Gelweave graft, a single saphenous vein bypass to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The procedure was uncomplicated, with minimal blood loss and no transfusions required, and the patient was weaned from cardiopulmonary bypass without difficulty and displayed stable haemodynamics.

The patient's platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), this dropped to 155×10^9/L, indicating a mild decrease commonly seen after cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis was initiated on POD1, consisting of 5000 U dalteparin given via subcutaneous injection. Higher doses (7500 U) were administered on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, from POD1 to POD5, the platelet count continued to fall, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Clinically, the patient remained stable except for postoperative delirium. On POD5, dalteparin was withheld and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started, with the first dose administered at 18:00.

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, deterioration in delirium and further reduction in platelet count on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (repeat value 24×10^9/L) led to brain CT imaging. The scan revealed an acute cerebral infarction, with a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count indicated a platelet count of 50×10^9/L and a haemoglobin value of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), which matched haemoglobin levels seen over the prior three days, corresponding to anticipated early surgical blood loss without further evidence of bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocytes measured 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L), within normal limits. Haptoglobin was raised at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), aligning with no signs of haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 had returned to normal values.

Coagulation testing showed normal prothrombin time and an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), a partial thromboplastin time of 34 s (reference range 25–37 s), and a fibrinogen concentration of 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), indicative of the acute phase response commonly seen shortly after surgery, given the marked rise in fibrinogen. On postoperative day 5, a blood sample was submitted to a referral laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody testing.

Other laboratory results were normal, including renal and liver chemistries (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, bilirubin). Both blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography showed no significant findings.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear performed on postoperative day 7 confirmed thrombocytopenia (with no platelet aggregation), and revealed no schistocytes or 'toxic' alterations in white blood cells. The immunoassay screening for HIT, the PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), was markedly elevated at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units), whereas the serotonin-release assay (SRA), which serves as a confirmatory functional test, returned an 'indeterminate' result (to be further discussed).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of pronounced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock/critical illness and infection, appeared improbable, considering both clinical status and laboratory data (hemodynamics were stable; blood and urine cultures yielded no growth). Unusual etiologies such as post-transfusion purpura (in absence of transfused blood products), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (evidenced by normal haptoglobin and no initial schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia were similarly deemed unlikely—the timeline for platelet decrease preceded exposure to both perioperative medications (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) administered solely on the operative day and postoperative medications (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

Despite the rapidity of platelet decline appearing too soon to attribute to intraoperative UFH used during cardiopulmonary bypass, further review revealed UFH had also been given during preoperative cardiac catheterization. The combination of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA led to HIT being considered the leading diagnosis, presumed to be induced by UFH administered before surgery during cardiac catheterization. Clarification of the ‘indeterminate’ SRA findings was solicited from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which provided the result.

A thorough evaluation of the SRA findings was performed. The patient's POD5 serum elicited pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), indicative of platelet-activating HIT antibodies. Moreover, no serotonin release occurred (0%) when supratherapeutic UFH was used (100 U/mL), which is a hallmark of HIT, since PF4-containing immune complexes cannot form at high UFH levels. Nonetheless, two unusual aspects of the SRA results emerged. First, the serum triggered robust serotonin release even without UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a phenomenon typical of aHIT and not classical heparin-dependent HIT. Second, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) was added, it did not fully block serotonin release induced by the patient's serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH, resulting in 76% release in the presence of IV.3, rather than reducing serotonin release below 20% as is usually expected for HIT antibodies. This led to an initial SRA interpretation as ‘indeterminate’. The subsequent rationale clarified that the patient’s diagnosis was indeed aHIT, but the HIT antibodies were unusually potent, yielding only partial inhibition with IV.3 rather than complete suppression. To date, this atypical SRA response profile has not been previously documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic doses was stopped, and argatroban—a direct thrombin inhibitor—was initiated at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, targeting a PTT of 55–85 s. Despite therapeutic fondaparinux being an effective off-label option for HIT management, the haematologist elected to use argatroban due to its shorter half-life, allowing rapid reversal if bleeding developed, especially considering the recent stroke and unusually pronounced thrombocytopenia. Additionally, intravenous immunoglobulin was administered in high doses: 100 g daily for two days initially, followed by another 100 g dose, since platelet counts did not show an initial rise during the first two days of IVIg. Fibrinogen and d-dimer levels were monitored serially to indirectly assess anticoagulation, as increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer would support successful anticoagulant suppression of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

The initial management was marked by a gradual decline in fibrinogen concentrations from postoperative day 6 (POD6) through postoperative day 17 (POD17), reaching a lowest level of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears identified the presence of schistocytes between POD9 and POD16; together, these laboratory abnormalities indicated a severe, advancing HIT-related consumptive coagulopathy. Additionally, the platelet count demonstrated only a slight rise after the administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, increasing from 13×10^9/L prior to IVIg administration (POD7) to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L (POD14), which is considerably lower than the expected platelet response observed in patients treated with high-dose IVIg for acute HIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulant therapy was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux, administered at a therapeutic dosage of 10 mg subcutaneously once per day. The switch was prompted by several factors: fondaparinux’s simpler dosing regimen, requiring only a daily subcutaneous injection instead of the continuous intravenous administration and PTT-based adjustments necessary with argatroban, and concerns that argatroban may not have been adequately addressing HIT-related hypercoagulability. This concern was based on observed laboratory findings, including decreasing fibrinogen levels, increasing d-dimer values, and the presence of schistocytes, all noted while the patient was receiving argatroban. Because fondaparinux functions as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to starting fondaparinux. At this time, the patient’s antithrombin activity was slightly low, measured at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count steadily returned to within normal limits (155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L at POD87), and the patient regained full neurological function. Fondaparinux therapy was continued up to POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dose of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Consecutive SRA tests remained strongly positive over several months; subsequent SRA evaluations demonstrated a pattern more typical for aHIT (see figure 2). The most recent blood sample, collected on POD124 after normalization of the platelet count, revealed results indicative of mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release was 15% without UFH, while it reached 100% with UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

This patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serologic characteristics, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Initially, thrombocytopenia associated with HIT manifested earlier than is typical, as the majority of cardiac surgery patients experience the onset between postoperative days (POD) 5 and 10. Only approximately 10% of individuals who undergo cardiac surgery and develop HIT display a decline in platelet count before POD5—a pattern associated with recent pre-surgical heparin exposure, which was also observed in this case.

Additionally, the patient's platelet count decreased to an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreased fibrinogen levels, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. These findings reflect the profound hypercoagulable state seen in severe HIT and are indicative of aHIT.

Third, the patient did not demonstrate rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia, which typically occurs when patients with aHIT receive high-dose intravenous Ig; this suggests a particularly severe manifestation of the condition. Supporting this, serological findings pointed to an unusually severe variant of aHIT, as only partial suppression of platelet activation was seen in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present. This distinctive serological pattern has not, to our knowledge, been previously described in connection with aHIT; recognizing it may help prevent missed diagnoses of aHIT, thereby reducing the risk of negative clinical outcomes. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT was definitively established in our patient, based on three key points. First, sera collected during the extended period of platelet count recovery exhibited SRA reactivity patterns more in line with aHIT. Second, the most recent sample demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA response characteristic of conventional HIT, indicating a decline in aHIT antibodies. Third, no alternative diagnosis was reasonable for the patient’s atypical clinical presentation. Ultimately, the patient experienced an excellent recovery, with no lasting neurological deficits.
