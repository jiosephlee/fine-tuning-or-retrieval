Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient underwent cardiac surgery using heparin for anticoagulation; after surgery, his platelet count progressively declined, falling to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5). This decrease led to testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Imaging of the brain detected a parietal stroke. An initial screening immunoassay for HIT antibodies produced a markedly positive result. The platelet activation assessment (serotonin-release assay) revealed an unusual pattern: there was pronounced serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, and only incomplete suppression with a monoclonal antibody that blocks the platelet Fc receptor. The anticoagulation regimen was adjusted to argatroban combined with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to ongoing hypercoagulability, the patient was switched to fondaparinux, after which there was a slow improvement in platelet counts (155×10⁹/L by POD62), accompanied by resolution of neurological deficits. Subsequent laboratory testing identified HIT antibodies that activated platelets independently of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to marked thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), notable features of this aHIT case included unusually early postoperative onset—attributable to pre-surgical heparin administration—and an uncommon laboratory aHIT antibody profile that demonstrated partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant intraoperative anticoagulant in cardiac surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). This preference is attributable to UFH's several key benefits, such as its ability to inhibit haemostasis on artificial surfaces, its clearance mechanisms that do not depend on renal or hepatic pathways, the capacity to monitor its anticoagulant effect intraoperatively via activated clotting time, and its relatively low expense. Despite these advantages, UFH is associated with a significant immune-mediated complication—heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). HIT occurs relatively frequently after cardiac procedures, with an incidence ranging from 0.5\% to 1.0\%, influenced by both the duration and modality of heparin-based postoperative thromboprophylaxis (comparing UFH and low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). HIT is especially notable due to its strong linkage with thrombotic events, conferring an approximate fifteen-fold increased risk of thrombosis.

Traditionally, HIT is understood as a syndrome driven by marked platelet activation via platelet Fc receptor aggregation, initiated when heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies target platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to the polyanionic heparin molecule. Recently, however, clinical recognition has grown for cases exhibiting atypical, and frequently severe, disease progression as a result of HIT antibodies that are capable of inducing platelet activation independent of heparin. This variant, known as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT), represents a distinct subset within the broader HIT spectrum (see review). Here, we present a case involving aHIT characterized by exceptionally profound and persistent thrombocytopenia, further complicated by a thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. This case expands the clinical understanding of aHIT by illustrating an unprecedentedly severe serological manifestation, marked by partial insensitivity to Fc receptor inhibition. Moreover, it underscores the diagnostic complexity associated with thrombocytopenia in the immediate postoperative period, as the unusually early presentation of aHIT in this patient was attributable to preoperative exposure to UFH. Ultimately, the patient experienced a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, presenting with a two-year history of exertional dyspnoea and chronic atrial fibrillation (maintained on oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was newly identified as having a systolic murmur. Despite not discontinuing apixaban before his scheduled diagnostic heart catheterisation, the procedure was carried out via a transradial artery access point. To mitigate bleeding risk, the intraprocedural anticoagulation was reduced (administering only 2000 U of UFH). Angiography revealed 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta, leading to a recommendation for cardiac surgery the following week.

Cardiac surgery was undertaken with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and anticoagulation was maintained using 45 000 U of UFH. The operative procedures consisted of aortic valve replacement with a #27 bioprosthetic valve; replacement of the ascending aorta utilizing a #30 Gelweave graft; single-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting with a saphenous vein conduit to the obtuse marginal branch; and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation progressed smoothly, characterized by minimal blood loss, no necessity for transfusion, and an uncomplicated weaning from bypass with stable haemodynamics.

The patient’s platelet count immediately before surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range (RR) 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), this value had decreased to 155×10^9/L, representing only a modest reduction commonly observed after cardiac surgery. Standard thromboprophylaxis using LMWH was initiated on POD1 with a subcutaneous injection of 5000 U dalteparin, subsequently increased to 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Despite these measures, the platelet count continued to fall, reaching 50×10^9/L by POD5 (see figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient exhibited no other significant complications. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic-dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was introduced at 18:00 that same day.

Although there were no focal neurological deficits detected, the combination of worsening postoperative delirium and a further decline in platelet count on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (confirmed on repeat testing at 24×10^9/L) led to a CT scan of the brain. Imaging identified an acute cerebral infarction characterized by a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, with involvement extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On POD5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, while haemoglobin measured 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), which matched the haemoglobin values observed over the previous three days—findings in line with anticipated early surgical blood loss in the absence of ongoing haemorrhage. The white blood cell count stood at 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) and featured a normal differential. Reticulocyte levels were within expected limits at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin concentration was elevated to 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no evident haemolysis. Additionally, serum vitamin B12 was found to be within the normal range.

Coagulation assessment demonstrated a normal prothrombin time, with an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1) and a partial thromboplastin time of 34 s (RR 25–37 s). Fibrinogen levels were markedly increased at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), an anticipated postoperative finding as fibrinogen, being an acute phase reactant, generally shows significant elevations following surgery. A blood specimen collected on POD5 was forwarded to a specialized laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody testing.

Additional laboratory investigations showed normal results for metabolic parameters including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Blood and urine cultures were obtained, and chest radiography did not identify significant pathology.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on POD7 corroborated the thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet clumping, red cell fragmentation (schistocytes), or notable morphological changes in white blood cells. The PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) used for initial HIT screening returned a robustly positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units). However, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), utilized for confirmatory testing of HIT, produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (explored further below).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of pronounced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, or infection, appeared improbable in light of the patient's stable haemodynamics and consistently negative blood and urine cultures. Similarly, rare etiologies—including post-transfusion purpura (in the absence of transfused blood products), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (as haptoglobin levels were normal and schistocytes were initially absent), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia—were considered unlikely, as the timing of thrombocytopenia onset was not compatible with the administration of any postoperative medications, whether restricted to the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or administered during the recovery period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

While the rapid decline in platelet count did not temporally coincide with intraoperative UFH given during CPB, further investigation revealed exposure to intraprocedural UFH at the time of preoperative heart catheterisation. When considered alongside the development of a thrombotic stroke and a markedly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, HIT emerged as the leading diagnosis, plausibly initiated by the UFH received during heart catheterisation. Additional clarification regarding the 'indeterminate' SRA was sought from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which provided the original laboratory report.

A critical assessment was performed on the SRA findings. The patient’s POD5 serum demonstrated robust platelet activation, evidenced by marked serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release). Notably, no serotonin release (0%) occurred at supratherapeutic UFH concentrations (100 U/mL), a hallmark of HIT due to the prevention of PF4-immune complex formation at excessive heparin levels. Two further observations from the SRA data are significant. Firstly, in the absence of UFH, the patient’s serum still induced a high degree of serotonin release (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a finding typical of aHIT but not traditional heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, the introduction of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 did not fully suppress serotonin release in the presence of patient serum and 0.1 U/mL UFH; instead, 76% serotonin release was recorded with IV.3, whereas classic HIT antibodies would generally result in suppression to below 20%. This led to an initial interpretation of the SRA as ‘indeterminate’. Ultimately, the patient was diagnosed with aHIT, and it was posited that the HIT antibodies exhibited atypically strong reactivity, which resulted in only partial inhibition by IV.3, as opposed to the complete inhibition commonly observed. To the best of our knowledge, this unusual SRA response pattern has not been previously documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux, administered at a prophylactic dose, was halted, and argatroban—a direct thrombin inhibitor—was introduced for anticoagulation at a dosage of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 s. While therapeutic dosing of fondaparinux represents an effective off-label option for managing HIT, the attending haematologist opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, considering both the patient’s recent cerebrovascular event and the unusually profound thrombocytopenia, which could increase bleeding risk. The patient also received high-dose intravenous Ig, starting with 100 g on each of two days and then a third 100 g dose, as platelet counts failed to rise after the initial two administrations. To monitor the response to anticoagulation, serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were obtained; effective suppression of HIT-associated hypercoagulability would be indicated by an increase in fibrinogen and a decrease in d-dimer levels.

During the initial phase of treatment, fibrinogen concentrations declined steadily from POD6 to POD17, reaching a lowest point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of schistocytes between POD9 and POD16. Collectively, these observations were indicative of a pronounced and advancing consumptive coagulopathy associated with severe HIT. Additionally, the patient showed only a slight increase in platelet count after receiving high-dose intravenous Ig—rising from 13×10^9/L prior to Ig administration (POD7) to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L by POD14. This increment is significantly less than the typical platelet recovery expected after high-dose intravenous Ig therapy in cases of aHIT (see review).

On POD14, the patient’s anticoagulant regimen was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg once daily, administered subcutaneously. The rationale for selecting fondaparinux included the convenience of once-daily subcutaneous administration compared to argatroban, which necessitates continuous intravenous infusion with PTT monitoring. Furthermore, there were concerns that argatroban might not be adequately managing HIT-associated hypercoagulability, as evidenced by a decline in fibrinogen, an increase in d-dimer levels, and the presence of schistocytes observed during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux functions as an indirect antithrombin (AT)-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, a supplemental dose of AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered before initiating fondaparinux, as the patient’s AT level was modestly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range: 0.80–1.40 U/mL) prior to infusion.

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count steadily returned to the normal range (155×109/L at POD62 and 202×109/L at POD87), and the patient experienced full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was maintained up to POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dosage of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial SRA assessments continued to be strongly positive over the course of several months, with subsequent profiles displaying features more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The most recent blood sample, collected at POD124 after normalization of the platelet count, demonstrated findings indicative of primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release was 15% in the absence of UFH, increasing to 100% in the presence of UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient presented with several uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. To begin with, the decline in platelet count associated with HIT occurred prior to the typical time frame, which for individuals undergoing cardiac surgery is generally between POD5 and POD10. Fewer than 10% of post-cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a reduction in platelet count before POD5, a pattern frequently correlated with recent preoperative exposure to heparin, as seen in this patient.

Additionally, this patient’s platelet count dropped to an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer, and schistocytes noted during anticoagulation with argatroban. These findings, indicative of pronounced HIT-related hypercoagulability, are characteristic of aHIT.

Third, our patient’s thrombocytopenia did not resolve rapidly following administration of high-dose intravenous Ig, a response that is commonly seen in cases of aHIT. This suggests that the patient experienced an especially severe manifestation of the condition. In fact, serological testing revealed only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro when exposed to the monoclonal antibody IV.3, indicating an atypically severe subtype of aHIT; as far as we are aware, this unique serological pattern has not previously been associated with aHIT in the literature. Recognizing this distinct serological presentation may help prevent underdiagnosis of aHIT, which could have serious clinical implications. Despite these complexities, the diagnosis of aHIT was conclusively established in this case, based on three main findings. First, analysis of later serum samples collected during the patient’s extended recovery from thrombocytopenia showed SRA reactivity profiles aligning more closely with those seen in conventional aHIT. Second, the final serum sample displayed a heparin-dependent SRA response typical of classic HIT, consistent with the anticipated decline of aHIT antibodies over time. Third, alternative diagnoses were thoroughly considered and excluded as explanations for the patient’s unusual clinical progression. Ultimately, the patient recovered completely and did not experience any lasting neurological impairment.
