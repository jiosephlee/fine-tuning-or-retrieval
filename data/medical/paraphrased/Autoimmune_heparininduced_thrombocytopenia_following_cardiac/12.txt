Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin anticoagulation during cardiac surgery; afterward, his platelet count progressively decreased, reaching 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to an evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging identified a parietal lobe stroke. The initial immunoassay for HIT antibodies was markedly positive; however, the platelet activation test (serotonin-release assay) showed an unusual result: there was robust serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, but only partial suppression with a monoclonal antibody targeting the platelet Fc receptor. The patient’s anticoagulation regimen was altered to argatroban along with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Because of persistent hypercoagulability, therapy was later switched to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count gradually returned to 155×10⁹/L by POD62; the patient’s neurological deficits resolved. Further laboratory tests verified the presence of HIT antibodies that could activate platelets without the need for heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Notably, the case was characterized by profound thrombocytopenia (nadir, 13×10⁹/L), an unusually early onset after surgery (before POD5) linked to heparin given preoperatively, and a distinctive laboratory antibody pattern that showed partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the primary anticoagulant administered during heart surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to benefits such as its efficacy in suppressing haemostatic responses to artificial surfaces, elimination pathways independent of renal and hepatic function, capability for intraoperative anticoagulation assessment via activated clotting time, and its economical cost. Nonetheless, a significant complication linked to UFH use is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-mediated reaction. After cardiac surgery, HIT arises with a frequency estimated between 0.5% and 1.0%, which varies based on both the length and type of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (UFH in comparison to low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). This condition is particularly concerning due to its marked increase in thrombotic events, carrying an approximate 15-fold elevated risk.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) has traditionally been characterized as a condition marked by significant platelet activation via Fc receptor aggregation on platelets, initiated by immune complexes formed between heparin-dependent IgG antibodies and the positively charged platelet factor 4 (PF4) protein, when it is complexed with the highly negative heparin molecule. Nonetheless, emerging evidence indicates that certain patients experience atypical and often severe manifestations of HIT due to antibodies that can activate platelets independently of heparin exposure; this particular subset, termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT), has been increasingly recognized in the literature (for review). We present herein a case of aHIT that demonstrated notably profound and persistent thrombocytopenia, which was further complicated by thrombotic stroke, following cardiac surgery. This case adds to the current understanding of aHIT by detailing an unusually severe serologic profile, including a partial lack of response to blockade of platelet Fc receptors. Additionally, the report underscores the diagnostic complexity associated with thrombocytopenia occurring soon after surgery, as the patient’s early development of aHIT was attributable to preoperative administration of unfractionated heparin (UFH). The patient ultimately experienced a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had experienced exertional dyspnea for two years and had a history of persistent atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a newly identified systolic murmur. Diagnostic cardiac catheterization was performed; however, the patient had not interrupted his apixaban prior to the procedure. To address this, the cardiologist opted for a transradial arterial access, utilizing a reduced intraoperative anticoagulation dose (administering only 2000 U of unfractionated heparin). Coronary angiography revealed a 60% narrowing in the obtuse marginal branch, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Plans were made for him to undergo cardiac surgery one week following this evaluation.

Cardiac surgery was carried out utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and anticoagulation was maintained with 45,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). The operative procedures included replacement of the aortic valve with a size 27 bioprosthetic valve, substitution of the ascending aorta using a size 30 Gelweave graft, a solitary coronary artery bypass employing a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal branch, as well as excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation proceeded smoothly, without significant blood loss or requirement for transfusion, and the patient was weaned from bypass without difficulty, displaying optimal haemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately before surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). By postoperative day 1 (POD1), it had decreased to 155×10^9/L, which constitutes a modest drop commonly observed after cardiac procedures. Standard thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1 with a subcutaneous injection of 5000 units of dalteparin. This dose was increased to 7500 units on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, platelet levels continued to decline steadily from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from experiencing postoperative delirium, the patient remained clinically stable. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started instead (first dose administered at 18:00 on POD5).

Despite the absence of localized neurological deficits, the patient developed worsening postoperative delirium. Alongside a further decrease in platelet count to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (with a repeat count of 24×10^9/L), these findings led to a CT scan of the brain. Imaging demonstrated an acute cerebral infarction in the left parietal lobe, characterized by an area of hypodensity measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm extending through to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, with a haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), mirroring haemoglobin levels from the prior three days, which reflected expected early postoperative blood loss without evidence of ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was measured at 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), and the differential was within normal limits. Reticulocyte count was within the reference range at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was increased to 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no signs of overt hemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 level had normalized.

Coagulation testing revealed a prothrombin time within normal limits and an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1). The partial thromboplastin time (PTT) was also within the normal range at 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds). Fibrinogen levels, however, were elevated at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), consistent with the acute phase response seen in the early postoperative period, during which fibrinogen concentrations typically rise significantly. On postoperative day 5 (POD5), a blood specimen was submitted to a reference laboratory for evaluation of HIT antibodies.

Other blood work included normal chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were drawn. A chest X-ray showed no major abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear obtained on POD7 verified thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet clumping, schistocytes, or abnormal 'toxic' changes in white blood cells. The initial immunological test for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), the PF4/polyanion ELISA, showed a markedly elevated value of 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units). However, the confirmatory platelet activation assay, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), produced an 'indeterminate' result, which will be elaborated on later.

Differential diagnosis:

Frequently encountered causes of significant thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, or infection, appeared improbable given the clinical presentation and laboratory findings (stable haemodynamics; both blood and urine cultures were negative). Less common causes, including post-transfusion purpura (the patient did not receive any blood transfusions), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (haptoglobin levels were within normal limits, and schistocytes were initially absent), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also considered unlikely. This was because the decline in platelet count occurred too soon after exposure to newly initiated postoperative medications, with drugs administered exclusively on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid) and those introduced during the postoperative phase (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine) not fitting the expected timeline.

While the decrease in platelet count also happened earlier than would be expected from UFH exposure during CPB, further investigation revealed that UFH had been given during preoperative cardiac catheterization. In view of the thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, HIT was considered the most likely diagnosis, probably precipitated by UFH administered at the time of the preoperative catheterization. Additional information regarding the ‘indeterminate’ SRA result was requested from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which performed the testing.

The SRA findings were evaluated in detail. In alignment with the existence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum collected on postoperative day 5 produced marked serotonin release at UFH concentrations of 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range [RR] <20%), and showed no serotonin release (0%) at a very high UFH concentration (100 U/mL). The absence of platelet activation at supratherapeutic UFH levels is indicative of HIT, attributable to the prevention of PF4-immune complex formation under these conditions. Nonetheless, two additional aspects of the SRA emerged. First, the patient’s serum triggered a high level of serotonin release in the absence of heparin (96% at 0 U/mL; RR <20%), a typical finding for aHIT, but not for traditional heparin-dependent HIT. Second, application of the Fc receptor-blocking antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress the serotonin release associated with patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release remained at 76% in the presence of IV.3, rather than falling below the 20% threshold commonly observed with HIT antibodies; this led to an initial interpretation of an ‘indeterminate’ SRA. The underlying reason appeared to be that the patient indeed had aHIT, but the patient’s HIT antibodies displayed unusually high reactivity, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3 compared with what is typically observed. To the best of our knowledge, this unusual SRA response pattern has not been previously described.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and therapy with argatroban— a direct thrombin inhibitor—was initiated at a dosage of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While fondaparinux at therapeutic levels is recognized as an effective ‘off-label’ option for HIT, the hematologist chose argatroban instead due to its shorter half-life, allowing greater flexibility in the event of bleeding, particularly considering the patient’s recent cerebrovascular accident and marked thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) was administered, with 100 g given on each of two consecutive days, and a third 100 g dose added after no rise in platelet count was observed following the first two doses. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer levels were also performed as indirect markers of anticoagulant effectiveness, since increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer concentrations would indicate successful management of HIT-induced hypercoagulability with anticoagulation.

During the initial therapy, there was a gradual reduction in fibrinogen levels from POD6 to POD17, reaching a lowest point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer concentrations peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood films showed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16. Together, these findings indicated a severe and advancing consumptive coagulopathy related to HIT. Additionally, after the administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, the patient’s platelet count demonstrated only a slight increase—from 13×10^9/L on POD7 prior to IVIg, to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This increment was substantially less than what is typically observed in patients with aHIT treated with high-dose intravenous Ig (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg subcutaneously once daily. Factors influencing this decision included the more convenient dosing schedule of fondaparinux, which is administered subcutaneously once per day, as opposed to argatroban, which necessitates intravenous infusion with ongoing PTT-based dose adjustments. Additionally, there was concern that argatroban might not have been adequately controlling HIT-related hypercoagulability, as reflected by a decline in fibrinogen, elevation of d-dimer, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Because fondaparinux exerts its effects as an indirect factor Xa inhibitor dependent on antithrombin (AT), a supplemental dose of AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to initiation of fondaparinux; the AT level before this infusion was slightly below normal at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count progressively returned to within normal limits (measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87), and the patient achieved full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux therapy continued through POD65, after which apixaban was reinstated at a dosage of 5 mg orally twice daily for long-term anticoagulation management. Repeated SRA testing demonstrated persistently high positivity over several months, with later SRA patterns reflecting features typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The most recent sample, drawn at POD124 after normalization of platelet counts, indicated primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release was 15% without UFH and rose to 100% when UFH was added at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited several uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serologic characteristics, all of which were eventually explained by the diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, his decrease in platelets associated with HIT developed earlier than the typical timeframe for HIT onset—generally post-operative days 5 through 10 (inclusive) among individuals undergoing cardiac surgery. In fact, fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients with HIT show a decline in platelet count before post-operative day 5, a pattern that is associated with recent exposure to heparin before surgery, which was also observed in this case.

Additionally, our patient’s platelet count declined to a particularly low minimum of 13×10⁹/L, accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen concentrations, elevated d-dimer measurements, and the presence of schistocytes during treatment with argatroban anticoagulation. These manifestations, characteristic of profound hypercoagulability in severe HIT, are suggestive of aHIT.

Third, our patient did not show the rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia that is typically seen when patients with aHIT receive high-dose intravenous Ig, suggesting that this was an especially severe manifestation of aHIT. In fact, serological findings consistent with a highly severe aHIT subtype were observed, as evidenced by only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present. As far as we are aware, this unique serological pattern has not previously been documented in the context of aHIT; recognition of this could help prevent missed aHIT diagnoses and the resulting potential for harmful clinical outcomes. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT in our patient was ultimately verified on the basis of three main factors. First, repeat testing of serum samples collected during the lengthy phase of platelet count improvement revealed SRA reactivity profiles that more closely resembled those typically associated with aHIT. Second, the most recent sample exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA reaction consistent with classic HIT, indicating decreasing levels of aHIT antibodies. Third, no alternative diagnosis sufficiently accounted for the patient’s unusual progression. The patient ultimately experienced a full recovery without lasting neurological effects.
