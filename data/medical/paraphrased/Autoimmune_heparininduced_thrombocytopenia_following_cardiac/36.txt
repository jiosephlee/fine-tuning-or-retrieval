Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin anticoagulation during cardiac surgery and subsequently experienced a progressive decline in platelet count, which dropped to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5). This decrease triggered investigation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies, especially after brain imaging identified a parietal stroke. The HIT antibody screening immunoassay demonstrated a strong positive response, while the serotonin-release assay revealed an unusual pattern: significant serotonin release at heparin concentrations of 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL, and only incomplete suppression by a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Argatroban therapy, combined with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, replaced heparin. Due to ongoing hypercoagulability, treatment transitioned to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count progressively returned to normal (155×10⁹/L at POD62) and the patient’s neurological deficits resolved. Further laboratory testing demonstrated HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin, diagnostic of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Notable features of this aHIT case include severe thrombocytopenia (minimum platelet count of 13×10⁹/L), unusually early postoperative onset attributed to prior heparin exposure, and an atypical aHIT antibody laboratory profile exhibiting partial resistance to inhibition by Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant anticoagulant during cardiac surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Its popularity stems from several advantages: effective suppression of haemostatic activation by artificial surfaces, elimination pathways that do not rely on renal or hepatic function, the capability for real-time intraoperative monitoring through activated clotting time, and affordability. Nevertheless, UFH carries a significant risk for an immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). After cardiac procedures, HIT develops in approximately 0.5%–1.0% of patients, contingent on factors such as the form and duration of postoperative heparin-based thromboprophylaxis (either UFH or low-molecular-weight heparin [LMWH]), and is distinguished by a markedly increased risk of thrombotic events, with a relative risk around 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been characterized as a disorder involving potent platelet activation via Fc receptor engagement, prompted by the assembly of immune complexes comprised of immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies that recognize platelet factor 4 (PF4)—a cationic protein—once it has formed a complex with the polyanionic drug heparin. However, recent evidence points to a subset of patients exhibiting unusual and often severe manifestations, associated with HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin. This subgroup, now referred to as 'autoimmune HIT' (aHIT), has been detailed in the literature. In this paper, we present a case of aHIT in which the patient exhibited profound and persistent thrombocytopenia, further complicated by a thrombotic cerebrovascular accident, following cardiac surgery. This report expands the clinical understanding of aHIT by identifying an exceptionally severe serological profile, with only partial responsiveness to Fc receptor inhibition. The case also illustrates the diagnostic challenges associated with early postoperative thrombocytopenia, since the unusually prompt onset of aHIT in this patient could be attributed to prior exposure to UFH. The patient ultimately achieved a favorable clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies with a two-year duration of exertional shortness of breath and a longstanding history of atrial fibrillation—managed with apixaban 5 mg twice daily—was noted to have a newly identified systolic murmur. He underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization, but had not discontinued apixaban prior to the procedure; therefore, the cardiologist opted for a transradial arterial access and administered a reduced dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (only 2000 U of UFH). Angiography revealed a 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal branch, moderate aortic stenosis, and a widened ascending aorta, leading to his planned cardiac surgery the following week.

Cardiac surgery was conducted utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) after administering 45,000 U of UFH for anticoagulation. The operative procedures involved aortic valve replacement using a #27 bioprosthesis, ascending aorta replacement with a #30 Gelweave graft, single saphenous vein graft bypass to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation proceeded without complications, with no significant intraoperative bleeding or requirement for blood transfusion, and the patient was weaned from CPB without difficulty, maintaining stable haemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately before surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference interval 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the count was 155×10^9/L, indicating a modest reduction in platelets often observed after cardiac operations. Standard thromboprophylaxis using low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, with 5000 units of dalteparin given subcutaneously. The dosage was increased to 7500 units on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, a continuous decline in platelet count was observed from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). The patient’s clinical condition remained stable except for experiencing postoperative delirium. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was introduced, with the initial dose administered on POD5 at 18:00.

Despite the absence of localized neurological deficits, the escalation in postoperative delirium combined with a further drop in platelet count on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (confirmed as 24×10^9/L on repeat testing) led to brain CT imaging. The scan identified an acute cerebral infarction, visualized as a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe with extension into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

The complete blood count performed on postoperative day 5 revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, with haemoglobin measuring 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), comparable to haemoglobin levels recorded over the previous three days—findings that align with anticipated early postoperative blood loss without evidence of persistent bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count was within expected limits at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was increased at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting the lack of pronounced haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 was restored to normal.

Coagulation testing demonstrated a normal prothrombin time, with an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), partial thromboplastin time measured at 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds), and fibrinogen was elevated at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), in line with the typical rise seen in the early postoperative period, given that fibrinogen is an acute phase reactant. On POD5, a blood sample was submitted to a reference laboratory for testing of HIT antibodies.

Additional laboratory tests showed unremarkable chemistry (including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin). Cultures from both blood and urine were collected. Chest radiography revealed no significant findings.

Examination of the peripheral blood film on POD7 confirmed thrombocytopenia, without evidence of platelet clumping, schistocytes (red cell fragments), or abnormal (‘toxic’) changes in white blood cells. Screening for HIT using the PF4/polyanion ELISA yielded a markedly positive result at 2.2 optical density units (reference range <0.4 OD units); however, the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (discussed in detail later).

Differential diagnosis:

Common causes of significant thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infectious processes, appeared improbable based on clinical assessment and laboratory data (hemodynamics were stable; blood and urine cultures were negative). Less frequent causes, such as post-transfusion purpura (no transfusions had occurred), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (haptoglobin levels normal, schistocytes not initially detected), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia were also discounted, as the platelet reduction transpired too soon for medications started either intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid) or postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine) to have had an effect.

While the timing of the thrombocytopenia also preceded any impact expected from intraoperative UFH administered during CPB, further review revealed that the patient had received intraprocedural UFH during a preoperative cardiac catheterisation. Considering the concurrent thrombotic stroke and strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was considered the most likely etiology, presumably precipitated by the UFH administered at preoperative catheterisation. Additional clarification on the ‘indeterminate’ SRA was solicited from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which had performed the test.

A thorough analysis of the SRA findings was conducted. The patient’s serum on postoperative day 5 demonstrated marked serotonin release at UFH concentrations of 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range [RR] <20%), indicating the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies. Furthermore, there was no serotonin release (0%) when the UFH concentration was 100 U/mL, a supratherapeutic level. The lack of platelet activation at elevated heparin concentrations is typical in HIT, reflecting the inability to form immune complexes containing PF4 at such high levels of UFH. Nevertheless, the SRA results also exhibited two additional noteworthy characteristics. First, a pronounced serotonin release occurred even without UFH exposure (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR <20%), which is indicative of aHIT rather than the classic heparin-dependent form of HIT. Second, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 was added, there was only partial inhibition of serotonin release: with 0.1 U/mL UFH, serotonin release was 76% in the presence of IV.3, whereas inhibition below 20% would ordinarily be expected for HIT antibodies. This led to the initial classification of the SRA result as ‘indeterminate.’ Upon review, the reasoning was that the patient indeed had aHIT, but with HIT antibodies exhibiting unusually strong reactivity, resulting in only partial blockade by IV.3 instead of the typical degree of inhibition. To our knowledge, this atypical SRA response profile has not been documented previously.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosing was stopped, and argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was initiated at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min with a goal activated partial thromboplastin time (PTT) between 55–85 seconds. Although therapeutic fondaparinux is a well-supported off-label option for HIT, the hematologist elected to use argatroban, citing its shorter half-life which would allow for more rapid cessation in the event of hemorrhage, especially considering the patient’s recent stroke and pronounced thrombocytopenia. Furthermore, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IV Ig) was administered, starting with 100 g daily for two days, followed by an additional 100 g on the third day because the platelet count did not increase after the initial two days. To indirectly assess anticoagulant effectiveness, repeated measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer levels were obtained; increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer would indicate that HIT-related hypercoagulability was being suppressed.

During the initial phase of therapy, fibrinogen concentrations progressively declined between POD6 and POD17, with a lowest value measured at 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked near POD12, and the peripheral blood smear demonstrated the presence of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16. Collectively, these three findings were indicative of a severe and worsening consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. In addition, after administering high-dose intravenous Ig, the patient’s platelet count increased only minimally—from a baseline of 13×10^9/L (POD7) to a transient peak of 54×10^9/L (POD14)—which represents a significantly smaller rise in platelet count than typically observed following high-dose intravenous Ig in cases of aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient's anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux at 10 mg administered once daily subcutaneously. The decision to transition to fondaparinux took into account its once-daily subcutaneous dosing, which is more convenient compared to the continuous intravenous infusion of argatroban that requires aPTT monitoring. Additionally, there was concern that argatroban might not have been adequately controlling the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, as evidenced by decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer values, and the emergence of schistocytes while on argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect factor Xa inhibitor dependent on antithrombin (AT), the patient received 1000 units of AT concentrate prior to initiating fondaparinux. The AT level before infusion was mildly below the reference range at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet levels steadily returned to the normal range, measuring 155×10⁹/L on POD62 and 202×10⁹/L on POD87, and the patient achieved full neurological restoration. Fondaparinux therapy was maintained until POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Repeated SRA assessments continued to yield strongly positive results over several months, with subsequent tests displaying an SRA pattern more indicative of aHIT (refer to figure 2). The final blood sample, collected at POD124 after normalization of the platelet count, demonstrated findings compatible with primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: there was 15% serotonin release without UFH and 100% release when UFH at 0.3 U/mL was added.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which could be explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Firstly, the decline in platelet count associated with HIT occurred before the typical timeframe for HIT onset, which ranges from POD5 to POD10 in patients who have undergone cardiac surgery. In this population, only approximately 10% experience HIT-induced thrombocytopenia before POD5, a scenario connected to prior exposure to heparin before surgery, as was observed in this patient.

Secondly, the patient’s platelet level dropped to a notably low nadir of 13×10^9/L. This was accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen concentrations, increasing d-dimer values, and the presence of schistocytes during treatment with argatroban. These findings suggest profound HIT-associated hypercoagulability, consistent with aHIT.

Third, unlike the typical rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia observed after administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) in patients with aHIT, our patient did not experience such a prompt response; this suggests that her aHIT was of an unusually severe variant. Supporting this, serological testing demonstrated only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro with monoclonal antibody IV.3, an atypical finding. To the best of our knowledge, this unique serological pattern has not previously been linked to aHIT, and recognizing it could assist in preventing misdiagnosis of aHIT, thereby averting potential clinical complications. Nonetheless, a diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately confirmed for this patient, supported by at least three points. Firstly, sera collected later during the extended recovery of platelet counts showed some SRA reactivity patterns more commonly associated with aHIT. Secondly, the final specimen displayed a heparin-dependent SRA response typical of classic HIT, likely indicating declining aHIT antibody levels. Thirdly, there was no alternative diagnosis that would adequately account for the patient’s uncommon clinical presentation. Fortunately, the patient went on to recover fully, without any lingering neurological deficits.
