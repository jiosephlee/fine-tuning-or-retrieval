Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received cardiac surgery using heparin for anticoagulation. After surgery, his platelet count progressively declined, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by the fifth postoperative day (POD5), leading to evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Cerebral imaging identified a parietal infarct. HIT antibody screening by immunoassay was strongly positive, and the platelet activation assay (serotonin-release assay) revealed an unusual pattern—marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin that could only be partially suppressed by an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Anticoagulation therapy was transitioned to argatroban in addition to high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing hypercoagulability resulted in initiation of fondaparinux, which was followed by a gradual restoration of platelet counts (155×10⁹/L at POD62) and resolution of neurological symptoms. Laboratory testing verified the presence of HIT antibodies capable of heparin-independent platelet activation, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to marked thrombocytopenia (platelet count nadir of 13×10⁹/L), notable features of this aHIT case were its early appearance postoperatively (prior to POD5), attributed to preoperative heparin administration, as well as a distinctive antibody pattern with only incomplete susceptibility to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

Unfractionated heparin (UFH) has remained the predominant anticoagulant used intraoperatively throughout the seven decades of cardiac surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to several advantages such as suppression of haemostasis related to artificial surfaces, clearance independent of renal or hepatic pathways, availability of intraoperative monitoring via activated clotting time, and affordability. Nonetheless, UFH is associated with a significant immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). The incidence of HIT following cardiac surgery ranges from approximately 0.5% to 1.0%, varying based on the type and length of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and is marked by a high propensity for thrombotic events, with a relative thrombosis risk near 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been characterized by pronounced platelet activation mediated by Fc receptor clustering on platelets, which occurs when immune complexes form involving immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies that target the positively charged protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) in the presence of polyanionic heparin. In recent times, recognition has grown regarding patients presenting with atypical—and frequently severe—clinical manifestations attributed to HIT antibodies that are capable of heparin-independent platelet activation; this distinct entity has been termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (as discussed in review). Herein, we report a case of aHIT that was notable for marked and sustained thrombocytopenia, further complicated by thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. This case reveals new perspectives on the breadth of aHIT by detailing a particularly severe serologic profile with partial insensitivity to Fc receptor blockade. Additionally, it underscores the complexities involved in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the unusually prompt onset of aHIT in this patient was attributed to preoperative UFH exposure. The patient ultimately experienced a positive outcome.

Case presentation:

A man in his seventies with a two-year duration of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily) was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization; however, his apixaban had not been discontinued before the procedure. In response, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery approach and used a lower-than-standard dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (administering only 2000 U of UFH). Angiography revealed a 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic valve stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Cardiac surgery was subsequently planned for one week later.

The heart surgery utilized cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), with anticoagulation achieved through administration of 45,000 U of UFH. The procedure involved aortic valve replacement with a size 27 bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta using a size 30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary artery bypass graft using saphenous vein to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was completed without complication, there were no significant blood losses or requirements for transfusion, and the patient was weaned from CPB without difficulty, demonstrating excellent haemodynamic status.

The platelet count immediately before surgery was measured at 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the count had decreased to 155×10^9/L, which is considered a modest early drop typical in the setting of cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis using low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) began on POD1, with the patient receiving 5000 units of dalteparin subcutaneously. Subsequently, higher doses of 7500 units were given on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, between POD1 and POD5, the platelet count continued to fall, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from experiencing postoperative delirium, the patient remained otherwise stable. On POD5, dalteparin was withheld, and instead, a prophylactic dose of fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was initiated, with the first dose administered at 18:00.

Despite the absence of focal neurological signs, the combination of worsening delirium and a further decrease in platelet count on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (with a repeat result of 24×10^9/L) led to a CT scan of the brain. Imaging revealed an acute cerebral infarction, characterized by an area of hypodensity measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, which extended through to the cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, with a haemoglobin of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), mirroring values observed over the prior three days, which aligned with the anticipated postoperative decline due to surgical blood loss without ongoing bleeding. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) with a normal differential. The reticulocyte count was within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was elevated to 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no evident haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 levels had normalized.

Coagulation parameters demonstrated a normal prothrombin time, with an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), partial thromboplastin time of 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 s), and a raised fibrinogen concentration at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), consistent with the acute phase response typically observed in the immediate postoperative period. A blood sample collected on POD5 was forwarded to a reference center for testing of HIT antibodies.

Additional laboratory assessments showed normal serum chemistry, including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography did not reveal significant findings.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear from postoperative day 7 corroborated thrombocytopenia, with no platelet clumping and without the presence of red cell fragments (schistocytes) or abnormal toxic changes in white blood cells. Screening for HIT with the PF4/polyanion ELISA was markedly positive, yielding an optical density (OD) value of 2.2 (reference range <0.4 OD units); however, the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) resulted in an ‘indeterminate’ finding (to be discussed below).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of significant thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infection, appeared improbable based on the patient's clinical presentation and laboratory findings (hemodynamics remained stable; both blood and urine cultures were negative). Uncommon etiologies for postoperative thrombocytopenia were also considered unlikely: post-transfusion purpura was excluded as the patient had not received any blood products; postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura was deemed improbable given normal haptoglobin levels and the initial absence of schistocytes; and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was unlikely because the decline in platelet count began too soon after exposure to any perioperative or postoperative medications (intraoperative vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid, and postoperative furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

Although the timing of the thrombocytopenia was inconsistent with intraoperative UFH administration during cardiopulmonary bypass, further review revealed that the patient had also received UFH during preoperative cardiac catheterization. In the context of an ischemic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was determined to be the most probable diagnosis, with UFH exposure at the time of preoperative catheterization being the likely inciting event. The laboratory that performed the serotonin-release assay (SRA), the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, was contacted for clarification on the ‘indeterminate’ SRA result.

The SRA findings underwent thorough evaluation. The patient’s serum collected on POD5 induced significant serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20% release), which aligns with the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies. Conversely, no serotonin release was observed (0%) when supratherapeutic UFH levels (100 U/mL) were used; this absence of platelet activation at very high heparin concentrations is typical of HIT and indicates that PF4-containing immune complexes cannot form under such conditions. Yet, two distinct aspects of the SRA data were also apparent. Firstly, marked serotonin release was observed when patient serum was tested without UFH (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; RR <20%), a finding characteristic of aHIT rather than the classic heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, the use of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress the serotonin release induced by the patient’s serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH; instead, there was 76% release in the presence of IV.3, rather than the typical reduction to below 20% seen with HIT antibodies. This led initially to the SRA result being classified as ‘indeterminate’. The reasoning, however, was that the patient indeed had aHIT, but their HIT antibodies were exceptionally reactive, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3, which is typically more complete. According to available reports, such an unusual SRA response pattern has not been described before.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosing was stopped, and argatroban—a direct thrombin inhibitor—was started for anticoagulation at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While therapeutic dosing of fondaparinux can serve as an ‘off-label’ option for HIT treatment, the hematologist managing the case opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, allowing for more rapid reversal should bleeding develop, particularly given the patient's recent stroke and particularly severe thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IV Ig) was administered, beginning with 100 g on two consecutive days, followed by a third 100 g dose because there was no significant platelet count rise after the initial two days. Serial assessments of fibrinogen and d-dimer were conducted to indirectly monitor the effectiveness of anticoagulation therapy; increasing fibrinogen levels coupled with declining d-dimer concentrations would suggest that anticoagulant treatment was effectively controlling the hypercoagulability seen in HIT.

During the initial course of treatment, fibrinogen levels steadily decreased from postoperative day 6 through day 17, reaching a lowest point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer concentrations peaked around postoperative day 12, and peripheral blood smears demonstrated the presence of schistocytes between postoperative day 9 and day 16; collectively, these findings indicated severe and progressive consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. Additionally, after administration of high-dose intravenous Ig, the patient's platelet count showed only a modest improvement, rising from 13×10^9/L on postoperative day 7 (prior to intravenous Ig) to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on day 14, which represented a significantly smaller increase in platelet count than typically observed in patients with aHIT treated with high-dose intravenous Ig (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the anticoagulant regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered once daily via subcutaneous injection. Factors influencing this transition included the simplicity of fondaparinux dosing, which allows for once-daily subcutaneous administration, compared to the need for intravenous argatroban dosing adjusted according to PTT levels. Additionally, there were concerns about argatroban’s effectiveness in managing HIT-associated hypercoagulability, as reflected by declining fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux functions as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, the patient received supplemental antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) prior to commencing fondaparinux; the antithrombin level measured before infusion was slightly below normal at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet levels returned to within normal limits (155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87), and neurological function fully recovered. Fondaparinux therapy was continued until POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dose of 5 mg twice daily orally for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial serotonin release assay (SRA) tests remained strongly positive over several months, with later profiles demonstrating SRA patterns more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The most recent SRA, performed on a blood sample collected at POD124 after platelet normalization, showed primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: there was only 15% serotonin release in the absence of UFH, but 100% release when 0.3 U/mL of UFH was added.

Discussion:

This patient demonstrated multiple atypical clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were ultimately ascribed to a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, the development of thrombocytopenia associated with HIT occurred before the standard time frame for HIT onset, typically between postoperative days 5 and 10 for individuals undergoing cardiac surgery. Approximately 10% of patients in this group who go on to develop HIT exhibit a reduction in platelet count before postoperative day 5; this early manifestation is often associated with recent preoperative exposure to heparin, which was observed in this patient.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count reached an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreased fibrinogen levels, elevated d-dimer, and evidence of schistocytes while receiving argatroban anticoagulation. These manifestations of pronounced HIT-related hypercoagulability are suggestive of aHIT.

Third, unlike the typical rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia observed with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin administration in cases of aHIT, our patient’s platelet count did not respond quickly, indicating a particularly severe manifestation of aHIT. Serologically, only partial suppression of platelet activation occurred in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present, suggesting a rare and severe aHIT subtype. To our knowledge, this distinct serological pattern has not been previously described in association with aHIT. Recognizing this novel profile could help prevent misdiagnosis of aHIT and avoid possible negative outcomes. Despite these findings, the diagnosis of aHIT was confirmed for our patient based on several points. First, sera obtained at later stages during the extended recovery of platelet counts exhibited SRA reactivity more typical of aHIT. Second, the final serum sample tested displayed a heparin-dependent SRA result indicative of classical HIT, consistent with decreasing aHIT antibody levels. Third, no alternative explanation accounted for the patient’s unusual clinical presentation. The patient ultimately experienced a full recovery without lingering neurological deficits.
