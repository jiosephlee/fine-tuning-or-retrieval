Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received cardiac surgery with heparin-based anticoagulation; his platelet count progressively decreased, reaching 50×10⁹/L on postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Neuroimaging revealed a parietal lobe stroke. A screening immunoassay for HIT antibodies was markedly positive. The platelet activation (serotonin-release) assay displayed an unusual result—marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin concentrations that was only partially reduced by a monoclonal antibody blocking the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulant therapy was altered to argatroban in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. As hypercoagulable signs persisted, fondaparinux was started, after which platelet counts gradually rose (to 155×10⁹/L on POD62); neurologic deficits resolved. Further testing detected HIT antibodies exhibiting heparin-independent platelet activation, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Unique features of this aHIT case included profound thrombocytopenia (platelet nadir, 13×10⁹/L), onset before POD5 likely due to pre-surgical heparin exposure, and a distinctive laboratory pattern showing partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the primary anticoagulant used intraoperatively during cardiac procedures with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to several benefits such as suppression of haemostatic responses induced by contact with artificial surfaces, elimination independent of renal and hepatic function, real-time anticoagulant monitoring via activated clotting time, and its affordability. Nevertheless, UFH is associated with a significant complication: heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-mediated reaction. After cardiac surgery, HIT occurs relatively frequently, with an incidence estimated between 0.5% and 1.0%, which is influenced by both the type and duration of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (comparison of UFH and low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and it is notable for its robust link to thrombosis, conferring an approximate 15-fold increased relative risk.

Traditionally, HIT is considered a disorder characterized by marked platelet activation, initiated by platelet Fc receptor clustering, as a result of immune complexes generated by IgG antibodies that recognize platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to heparin, a polyanionic molecule. More recently, it has been identified that a subset of patients experience atypical and frequently severe clinical manifestations due to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets in the absence of heparin; this variant, referred to as 'autoimmune HIT' (aHIT) (see review), is increasingly acknowledged. The present report details a case of aHIT presenting with unusually profound and prolonged thrombocytopenia, further complicated by thrombotic stroke, occurring after cardiac surgery. This case offers new understanding regarding the diversity of aHIT, revealing an exceptionally severe serological pattern with only partial response to Fc receptor inhibition. Furthermore, it underscores the complexities involved in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the unusually rapid onset of aHIT was attributed to UFH exposure before surgery. The patient ultimately recovered with a positive outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies with a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily) was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He proceeded to diagnostic cardiac catheterization, having continued his apixaban regimen prior to the procedure. Consequently, the interventional cardiologist selected a transradial arterial access and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulation dose (just 2000 U of unfractionated heparin). Angiography revealed 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta; surgical intervention was planned for the following week.

The patient underwent cardiac surgery using cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), for which anticoagulation was attained with 45 000 U of unfractionated heparin. The operative procedures included replacement of the aortic valve using a #27 bioprosthesis, substitution of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, single-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting with a saphenous vein conduit to the obtuse marginal branch, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was completed smoothly, with no significant intraoperative bleeding or transfusion requirements, and the patient separated from CPB with stable haemodynamics.

The patient’s platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). By postoperative day 1 (POD1), the count had decreased to 155×10^9/L, representing a modest early drop commonly seen following cardiac surgery. Routine thromboprophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, with dalteparin 5000 units given subcutaneously, followed by an increased dosage of 7500 units on POD2, POD3, and POD4. However, the platelet count unexpectedly continued to fall each day from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (figure 1). The patient’s only complication at this time was postoperative delirium. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and instead, fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose of 2.5 mg was started (first dose administered at 18:00 on POD5).

Although no focal neurologic signs were present, the worsening delirium and further drop in platelets on POD6, with values of 26×10^9/L (and on repeat, 24×10^9/L), led to brain CT imaging. This revealed an acute cerebral infarction, with the hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending through to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

The complete blood count performed on postoperative day 5 demonstrated a platelet count of 50×10^9/L and a haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), consistent with prior haemoglobin values from the preceding three days, suggesting expected early postoperative blood loss without evidence of continued bleeding. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocytes were within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was increased to 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no significant haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 levels had normalized.

Coagulation parameters revealed a normal prothrombin time and international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), with a normal partial thromboplastin time of 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds). Fibrinogen was markedly elevated at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), in keeping with the acute phase response typically observed in the early postoperative period. On postoperative day 5, a specimen was submitted to a reference laboratory for evaluation of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies.

Additional investigations demonstrated normal results for serum chemistry, including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Both blood and urine cultures were collected. Chest radiography did not reveal significant abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear from postoperative day 7 showed thrombocytopenia with an absence of platelet clumping, no schistocytes, and no evidence of abnormal or ‘toxic’ white blood cells. The screening immunoassay for HIT using the PF4/polyanion ELISA was strongly positive at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units), while the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) was reported as ‘indeterminate’ (discussed in detail later).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of severe thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infection, were considered improbable due to stable haemodynamic parameters and negative results from blood and urine cultures. Other, less common causes were also felt to be unlikely. These included post-transfusion purpura—ruled out as no transfusions had been administered—post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP)—discounted given a normal haptoglobin level and lack of schistocytes at presentation—and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, given that the thrombocytopenia developed earlier than would be expected for postoperative drugs introduced either on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or those started during the postoperative period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

Despite the platelet count decline appearing too early for intraoperative unfractionated heparin (UFH) exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) to be causal, further investigation uncovered that UFH had been given intraprocedurally during a preoperative cardiac catheterisation. In light of the presence of thrombotic stroke, a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, and the clinical timeline, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) became the leading diagnosis, most plausibly linked to the UFH administered during the earlier cardiac catheterisation. Additional information regarding the ‘indeterminate’ serotonin-release assay (SRA) result was sought from the testing site, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings underwent thorough evaluation. Reflecting the existence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s POD5 serum induced pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), while there was no serotonin release (0%) observed at supra-therapeutic UFH levels (100 U/mL). This pattern—the absence of platelet activation at very high heparin doses—is typically seen in HIT and is attributable to the inability of PF4-containing immune complexes to assemble at these concentrations. Nonetheless, two other distinctive aspects were identified in the SRA data. Firstly, strong serotonin release occurred with patient serum even when UFH was absent (96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a hallmark of aHIT but not classic heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, the inclusion of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress serotonin release generated by patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: instead of inhibition below 20%, there was still 76% release, which contrasts with the typical suppression seen with HIT antibodies. This led initially to an 'indeterminate' SRA result. Ultimately, the interpretation was that the patient had aHIT, and that their HIT antibodies were exceptionally reactive, leading to only partial blockade by IV.3. To date, this unusual SRA response profile has not been documented in previous literature.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and the patient was initiated on argatroban—a direct thrombin inhibitor—at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While fondaparinux at therapeutic doses serves as an effective ‘off-label’ option for managing HIT, the consulting haematologist elected to use argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which would allow greater flexibility should bleeding arise, particularly considering the patient's recent stroke and notably severe thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous Ig was administered, starting with 100 g on two consecutive days, then a third 100 g dose, because the platelet count had not responded after the initial 2 days of treatment. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were carried out to provide indirect confirmation of anticoagulation efficacy; increasing fibrinogen and declining d-dimer results would indicate that HIT-related hypercoagulability was under anticoagulant control.

During the initial management period, fibrinogen levels steadily declined from postoperative day 6 (POD6) to postoperative day 17 (POD17), reaching a minimum of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer concentrations peaked near POD12, and peripheral blood smears identified schistocytes between POD9 and POD16. Collectively, these findings indicated the presence of pronounced and progressive HIT-related consumptive coagulopathy. Additionally, after high-dose intravenous Ig was administered, the patient's platelet count rose only modestly, increasing from 13×10^9/L prior to treatment (POD7) to a maximum of 54×10^9/L by POD14. This represents a much smaller platelet response than what is typically observed in patients with aHIT treated with high-dose intravenous Ig (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient's anticoagulation was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dosage of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. The rationale for transitioning to fondaparinux included its simpler once-daily subcutaneous administration compared to the continuous intravenous infusion of argatroban that requires PTT-based dose adjustments. Additionally, there was concern that argatroban might not adequately control HIT-related hypercoagulability, as evidenced by declining fibrinogen levels, elevated d-dimer, and the presence of schistocytes noted during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux functions as an indirect antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, an AT concentrate (1000 units) was administered before initiating fondaparinux, due to a slightly decreased pre-infusion antithrombin level of 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet count steadily returned to normal levels (measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87), alongside full neurological improvement. Fondaparinux therapy continued until POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Sequential SRA assays remained persistently positive over several months, with subsequent testing demonstrating SRA patterns typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The final blood specimen, collected at POD124 after normalization of the platelet count, revealed findings indicating mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release measured 15% in the absence of UFH and 100% in the presence of 0.3 U/mL UFH.

Discussion:

This patient exhibited several uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. To begin with, his HIT-related thrombocytopenia occurred earlier than the standard window for HIT manifestation, which typically ranges from postoperative day 5 through day 10 in individuals who have undergone cardiac surgery. Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients developing HIT demonstrate a platelet decrease prior to postoperative day 5; this pattern has been correlated with recent exposure to heparin prior to surgery, a circumstance that was also observed in this case.

Additionally, the patient's platelet count decreased to a notably low nadir of 13×10^9/L, in conjunction with declining fibrinogen concentrations, elevated d-dimer measurements, and schistocytes on peripheral smear while receiving argatroban therapy. Such pronounced features of HIT-associated hypercoagulability signify the presence of aHIT.

Third, our patient failed to show the rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia that is typically observed when high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin is administered to individuals with aHIT; this suggests the presence of an especially severe form of aHIT. In fact, laboratory evidence indicating this unusually severe subtype consisted of only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro with monoclonal antibody IV.3. To the best of our knowledge, this distinctive serological finding has not been previously described in connection with aHIT; recognizing this could prevent a missed diagnosis, which may lead to significant clinical consequences. However, in our case, the diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately substantiated based on at least three factors. First, testing of serum samples obtained later, during the extended period of platelet count normalization, demonstrated some SRA reactivity patterns more in line with those seen in aHIT. Second, the final sample assessed displayed a heparin-dependent SRA response typical of conventional HIT, indicative of declining aHIT antibodies. Third, there was no alternative diagnosis that could adequately account for the patient’s unusual clinical progression. The patient ultimately experienced a full clinical recovery without any lasting neurological deficits.
