Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An elderly male patient received heparin for anticoagulation during cardiac surgery, after which his platelet count progressively decreased, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5). This decline led to testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies; brain imaging identified a parietal stroke. A screening immunoassay for HIT antibodies revealed a strong positive, while the platelet activation assay (serotonin-release assay) demonstrated an unusual pattern: pronounced serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with only partial suppression by a monoclonal antibody that blocks the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulation therapy was switched to argatroban combined with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing hypercoagulability necessitated transitioning to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count gradually normalized (155×10⁹/L by POD62) and neurological deficits resolved. Additional laboratory testing identified HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independent of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to marked thrombocytopenia (nadir 13×10⁹/L), distinct features of this aHIT case included onset soon after surgery (before POD5) likely due to prior heparin exposure, and a rare antibody profile characterized by partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For the past seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been used almost exclusively as the intraoperative anticoagulant during cardiac surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to several benefits such as inhibition of haemostatic activation caused by contact with artificial surfaces, clearance independent of renal or hepatic function, intraoperative monitoring by activated clotting time, and affordability. Despite these advantages, UFH is associated with a significant immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). HIT occurs with notable frequency after cardiac procedures, estimated at 0.5%–1.0%, with incidence varying according to postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis duration and whether UFH or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) is used. HIT is particularly concerning because of its strong link to thrombotic events, demonstrating an approximate 15-fold increase in risk.

Traditionally, HIT has been characterized as a disease marked by pronounced platelet activation, mediated by clustering of platelet Fc receptors, resulting from immune complexes containing immunoglobulin G (IgG) class antibodies against the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) when bound to polyanionic heparin. More recently, it has become apparent that a subset of patients exhibit atypical and frequently severe manifestations caused by HIT antibodies with platelet-activating capability that does not depend on heparin; this variant is termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (for review). Here, we report a case of aHIT presenting with exceptionally severe and prolonged thrombocytopenia, which was further complicated by a thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. This case offers new perspectives on the diversity of aHIT by describing an unusually severe serologic profile, including partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. Additionally, the case underscores the diagnostic difficulties inherent in postoperative thrombocytopenia, noting that the patient’s remarkably early development of aHIT was attributable to preoperative UFH exposure. The patient ultimately experienced a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his 70s, who had been experiencing exertional dyspnoea for two years and had a longstanding history of atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was noted to have a newly detected systolic murmur. He underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterisation, but apixaban therapy was not withheld prior to the procedure; therefore, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery approach and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulant dose of only 2000 U of UFH. Angiography revealed a 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal branch, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Based on these findings, cardiac surgery was scheduled for the following week.

Cardiac surgery was subsequently conducted with the use of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and anticoagulation accomplished using 45,000 U of UFH. The operative procedures included aortic valve replacement utilizing a #27 bioprosthesis, substitution of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, a solitary coronary artery bypass with a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was completed without complications, with no significant blood loss or transfusion requirements, and the patient was weaned from CPB with stable and excellent haemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), it was 155×10^9/L, indicating a modest decrease typically seen after cardiac surgery. Standard thromboprophylaxis using LMWH was initiated on POD1, starting with 5000 U dalteparin administered subcutaneously; subsequent doses of 7500 U were given on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, the platelet count dropped steadily from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Apart from experiencing postoperative delirium, the patient was clinically stable. On POD5, dalteparin was withheld and prophylactic fondaparinux at a dose of 2.5 mg was started, with the initial dose given at 18:00.

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the exacerbation of postoperative delirium and further reduction in platelets on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (confirmed at 24×10^9/L upon retesting) led to a CT scan of the brain. Imaging demonstrated an acute infarct in the left parietal lobe, with a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm and involvement extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count showed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, with a corresponding hemoglobin of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), a value that had remained stable over the previous three days—consistent with anticipated early postoperative blood loss without evidence of active hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), and the differential was within normal limits. Reticulocyte count was within the normal range at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was increased at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating that there was no apparent hemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 concentration had normalized.

Coagulation parameters included a normal prothrombin time, with an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds), and an elevated fibrinogen level of 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), in line with the acute phase response typical in the immediate postoperative setting. Fibrinogen generally reaches markedly increased levels during early recovery. A sample collected on POD5 was forwarded to a reference center for testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies.

Other laboratory evaluations, including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin, were within normal ranges. Blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest X-ray imaging was unremarkable.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on POD7 reaffirmed the presence of thrombocytopenia, without evidence of platelet aggregation, red cell fragmentation (no schistocytes), or pathologic white blood cell features. A screening assay for HIT using the PF4/polyanion ELISA demonstrated a marked elevation at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units), while the confirmatory functional assay—the serotonin-release assay (SRA)—produced an “indeterminate” result, which is discussed below.

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery—such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, and infection—appeared improbable based on both clinical presentation and laboratory data (including stable haemodynamics and negative blood and urine cultures). Less common etiologies, including post-transfusion purpura (no transfusions were performed), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (with normal haptoglobin and initial absence of schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also thought unlikely given the timing of the platelet drop relative to drug administration. Specifically, newly introduced medications administered postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine), as well as those administered on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid), could not account for the early reduction in platelets.

While the temporal relationship between the observed thrombocytopenia and intraoperative UFH exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass was insufficient, a detailed history revealed the use of intraprocedural UFH during a preoperative cardiac catheterisation. Considering the development of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) became the leading diagnostic consideration, most likely induced by UFH used in preoperative heart catheterisation. Additional information regarding the ‘indeterminate’ serotonin release assay (SRA) was subsequently sought from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which provided the original result.

The SRA findings were thoroughly evaluated. Indicative of the existence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient's serum from POD5 triggered pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20%), while no serotonin release occurred at supratherapeutic UFH levels (100 U/mL, 0% release). This lack of platelet activation at high heparin concentrations is typical for HIT, reflecting an inability of PF4-immune complexes to form at these doses. Nonetheless, two further notable aspects emerged from the SRA data. Firstly, robust serotonin release was produced by the patient’s serum even in the absence of UFH (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), which aligns with aHIT but diverges from classic heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, the addition of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress serotonin release when the patient serum was tested at 0.1 U/mL UFH: the observed serotonin release was 76% in the presence of IV.3, instead of the expected reduction to below 20%, a typical response for HIT antibodies. Consequently, the SRA result was initially termed ‘indeterminate’. However, it was clarified that the diagnosis was aHIT and that the patient’s HIT antibodies displayed unusually strong reactivity, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3, less than is generally seen. This atypical SRA reaction pattern has not been described previously in the literature, to the best of our knowledge.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosing was stopped, and therapeutic-dose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was initiated at 0.75 mcg/kg/min aiming for a PTT of 55–85 seconds. Although fondaparinux at therapeutic levels is an accepted ‘off-label’ anticoagulation strategy for HIT, the attending hematologist opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which offers an advantage if bleeding were to occur, considering the patient’s recent cerebrovascular event and marked thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IV Ig) was administered: initially 100 g per day for two consecutive days, and a third 100 g dose was added since the platelet count failed to rise after the first two days of IV Ig. Fibrinogen and d-dimer levels were checked serially as surrogate markers to evaluate the efficacy of anticoagulation, with increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer results interpreted as signs of effective anticoagulant management of HIT-driven hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of therapy, fibrinogen concentrations progressively declined between POD6 and POD17, reaching a minimum of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer values peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears identified schistocytes from POD9 through POD16. Together, these three observations indicated pronounced, advancing HIT-related consumptive coagulopathy. In addition, the increase in platelet numbers following high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was only modest; the count rose from 13×10^9/L at POD7 (pre-intravenous Ig) to a maximum interim level of 54×10^9/L at POD14. This degree of platelet recovery is considerably less than the typical response observed in aHIT patients after high-dose intravenous Ig administration (for review).

On postoperative day 14, anticoagulation therapy was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once per day. Factors influencing this decision included the convenience of fondaparinux's once-daily subcutaneous administration, in contrast to argatroban which requires intravenous infusion with partial thromboplastin time adjustment. Additionally, there were concerns that argatroban might not adequately manage the hypercoagulability associated with HIT, suggested by declining fibrinogen levels, elevated d-dimer, and the presence of schistocytes noted during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux exerts its effect as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) was administered before initiating fondaparinux. The patient's antithrombin level measured prior to infusion was slightly below reference range at 0.70 U/mL (reference range: 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count steadily returned to normal levels (measured at 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87), and they achieved full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was continued through POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dosage of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial serotonin release assays (SRA) remained strongly positive for several months, with subsequent tests showing SRA patterns more typical of atypical heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) (see figure 2). The final tested blood sample, drawn at POD124 after normalization of platelet counts, demonstrated results compatible with primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies—15% serotonin release without UFH and 100% release in the presence of 0.3 U/mL UFH.

Discussion:

This patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, each of which was ultimately explained by the diagnosis of severe aHIT. Firstly, his development of thrombocytopenia associated with HIT occurred prior to the typical time frame for this complication, which typically spans from postoperative day 5 through day 10 in patients who have undergone cardiac surgery. Only approximately 10% of post-cardiac surgery patients with HIT display a heparin-induced decline in platelet count before postoperative day 5, a phenomenon usually associated with recent heparin administration before surgery, which was observed in this individual.

Secondly, the patient’s platelet count declined to a notably low nadir of 13×10^9/L. This was accompanied by reductions in fibrinogen levels, elevation of d-dimer concentrations, and the appearance of schistocytes on the blood smear, all while receiving argatroban therapy. Such manifestations of pronounced HIT-related hypercoagulability are characteristic of aHIT.

Third, the patient failed to demonstrate a rapid improvement in platelet counts, which is commonly observed when high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin is administered for aHIT. This suggests the presence of a particularly severe variant of the condition. Notably, serologic testing revealed only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was used. To our knowledge, this unique serological finding has not been previously associated with aHIT; recognizing this pattern may be important in preventing the oversight of aHIT diagnosis and associated negative clinical outcomes. Despite these atypical features, the diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately established based on several factors. First, sera obtained during the extended phase of platelet count normalization exhibited SRA reactivity patterns more typical of aHIT. Second, the final tested sample produced a heparin-dependent SRA response consistent with classic HIT, indicating a decline in aHIT antibody levels. Third, alternative diagnoses that could account for the patient’s unusual clinical progression were not identified. Ultimately, the patient achieved a complete recovery, without lingering neurological deficits.
