Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin anticoagulation during cardiac surgery. In the postoperative period, his platelet count consistently declined, reaching 50×10⁹/L by the fifth postoperative day (POD5), which led to testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging identified a stroke in the parietal region. Screening with an immunoassay for HIT antibodies was strongly positive, while the platelet activation (serotonin-release) assay revealed an unusual pattern: significant serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with only partial suppression by a monoclonal antibody against the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulation therapy was switched to argatroban in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing signs of hypercoagulability prompted a transition to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count gradually normalized (155×10⁹/L by POD62) and neurological symptoms subsided. Further laboratory testing established the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets even in the absence of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), distinct features of this aHIT case included an early postoperative onset (prior to POD5) attributed to heparin exposure before surgery, and a distinctive antibody profile displaying partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For the past 70 years, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served almost exclusively as the intraoperative anticoagulant of choice during cardiac surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), primarily because it offers multiple benefits: it suppresses haemostasis triggered by artificial surfaces, is eliminated independently of renal or hepatic function, allows for intraoperative monitoring via activated clotting time, and is relatively inexpensive. Nevertheless, UFH carries a significant risk of an immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). HIT occurs with notable frequency following cardiac surgery—estimated at 0.5% to 1.0%, varying according to the length and type of postoperative heparin used for thromboprophylaxis (UFH compared to low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH))—and is distinguished by its substantial association with thrombotic events, conferring an approximate 15-fold increase in relative risk for thrombosis.

Historically, HIT has been characterized as a condition involving pronounced platelet activation mediated by clustering of platelet Fc receptors, which occurs when heparin-dependent IgG antibodies form immune complexes targeting the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) in complex with polyanionic heparin. More recently, evidence has emerged that certain patients experience atypical and often severe manifestations related to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets even in the absence of heparin; this variant is referred to as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). Herein, we present a case of aHIT marked by exceptionally severe and persistent thrombocytopenia, which was further complicated by a thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. This case offers new perspective on the aHIT disease continuum by demonstrating a distinct serological profile characterized by partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. Furthermore, it underscores the complexity of diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the particularly early onset of aHIT in this patient was attributable to preoperative administration of UFH. The patient ultimately achieved a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had experienced exertional shortness of breath for two years and had chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a new systolic murmur on examination. Diagnostic cardiac catheterisation was performed; however, his apixaban was not withheld before the procedure. In response, the cardiologist opted for a transradial arterial access and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulant dose of only 2000 U of unfractionated heparin. Angiography revealed a 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and enlargement of the ascending aorta. Cardiac surgery was planned for one week later.

Cardiac surgery was carried out with the aid of CPB, and anticoagulation was maintained with 45,000 U of UFH. The operative procedures involved aortic valve replacement using a #27 bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary artery bypass graft to the obtuse marginal coronary artery utilizing a saphenous vein, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was uncomplicated, with minimal blood loss, no requirement for transfusion, and the patient was weaned from bypass easily with stable haemodynamic status.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference interval 150–400). By the first postoperative day (POD1), the count had decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicating a mild early drop, which is common following cardiac surgery. Standard thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, involving 5000 units of dalteparin administered subcutaneously, followed by increased doses of 7500 units on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, platelet levels continued to fall steadily from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from the presence of postoperative delirium, the patient showed no other major issues. On POD5, dalteparin was discontinued, and fondaparinux at a prophylactic dosage (2.5 mg) was started, with the first dose given at 18:00 that evening.

Despite no focal neurological deficits on examination, the progression of delirium coupled with a further reduction in platelet count to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (confirmed at 24×10^9/L with repeat measurement) led to the decision to perform a brain CT. Imaging identified an acute infarct within the left parietal lobe, characterized by a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm and extending into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet level of 50×10⁹/L, with a concurrent hemoglobin measurement of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), mirroring the hemoglobin concentrations noted over the previous three days, which is in line with anticipated early postoperative blood loss in the absence of continued hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10⁹/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10⁹/L) and featured a normal differential. Reticulocyte count was within normal limits at 73×10⁹/L (RR 10–100×10⁹/L). Haptoglobin was measured at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no evidence of active hemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 concentration was also within the normal range.

Coagulation assessment demonstrated a prothrombin time within normal limits, with a corresponding international normalised ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1). The partial thromboplastin time was also within the reference interval at 34 s (RR 25–37 s). Fibrinogen was markedly elevated at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), a finding consistent with the early postoperative phase, given that fibrinogen, as an acute phase reactant, often increases substantially following surgery. On postoperative day 5, a blood specimen was submitted to a reference laboratory for evaluation of HIT antibodies.

Other blood work included normal chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were drawn. A chest X-ray showed no major abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on postoperative day 7 revealed thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet aggregation, red blood cell fragments (schistocytes), or toxic changes in leukocytes. The screening immunoassay for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)—PF4/polyanion ELISA—returned a markedly elevated value of 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units). However, the confirmatory platelet function test, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), produced an 'indeterminate' result, as discussed further below.

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of pronounced thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock, critical illness, and infection, were considered improbable in light of the patient's clinical presentation and laboratory results (stable haemodynamics; negative blood and urine cultures). Less frequent causes, such as post-transfusion purpura (the patient did not receive any blood transfusions), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (haptoglobin levels were within normal limits and schistocytes were initially absent), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also deemed unlikely. This was because the decline in platelet count happened too soon to be attributable to newly administered postoperative medications—either intraoperative agents (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid) or those introduced postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

Although the decrease in platelet count happened earlier than would be expected from intraoperative UFH used during CPB, further investigation revealed the presence of intraprocedural UFH administration during preoperative cardiac catheterization. The combination of a thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA indicated that HIT was the most likely diagnosis, probably initiated by UFH given at the time of preoperative heart catheterization. Additional clarification about the 'indeterminate' SRA was sought from the reporting laboratory, McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

A thorough evaluation of the SRA findings was undertaken. The patient’s POD5 serum elicited marked serotonin release at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), in keeping with the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies. There was, however, no serotonin release (0%) at a supratherapeutic concentration of UFH (100 U/mL), a finding that is typical of HIT as it demonstrates the failure of PF4-containing immune complexes to form at very high UFH levels. Notably, two additional observations emerged from the SRA results. Firstly, robust serotonin release occurred even in the absence of UFH (96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), which is characteristic of aHIT and not conventional, heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) was added, it did not completely suppress serotonin release in response to patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH; rather, serotonin release remained at 76% in the presence of IV.3, instead of falling below 20% as is typically expected with HIT antibodies. This led initially to classifying the SRA as ‘indeterminate.’ The resolution was that the patient had aHIT, but the HIT antibodies displayed unusually high reactivity, leading to only partial reduction with IV.3—contrasting with the more pronounced inhibition usually observed. According to current knowledge, this unusual SRA pattern has not previously been documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosing was stopped, and therapy was initiated with argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at a therapeutic infusion rate of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is an effective off-label anticoagulant in HIT, the hematologist overseeing treatment elected to use argatroban due to its shorter half-life, in the event of a bleeding complication, considering the patient's recent cerebrovascular accident and marked thrombocytopenia. The patient also received high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, starting with 100 g daily for two days, and an additional 100 g dose was administered because the platelet count did not increase after the first two days of IVIg. Repeated measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were performed to monitor for indirect indicators of anticoagulant effectiveness, as increasing fibrinogen levels and decreasing d-dimer concentrations would suggest adequate anticoagulant suppression of HIT-related hypercoagulable state.

The patient’s initial management was characterized by a gradual reduction in fibrinogen concentrations between postoperative day (POD) 6 and POD17, reaching a lowest value of 2.49 g/L, and a maximum d-dimer level around POD12. Additionally, the presence of schistocytes was identified on peripheral blood smear from POD9 through POD16. Collectively, these three observations indicated the presence of pronounced, progressing HIT-related consumptive coagulopathy. Furthermore, despite administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, the increase in platelet count was only minimal, rising from 13×10^9/L prior to infusion (POD7) to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L (POD14). This increment in platelets was substantially lower than what is typically observed following high-dose intravenous Ig in patients with aHIT (see review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulation was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg subcutaneously once daily. The rationale for transitioning to fondaparinux included its more convenient dosing regimen, requiring only daily subcutaneous injections compared to the need for intravenous administration of argatroban with PTT-based dose adjustments. Additional motivation stemmed from concerns that argatroban may not have been sufficiently controlling HIT-related hypercoagulability, as suggested by declining fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer, and new schistocytes observed during argatroban therapy. Because fondaparinux acts as an indirect factor Xa inhibitor that requires antithrombin (AT) for efficacy, the patient received an AT concentrate dose of 1000 units prior to starting fondaparinux. At that time, the antithrombin level was mildly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count progressively returned to within normal limits (recorded at 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87); neurologically, the patient achieved full recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was maintained up to POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at an oral dose of 5 mg twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Sequential SRA assessments continued to demonstrate strong positivity over several months, with multiple follow-up profiles displaying a more classic SRA pattern seen in aHIT (figure 2); testing of the final blood sample collected at POD124, after platelet normalization, indicated predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, characterized by 15% serotonin release without UFH and complete (100%) release upon the addition of UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited several uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serologic findings, all of which were ultimately attributable to a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, the onset of his HIT-associated thrombocytopenia occurred prior to the typical timeframe observed in HIT, which ranges from postoperative day (POD) 5 to POD 10 in individuals following cardiac surgery. Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who subsequently develop HIT experience a platelet count drop due to HIT prior to POD 5—a pattern associated with recent heparin exposure before surgery, as was observed in this case.

Additionally, this patient’s platelet count reached a notably low minimum of 13×10^9/L, concurrent with decreasing fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and schistocytosis during treatment with argatroban. These findings reflect the pronounced hypercoagulable state characteristic of aHIT.

Third, unlike the typical rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia observed after administration of high-dose intravenous Ig in patients with aHIT, our patient’s platelet count did not recover swiftly; this suggests that she experienced a particularly severe variant of the condition. Notably, serologic evaluation supported the presence of an especially severe aHIT subtype, as evidenced by only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro when exposed to monoclonal antibody IV.3. To our knowledge, this unique serologic finding has not been previously described in the context of aHIT. Awareness of this presentation may help prevent missed diagnoses, which could otherwise result in negative clinical outcomes. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately substantiated in our case for several reasons. First, repeat serum samples collected during the extended phase of platelet count normalization exhibited SRA reactivity profiles more typical of aHIT. Second, the final serum specimen demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA activation pattern characteristic of conventional HIT, indicative of declining aHIT antibody levels. Third, alternative explanations for the patient’s atypical clinical trajectory were lacking. Encouragingly, the patient achieved a full clinical recovery without any lingering neurologic deficits.
