Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient underwent cardiac surgery utilizing heparin for anticoagulation; following surgery, his platelet count declined progressively, reaching 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5). This decline led to testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging identified a parietal stroke. An initial screening immunoassay for HIT antibodies was markedly positive, while the platelet activation (serotonin-release) assay displayed an unusual pattern: marked serotonin release occurred at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with only incomplete suppression by a monoclonal antibody that blocks the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulant therapy was switched to argatroban in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing hypercoagulability led to a further transition to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count gradually recovered (155×10⁹/L on POD62), and neurological deficits resolved. Laboratory analyses confirmed the presence of HIT antibodies capable of heparin-independent platelet activation, confirming a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (with a minimum platelet count of 13×10⁹/L), unique features of this aHIT presentation included an early postoperative onset (prior to POD5) related to preoperative heparin exposure, and a distinct laboratory antibody profile exhibiting partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For the past seventy years, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been almost exclusively utilized as the intraoperative anticoagulant during heart surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), largely because of its multiple benefits, such as suppression of artificial surface-induced haemostasis, clearance independent of renal or hepatic pathways, the ability to monitor anticoagulation with activated clotting time, and its low cost. Nonetheless, a significant drawback of UFH is the development of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-mediated complication. Post-cardiac surgery, HIT is relatively frequent, occurring in approximately 0.5%–1.0% of cases depending on the type and duration of postoperative heparin-based thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). Notably, HIT is strongly linked to thrombosis, with a relative risk around 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been understood as a disorder marked by potent platelet activation via clustering of platelet Fc receptors, instigated by the formation of immune complexes involving immunoglobulin G (IgG) class heparin-dependent antibodies that target the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) when complexed with polyanionic heparin. Recently, it has become apparent that a subset of patients experiences atypical—and often severe—manifestations due to HIT antibodies that can activate platelets independently of heparin. This variant has been termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (for review). Herein, we present a case of aHIT characterized by unusually profound and prolonged thrombocytopenia, which was further complicated by thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. This case offers new understanding into the diversity of aHIT, as it documents a distinct serological phenotype marked by partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. Moreover, it underscores the diagnostic difficulties encountered with early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as this patient's exceptionally rapid onset of aHIT was attributable to prior exposure to UFH before surgery. Ultimately, the patient recovered favorably.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had experienced exertional shortness of breath for two years and had chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a newly detected systolic murmur. He underwent cardiac catheterization but did not discontinue his apixaban beforehand; consequently, the procedure was performed by the cardiologist through a transradial arterial route with a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulation dose (only 2000 U of UFH). Angiography revealed 60% narrowing in the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta, and he was booked for cardiac surgery the following week.

The cardiac operation was conducted utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and anticoagulation was established with 45,000 U of UFH. The interventions included aortic valve replacement with a #27 bioprosthetic valve; replacement of the ascending aorta using a #30 Gelweave graft; a single saphenous vein bypass graft to the obtuse marginal coronary artery; and excision of the left atrial appendage. The procedure was completed without incident, no significant blood loss or transfusions were necessary, and separation from bypass was smooth with optimal hemodynamic status.

The platelet count immediately before surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the platelet count decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicating only a mild drop early after cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was started on POD1, using 5000 units of dalteparin via subcutaneous injection. Higher doses of 7500 units were administered on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, the platelet count continued to fall from POD1 through POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient’s condition was stable. On POD5, dalteparin was withheld, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was initiated, with the first dose given at 18:00.

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, worsening delirium along with a further drop in platelets on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (with a repeated measurement of 24×10^9/L) led to a CT scan of the brain. Imaging demonstrated an acute cerebral infarction, showing a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm within the left parietal lobe, extending to the cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, while haemoglobin measured 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), in line with values obtained over the prior three days, reflecting expected early surgical blood loss but no evidence of active hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocytes numbered 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L), indicating an unremarkable result. Haptoglobin was increased at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), which is consistent with a lack of clear haemolysis. Vitamin B12 serum concentration normalized.

Coagulation parameters demonstrated a standard prothrombin time with an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), a partial thromboplastin time of 34 s (reference range 25–37 s), and elevated fibrinogen at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), indicative of an early postoperative phase, as fibrinogen—an acute phase reactant—commonly rises significantly after surgery. A specimen collected on postoperative day 5 was forwarded to a reference lab for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody testing.

Other laboratory assessments (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin) were within normal limits. Blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography was without significant findings.

Peripheral blood film examination on postoperative day 7 verified thrombocytopenia, with no platelet clumping, no schistocytes, and no abnormal “toxic” features of the white cells. Screening for HIT with the PF4/polyanion ELISA was markedly positive at 2.2 optical density units (reference range <0.4 OD units); however, the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) produced an indeterminate outcome (as discussed below).

Differential diagnosis:

Frequent causes of significant thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery—such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, or infection—were deemed improbable based on the patient’s hemodynamic stability and negative blood and urine cultures. Uncommon etiologies, including post-transfusion purpura (in the absence of transfused blood products), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (as indicated by normal haptoglobin levels and initial lack of schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also discounted. This is because the platelet decline occurred earlier than would be expected for newly introduced postoperative medications, including both agents administered only intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid) and those given after surgery (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

While the early reduction in platelet count also preceded the effects of intraoperative UFH administered during cardiopulmonary bypass, later investigation revealed the use of intraprocedural UFH during a preoperative cardiac catheterization. Considering this, along with the occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a markedly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, the most likely diagnosis was heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), probably initiated by UFH exposure during the preoperative catheterization. Additional information regarding the ‘indeterminate’ SRA was solicited from the reference laboratory, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings were thoroughly evaluated. The patient's POD5 serum exhibited strong platelet activation in the form of serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99% release, respectively; with a reference range of less than 20%), aligning with the existence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies. In contrast, no serotonin release (0%) was observed when high-dose UFH (100 U/mL) was used, a distinctive marker of HIT, representing the inability to form PF4-immune complexes at such elevated UFH concentrations. Two further aspects of the SRA findings were highlighted. Firstly, robust serotonin release occurred even without UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), which is typical of aHIT, but not conventional, heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) was introduced, it did not fully suppress the serotonin release induced by the patient's serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release persisted at 76% despite IV.3, instead of falling below the 20% expected in the setting of HIT antibodies. This led to the initial labeling of the SRA outcome as ‘indeterminate.’ Ultimately, it was determined that the patient was affected by aHIT, with the HIT antibodies displaying unusually strong reactivity so that IV.3’s inhibitory effect was only partial. To date, this unusual SRA response pattern has not been documented in previous reports.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and therapy was initiated with argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at a dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is considered an effective off-label treatment for HIT, the hematologist managing the case opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which would allow for easier management should bleeding arise, considering the patient’s recent stroke and unusually pronounced thrombocytopenia. Additionally, intravenous immunoglobulin was administered at a high dose: 100 g on each of two consecutive days, followed by an additional 100 g dose, as the platelet count had not responded during the first 2 days of IVIG. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were taken to assess indirect markers of anticoagulant effectiveness, since increases in fibrinogen and decreases in d-dimer would indicate successful suppression of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of therapy, the patient’s fibrinogen levels showed a steady decline from postoperative day (POD) 6 through POD17, reaching their lowest point at 2.49 g/L. Concurrently, d-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of schistocytes between POD9 and POD16. Collectively, these laboratory abnormalities indicated a severe, advancing consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. Additionally, despite treatment with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, the rise in platelet count was limited—from a pre-intravenous Ig level of 13×10^9/L on POD7 to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14—representing a significantly smaller increase than typically observed in patients with aHIT after such therapy (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. The decision to transition was influenced by the practicality of fondaparinux, which can be delivered once daily via subcutaneous injection, unlike argatroban that requires intravenous dosing adjusted based on PTT. Additional rationale included concerns about argatroban’s ability to sufficiently address the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, given the declining fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the emergence of schistocytes observed during argatroban therapy. As fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to fondaparinux initiation; the patient’s antithrombin level measured before infusion was slightly below reference, at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count progressively returned to normal values (measuring 155×10^9/L at POD62 and 202×10^9/L at POD87), and the patient also achieved full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was continued through POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dose of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial serotonin release assay (SRA) results remained strongly positive over several months, with subsequent SRAs demonstrating features more typical of aHIT (figure 2); the final blood sample, taken at POD124 after normalization of the platelet count, showed a pattern indicative of predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies—serotonin release was 15% in the absence of UFH and 100% in the presence of 0.3 U/mL UFH.

Discussion:

The patient demonstrated several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, each ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. To begin with, the development of HIT-associated thrombocytopenia in this individual occurred prior to the typical time frame for HIT, which ranges from postoperative day (POD) 5 through POD 10 in patients following cardiac surgery. Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients with HIT experience a platelet count decline attributable to HIT before POD5; this earlier onset is associated with recent preoperative heparin exposure, a circumstance that was observed in our patient.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count reached a strikingly low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreased fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer, and schistocytes on peripheral smear, all occurring during anticoagulation with argatroban. These manifestations of pronounced hypercoagulability in severe HIT are characteristic of aHIT.

Third, our patient did not demonstrate the rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia that is commonly observed when high-dose intravenous Ig is administered to individuals with aHIT; this suggests that the patient was experiencing an exceptionally severe manifestation of this condition. In fact, serological findings indicated only partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro in the presence of monoclonal antibody IV.3, suggestive of a particularly severe aHIT subtype. To our knowledge, this distinct serological profile has not previously been described in the context of aHIT; recognition of this may help prevent failure to diagnose aHIT and thus avert potential clinical complications. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT was eventually confirmed in our patient based on three main points. First, subsequent serum samples obtained during the extended recovery period of platelet counts displayed SRA activity patterns more typical of aHIT. Second, the final specimen tested exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA reaction profile consistent with classic HIT, indicating a decline in aHIT antibody levels. Third, there was no alternative diagnosis to account for the patient's atypical clinical progression. Thankfully, the patient achieved an excellent recovery without any persistent neurological deficits.
