Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An elderly male patient received heparin anticoagulation during cardiac surgery; after the procedure, his platelet count progressively declined, reaching 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to the evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging identified a parietal stroke. Testing with a HIT antibody immunoassay was strongly positive, while the serotonin-release assay for platelet activation demonstrated an unusual pattern, with marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin and only partial inhibition when a monoclonal antibody blocking the platelet Fc receptor was applied. Anticoagulation was switched to argatroban and high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Continued evidence of hypercoagulability resulted in further change to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count recovered gradually (reaching 155×10⁹/L by POD62), and the patient’s neurological symptoms resolved. Further lab analysis confirmed the presence of HIT antibodies capable of inducing platelet activation independent of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). This case featured not only severe thrombocytopenia (nadir platelet count of 13×10⁹/L) but also notable features such as onset in the early postoperative phase—attributed to prior heparin exposure before surgery—and an uncommon aHIT antibody laboratory profile showing partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant anticoagulant administered intraoperatively during heart surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). This preference is attributed to several benefits, such as suppression of hemostatic activation on artificial surfaces, elimination that does not rely on hepatic or renal function, the feasibility of monitoring anticoagulation via activated clotting time intraoperatively, and affordability. Nevertheless, UFH is linked to a significant immune-mediated complication—heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Following cardiac surgery, HIT occurs at a relatively notable rate, estimated between 0.5% and 1.0%, which varies according to the length and specific type (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin [LMWH]) of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis. HIT is also particularly significant for its robust connection to thrombotic events, conferring an approximate 15-fold increase in relative risk for thrombosis.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) has traditionally been characterized as a condition involving pronounced platelet activation via clustering of Fc receptors on platelets, instigated by immune complexes formed between heparin-dependent IgG antibodies and platelet factor 4 (PF4) when it is associated with the polyanion heparin. More recently, there has been increased awareness of patients experiencing unusual and sometimes markedly severe clinical manifestations stemming from HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independent of heparin; this distinct variant has been termed 'autoimmune HIT' (aHIT) (for review). Here, we report a case of aHIT presenting as an exceptional and persistent thrombocytopenia, which was further complicated by the development of a thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. The case offers new perspectives on the range of aHIT presentations, notably by revealing a particularly severe serological profile that demonstrated partial resistance to inhibition of Fc receptors. In addition, this case underscores the diagnostic complexities associated with early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the extraordinarily rapid onset of aHIT in our patient could be attributed to exposure to unfractionated heparin (UFH) prior to surgery. Despite these complications, the patient ultimately achieved a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male in his 70s, known for two years of exertional dyspnea and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He proceeded to undergo diagnostic cardiac catheterization without interruption of his apixaban prior to the procedure. As a result, the cardiologist opted for transradial artery access and utilized a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulation dose, administering only 2000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). Angiography revealed 60% luminal narrowing of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. He was consequently scheduled for cardiac surgery in one week.

Cardiac surgery was carried out utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and anticoagulation was provided by administering 45,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). The procedures performed included an aortic valve replacement with a size 27 bioprosthetic valve, replacement of the ascending aorta using a size 30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) employing a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation progressed without complications, significant hemorrhage, or requirement for blood transfusions, and the patient was successfully weaned from CPB with stable and optimal haemodynamic parameters.

The platelet count immediately before surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference interval: 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the count had decreased to 155×10^9/L, which constitutes a modest early drop commonly observed after cardiac surgery. Standard thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, with a subcutaneous dose of 5000 U dalteparin, followed by increased doses of 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, the platelet count continued to decline each day, reaching 50×10^9/L by POD5 (see figure 1). Apart from postoperative delirium, the patient remained clinically stable. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started (administered first at 18:00 on POD5).

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, progressive worsening of the delirium in conjunction with a further decrease in platelet count to 26×10^9/L (confirmed at 24×10^9/L) on POD6 prompted a CT scan of the brain. Imaging demonstrated an acute infarction within the left parietal lobe, identified as a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count showed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L and a haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), which was comparable to haemoglobin measurements from the prior three days—findings consistent with anticipated early postoperative blood loss in the absence of continued bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) with a normal differential. The reticulocyte count measured 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L), which was within the expected range. Haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), a result suggestive of no significant haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 level normalized.

The coagulation panel demonstrated a prothrombin time within the expected range, reflected by an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds), and an increased fibrinogen concentration of 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L). This fibrinogen elevation aligns with the early postoperative phase, during which levels often rise significantly due to its role as an acute phase reactant. On postoperative day 5 (POD5), a blood specimen was collected and forwarded to a reference laboratory for evaluation of HIT antibodies.

Other blood work included normal chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were drawn. A chest X-ray showed no major abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on postoperative day 7 verified thrombocytopenia, noting no platelet clumping, schistocytes, or white blood cell features indicative of toxicity. Testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) using the PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) showed a markedly elevated result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units). However, the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) returned an 'indeterminate' result, which is further addressed below.

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infection, were considered improbable based on the patient's clinical presentation and laboratory data (stable haemodynamic status; negative blood and urine cultures). Uncommon etiologies, including post-transfusion purpura (no history of blood transfusion), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (normal haptoglobin level, absence of schistocytes on initial evaluation), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also considered unlikely since the decline in platelet count occurred too soon after surgery to be attributed to any recently introduced medications, whether administered intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid) or prescribed during the postoperative period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

While the drop in platelet count happened earlier than would be expected from the UFH given during cardiopulmonary bypass surgery, additional investigation revealed that UFH had also been administered during preoperative cardiac catheterization. Considering the occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA result, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was determined to be the most likely diagnosis, probably initiated by the UFH exposure during preoperative heart catheterization. The reporting facility, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, was asked to provide more information concerning the 'indeterminate' SRA result.

The SRA findings underwent detailed evaluation. The patient’s serum collected on POD5 demonstrated pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20% release), which aligns with the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies. Conversely, at a supratherapeutic UFH concentration of 100 U/mL, there was no serotonin release (0%), a result that typifies HIT due to the disruption of PF4-immune complex formation at elevated heparin levels. Nonetheless, two further aspects of the SRA data warranted attention. First, the patient’s serum induced robust serotonin release even without UFH present (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; reference range <20%), a hallmark observed in aHIT rather than conventional heparin-dependent HIT. Second, incorporation of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 did not fully suppress serotonin release in the context of 0.1 U/mL UFH and patient serum: serotonin release remained high at 76% with IV.3, not declining below 20% as typically expected with HIT antibodies, resulting initially in a classification of the SRA findings as ‘indeterminate.’ The rationale for this, however, was that the patient indeed had aHIT, but possessed HIT antibodies that demonstrated an unusually high level of reactivity, which led to only partial inhibition by IV.3—unlike the near-complete inhibition usually seen. To date, this distinctive SRA response pattern has not been described in prior literature.

Treatment:

Prophylactic fondaparinux was stopped, and therapeutic argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was initiated at 0.75 mcg/kg/min, with a target PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While therapeutic doses of fondaparinux are considered a valid ‘off-label’ anticoagulant choice for HIT, the hematologist elected to use argatroban due to its shorter half-life, considering the recent cerebrovascular event and the patient's pronounced thrombocytopenia, as this would facilitate rapid reversal if bleeding developed. High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) was also administered, with 100 g infused on each of two consecutive days, and a third 100 g dose added because platelet counts did not rise after the first two IVIg treatments. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were undertaken to monitor the anticoagulant's effectiveness indirectly, since increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer levels would be indicative of improved control of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of therapy, there was a steady reduction in fibrinogen concentration from postoperative day 6 to day 17, reaching a lowest value of 2.49 g/L. The d-dimer levels peaked near postoperative day 12, and peripheral blood smears revealed occasional schistocytes between postoperative days 9 and 16. Collectively, these laboratory findings were indicative of a severe, advancing consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. Additionally, the increase in platelet count after high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin administration was limited, rising from 13×10^9/L before the infusion on postoperative day 7 to a peak of 54×10^9/L on postoperative day 14. This increment was substantially less than the typical platelet response generally observed in patients with aHIT following high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (for review).

On postoperative day 14, anticoagulation was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. The rationale for selecting fondaparinux included the convenience of once-daily subcutaneous dosing compared to argatroban, which necessitates PTT-adjusted intravenous infusion, as well as concerns that argatroban may not have sufficiently managed HIT-related hypercoagulability—evidenced by a decrease in fibrinogen, increase in d-dimer, and emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect antithrombin (AT)-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, 1000 units of AT concentrate were administered prior to initiation. The AT level before the infusion was slightly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet levels steadily returned to the normal range (155×10^9/L at postoperative day 62; 202×10^9/L at postoperative day 87), accompanied by full resolution of neurological deficits. Fondaparinux therapy continued until postoperative day 65, after which apixaban was restarted at 5 mg orally twice daily for extended anticoagulation management. Consecutive serotonin release assay (SRA) tests remained markedly positive for several months, with subsequent assessments demonstrating a SRA response pattern more typical of autoimmune HIT (refer to figure 2). The final tested sample, drawn on postoperative day 124 after normalization of platelet count, produced findings indicative of mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, with only 15% serotonin release observed without unfractionated heparin, but reaching 100% release in the presence of UFH at a concentration of 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

Our patient exhibited several atypical clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, each of which was ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Firstly, thrombocytopenia linked to HIT in this case developed earlier than the typical time frame, which for individuals undergoing cardiac surgery usually spans POD5 to POD10 (inclusive). Less than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a reduction in platelet count before POD5, an occurrence commonly associated with recent preoperative heparin administration, as seen in this patient.

Secondly, this individual’s platelet count declined to an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, concurrent with decreasing fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer, and schistocytosis while receiving argatroban therapy. These manifestations, indicative of pronounced hypercoagulability related to HIT, point toward a diagnosis of aHIT.

Third, unlike the typical prompt resolution of thrombocytopenia seen in aHIT patients treated with high-dose intravenous Ig, our patient’s platelet count did not rapidly improve, indicating a particularly severe variant of aHIT. Supporting this, serological testing revealed only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro with the monoclonal antibody IV.3—a novel serological pattern for aHIT that, to our knowledge, has not been previously described. Recognizing this unique profile may help prevent missed diagnoses of aHIT and subsequent negative outcomes. Despite these unusual findings, a diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately established in this case for several reasons. First, later sera collected during the extended recovery of the platelet count showed some SRA reactivity patterns more in line with aHIT. Second, the final tested sample demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA response typical of conventional HIT, suggesting the aHIT antibodies were diminishing. Third, alternative diagnoses were not sufficient to account for the patient’s atypical presentation. Ultimately, the patient achieved a full clinical recovery without lingering neurologic deficits.
