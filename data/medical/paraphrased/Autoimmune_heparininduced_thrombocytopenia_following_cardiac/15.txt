Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient underwent cardiac surgery using heparin for anticoagulation. Following surgery, his platelet count progressively decreased, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led clinicians to test for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Neuroimaging identified a stroke in the parietal region. An initial immunoassay to detect HIT antibodies was strongly positive, and subsequent serotonin-release assay results were unusual, showing robust serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin concentrations that was only partially inhibited by the addition of a monoclonal antibody targeting the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulation was transitioned to argatroban combined with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to ongoing hypercoagulability, therapy was then changed to fondaparinux, leading to a gradual normalization of the platelet count (155×10⁹/L, POD62) and resolution of neurological symptoms. Further laboratory investigation established the presence of HIT antibodies with platelet-activating effects independent of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Noteworthy features of this aHIT case, in addition to profound thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count 13×10⁹/L), included its rapid development early in the postoperative period (prior to POD5) related to preoperative exposure to heparin, along with a distinctive antibody laboratory pattern exhibiting partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant intraoperative anticoagulant in cardiac surgeries employing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). This preference is attributed to multiple benefits, such as its capacity to suppress haemostasis triggered by contact with artificial surfaces, elimination independent of renal or hepatic function, the feasibility of monitoring anticoagulation intraoperatively via activated clotting time, and its affordability. Nevertheless, UFH is associated with a significant immune-mediated complication—heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Following cardiac surgery, HIT arises at a rate estimated between 0.5% and 1.0%, a figure that varies with the type and length of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (UFH compared to low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). HIT is particularly concerning due to its strong linkage with thrombotic events, conferring an approximate 15-fold elevated risk of thrombosis.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) has traditionally been characterized as a condition involving robust platelet activation via platelet Fc receptor cross-linking, initiated by immune complexes formed with heparin-dependent IgG antibodies targeting the positively charged platelet factor 4 (PF4) when complexed with negatively charged heparin. More recently, there has been growing awareness that certain patients experience unusual and often severe clinical courses due to HIT antibodies that can activate platelets independent of heparin; this subset has been termed “autoimmune HIT” (aHIT) (for review). In this article, we present a case of aHIT with exceptionally profound and prolonged thrombocytopenia, further complicated by thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. The described case contributes new understanding to the spectrum of aHIT, noting an especially severe serological presentation, including partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. This case further illustrates the diagnostic difficulties associated with early postoperative thrombocytopenia, with the patient’s atypically early presentation of aHIT attributed to preoperative administration of unfractionated heparin (UFH). The patient ultimately experienced a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had experienced exertional shortness of breath for two years and had chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He was referred for diagnostic cardiac catheterization, but his apixaban was not discontinued before the procedure. Therefore, the cardiologist chose a transradial artery access and administered a reduced dose of anticoagulant intraoperatively (only 2000 units of UFH). Angiography revealed 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Cardiac surgery was subsequently planned for the following week.

Cardiac surgery was conducted with the use of CPB, and anticoagulation was managed by administering 45,000 U of UFH. The procedure entailed replacement of the aortic valve using a #27 bioprosthesis, substitution of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, single-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting employing a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation progressed without complications, significant hemorrhage, or the requirement for transfusion, and the patient was weaned from CPB with stable and favorable haemodynamics.

Immediately prior to surgery, the patient’s platelet count measured 210×10^9/L (reference interval 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the count had decreased to 155×10^9/L, a modest reduction frequently seen after cardiac surgery. Standard prophylaxis for thrombosis was started on POD1 using low molecular weight heparin (LMWH), with 5000 U of dalteparin given subcutaneously. On subsequent days—POD2, POD3, and POD4—the dalteparin dose was increased to 7500 U daily. Unexpectedly, between POD1 and POD5, the platelet count continued to fall, reaching 50×10^9/L by POD5 (see figure 1). The patient’s recovery was unremarkable apart from episodes of postoperative delirium. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and instead, fondaparinux was initiated at a prophylactic dose (2.5 mg, first administration at 18:00 on POD5).

Though there were no localized neurological deficits, a worsening in postoperative delirium combined with a further reduction in platelet count on POD6—to 26×10^9/L, and then 24×10^9/L on repeat testing—led clinicians to order a CT scan of the brain. Imaging revealed a recent cerebral infarction, indicated by an area of decreased attenuation measuring 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, with involvement extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L and a haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), which was comparable to the haemoglobin measurements from the prior three days—findings consistent with anticipated early postoperative blood loss rather than active hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) and the differential was within normal limits. Reticulocyte count measured 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L), and was not remarkable. Haptoglobin was increased at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating that significant haemolysis was not present. The serum vitamin B12 concentration was within normal range.

Coagulation testing revealed a prothrombin time within the standard range, with an international normalized ratio measured at 1.1 (reference range [RR] 0.9–1.1), and a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (RR 25–37 s), both within normal parameters. Fibrinogen levels were found to be elevated at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), which aligns with the early postoperative period when this acute phase reactant commonly becomes markedly increased. On postoperative day 5 (POD5), a blood specimen was submitted to an external laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody analysis.

Other blood work included normal chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were drawn. A chest X-ray showed no major abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear obtained on postoperative day 7 verified thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet aggregates, schistocytes, or abnormal toxic changes in white blood cells. The initial screening test for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)—the PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)—returned a markedly elevated value of 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units). However, the subsequent confirmatory test, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (to be elaborated upon below).

Differential diagnosis:

Common causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, or infection, appeared improbable due to the patient’s stable haemodynamics and negative results from blood and urine cultures. Uncommon etiologies, including post-transfusion purpura (since the patient had not received any blood transfusions), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (given normal haptoglobin and initially no schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia were also deemed unlikely. This was because the decline in platelet count happened too soon to be attributed to any medications introduced postoperatively, whether administered on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or during the postoperative period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

Although the decrease in platelet count occurred too early to attribute solely to the UFH administered during CPB, further investigation revealed that UFH had also been given intraprocedurally during the preoperative cardiac catheterization. In conjunction with the thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, HIT was considered the most likely diagnosis, presumably initiated by the UFH given at preoperative heart catheterization. Additional information regarding the ‘indeterminate’ SRA was sought from the reporting institution, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

A critical assessment of the SRA findings was performed. In line with the detection of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient's serum obtained on POD5 triggered pronounced serotonin release at UFH concentrations of 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20%), and demonstrated an absence of release (0%) when exposed to a supratherapeutic dose of UFH (100 U/mL); this pattern, with no platelet activation at high heparin levels, is a hallmark of HIT and illustrates that formation of PF4-immune complexes is hindered at excessive UFH concentrations. Nevertheless, two further aspects of the SRA results were apparent. Firstly, the patient's serum also induced strong serotonin release without the presence of UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; reference range <20%), a pattern typical of aHIT but not of the conventional heparin-dependent form of HIT. Secondly, the introduction of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 did not fully suppress serotonin release in response to patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: a release of 76% was observed with IV.3, as opposed to the expected suppression below 20% typically seen with HIT antibodies; this observation resulted in the original classification of the SRA result as ‘indeterminate’. The underlying rationale was that the patient's actual condition was aHIT, but that their HIT antibodies exhibited unusually high reactivity, leading to only partial reduction of serotonin release by IV.3 compared to standard cases. To the best of our knowledge, this distinctive SRA profile has not been previously documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosing was stopped, and argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was initiated for therapeutic anticoagulation at a rate of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, with a goal partial thromboplastin time (PTT) between 55 and 85 seconds. While therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is considered an effective, albeit off-label, option for anticoagulation in HIT, the hematologist elected to use argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which was preferable in the context of recent stroke and marked thrombocytopenia should bleeding develop. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) was administered—100 g for two days, then an additional 100 g dose—because there was no initial rise in platelet count following the first two days of IVIg. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were obtained to indirectly assess anticoagulant efficacy, as increased fibrinogen and decreased d-dimer would indicate control of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

The patient's initial management was marked by a steady reduction in fibrinogen levels from postoperative day (POD) 6 through POD17, with the lowest point recorded at 2.49 g/L. D-dimer values peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16. Collectively, these laboratory findings indicated a severe, progressive consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. In addition, despite administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, the rise in platelet count was limited—from 13×10^9/L on POD7 (prior to intravenous Ig) to a maximum of 54×10^9/L by POD14—a response notably less robust than is typically observed in patients with aHIT receiving high-dose intravenous Ig (for review).

On postoperative day 14, anticoagulation therapy was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once per day. The rationale behind selecting fondaparinux included its convenient once-daily subcutaneous administration compared to the need for continuous intravenous infusion and PTT-based dose adjustment required for argatroban. Additionally, there were concerns about argatroban's potential inadequacy in managing the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, as suggested by the observed decline in fibrinogen, increased d-dimer levels, and emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Because fondaparinux is an indirect factor Xa inhibitor that requires antithrombin (AT) for activity, a supplemental dose of AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to starting fondaparinux; the patient’s AT level before the infusion was mildly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range: 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count steadily normalized, reaching 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87. The patient also achieved full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux was administered until POD65, after which apixaban was reinstated at a dose of 5 mg orally twice daily for extended anticoagulation. Repeated SRA assays remained strongly positive over several months, with later profiles displaying SRA patterns more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The final blood specimen, drawn at POD124 after the platelet count normalized, demonstrated findings compatible with predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release was 15% without UFH but increased to 100% in the presence of UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient presented with several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings that were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, the thrombocytopenia linked to HIT emerged before the typical timeframe for this complication, which in individuals undergoing cardiac surgery generally falls between postoperative day (POD) 5 and POD10. It is rare—seen in approximately 10% of post-cardiac surgery HIT cases—for the platelet count decrease to occur before POD5, a pattern usually associated with recent exposure to heparin prior to surgery, as was observed in this case.

Additionally, the patient experienced a markedly low platelet count, reaching a nadir of 13×10^9/L, in conjunction with declining fibrinogen levels, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and evidence of schistocytes during treatment with argatroban. These indicators, characteristic of the pronounced hypercoagulable state seen in severe HIT, support the diagnosis of aHIT.

Third, our patient’s thrombocytopenia did not resolve rapidly following high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin therapy, which is the typical response observed in cases of aHIT; this suggests an exceptionally severe manifestation of the disorder in this instance. Serologically, only partial inhibition of platelet activation by monoclonal antibody IV.3 was seen in vitro—a finding indicative of a severe aHIT subtype. To our knowledge, this distinct serologic profile has not been described previously in association with aHIT, and recognizing it may prevent the oversight of aHIT diagnosis and its associated risks. Despite these challenges, the diagnosis of aHIT in this patient was eventually substantiated for several reasons. First, serial testing of serum collected during the extended period of platelet count normalization showed SRA reactivity patterns more aligned with aHIT. Second, the final specimen demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA response characteristic of conventional HIT, signaling a decline of the aHIT antibodies. Third, alternative explanations for the patient’s atypical clinical progression were lacking. Ultimately, the patient experienced a full clinical recovery without lingering neurological deficits.
