Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient had cardiac surgery and received heparin for anticoagulation. Following the operation, his platelet count progressively decreased, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Neuroimaging revealed a parietal stroke. An initial immunoassay for HIT antibodies returned strongly positive, while the platelet activation test (serotonin-release assay) displayed an unusual pattern: significant serotonin release was seen at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin concentrations, and was only partially suppressed by a monoclonal antibody targeting the platelet Fc receptor. The anticoagulant regimen was switched to argatroban and high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Because hypercoagulability persisted, fondaparinux was subsequently started, resulting in a gradual improvement of platelet counts (155×10⁹/L, POD62) and resolution of neurological deficits. Laboratory studies demonstrated the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independent of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (nadir platelet count of 13×10⁹/L), distinctive features of this aHIT case included onset shortly after surgery (before POD5), attributed to preoperative heparin administration, as well as a distinctive laboratory antibody pattern showing partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

Throughout the seven decades in which cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) has been utilized for heart surgery, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant intraoperative anticoagulant. Its widespread use stems from numerous benefits, including suppression of haemostatic activation by artificial surfaces, clearance independent of renal and hepatic function, facilitation of real-time monitoring of anticoagulation with the activated clotting time, and affordability. Nevertheless, UFH is associated with a significant immune-mediated complication, known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Following cardiac procedures, HIT occurs at a rate estimated between 0.5% and 1.0%, varying according to the length and type of postoperative heparin prophylaxis—whether UFH or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) is used—and is especially notable due to its pronounced correlation with thrombotic events, with an approximate 15-fold increased risk.

Historically, HIT has been characterized by pronounced platelet activation via platelet Fc receptor cross-linking, which is instigated by immune complexes formed from heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies targeting the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) when it complexes with polyanionic heparin. More recently, it has been acknowledged that certain patients experience non-standard, and frequently severe, disease courses owing to HIT antibodies that induce platelet activation in a heparin-independent manner; this particular variant has been termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (for review). This article presents a case of aHIT exhibiting exceptional and persistent thrombocytopenia, complicated by a thrombotic cerebrovascular event following cardiac surgery. The case expands the understanding of aHIT by illustrating an unusually severe serological profile that demonstrated partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. Furthermore, it underscores the difficulties inherent in diagnosing postoperative thrombocytopenia, as this patient's atypically rapid onset of aHIT was attributable to prior exposure to UFH before surgery. The patient's ultimate recovery was positive.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies with a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (maintained on apixaban 5 mg twice daily) was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic heart murmur. He underwent cardiac catheterization without withholding his apixaban before the procedure. To accommodate this, the cardiologist selected a transradial arterial approach and administered a reduced intraprocedural dose of anticoagulation (only 2000 units of unfractionated heparin). Angiography revealed a 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Cardiac surgery was planned to take place one week later.

During surgery, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) was initiated and anticoagulation was achieved with 45,000 units of UFH. The operative procedures included implantation of a #27 bioprosthetic aortic valve, replacement of the ascending aorta using a #30 Gelweave graft, single coronary artery bypass grafting of the obtuse marginal artery utilizing a saphenous vein, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was completed without complications, with no significant blood loss or requirement for transfusion, and the patient was weaned from bypass with stable and robust hemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery was measured at 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the platelet count had dropped to 155×10^9/L, which indicated only a modest decrease often observed following cardiac procedures. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) commenced on POD1, utilizing 5000 U of dalteparin via subcutaneous injection; higher doses (7500 U) were subsequently given on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, between POD1 and POD5, platelet levels continued to fall, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from experiencing postoperative delirium, the patient’s clinical status remained stable. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and a prophylactic dose of fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started, with the initial administration at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite the absence of specific neurological deficits, the patient exhibited worsening delirium postoperatively, and the platelet count decreased further on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (repeat measurement, 24×10^9/L). This prompted a brain CT scan, which identified an acute cerebral infarction manifested as an area of hypodensity measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe with extension into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count revealed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, alongside a haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), which was comparable to haemoglobin values observed over the previous three days, indicating expected early post-surgical blood loss without evidence of persistent bleeding. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) with a normal differential. The reticulocyte count was within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was increased at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), suggesting no active haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 level was normal.

Coagulation testing showed prothrombin time within normal limits, and the international normalised ratio (INR) was 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1). Partial thromboplastin time (PTT) measured 34 seconds (RR 25–37 s), while fibrinogen was elevated at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), reflecting the postoperative state, as fibrinogen is an acute phase reactant that typically increases considerably during the early period following surgery. A blood specimen collected on POD5 was sent for HIT antibody analysis at a reference lab.

Additional laboratory assessments included normal values for urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Both blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography did not reveal significant abnormalities.

Assessment of the peripheral blood film on POD7 substantiated the presence of thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet clumping, red cell fragmentation (schistocytes), or marked white cell abnormalities. HIT screening via the PF4/polyanion ELISA showed a strongly positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units), but the serotonin-release assay (SRA), which serves as the confirmatory platelet activation test, produced an ‘indeterminate’ finding (elaborated later).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of significant thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infection, appeared improbable due to both the patient's stable haemodynamic parameters and negative results from blood and urine cultures. Less common causes, such as post-transfusion purpura (as no transfusions had been performed), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura following surgery (given normal haptoglobin levels and no initial schistocytes observed), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also considered unlikely since the onset of thrombocytopenia preceded exposure to any new postoperative medications, which included those administered only intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) and those initiated after surgery (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

Even though the timing of platelet reduction was too soon to be attributed to unfractionated heparin (UFH) given during CPB, further review of the patient’s history revealed intra-arterial UFH was used during the preoperative cardiac catheterisation. When coupled with the development of a thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) became the leading diagnosis, most likely triggered by the UFH exposure during preoperative catheterisation. Additional clarification on the ‘indeterminate’ SRA was solicited from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

A thorough evaluation of the SRA findings was performed. In alignment with the detection of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s post-operative day 5 serum induced pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release). Furthermore, there was a complete absence of serotonin release (0%) at a supratherapeutic UFH concentration (100 U/mL); this lack of platelet activation at elevated heparin levels is indicative of HIT and arises because PF4-containing immune complexes do not form at these high UFH concentrations. Nevertheless, two other notable aspects of the SRA were observed. First, robust serotonin release was triggered by the patient’s serum even in the absence of UFH (96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a finding typical for aHIT and not characteristic of classic, heparin-dependent HIT. Second, the application of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress the serotonin release detected when the patient serum was present at 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release was reduced to only 76% with IV.3, instead of dropping below 20% as is standard with HIT antibodies. As a result, the SRA outcome was initially deemed 'indeterminate.' The underlying explanation, however, was that the diagnosis was indeed aHIT, yet the patient’s HIT antibodies displayed unusually strong reactivity, which only led to partial inhibition by IV.3, unlike the more complete inhibition commonly observed. This atypical SRA response pattern, to our knowledge, has not been previously documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux, which had been administered at a prophylactic dose, was stopped, and argatroban—a direct thrombin inhibitor—was started at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Although therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is considered an effective off-label option for anticoagulation in HIT, the hematologist opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, allowing for more rapid discontinuation should bleeding arise, particularly in light of the patient’s recent cerebrovascular accident and pronounced thrombocytopenia. High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was also administered, initially at 100 g daily for two days, followed by another 100 g dose after no significant platelet count response was seen following the first two days of IV Ig. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were taken as indirect markers to assess anticoagulation efficacy, since increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer would indicate effective suppression of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of treatment, the patient experienced a gradual reduction in fibrinogen levels from postoperative day (POD) 6 through POD17, reaching a lowest value of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer concentrations peaked around POD12, and the peripheral blood smear revealed the presence of schistocytes between POD9 and POD16. Collectively, these findings indicated a significant and advancing consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. Additionally, after the administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), the platelet count increased only minimally—from 13×10^9/L prior to IVIg treatment on POD7 to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L by POD14. This increment was substantially less than the typical platelet response observed following high-dose IVIg in patients with aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, anticoagulation was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg once daily administered subcutaneously. Factors influencing the selection of fondaparinux included the convenience of once-daily subcutaneous injections compared to the need for PTT-adjusted intravenous dosing with argatroban, and concerns that argatroban might not have been adequately controlling the HIT-related hypercoagulable state, as suggested by decreasing fibrinogen levels, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux exerts its anticoagulant effect as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, a dose of antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) was administered before beginning fondaparinux, with the patient's antithrombin level prior to infusion slightly low at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet level steadily returned to normal, reaching 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87; the patient also achieved full neurological improvement. Fondaparinux treatment continued until POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Multiple SRA tests remained strongly positive for several months, with subsequent assays displaying SRA patterns more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The final blood sample, taken on POD124 after platelet normalization, demonstrated findings consistent with primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies—only 15% serotonin release without UFH and 100% release in the presence of UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

This patient exhibited several atypical clinical, laboratory, and serologic findings, all ultimately attributable to a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, the onset of HIT-associated thrombocytopenia occurred prior to the typical window for HIT, which, in the context of cardiac surgery, spans POD5 to POD10. HIT-related platelet decreases manifest before POD5 in only approximately 10% of post-cardiac surgery patients who develop the condition; this occurrence is associated with recent preoperative heparin administration, a circumstance present in this case as well.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count decreased to a particularly low nadir of 13×10^9/L, along with decreasing fibrinogen levels, increasing d-dimer, and schistocytosis during argatroban therapy. These manifestations, signifying severe HIT-related hypercoagulability, point to a diagnosis of aHIT.

Thirdly, our patient did not experience the rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia commonly seen following administration of high-dose intravenous Ig to individuals with aHIT, indicating an atypically severe manifestation of this disorder. In fact, serological testing pointed to a particularly severe subtype of aHIT, demonstrated by only partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present; as far as we are aware, this distinctive serological profile has not been documented in relation to aHIT, and recognition of this finding may prevent missed diagnoses of aHIT with possible detrimental outcomes. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT was eventually established for our patient, supported by at least three factors. First, testing with sera obtained during the extended recovery of the platelet count revealed SRA reactivity patterns that were more typical of aHIT. Second, the final specimen tested exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA profile consistent with classic HIT, suggesting decreasing levels of aHIT antibodies. Third, no alternative diagnosis could sufficiently account for the patient’s unusual clinical trajectory. Encouragingly, the patient achieved a full clinical recovery, and no lingering neurological deficits were observed.
