Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin anticoagulation during cardiac surgery and subsequently exhibited a progressive decline in platelet count, falling to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to investigation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging detected a parietal lobe infarct. HIT antibody screening using an immunoassay was strongly positive, while the platelet activation assay (serotonin-release assay) demonstrated an unusual response: substantial serotonin release at heparin concentrations of 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL, with only partial inhibition achieved by a monoclonal antibody blocking the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulation therapy was shifted to argatroban along with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Persistent hypercoagulable status led to a further transition to fondaparinux, after which platelet numbers gradually rose (reaching 155×10⁹/L by POD62) and neurological symptoms resolved. Further laboratory studies revealed HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin, indicative of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Key features of this aHIT case, beyond severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count nadir 13×10⁹/L), included an unusually early onset post-surgery (preceding POD5) attributable to heparin exposure prior to surgery, as well as a distinctive laboratory pattern of aHIT antibodies with partial resistance to inhibition via Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant intraoperative anticoagulant during cardiac procedures utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), primarily owing to its numerous benefits, such as suppression of haemostatic activity triggered by artificial surfaces, clearance mechanisms independent of both renal and hepatic systems, capability for intraoperative monitoring via activated clotting time, and affordable cost. Nevertheless, UFH can induce a significant immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). HIT occurs with notable frequency after cardiac surgery, affecting approximately 0.5%–1.0% of cases, contingent upon the duration and choice of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (either UFH or low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). Its substantial correlation with thrombotic events is highlighted by an approximate 15-fold increase in relative risk for thrombosis.

Traditionally, HIT has been regarded as a condition marked by potent platelet activation mediated through clustering of platelet Fc receptors, which results from immune complexes involving heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies directed against platelet factor 4 (PF4)—a cationic protein—complexed with polyanionic heparin. Recent evidence has revealed that certain patients experience atypical, often severe, clinical manifestations attributed to HIT antibodies capable of triggering platelet activation without heparin. This distinct variant, termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT), has been discussed in the literature (for review). In this case report, we present a patient with aHIT exhibiting pronounced and persistent thrombocytopenia, further complicated by thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. The report sheds light on the heterogeneity of aHIT by revealing an exceptionally severe serological profile characterized by partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. Additionally, it illustrates the difficulties involved in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia; the patient's unusually rapid onset of aHIT was traced to preoperative administration of UFH. Ultimately, the patient’s prognosis was favourable.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, with a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a newly detected systolic murmur. Diagnostic cardiac catheterisation was undertaken, during which the patient continued apixaban rather than withholding it pre-procedure. Consequently, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery approach and administered a reduced dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (only 2000 U of UFH). Angiography revealed a 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta; plans were made for cardiac surgery in one week.

Cardiac surgery was conducted utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and anticoagulation was accomplished with 45 000 U of UFH. The operative procedure involved aortic valve replacement using a #27 bioprosthesis, substitution of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, single-vessel coronary artery bypass using a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal branch, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was uneventful, with no significant blood loss or requirement for transfusion, and the patient was weaned from bypass without difficulty, displaying stable haemodynamic status.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), it measured 155×10^9/L, indicating a mild decrease commonly seen after cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with LMWH was initiated on POD1; the patient received 5000 U dalteparin via subcutaneous injection. On POD2, POD3, and POD4, a higher dose of 7500 U was administered. Contrary to expectations, the platelet count continued to drop steadily from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (figure 1). Clinically, the patient was stable except for postoperative delirium. Dalteparin was omitted on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started, with the first dose given at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the patient experienced worsening postoperative delirium and a further reduction in platelet count on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (with a repeat value of 24×10^9/L). This led to a CT brain scan, which demonstrated an acute cerebral infarction characterized by a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, with haemoglobin measured at 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), consistent with previous values recorded over the prior three days, aligning with anticipated early postoperative blood loss and no evidence of ongoing bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) and showed a normal differential. Reticulocytes were measured at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L), which was within normal limits. Haptoglobin was raised to 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), which supports the absence of clinically significant haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 level had normalized.

Coagulation testing revealed a normal prothrombin time and an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), with partial thromboplastin time at 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds), both within normal parameters. Fibrinogen was markedly increased at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), a finding in keeping with the acute phase response frequently observed shortly after surgery. On postoperative day 5, blood was submitted to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody analysis.

Additional laboratory investigations included standard chemistry—urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin—all returning within normal ranges. Both blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography demonstrated no significant abnormalities.

On postoperative day 7, evaluation of the peripheral blood smear verified thrombocytopenia (with absence of platelet clumping), and there were no schistocytes or abnormal ‘toxic’ white cell findings noted. The PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for HIT screening yielded a strongly positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units). However, the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) produced an ‘indeterminate’ result (which will be addressed later).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of profound thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infection, were considered improbable due to the patient's stable hemodynamic status and the absence of infection (negative blood and urine cultures). Less common etiologies of postoperative thrombocytopenia, including post-transfusion purpura (without any transfusions), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura following surgery (as evidenced by normal haptoglobin and lack of schistocytes at presentation), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia were also deemed unlikely. The rapid onset of thrombocytopenia occurred before sufficient exposure to newly introduced perioperative and postoperative medications, including intraoperative vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid, as well as postoperative furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine.

Despite the platelet count drop manifesting earlier than expected from the intraoperative UFH used during cardiopulmonary bypass, further investigation revealed intraprocedural UFH was administered at the time of preoperative cardiac catheterization. The combination of acute ischemic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA supported HIT as the most likely diagnosis, probably induced by UFH exposure during the catheterization procedure. Additional clarification on the ‘indeterminate’ SRA result was requested from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which reported the assay.

A thorough evaluation of the SRA findings was undertaken. In line with the existence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum collected on POD5 elicited robust serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20%), while no serotonin release (0%) occurred at supratherapeutic UFH (100 U/mL). The lack of platelet activation with very high heparin is a typical aspect of HIT, attributed to the inability to form PF4-containing immune complexes at elevated UFH levels. Two additional observations from the SRA emerged. Firstly, in the absence of UFH, the patient’s serum alone produced marked serotonin release (96% at 0 U/mL UFH; reference range <20%), a finding typical of aHIT but not classical heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, addition of the Fc receptor–blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not entirely suppress the serotonin release when the patient’s serum was present at 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release persisted at 76% with IV.3, instead of the expected reduction to below 20% typically noted with HIT antibodies; thus, the SRA outcome was initially termed 'indeterminate.' It was subsequently determined that the underlying diagnosis was indeed aHIT, though the patient’s HIT antibodies exhibited atypical potency, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3 rather than the near-complete inhibition generally expected. As far as we are aware, such an unusual SRA profile has not been previously documented.

Treatment:

Prophylactic fondaparinux was stopped, and argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was started at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Although therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is recognized as a valid ‘off-label’ option for HIT anticoagulation, the hematologist elected to use argatroban, which has a shorter half-life, to mitigate bleeding risk due to the patient’s recent stroke and particularly profound thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous Ig was administered—100 g on each of two consecutive days, followed by an additional 100 g dose—because platelet counts did not initially rise after the first two days of IV Ig. Repeated measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were conducted to indirectly assess anticoagulation effectiveness; increases in fibrinogen and decreases in d-dimer would support adequate control of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of management, the patient showed a gradual decline in fibrinogen concentrations between POD6 and POD17, reaching a low point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and the peripheral blood smear revealed schistocytes between POD9 and POD16. These three laboratory abnormalities were indicative of a severe and progressively worsening HIT-related consumptive coagulopathy. Additionally, after receiving high-dose intravenous Ig, the patient's platelet count increased only slightly, rising from 13×10^9/L on POD7 prior to the infusion to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This platelet response was considerably lower than what is typically observed following high-dose intravenous Ig in cases of aHIT (see review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to therapeutic fondaparinux, administered as 10 mg subcutaneously once daily. The rationale for this adjustment included the convenience of fondaparinux, which requires only daily subcutaneous administration, in contrast to argatroban’s need for intravenous dosing titrated to PTT, as well as concerns that argatroban might not adequately suppress the HIT-related prothrombotic state. This concern arose from observed laboratory trends of decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer, and the presence of schistocytes while the patient was receiving argatroban. Because fondaparinux acts as an indirect factor Xa inhibitor dependent on antithrombin (AT), the patient was given supplemental AT concentrate (1000 U) prior to initiating fondaparinux. The AT level measured prior to infusion was mildly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count progressively returned to normal values (155×10^9/L at postoperative day 62, 202×10^9/L at postoperative day 87), accompanied by full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux was administered up until postoperative day 65, at which point apixaban was restarted at a dose of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation management. Serial serotonin release assay (SRA) results continued to be strongly positive over several months, with multiple subsequent profiles exhibiting features more typical of atypical heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) (see figure 2). The final blood specimen, collected on postoperative day 124 after normalization of the platelet count, indicated primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, demonstrated by only 15% serotonin release without unfractionated heparin (UFH) and 100% serotonin release in the presence of UFH at a concentration of 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Firstly, thrombocytopenia related to HIT appeared earlier than typically expected; the standard timeframe for HIT onset in cardiac surgery patients ranges from POD5 to POD10. In contrast, fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a decrease in platelet count prior to POD5, a pattern associated with recent exposure to heparin before surgery—an event observed in this case.

Secondly, this patient's platelet count reached a strikingly low nadir of 13×10⁹/L, accompanied by declining fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer, and schistocytes seen during argatroban anticoagulation. These manifestations of pronounced HIT-related hypercoagulability are indicative of aHIT.

Thirdly, our patient failed to demonstrate rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia, which is typically observed when patients with aHIT receive high-dose intravenous Ig; this suggests that the patient was experiencing an exceptionally severe variant of the condition. Notably, serological findings indicative of a particularly severe form of aHIT were apparent: only partial suppression of platelet activation was seen in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present. To the best of our knowledge, this distinctive serological pattern has not previously been documented in cases of aHIT, and recognizing it may help prevent overlooked diagnoses of aHIT and the attendant risks. In spite of this, the diagnosis of aHIT in our patient was confirmed based on at least three factors. First, analysis of sera obtained during the extended period of platelet recovery revealed SRA reactivity patterns that were more aligned with aHIT. Second, the final sample analyzed displayed a heparin-dependent SRA reaction consistent with typical HIT, indicative of decreasing aHIT antibody levels. Third, no alternative diagnosis sufficiently accounted for the patient's atypical clinical presentation. The patient ultimately experienced a full clinical recovery, without any lingering neurological deficits.
