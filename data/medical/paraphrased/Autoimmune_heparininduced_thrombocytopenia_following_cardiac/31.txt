Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin anticoagulation during cardiac surgery; following the procedure, his platelet count progressively declined, reaching 50×10⁹/L by the fifth postoperative day (POD5), leading to evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging revealed a parietal lobe stroke. HIT antibody screening by immunoassay showed a strong positive result, while the serotonin-release assay demonstrated an atypical pattern, with pronounced serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin and only partial inhibition when a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody was added. Anticoagulant therapy was transitioned to argatroban combined with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to ongoing hypercoagulability, fondaparinux was subsequently initiated, resulting in a gradual increase in platelets (155×10⁹/L on POD62); neurological deficits resolved. Laboratory testing confirmed the presence of HIT antibodies with the capacity to activate platelets independently of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Notable features of this case include severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), unusually early postoperative presentation (prior to POD5) due to preoperative exposure to heparin, and a distinctive laboratory profile of aHIT antibodies characterized by partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant anticoagulant during heart surgery employing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to its multiple benefits, such as inhibition of haemostatic activation from artificial surfaces, clearance independent of renal or hepatic function, intraoperative monitoring via activated clotting time, and affordability. Despite these advantages, UFH carries the significant risk of an immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). After cardiac operations, HIT occurs with an estimated prevalence between 0.5% and 1.0%, influenced by the duration and type of postoperative heparin used for thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin [LMWH]). Importantly, HIT is associated with an increased risk of thrombotic events, reflected by an approximate 15-fold rise in relative risk.

Traditionally, HIT has been understood as a condition marked by robust platelet activation via clustering of platelet Fc receptors, caused by immune complexes formed between IgG class heparin-dependent antibodies and the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to polyanionic heparin. Recently, however, it has been recognized that some individuals experience atypical, often severe clinical courses resulting from HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin; this variant has been designated 'autoimmune HIT' (aHIT) (see review). In the present report, we detail a case of aHIT characterized by unusually profound and persistent thrombocytopenia, which was further complicated by a thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. This case sheds light on the spectrum of aHIT, documenting a singularly severe serological profile exhibiting partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. Additionally, the case underscores the complexity in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the notably prompt onset of aHIT was attributed to the patient's preoperative UFH exposure. Ultimately, the patient achieved a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male in his seventies with a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily) was noted to have a new systolic murmur. He proceeded to diagnostic cardiac catheterization but had not discontinued his apixaban before the procedure; as a result, the interventional cardiologist chose a transradial artery approach and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulation dose (only 2000 U of unfractionated heparin). Coronary angiography revealed a 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta; based on these findings, he was scheduled for cardiac surgery one week later.

The patient underwent cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, utilizing a total of 45,000 U of unfractionated heparin for anticoagulation. The operation consisted of aortic valve replacement using a #27 bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, single-vessel coronary bypass with a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The procedure was completed without complications, excessive bleeding, or the need for transfusion, and the patient was easily weaned from bypass with excellent hemodynamic stability.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range (RR) 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), this value had decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicative of a modest early reduction often seen following cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with LMWH was initiated on POD1; the patient received 5000 U dalteparin subcutaneously, followed by increased dosing of 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, the platelet count continued to drop steadily from POD1 through POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Other than postoperative delirium, the patient was clinically stable. Dalteparin administration was withheld on POD5, and instead prophylactic fondaparinux was started (first dose of 2.5 mg, administered at 18:00 on POD5).

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the intensification of delirium and a further reduction in platelet count to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (with a repeated value of 24×10^9/L) led to brain imaging with CT. This identified an acute cerebral infarction, showing an area of hypodensity measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, a haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L) that matched values seen over the previous three days (consistent with anticipated early postoperative blood loss and no evidence of continued bleeding), and a white blood cell count of 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) with a normal differential. The reticulocyte number was within the expected range at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was found to be raised at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting the absence of significant haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 had returned to normal values.

Coagulation profiles displayed a normal prothrombin time and an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (RR 25–37 s), and increased fibrinogen at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), findings typical for the early postoperative period due to the acute phase response with significant fibrinogen elevation. On POD5, a blood specimen was forwarded to a reference lab for HIT antibody assessment.

Additional laboratory analysis demonstrated normal values for renal (urea, creatinine) and hepatic function markers (alanine transaminase, bilirubin). Both blood and urine cultures were obtained. The chest X-ray showed no significant pathology.

Assessment of the peripheral blood film from POD7 verified thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet aggregation or schistocytes (red cell fragments), and no 'toxic' changes in white blood cells. The PF4/polyanion ELISA immunoassay for screening HIT was markedly positive at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units), whereas the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) yielded an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (discussed elsewhere).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of pronounced thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock, severe illness, or infection, appeared improbable based on clinical and laboratory data (hemodynamics remained stable; blood and urine cultures were negative). Less common etiologies like post-transfusion purpura (patient received no transfusions), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (haptoglobin levels were normal, and initial blood smears showed no schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia were also considered unlikely, as the decline in platelet count occurred too soon for involvement of any recently introduced perioperative or postoperative medications (limited to intraoperative vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid, and postoperative furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

Despite the rapid decrease in platelets not matching the typical timeline expected after intraoperative UFH exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass, further review revealed that UFH had been administered during a preoperative cardiac catheterization. When combined with evidence of a thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) became the leading diagnosis, most likely precipitated by preoperative UFH. Additional clarification about the “indeterminate” serotonin release assay (SRA) result was requested from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings underwent thorough evaluation. The patient’s POD5 serum demonstrated robust serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), which aligns with platelet-activating HIT antibodies. Additionally, there was no serotonin release (0%) at a supratherapeutic UFH level (100 U/mL); this lack of platelet activation at elevated heparin concentrations is typical in HIT, as PF4-containing immune complexes cannot form under these conditions. Nonetheless, two further aspects of the SRA findings were observed. First, significant serotonin release was induced by the patient’s serum even in the absence of UFH (96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), which is characteristic of aHIT but not of standard heparin-dependent HIT. Second, using the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not entirely block the serotonin release caused by patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH; serotonin release remained high at 76% with IV.3, rather than dropping below 20%, as is typical with HIT antibodies, leading to an initial SRA designation of ‘indeterminate’. Ultimately, this was attributed to the diagnosis of aHIT, with the patient’s HIT antibodies showing atypically strong reactivity and partial inhibition by IV.3, contrary to usual expectations. This unusual SRA profile has not been previously described, to our knowledge.

Treatment:

Prophylactic fondaparinux was stopped, and the patient was started on therapeutic argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at a rate of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, with a target PTT of 55–85 seconds. While fondaparinux at therapeutic doses is considered an effective ‘off-label’ option for treating HIT, the haematologist managing the case opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, allowing greater flexibility in managing potential bleeding complications, particularly given the patient’s recent stroke and unusually severe thrombocytopenia. Additionally, the patient received high-dose intravenous Ig: 100 g on two consecutive days, and a third 100 g dose was administered due to the absence of a rise in platelet count after the first two days. To indirectly monitor anticoagulant effectiveness, sequential fibrinogen and d-dimer measurements were obtained; a pattern of increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer values would indicate adequate control of HIT-related hypercoagulability with anticoagulation.

During the initial phase of therapy, fibrinogen concentrations steadily declined from postoperative day 6 through day 17, reaching a low point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer values peaked around postoperative day 12, and peripheral blood smear analysis revealed schistocytes between postoperative days 9 and 16. Together, these three laboratory abnormalities were indicative of a severe and progressive consumptive coagulopathy related to HIT. Additionally, after receiving high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, the patient experienced only a slight elevation in platelet count, increasing from 13×10^9/L on postoperative day 7 to a temporary high of 54×10^9/L by postoperative day 14. This degree of platelet recovery was considerably less than what is commonly observed following high-dose intravenous Ig therapy in patients with aHIT (see review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulant regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. This switch was made primarily due to the convenience of fondaparinux’s dosing schedule, which contrasts with argatroban’s need for PTT-adjusted intravenous infusion, and concerns that argatroban may not have been adequately suppressing the hypercoagulability associated with HIT, as evidenced by declining fibrinogen levels, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, an AT concentrate (1000 U) was infused before initiating fondaparinux, as the patient’s antithrombin level before infusion was slightly below normal at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count progressively returned to normal values (155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87), and the patient experienced full neurological restoration. Fondaparinux therapy was continued until POD65, at which point apixaban was restarted at a dose of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial SRA tests remained strongly positive over several months, with later results displaying a pattern more typical of aHIT (figure 2); the most recent sample analyzed, taken at POD124 after platelet normalization, demonstrated findings indicative of mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, as evidenced by 15% serotonin release in the absence of UFH and 100% release when UFH was present at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

This patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were ultimately due to a diagnosis of severe autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT). Firstly, the onset of HIT-associated thrombocytopenia occurred prior to the typical timeframe seen in HIT, which is usually between postoperative day 5 (POD5) and POD10 in individuals following cardiac surgery. Only approximately 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a decline in platelet count before POD5, a pattern associated with recent preoperative exposure to heparin, as was also observed in this patient.

Secondly, the patient’s platelet count reached an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, alongside decreasing fibrinogen levels, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes during anticoagulation with argatroban. These manifestations of marked HIT-related hypercoagulability are characteristic of aHIT.

Thirdly, the patient did not demonstrate a rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia, which typically follows administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin in cases of aHIT, suggesting an exceptionally severe manifestation of the condition. The serological findings also indicated a rare, severe variant of aHIT, as only partial inhibition of platelet activation was seen in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present; this specific serological phenotype has not, to our knowledge, been previously described in connection with aHIT. Recognition of this pattern could be important for preventing misdiagnosis and the associated negative clinical outcomes. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT in the patient was eventually established based on several factors. First, subsequent serum samples collected during the extended recovery of the platelet count revealed SRA reactivity more typical for aHIT. Second, the final tested sample displayed a heparin-dependent SRA response consistent with classic HIT, indicating diminishing aHIT antibody levels. Third, no alternative diagnosis could account for the patient's atypical clinical course. Ultimately, the patient experienced a full clinical recovery without any persistent neurological deficits.
