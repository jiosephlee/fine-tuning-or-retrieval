Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An elderly male patient underwent cardiac surgery utilizing heparin anticoagulation. In the postoperative period, his platelet count progressively declined, reaching 50×10⁹/L by the fifth postoperative day (POD5), leading to assessment for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Neuroimaging revealed a parietal lobe stroke. HIT antibody screening via immunoassay returned a strongly positive result; the platelet activation assay (serotonin-release assay) demonstrated an unusual pattern—marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin with only partial suppression by a monoclonal antibody targeting the platelet Fc receptor. The patient's anticoagulation regimen was switched to argatroban combined with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Persistent hypercoagulability necessitated a transition to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count gradually improved to 155×10⁹/L by POD62; the neurological deficits also resolved. Further laboratory analysis established the presence of HIT antibodies capable of heparin-independent platelet activation, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Distinctive features of this aHIT case, beyond severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), included onset within five days postoperatively due to preoperative heparin exposure and a unique laboratory profile showing partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For nearly seventy years, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant anticoagulant administered during cardiac surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). This preference is attributed to UFH’s multiple benefits, which include prevention of haemostasis triggered by artificial surfaces, clearance mechanisms that do not rely on renal or hepatic pathways, intraoperative anticoagulation monitoring via activated clotting time, and economical cost. Nonetheless, UFH use carries a significant risk of an immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). After cardiac surgery, HIT occurs with a frequency of approximately 0.5%–1.0%, influenced by the type and duration of postoperative heparin prophylaxis (UFH compared to low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and is particularly significant due to its strong propensity to cause thrombosis, with a relative risk nearing 15.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) has traditionally been considered a condition characterized by marked platelet activation via clustering of Fc receptors on platelets, occurring when immune complexes are formed by immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies directed against platelet factor 4 (PF4) in the presence of heparin. More recently, it has become evident that certain patients develop unusual and sometimes severe clinical manifestations attributable to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin; this variant is referred to as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). Here, we present a case of aHIT marked by profound and persistent thrombocytopenia, which was further complicated by thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. This case adds new knowledge to the spectrum of aHIT by illustrating an especially severe serological presentation that demonstrated only partial response to Fc receptor inhibition. In addition, it underscores the diagnostic complexities associated with early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the patient’s atypically rapid aHIT onset was attributed to prior exposure to unfractionated heparin (UFH) before surgery. Despite these challenges, the patient ultimately experienced a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had experienced exertional dyspnoea for two years and had a history of chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a newly discovered systolic murmur. He proceeded to undergo diagnostic cardiac catheterisation, although he had not discontinued his apixaban beforehand. Consequently, the procedure was performed by the cardiologist using a transradial artery access and administering a lower than standard dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (UFH 2000 U only). Angiography revealed 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. Plans were made to proceed with cardiac surgery the following week.

The cardiac procedure was carried out with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), achieving anticoagulation through administration of 45,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). Surgical interventions comprised aortic valve replacement using a #27 bioprosthetic valve, ascending aorta replacement with a #30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary artery bypass graft to the obtuse marginal artery using a saphenous vein graft, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was completed without complications, with minimal blood loss, no requirement for transfusion, and the patient was smoothly weaned from CPB while maintaining stable haemodynamic parameters.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery was measured at 210×10^9/L (reference interval 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the platelet level had decreased to 155×10^9/L, reflecting only a modest early reduction typically seen after cardiac surgery. Standard thromboprophylaxis using LMWH began on POD1, with dalteparin 5000 units given subcutaneously, followed by increased dosing of 7500 units on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, a progressive drop in platelet count was observed from POD1 through POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). The patient's postoperative course was unremarkable aside from the presence of delirium. Dalteparin was discontinued on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was initiated, with the first dose given at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite the absence of localized neurological deficits, the patient’s postoperative delirium worsened and the platelet count further decreased to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (with a repeat value of 24×10^9/L). This prompted a CT scan of the brain, which identified an acute infarction measuring 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm within the left parietal lobe, extending to the cortical surface.

Investigations:

The complete blood count performed on POD5 revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L and a haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), which was comparable to haemoglobin measurements from the previous three days, aligning with anticipated early postoperative blood loss and showing no signs of ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count was within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicative of no significant haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 concentration had normalized.

Coagulation panels demonstrated a prothrombin time within normal limits, yielding an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), and a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds), which was also normal. Fibrinogen was found to be markedly elevated at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), a finding compatible with the acute phase response frequently observed in the early postoperative period. On postoperative day 5 (POD5), a blood specimen was obtained and sent to a reference laboratory to evaluate for HIT antibodies.

Other blood work included normal chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were drawn. A chest X-ray showed no major abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear obtained on postoperative day 7 verified thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet clumping, red cell fragmentation (schistocytes), or abnormal features in white blood cells indicative of toxicity. The initial immunoassay used for HIT screening, the PF4/polyanion ELISA, showed a markedly positive result, measuring 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units). However, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), which serves as the confirmatory functional platelet activation test, produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (addressed in detail later).

Differential diagnosis:

Frequently cited causes of pronounced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock, critical illness, and infection, appeared improbable in this case based on the clinical presentation and laboratory data, which showed stable haemodynamics and negative blood and urine cultures. Uncommon etiologies, such as post-transfusion purpura (no blood transfusions had been administered), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (with normal haptoglobin levels and initially no schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also considered unlikely. The decline in platelet count commenced too soon after surgery to be attributable to any recently initiated postoperative medications—namely, those administered only intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) as well as agents introduced in the postoperative phase (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

While the decrease in platelet count happened too early to be solely attributed to intraoperative UFH exposure during CPB, further investigation revealed administration of UFH during preoperative cardiac catheterization. In conjunction with the occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, HIT was considered the most likely diagnosis, with UFH given during preoperative catheterization as the probable inciting event. Additional information concerning the ‘indeterminate’ SRA result was sought from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which performed the test.

The SRA findings underwent thorough evaluation. Indicative of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s POD5 serum produced pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20% release), and demonstrated an absence of serotonin release (0%) when exposed to supratherapeutic UFH concentrations (100 U/mL). This pattern—a lack of platelet activation at elevated heparin levels—is typical for HIT, as high UFH concentrations prevent the formation of PF4-immune complexes. Nonetheless, two atypical aspects of the SRA data were identified. Firstly, substantial serotonin release was observed without added UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a hallmark of aHIT as opposed to classic, strictly heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, the use of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 did not fully suppress serotonin release in the setting of patient serum with 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release persisted at 76% despite IV.3, whereas suppression below 20% is typically expected for HIT antibody-mediated release. This led to the initial labeling of the SRA as ‘indeterminate’. Ultimately, it was determined that the patient had aHIT, but the patient’s HIT antibodies exhibited an atypically strong response, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3 compared to what is commonly observed. To date, such an unusual SRA reaction profile has not been documented in the literature.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosing was stopped, and the patient was initiated on therapeutic-dose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Although therapeutic-dose fondaparinux can serve as an effective ‘off-label’ anticoagulant for HIT, the hematologist overseeing the case opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which would be advantageous should bleeding arise, particularly given the recent cerebrovascular event and marked thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IV Ig) was administered—initially 100 g on two consecutive days, and then a third 100 g dose—since there was no initial improvement in platelet count after the first two days of IV Ig treatment. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were also obtained to monitor the effectiveness of anticoagulation, as an increase in fibrinogen and decrease in d-dimer would suggest successful mitigation of the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT.

During the initial therapy, fibrinogen levels progressively declined between POD6 and POD17, reaching their lowest at 2.49 g/L. D-dimer concentrations peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears identified red cell fragments (schistocytes) from POD9 to POD16. Collectively, these findings indicated severe, advancing HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. Additionally, after receiving high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, the patient's platelet count showed only a modest rise—from 13×10^9/L prior to treatment on POD7 to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This increment was notably less than the typical platelet increase expected after high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin administration in cases of aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, anticoagulation was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once per day. The rationale for changing to fondaparinux included its more convenient once-daily subcutaneous dosing compared to the need for PTT-adjusted intravenous infusion with argatroban, and concerns that argatroban might not be adequately controlling the HIT-associated hypercoagulable state, evidenced by decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer levels, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux functions as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, a 1000 U dose of antithrombin concentrate was administered before beginning fondaparinux, as the baseline antithrombin activity was slightly below normal at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count progressively returned to normal values (measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and reaching 202×10^9/L by POD87). Neurological function was fully restored. Fondaparinux treatment was continued up to POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial serotonin release assay (SRA) results continued to demonstrate strong positivity for several months, with some subsequent assays displaying a more typical SRA pattern for autoimmune HIT (see figure 2). The most recent tested blood sample, collected on POD124 after platelet normalization, indicated mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, demonstrated by 15% serotonin release in the absence of unfractionated heparin (UFH) and 100% release when UFH was present at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

Our patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics that were all ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Firstly, the thrombocytopenia associated with HIT in this case developed earlier than is typical, preceding the standard timeframe of POD5 to POD10 observed after cardiac surgery. Less than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a decrease in platelet count prior to POD5, a pattern that is correlated with recent exposure to heparin prior to surgery, which was also present in our case.

Secondly, our patient's platelet count declined to an extremely low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by reduced fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and schistocytosis while on argatroban therapy. Such manifestations of pronounced hypercoagulability in HIT suggest the presence of aHIT.

Third, unlike the typical rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia seen with high-dose intravenous Ig administration in cases of aHIT, our patient’s platelet count did not recover quickly, suggesting a particularly severe variant of the condition. In support of this, serologic testing demonstrated only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present—an atypical finding for aHIT. To our knowledge, this distinctive serologic pattern has not previously been documented in association with aHIT, and its recognition could be important to prevent missed diagnoses and associated adverse outcomes. However, aHIT was definitively diagnosed in our case for several reasons. First, serial testing on follow-up sera collected during the patient’s extended recovery from thrombocytopenia revealed SRA reactivity patterns that aligned more closely with classical aHIT. Second, the most recently obtained sample demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA result typical of conventional HIT, consistent with diminishing aHIT antibody levels. Third, alternative diagnoses could not plausibly account for the patient’s atypical presentation. Ultimately, the patient achieved a complete clinical recovery, with no persistent neurological deficits.
