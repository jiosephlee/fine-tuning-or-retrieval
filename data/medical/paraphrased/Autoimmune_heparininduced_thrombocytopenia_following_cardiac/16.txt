Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin anticoagulation during cardiac surgery; following the operation, his platelet count progressively declined, reaching 50×10⁹/L by the fifth postoperative day (POD5). This decrease led to an evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Imaging of the brain revealed a parietal infarct. The initial immunoassay for HIT antibodies was strongly positive, while the serotonin-release assay showed an unusual pattern—marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with only incomplete suppression by a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. The patient’s anticoagulation regimen was shifted to argatroban in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. As hypercoagulability persisted, treatment was changed to fondaparinux, after which platelet counts gradually rose (to 155×10⁹/L by POD62), and neurological deficits resolved. Laboratory tests confirmed the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independent of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to marked thrombocytopenia (minimum platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), this aHIT case was notable for its early occurrence after surgery (prior to POD5), attributed to heparin given before surgery, and for its distinctive antibody laboratory profile that showed partial resistance to inhibition by Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the primary intraoperative anticoagulant in cardiac surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), attributable to its multiple benefits such as effective inhibition of haemostasis linked to artificial surfaces, clearance mechanisms independent of renal and hepatic pathways, intraoperative monitoring capability via activated clotting time, and affordability. Nevertheless, a significant complication associated with UFH use is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), which is immune-mediated. HIT occurs with a notable incidence following cardiac surgery—estimated at 0.5% to 1.0%, varying by the type and length of postoperative heparin-based thromboprophylaxis (comparing UFH with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH))—and is distinguished by a markedly increased risk of thrombosis, with a relative risk approaching 15.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) has traditionally been characterized as a condition involving potent platelet activation through the clustering of platelet Fc receptors, triggered by immune complexes formed from IgG-class, heparin-dependent antibodies targeting the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) when it is complexed with polyanionic heparin. More recently, it has been recognized that a subset of patients exhibit unusual and sometimes severe clinical manifestations due to HIT antibodies that activate platelets independently of heparin; this atypical presentation has been designated as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). We present here a case of aHIT manifesting as extremely profound and persistent thrombocytopenia, further complicated by a thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. This case broadens understanding of the aHIT spectrum, as it illustrates a rare and markedly severe serological profile characterized by partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. Additionally, the report underscores the complexity of diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as this patient’s unusually rapid onset of aHIT was attributed to prior preoperative exposure to unfractionated heparin (UFH). Despite these challenges, the patient eventually achieved a good clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his 70s, with a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was noted to have a newly recognized systolic murmur. Diagnostic cardiac catheterization was carried out without prior discontinuation of his apixaban; as a result, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery approach and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulant dose (only 2000 U of UFH). Angiography demonstrated 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and dilation of the ascending aorta; elective cardiac surgery was planned for the following week.

Cardiac surgery was carried out utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and anticoagulation was maintained with 45,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). The operative procedures comprised aortic valve replacement using a #27 bioprosthetic valve, ascending aortic replacement with a #30 Gelweave vascular graft, a single saphenous vein coronary bypass to the obtuse marginal branch, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was completed smoothly, with no significant intraoperative hemorrhage or requirement for blood transfusion, and the patient was weaned from CPB easily with stable and robust haemodynamic parameters.

The platelet count measured immediately prior to surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference interval: 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the platelet count decreased to 155×10^9/L, representing only a mild initial reduction following cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative venous thromboembolism prophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was started on POD1, with 5000 units of dalteparin given subcutaneously, followed by increased doses of 7500 units on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, between POD1 and POD5, the platelet count further dropped to 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Apart from the development of postoperative delirium, the patient was otherwise stable. Dalteparin was omitted on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was initiated (first dose at 18:00 on POD5).

Despite the absence of localized neurological deficits, the worsening postoperative delirium along with an additional reduction in platelet count on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (confirmed on repeat at 24×10^9/L) led to a CT scan of the brain. Imaging identified an acute infarct characterized by a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm within the left parietal lobe, extending through to the cortical surface.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count showed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L and a haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), which was comparable to the haemoglobin measurements from the previous three days, aligning with anticipated early postoperative blood loss and no signs of continued bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count remained within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no evident haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 concentration was also within normal range.

Coagulation parameters revealed a prothrombin time within normal limits and an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range [RR] 0.9–1.1). The partial thromboplastin time (PTT) was also normal at 34 seconds (RR 25–37 seconds). However, fibrinogen levels were found to be elevated at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), a finding in line with the expected postoperative acute-phase response, as fibrinogen concentrations typically rise significantly in the immediate postoperative period. On postoperative day 5 (POD5), a blood sample was collected and forwarded to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody assessment.

Other blood work included normal chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were drawn. A chest X-ray showed no major abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear from postoperative day 7 verified thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet clumping, red cell fragments (schistocytes), or ‘toxic’ changes in white blood cells. Testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) using the PF4/polyanion ELISA produced a markedly positive result, with an optical density (OD) of 2.2 (reference range <0.4 OD units). However, the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) returned an ‘indeterminate’ result, which is addressed later in the report.

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of pronounced thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock, critical illness, and infection, were considered improbable based on the patient's stable haemodynamics and negative results from blood and urine cultures. Uncommon etiologies such as post-transfusion purpura (not applicable since the patient did not receive transfusions), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (unsupported by normal haptoglobin and no schistocytes on initial evaluation), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia were also deemed unlikely. The temporal pattern of thrombocytopenia onset was inconsistent with any postoperative medications, including intraoperative agents (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid) as well as medications started after surgery (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

Although the decline in platelet count developed earlier than would be anticipated from the UFH administered during intraoperative CPB, additional investigation revealed that UFH had been given during a preoperative cardiac catheterization. Coupled with the occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, HIT was considered the most likely diagnosis, presumably initiated by the UFH exposure at the time of preoperative cardiac catheterization. Additional information about the 'indeterminate' SRA result was subsequently requested from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which had provided the test report.

The SRA findings underwent detailed evaluation. In alignment with platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum obtained on POD5 produced marked serotonin release at 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range [RR]<20%), along with absent serotonin release (0%) at a supratherapeutic UFH dose (100 U/mL). This pattern—where high heparin concentrations fail to activate platelets—is typical of HIT, reflecting that PF4-containing immune complexes cannot assemble at these elevated UFH levels. Nonetheless, two further aspects of the SRA results were noteworthy. First, significant serotonin release occurred with the patient’s serum even in the absence of UFH (96% serotonin release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a pattern characteristic of aHIT rather than traditional heparin-dependent HIT. Second, introducing the monoclonal Fc receptor–blocking antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress serotonin release at 0.1 U/mL UFH; serotonin release remained elevated at 76%, whereas typical HIT antibodies would produce less than 20% serotonin release with IV.3 present. This observation initially led to the SRA being categorized as ‘indeterminate.’ The underlying explanation was that the patient had aHIT, with particularly reactive HIT antibodies, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3 rather than the usual robust suppression. To our knowledge, such an unusual SRA profile has not been documented previously.

Treatment:

Prophylactic-dose fondaparinux was stopped, and therapeutic-dose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was initiated at 0.75 mcg/kg/min, targeting a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While fondaparinux at therapeutic doses is recognized as an effective ‘off-label’ option for HIT, the hematologist in charge selected argatroban due to its shorter half-life, considering the risk of bleeding associated with the patient’s recent stroke and marked thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered, starting with 100 g for two consecutive days, and a third dose of 100 g was added when platelet counts did not improve after the initial 2 days of treatment. Serial monitoring of fibrinogen and d-dimer levels was conducted to assess the effectiveness of anticoagulation, as increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer values would indicate successful management of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of treatment, fibrinogen concentrations declined steadily from postoperative day 6 through postoperative day 17, reaching a low point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around postoperative day 12, and schistocytes were observed on peripheral blood smears between postoperative days 9 and 16. These three laboratory abnormalities indicated a severe and advancing consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. In addition, after the patient received high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, the platelet count rose only modestly—from a baseline of 13×10^9/L on postoperative day 7 to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on postoperative day 14—which is a significantly smaller platelet increment than typically observed following high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin therapy in patients with aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, anticoagulation was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. This decision took into account the convenience of fondaparinux, which offers once-daily subcutaneous dosing in contrast to the continuous, PTT-adjusted intravenous infusion necessary for argatroban. Additional concerns prompting this switch included suboptimal management of HIT-associated hypercoagulability with argatroban, as suggested by declining fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the emergence of schistocytes during its administration. As fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin (AT)-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, an AT concentrate (1000 U) was infused prior to initiation, since the AT level before infusion was slightly low at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet levels progressively returned to normal, measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87; neurologically, the patient achieved full recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was maintained until POD65, after which apixaban was reintroduced at an oral dose of 5 mg twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial SRA assessments remained strongly positive over several months, with multiple subsequent tests displaying an SRA pattern more typical of aHIT (see figure 2); the most recent blood specimen, collected at POD124 after normalization of platelet counts, demonstrated findings consistent with mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies—showing 15% serotonin release without UFH and 100% release when UFH at 0.3 U/mL was present.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, each ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, his HIT-associated thrombocytopenia developed earlier than the typical timeframe for HIT, which usually occurs between postoperative day 5 (POD5) and POD10 following cardiac surgery. Less than 10% of individuals who undergo cardiac surgery and develop HIT experience a decrease in platelet count before POD5, a pattern associated with recent exposure to heparin prior to surgery, which was noted in this case.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count declined to an especially low minimum of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreased fibrinogen levels, increased d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes, all of which were observed during anticoagulation with argatroban. This combination of findings—reflecting pronounced hypercoagulability linked to severe HIT—is characteristic of aHIT.

Thirdly, our patient did not demonstrate the rapid improvement in platelet count that is commonly observed in aHIT cases treated with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin; this suggests the patient’s case represented an unusually severe variant of this disorder. In fact, laboratory findings indicating an atypically severe subtype of aHIT were highlighted by only partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present; as far as we are aware, this distinctive serological pattern has not been previously described in aHIT, and recognition of this feature could help prevent overlooked aHIT diagnoses, thereby avoiding potential negative clinical outcomes. Nonetheless, the diagnosis of aHIT in our patient was conclusively established based on at least three factors. Firstly, repeated analysis of serum samples obtained over the extended period required for the platelet count to recover did reveal some SRA activity profiles more typical of aHIT. Secondly, the most recent sample analyzed displayed a heparin-dependent SRA reaction profile indicative of classic HIT, suggesting diminishing levels of aHIT antibodies. Lastly, no other reasonable explanation for the patient’s atypical clinical progression could be identified. The patient fortunately experienced an excellent recovery, with neurological function returning to baseline and no lasting deficits.
