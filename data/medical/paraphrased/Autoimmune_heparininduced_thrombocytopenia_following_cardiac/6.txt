Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received cardiac surgery with heparin used for anticoagulation. In the days following the operation, his platelet count progressively decreased, reaching 50×10⁹/L by the fifth postoperative day (POD5), leading to evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Imaging of the brain revealed a parietal stroke. An immunoassay designed to detect HIT antibodies showed a markedly positive result, while the serotonin-release assay, used for platelet activation testing, revealed an unusual pattern: significant serotonin release occurred at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, and platelet activation was only partially inhibited by a monoclonal antibody targeting the platelet Fc receptor. The patient's anticoagulation regimen was switched to argatroban in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing evidence of hypercoagulability led clinicians to transition to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count improved (155×10⁹/L by POD62), and the patient's neurological symptoms resolved. Subsequent laboratory analysis verified the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets even in the absence of heparin, signifying autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (with a lowest platelet count of 13×10⁹/L), this aHIT case exhibited several unusual features, including an onset shortly after surgery (prior to POD5) due to prior heparin exposure and a distinctive laboratory antibody profile showing partial resistance to inhibition by Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been the predominant intraoperative anticoagulant in heart surgeries employing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Its widespread use is attributable to several benefits: effective inhibition of haemostatic activation on artificial surfaces, elimination pathways that do not involve the liver or kidneys, the feasibility of intraoperative anticoagulation monitoring via the activated clotting time, and economical cost. Despite these strengths, UFH can trigger a significant immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). HIT occurs in approximately 0.5\%–1.0\% of post-cardiac surgery patients, with incidence rates influenced by both the duration and the type of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (comparison of UFH to low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). This condition is particularly important due to its marked association with thrombosis, as evidenced by an estimated relative risk of thrombosis around 15.

Traditionally, HIT is characterized by pronounced platelet activation via clustering of platelet Fc receptors, initiated when immune complexes form between heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies and the positively charged protein, platelet factor 4 (PF4), complexed with the polyanionic drug heparin. More recently, it has been recognized that a subset of patients experiences an atypical, often more severe, clinical progression, mediated by HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin; this variant is termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). The present report details a case of aHIT notable for strikingly severe and persistent thrombocytopenia and complicated by thrombotic stroke in the context of cardiac surgery. This case offers new perspectives on aHIT by illustrating an exceptionally severe serological profile that demonstrates partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. Moreover, it underscores the diagnostic complexities inherent to identifying early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as this patient’s unusually rapid onset of aHIT was attributable to prior UFH exposure before surgery. Ultimately, the patient's course concluded with a positive recovery.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had been experiencing exertional dyspnoea for two years and had a longstanding diagnosis of atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was discovered to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. Prior to his diagnostic heart catheterisation, he did not discontinue apixaban; as a result, the procedure was performed by the cardiologist via a transradial artery approach, utilizing a reduced dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (limited to 2000 U of UFH). Angiography revealed a 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and dilation of the ascending aorta. Based on these findings, he was listed for cardiac surgery scheduled for one week later.

Cardiac surgery was carried out with the aid of CPB, and anticoagulation was provided by administering 45 000 U of UFH. The operation involved aortic valve replacement with a #27 bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta using a #30 Gelweave graft, single-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting with a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The surgical procedure was uncomplicated, with no significant blood loss or transfusion requirements, and the patient was weaned from CPB smoothly, displaying excellent haemodynamic stability.

The preoperative platelet count was measured at 210×10^9/L (reference range (RR) 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), this value decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicating only a mild reduction in platelet count, which is not uncommon after cardiac surgery. Standard thromboprophylaxis using LMWH began on POD1, consisting of a 5000 U subcutaneous injection of dalteparin, with the dosage increased to 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, a progressive drop in platelet count was observed from POD1 through POD5, reaching a nadir of 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Clinically, the patient remained stable except for experiencing postoperative delirium. On POD5, dalteparin was withheld, and instead, a prophylactic dose of fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was initiated, administered at 18:00.

Despite the absence of localized neurological deficits, the combination of aggravated postoperative delirium and a further drop in platelet count on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (reconfirmed at 24×10^9/L) warranted neuroimaging with a CT scan of the brain. Imaging detected an acute cerebral infarction, evidenced by a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending to involve the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L, with a haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L). This haemoglobin measurement was in line with those from the preceding three days, which aligns with typical early postoperative blood loss and indicates no evidence of persistent bleeding. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count was within expected limits at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating that there was no significant haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 concentration had normalized.

Coagulation assessment demonstrated normal prothrombin time and an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), together with a normal partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 s (RR 25–37 s). Fibrinogen levels were elevated to 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), a typical finding in the immediate postoperative period, reflecting the acute phase response in which fibrinogen levels often rise substantially. A sample of blood collected on POD5 was submitted to a reference laboratory for HIT antibody analysis.

Additional laboratory results included normal values for chemistry parameters such as urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest X-ray findings were unremarkable.

Evaluation of the peripheral blood smear on POD7 confirmed thrombocytopenia with the absence of platelet clumping, and no evidence of red cell fragmentation (schistocytes) or toxic changes in white blood cells. The immunoassay for HIT (PF4/polyanion ELISA) yielded a markedly positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units). However, the confirmatory platelet activation assay—the serotonin-release assay (SRA)—produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome, as discussed later.

Differential diagnosis:

Conventional causes for marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock, critical illness, and infection, were considered improbable due to both unremarkable haemodynamic status and negative results from blood and urine cultures. In addition, less common etiologies such as post-transfusion purpura (in the absence of blood product administration), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (evidenced by normal haptoglobin and initially no schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia were deemed unlikely. The latter possibility was discounted on the basis that the decline in platelet count manifested too rapidly to be attributable to new postoperative pharmacotherapies, whether they were administered intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid) or during the postoperative period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

While the temporal relationship between the drop in platelet count and intraoperative UFH during CPB did not suggest causation, further review revealed administration of intraprocedural UFH during the preoperative cardiac catheterisation. In light of this, along with the presence of a thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) emerged as the leading diagnosis, most plausibly triggered by UFH used during the preoperative heart catheterisation. Additional information concerning the ‘indeterminate’ SRA was solicited from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which had performed the relevant assay.

The SRA findings underwent a thorough assessment. In line with the expected behavior of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum collected on POD5 elicited marked serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), while showing no serotonin release (0%) at an excessive UFH level (100 U/mL); this pattern—absence of platelet activation at high heparin doses—is characteristic of HIT, attributable to the inability of PF4-based immune complexes to form under such conditions. Nonetheless, two additional aspects of the SRA needed consideration. Firstly, the patient’s serum induced robust serotonin release even in the complete absence of UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a feature indicative of aHIT rather than traditional heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, the introduction of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 did not fully suppress serotonin release triggered by the patient’s serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release persisted at 76% in the presence of IV.3, whereas, with typical HIT antibodies, suppression to below 20% would be expected; this led to the SRA initially being classified as ‘indeterminate.’ The most plausible interpretation was that the patient indeed had aHIT, with HIT antibodies displaying atypically strong activity, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3 compared to standard HIT. To our knowledge, such an unusual SRA profile has not been previously documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and the patient was switched to therapeutic-dose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, initiated at 0.75 mcg/kg/min and titrated to achieve a PTT within 55–85 s. While fondaparinux administered at therapeutic levels is recognized as an effective ‘off-label’ anticoagulant option for HIT, the hematologist opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which provides a safety margin should bleeding arise—an important consideration in light of the patient's recent stroke and marked thrombocytopenia. Furthermore, high-dose intravenous Ig was administered, starting with 100 g daily for two days, followed by a third 100 g dose after the anticipated platelet count increase did not occur with the first two administrations. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were also obtained to indirectly monitor anticoagulation effectiveness, as evidence by increasing fibrinogen levels and decreasing d-dimer levels would indicate that the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT was being managed.

The patient’s early treatment phase was marked by a gradual reduction in fibrinogen concentrations between POD6 and POD17, reaching a lowest point of 2.49 g/L. Additionally, d-dimer values peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood films identified schistocytes from POD9 to POD16. Together, these findings were indicative of a severe, advancing consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. Furthermore, despite receiving high-dose intravenous Ig, the increase in platelet count was minimal, rising from a pre-infusion level of 13×109/L on POD7 to a maximum of 54×109/L on POD14. This increment in platelet count was substantially less than what is typically observed following high-dose intravenous Ig administration in cases of aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14 (POD14), the patient’s anticoagulant therapy was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. This decision was based on several factors: fondaparinux offers simpler administration via once-daily subcutaneous injections, compared to the need for continuous intravenous infusion with argatroban, which also requires frequent partial thromboplastin time (PTT) adjustments. In addition, there were concerns that argatroban might not have been adequately mitigating the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, as suggested by ongoing decreases in fibrinogen, elevations in d-dimer, and the presence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux functions as an indirect antithrombin (AT)-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to the first dose; the antithrombin level before infusion was slightly below normal at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet count progressively returned to the normal range (155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87), and full neurological function was restored. Fondaparinux therapy continued up to POD65, after which apixaban was reinitiated at a dosage of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation management. Serial SRA assessments remained markedly positive over several months, with subsequent profiles displaying a SRA response pattern more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The final blood specimen, collected at POD124 after normalization of the platelet count, demonstrated results indicating primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release was 15% without UFH but reached 100% when UFH was present at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient displayed several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, all of which could be explained by the diagnosis of severe aHIT. Notably, the thrombocytopenia associated with HIT manifested earlier than the typical period for patients post-cardiac surgery, which usually occurs between POD5 and POD10. Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a HIT-induced reduction in platelet count prior to POD5; this early presentation has been correlated with recent exposure to heparin before surgery, as observed in this case.

Additionally, the patient experienced an exceptionally profound decrease in platelet count, reaching a nadir of 13×10^9/L, concurrent with declining fibrinogen levels, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and the appearance of schistocytes while undergoing anticoagulation with argatroban. These manifestations of marked HIT-related hypercoagulability are suggestive of aHIT.

Third, unlike the typical rapid improvement in platelet counts observed in patients with aHIT treated with high-dose intravenous Ig, our patient’s thrombocytopenia did not resolve quickly. This suggests an exceptionally severe manifestation of aHIT. Supporting this, serological analysis revealed only partial inhibition of platelet activation by monoclonal antibody IV.3 in vitro—a serological pattern, to our knowledge, not previously described in relation to aHIT. Recognition of this distinct serological response is important, as it could prevent underdiagnosis of aHIT and mitigate the risk of related clinical complications. Nonetheless, aHIT was definitively diagnosed in our case based on three main factors. Firstly, follow-up sera obtained during the extended recovery of platelet counts exhibited some SRA activity patterns typical for aHIT. Secondly, the most recently analyzed specimen demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA activation pattern that is consistent with conventional HIT, indicative of diminishing aHIT antibodies. Thirdly, no alternative diagnosis accounted for the patient’s atypical clinical progression. Encouragingly, the patient achieved a full clinical recovery and did not experience lasting neurological deficits.
