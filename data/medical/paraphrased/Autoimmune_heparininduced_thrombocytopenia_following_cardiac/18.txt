Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received cardiac surgery utilizing heparin for anticoagulation. After the operation, his platelet count progressively decreased, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by the fifth postoperative day (POD5). This decline led to assessment for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging identified a stroke in the parietal region. A HIT antibody screening immunoassay returned a strong-positive result, while the platelet activation (serotonin-release) assay demonstrated an unusual pattern—substantial serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with only partial suppression by a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Anticoagulant therapy was switched to argatroban, supplemented with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing hypercoagulability necessitated a change to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count gradually improved, reaching 155×10⁹/L by POD62. The patient's neurological symptoms subsequently resolved. Further testing confirmed HIT antibodies capable of heparin-independent platelet activation, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to marked thrombocytopenia (nadir platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), noteworthy features in this aHIT case included onset shortly after surgery (before POD5), linked to preoperative heparin exposure, as well as a distinct antibody laboratory profile exhibiting partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant anticoagulant administered during cardiac surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), attributable to several benefits: suppression of haemostatic activation by artificial surfaces, elimination independent of renal or hepatic pathways, feasibility of intraoperative monitoring via activated clotting time, and economical pricing. Despite these advantages, UFH carries a significant risk of inducing heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-mediated complication. HIT occurs with notable frequency following cardiac surgery, affecting approximately 0.5%–1.0% of cases, contingent upon the type and duration of heparin-based postoperative thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). The condition is particularly significant due to its pronounced link with thrombotic events, conferring an estimated 15-fold increased risk of thrombosis.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) has traditionally been considered a condition marked by pronounced platelet activation via clustering of platelet Fc receptors. This process is mediated by immune complexes that include heparin-dependent IgG antibodies directed against platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to heparin. More recently, there has been growing recognition that certain patients exhibit atypical and often severe forms of HIT, attributed to antibodies that can activate platelets independently of heparin. This variant, designated ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT), has been described in the literature. We present a case of aHIT characterized by exceptionally profound and persistent thrombocytopenia and complicated by thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. This case reveals new aspects of aHIT by demonstrating an unusually severe serological profile that was only partially responsive to Fc receptor inhibition. It also illustrates the diagnostic complexity posed by early postoperative thrombocytopenia; in this instance, the patient’s early presentation of aHIT was attributable to preoperative exposure to unfractionated heparin (UFH). The patient ultimately experienced a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, with a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was noted to have an undocumented systolic murmur. He underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization, but had not discontinued his apixaban prior to the procedure. Consequently, the interventional cardiologist selected a transradial arterial access and utilized a reduced intraprocedural dose of anticoagulant (administering only 2000 U of unfractionated heparin). Angiography revealed 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and dilation of the ascending aorta. Plans were made for cardiac surgery to be performed one week later.

Cardiac surgery was conducted utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and anticoagulation was established with 45,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). The operative procedures consisted of aortic valve replacement using a #27 bioprosthesis, ascending aortic replacement with a #30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary artery bypass graft employing a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation progressed without complications, with no significant hemorrhage or requirement for blood transfusion, and the patient was weaned from CPB easily, maintaining stable haemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately before surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), this dropped to 155×10^9/L, reflecting a mild initial postoperative decrease often seen after cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboembolic prophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, consisting of 5000 units of dalteparin given subcutaneously, followed by an increased dosage of 7500 units on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, the platelet count continued to decrease each day, reaching 50×10^9/L by POD5 (see figure 1). The patient's postoperative course was unremarkable aside from delirium. On POD5, dalteparin was discontinued; in its place, prophylactic fondaparinux at a dose of 2.5 mg was started, with the first administration at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the combination of persistent delirium and a further reduction in platelet count to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (confirmed by repeat measurement of 24×10^9/L) led to a CT scan of the brain. Imaging demonstrated an acute cerebral infarction, with a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending to involve the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count showed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, accompanied by a haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), which matched the haemoglobin measurements from the prior three days and reflected anticipated initial postsurgical blood loss without evidence of continued bleeding. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a differential that was within normal parameters. Reticulocyte count was within the expected range at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was increased at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no clinical haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 level normalized.

Coagulation testing demonstrated a normal prothrombin time with an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (reference range [RR] 0.9–1.1), as well as a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) within normal limits at 34 seconds (RR 25–37 seconds). Fibrinogen levels were found to be elevated at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), a finding in line with the expected postoperative response, given that fibrinogen, as an acute phase reactant, commonly increases markedly shortly after surgery. On postoperative day 5, a blood specimen was sent to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody analysis.

Other blood work included normal chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were drawn. A chest X-ray showed no major abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on postoperative day 7 corroborated thrombocytopenia, showing no platelet aggregates and no evidence of red cell fragmentation such as schistocytes, nor any signs of 'toxic' changes in the white blood cells. The PF4/polyanion ELISA, a screening immunoassay for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), returned a markedly elevated result of 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units). However, the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA), which assesses platelet activation, produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (details provided later).

Differential diagnosis:

Common causes of pronounced thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock, critical illness, and infection, appeared improbable based on the patient’s stable haemodynamics and negative blood and urine cultures. Less frequent causes such as post-transfusion purpura were also excluded due to the absence of transfusions. Postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura was considered unlikely, given normal haptoglobin levels and the initial lack of schistocytes. Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was deemed improbable as the platelet decline occurred too soon after exposure to any new postoperative medications, including those administered only intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) as well as those introduced postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

While the decline in platelet count happened too early to be attributed to the UFH administered during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), further investigation revealed that UFH had been given intraprocedurally during a preoperative cardiac catheterization. Considering the thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA result, HIT was considered the most likely diagnosis, probably precipitated by UFH exposure during the preoperative catheterization. Additional clarification on the ‘indeterminate’ SRA result was sought from the reporting institution, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings underwent thorough examination. Supporting the existence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum obtained on postoperative day 5 induced marked serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL unfractionated heparin (UFH), with release values of 97% and 99%, respectively (reference range <20%). Conversely, there was no serotonin release (0%) at a markedly elevated UFH concentration (100 U/mL); this absence of platelet activation under high heparin levels is characteristic of HIT, due to the failure of PF4-antibody complexes to form in such an excess of heparin. Notably, two additional observations were made in the SRA. Firstly, the patient's serum provoked significant serotonin release without any added UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; reference range <20%), a finding typical of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) rather than the classic, heparin-dependent form. Secondly, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 was applied, it did not fully inhibit serotonin release in the presence of patient serum and 0.1 U/mL UFH—serotonin release remained high at 76%. This falls short of the expected inhibition to below 20% serotonin release commonly seen with typical HIT antibodies, thus resulting in an initially “indeterminate” SRA designation. The interpretation was ultimately that the patient did, in fact, have aHIT, but their antibodies displayed atypically strong reactivity, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3. To our knowledge, this unusual SRA reactivity pattern has not been reported before.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosing was stopped, and the patient was started on therapeutic-dose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at 0.75 mcg/kg/min aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is considered an effective off-label therapy for HIT, the haematologist managing the case opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, to better manage bleeding risk in the context of a recent stroke and marked thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered—100 g daily for two days, with a third 100 g infusion given subsequently after platelet counts failed to respond to the initial two doses. Serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were obtained to indirectly assess the effectiveness of anticoagulation; increases in fibrinogen and decreases in d-dimer would be indicative of adequate anticoagulant suppression of the HIT-related hypercoagulable state.

The patient’s initial management was marked by a gradual reduction in fibrinogen levels between POD6 and POD17, reaching a lowest point of 2.49 g/L, along with a maximum in d-dimer concentrations observed around POD12, and the emergence of schistocytes on peripheral blood smear from POD9 through POD16. Collectively, these laboratory changes indicated a severe, worsening consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. Additionally, after receiving high-dose intravenous Ig, the patient’s platelet count rose only modestly, increasing from 13×10^9/L on POD7 (prior to Ig administration) to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This platelet count response was notably lower than the increase typically documented in aHIT cases treated with high-dose intravenous Ig.

On postoperative day 14, anticoagulation therapy was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once per day. The rationale for this change included the greater convenience of fondaparinux's once-daily subcutaneous dosing compared to argatroban, which necessitates continuous intravenous infusion and monitoring using PTT, as well as apprehensions that argatroban may have inadequately controlled HIT-related hypercoagulability. This was suggested by declining fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer levels, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, an infusion of 1,000 units of AT concentrate was administered before initiating fondaparinux, as the patient's antithrombin level prior to infusion was mildly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count progressively normalized, reaching 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87; neurologically, the patient achieved full recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was continued through POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at 5 mg orally twice daily for extended anticoagulation. Serial SRA assays consistently returned strongly positive results over several months, with some later SRA readings demonstrating a profile more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The final tested blood sample, collected on POD124 after platelet levels had recovered, indicated primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, evidenced by only 15% serotonin release in the absence of UFH compared to 100% release when UFH at 0.3 U/mL was present.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited multiple unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, all of which were ultimately linked to a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Firstly, the thrombocytopenia associated with HIT in this case commenced prior to the typical time frame observed in HIT—commonly between postoperative day (POD) 5 and POD10 in patients post-cardiac surgery. Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who go on to develop HIT experience a HIT-related platelet decline before POD5, a pattern associated with recent heparin exposure before surgery, which was also identified in this case.

Secondly, the patient's platelet count dropped to a notably low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and evidence of schistocytes on the blood film while receiving argatroban. Such findings of profound HIT-associated hypercoagulability are suggestive of aHIT.

Third, unlike the typical rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia observed with high-dose intravenous Ig administration in aHIT cases, our patient’s platelet count did not recover quickly; this suggests a particularly severe variant of the condition. Specifically, serological testing indicated only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro by monoclonal antibody IV.3—a novel serological finding not previously documented in aHIT. Recognizing this unique profile could prevent failure to diagnose aHIT, thereby reducing potential clinical risks. Despite this atypical presentation, aHIT was definitively diagnosed in our patient based on multiple factors. First, follow-up serum samples collected during the slow normalization of the platelet count demonstrated SRA reactivity patterns more typically associated with aHIT. Second, the final serum tested produced a heparin-dependent SRA profile characteristic of classic HIT, consistent with a decrease in aHIT antibodies. Third, alternative diagnoses could not account for the patient’s unusual clinical progression. Ultimately, the patient experienced a full clinical recovery without lasting neurological impairment.
