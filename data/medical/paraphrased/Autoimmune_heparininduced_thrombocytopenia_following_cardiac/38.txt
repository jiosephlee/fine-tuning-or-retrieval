Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient underwent cardiac surgery using heparin for anticoagulation; in the days following the procedure, his platelet count progressively declined, falling to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Imaging of the brain identified a parietal stroke. HIT antibody testing by immunoassay showed a markedly positive result, while a functional platelet assay (serotonin-release assay) demonstrated an unusual response: robust serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, and only incomplete suppression by a monoclonal antibody that blocks the platelet Fc receptor. The patient’s anticoagulation was altered to argatroban in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Continued evidence of hypercoagulability led to the initiation of fondaparinux, resulting in a slow normalization of the platelet count (155×10⁹/L, POD62) and resolution of neurological symptoms. Further laboratory work confirmed the presence of HIT antibodies exhibiting heparin-independent platelet-activating activity, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Key distinguishing features of this aHIT case included not only severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count dropping to 13×10⁹/L), but also onset before POD5 (likely due to preoperative heparin exposure) and a laboratory HIT antibody profile demonstrating partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For nearly seventy years, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been the predominant anticoagulant used intraoperatively during heart surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to several advantages such as suppression of haemostatic activity associated with artificial surfaces, clearance independent of renal or hepatic function, real-time anticoagulation monitoring via activated clotting time, and its affordability. Nonetheless, UFH carries a significant risk for an immune-driven complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). Following cardiac surgery, HIT occurs with an incidence ranging from 0.5% to 1.0%, influenced by the type and duration of postoperative heparin for thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and is notable for its marked association with thrombotic events, conferring a relative risk for thrombosis of approximately 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been characterized as a condition marked by pronounced platelet activation resulting from clustering of platelet Fc receptors, which is triggered when heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies form immune complexes with the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) attached to polyanionic heparin. Recently, increased attention has been given to cases in which patients exhibit atypical, often severe, clinical manifestations due to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets without heparin; this variant is termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). The present report describes a patient with aHIT who experienced unusually severe and persistent thrombocytopenia along with thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. This case offers new perspectives on the spectrum of aHIT, documenting an exceptionally severe serological presentation that showed partial resistance to inhibition of Fc receptors. Additionally, it underscores the complexities involved in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the patient’s atypically early onset of aHIT was attributed to prior exposure to UFH before surgery. The outcome for the patient was ultimately favorable.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had a 2-year history of exertional dyspnea and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. Diagnostic cardiac catheterization was planned; however, he did not discontinue apixaban prior to the procedure. Therefore, the interventional cardiologist elected to use a transradial arterial access and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulation dose (2000 U of UFH). Angiography revealed a 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and enlargement of the ascending aorta. The patient was scheduled for cardiac surgery in one week.

Cardiac surgery was performed under cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), utilizing 45,000 U of UFH for anticoagulation. The operation entailed replacement of the aortic valve with a #27 bioprosthesis, substitution of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal branch for single-vessel coronary bypass, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The procedure was carried out without any significant bleeding or need for blood transfusion, and the patient was separated from CPB without difficulty and had stable hemodynamic status throughout.

The platelet count measured immediately before surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400), and on the first postoperative day (POD1), it had decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicating a modest drop typically seen after cardiac operations. Standard prophylactic anticoagulation with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) began on POD1, using dalteparin 5000 U delivered subcutaneously, which was increased to 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, between POD1 and POD5, there was a continued decrease in platelet count, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient's clinical course was otherwise unremarkable. Dalteparin was omitted on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started (first dose given at 18:00 on POD5).

Although there were no localizing neurological signs, the patient experienced worsening delirium postoperatively alongside a further reduction in platelet count to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (repeat value 24×10^9/L), leading to a CT scan of the brain. Imaging demonstrated an acute cerebral infarction with a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe extending into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count revealed a platelet concentration of 50×10^9/L, a haemoglobin of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), which was consistent with prior haemoglobin levels from the preceding three days, reflecting anticipated early surgical blood loss without evidence of continued hemorrhage. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) and the differential was within normal limits. Reticulocyte count was not remarkable at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was noted to be elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no significant haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 concentrations had normalized.

Coagulation parameters included normal prothrombin time and an international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), alongside a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 s (RR 25–37 s), both within normal ranges. Fibrinogen was elevated at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), consistent with the early postoperative period, during which this acute phase reactant often increases considerably. On POD5, a blood specimen was forwarded to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody testing.

Additional laboratory assessments demonstrated normal serum chemistry, including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Blood and urine cultures were obtained. A chest X-ray revealed no significant findings.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on POD7 confirmed thrombocytopenia with no platelet clumping, and showed an absence of schistocytes and pathologic white blood cell alterations. HIT screening immunoassay (PF4/polyanion ELISA) was strongly positive, measuring 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units), whereas the serotonin-release assay (SRA), used as a confirmatory test for platelet activation, produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (described later).

Differential diagnosis:

Common causes of pronounced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock or severe illness and infection, appeared implausible considering the patient's stable haemodynamics and the negative blood and urine cultures. Rare etiologies such as post-transfusion purpura (with no history of blood transfusion), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (supported by normal haptoglobin and an initial lack of schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia were also unlikely, since the decline in platelet count began too soon after introduction of any new postoperative medications. This included drugs administered on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid) and those started postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

Even though the timing of the platelet decrease was too rapid to attribute to UFH used during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), further evaluation revealed UFH had been administered intraprocedurally during a preoperative cardiac catheterisation. In light of the thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was deemed the most likely diagnosis, presumably triggered by UFH exposure during the preoperative catheterisation. The laboratory—the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory—was asked for more information regarding the ‘indeterminate’ SRA results.

A thorough analysis of the SRA findings was undertaken. The patient's postoperative day 5 serum provoked robust serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20%), which aligns with the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies. Moreover, no serotonin release (0%) occurred with an elevated UFH level (100 U/mL); this lack of platelet activation at supratherapeutic heparin levels is typical of HIT, indicating that PF4-immune complexes cannot form in such conditions. The SRA results also revealed two noteworthy features. Firstly, strong serotonin release occurred when no UFH was present (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a hallmark of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) but not classic, heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, introduction of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress the serotonin release in the presence of patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: release remained high at 76% with IV.3, rather than dropping below 20%, as generally expected with HIT antibodies. This led to the SRA result being labeled ‘indeterminate’ initially. Ultimately, it was determined that the patient indeed had aHIT; however, the patient’s HIT antibodies demonstrated atypically high reactivity, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3. To our knowledge, this unusual SRA reaction pattern has not previously been described in the literature.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dose was stopped, and the patient was started on argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Although fondaparinux administered at therapeutic levels serves as an effective 'off-label' option for anticoagulation in HIT, the consulting hematologist selected argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which offers greater flexibility in the event of bleeding, especially considering the patient’s recent stroke and marked thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) was administered: 100 g for two consecutive days, followed by a third 100 g dose, since platelet counts did not initially improve after the first 2 days of IVIg. Serial assessments of fibrinogen and d-dimer were performed to indirectly monitor anticoagulation effectiveness; specifically, an increase in fibrinogen and a decrease in d-dimer would indicate successful suppression of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of treatment, fibrinogen levels declined steadily between POD6 and POD17, reaching their lowest point at 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood film demonstrated the presence of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16; collectively, these three parameters indicated the development of a severe and progressive HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. In addition, the patient experienced only a slight elevation in platelet count after receiving high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, rising from 13×10^9/L prior to IV Ig administration (POD7) to a maximum interim value of 54×10^9/L on POD14, which represents a considerably smaller platelet count increase compared to what is typically observed in patients treated with high-dose intravenous Ig for aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the anticoagulant regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. Factors influencing this modification included the more convenient dosing schedule of fondaparinux, which requires only once daily subcutaneous injection, compared to the intravenous infusion of argatroban that necessitates adjustment based on partial thromboplastin time (PTT). Another consideration was concern about argatroban’s potential inadequacy in managing the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, evidenced by declining fibrinogen, rising d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes noted during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin (AT)-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, a dose of AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered before initiating fondaparinux; the AT level before infusion was mildly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count gradually returned to within normal limits (measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87), accompanied by full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was continued through POD65, after which apixaban was reintroduced at a dosage of 5 mg orally twice daily for long-term anticoagulation. Serial serotonin release assay (SRA) results stayed persistently and strongly positive for several months, and subsequent SRA assessments demonstrated patterns more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The final blood sample, collected at POD124 post-platelet recovery, indicated primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, evidenced by 15% serotonin release in the absence of UFH and 100% serotonin release when UFH was added at a concentration of 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient demonstrated several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. First, he developed HIT-associated thrombocytopenia prior to the typical post-operative timeframe, which spans POD5 to POD10 for individuals undergoing cardiac surgery. Fewer than 10% of post-cardiac surgery patients with HIT experience a platelet decline before POD5, a pattern generally associated with recent heparin exposure prior to surgery, as was observed in this case.

Second, his platelet count reached a particularly low nadir of 13×10^9/L, and this was accompanied by decreased fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes while being treated with argatroban. These manifestations of pronounced hypercoagulability linked to HIT are characteristic of aHIT.

Third, our patient did not show the rapid improvement in platelet counts commonly observed when patients with aHIT are treated with high-dose intravenous Ig, suggesting that this case represents an especially severe variant of aHIT. Specifically, serological findings demonstrated only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was used, indicative of an uncommon and particularly severe subtype; to our knowledge, this unique serological pattern has not previously been reported in the context of aHIT. Recognizing this could reduce the risk of overlooking aHIT and the associated negative clinical outcomes. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately established in the patient, based on at least three lines of evidence. First, subsequent sera obtained during the extended phase of platelet recovery revealed SRA activity patterns more typically seen in aHIT. Second, the most recent sample exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA response characteristic of classical HIT, corresponding with a decline in aHIT antibodies. Third, alternative explanations for the patient’s unusual clinical presentation were lacking. The patient went on to make a full clinical recovery, with no lingering neurological deficits.
