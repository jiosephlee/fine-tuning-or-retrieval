Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An elderly male patient received cardiac surgery with heparin as anticoagulation; following surgery, his platelet count progressively declined, reaching 50×10⁹/L on postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging identified a parietal infarct. HIT antibody immunoassay screening was strongly positive; the platelet activation assay (serotonin-release assay) demonstrated an unusual pattern: marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with only incomplete inhibition using a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Anticoagulation therapy was altered to argatroban and high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to continued hypercoagulability, therapy was switched to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count gradually increased (155×10⁹/L at POD62), and neurological symptoms resolved. Laboratory testing confirmed the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independent of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count nadir, 13×10⁹/L), distinguishing features in this aHIT case include its early onset after surgery (prior to POD5) due to preoperative heparin exposure and a distinctive laboratory antibody pattern with only partial susceptibility to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

Over the past 70 years of cardiac surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant intraoperative anticoagulant, mainly due to its multiple benefits: it prevents haemostasis linked to artificial surfaces, does not rely on renal or hepatic clearance, permits intraoperative monitoring with activated clotting time, and is economical. Nevertheless, UFH carries a significant immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). After heart surgery, HIT occurs relatively frequently, with reported rates between 0.5% and 1.0%, influenced by both the length and the type of postoperative heparin prophylaxis administered (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and is notable for a substantial increase in thrombosis risk, with a relative risk estimated at approximately 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been understood as a condition characterized by robust platelet activation through clustering of platelet Fc receptors, a process triggered by immune complexes formed between heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies and the cationic protein, platelet factor 4 (PF4), which is bound to polyanionic heparin. Recently, it has become apparent that certain patients exhibit distinctive, often severe clinical presentations attributable to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin. This form of atypical HIT is now referred to as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). In the present report, we discuss a case of aHIT marked by exceptionally severe and persistent thrombocytopenia, further complicated by a thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. This case adds to the understanding of aHIT by illustrating a particularly pronounced serological profile, including partial resistance to Fc receptor blockade. Furthermore, it emphasizes the diagnostic complexities associated with early postoperative thrombocytopenia, noting that the patient's unusually rapid development of aHIT was due to preoperative UFH exposure. The patient ultimately achieved a positive outcome.

Case presentation:

A man in his seventies, who had experienced exertional dyspnea for two years and had a longstanding history of atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was noted to have a newly identified systolic murmur. He was scheduled for diagnostic cardiac catheterization but did not withhold his apixaban before the procedure. Therefore, the interventional cardiologist chose to access through the transradial artery and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulant dose—only 2000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). Angiography revealed 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal branch, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta, and thus he was planned for cardiac surgery one week later.

The patient subsequently underwent heart surgery utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), with anticoagulation provided by 45,000 units of UFH. The surgical interventions included implantation of a #27 bioprosthetic aortic valve, replacement of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave vascular graft, coronary artery bypass using a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal vessel, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was completed without complications, there was no significant blood loss or requirement for transfusion, and the patient was easily weaned from CPB with stable hemodynamics.

Immediately before surgery, the patient's platelet count measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the platelet count dropped to 155×10^9/L, indicating a modest reduction typically observed after cardiac procedures. Standard postoperative prophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) began on POD1, involving subcutaneous dalteparin at 5000 U, followed by higher doses of 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. However, the platelet count continued to decline steadily from POD1 through POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Apart from experiencing postoperative delirium, the patient remained otherwise stable. Dalteparin administration was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux at 2.5 mg was introduced (first dose administered at 18:00 on POD5).

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the worsening postoperative delirium coupled with a further reduction in platelet count to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (repeat value, 24×10^9/L) led to the decision to perform a CT scan of the brain. Imaging identified an acute cerebral infarction, characterized by a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending through the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L alongside a haemoglobin level of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), comparable to haemoglobin results from the previous three days, which indicated anticipated early surgical blood loss without evidence of ongoing bleeding. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocytes were measured at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L) and were considered unremarkable. Haptoglobin was increased at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting the absence of clear haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 levels returned to normal values.

Coagulation parameters demonstrated a normal prothrombin time with an international normalised ratio (INR) of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), normal partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds), and an elevated fibrinogen of 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), which is expected in the immediate postoperative period due to the acute-phase reactant properties of fibrinogen. A blood sample collected on postoperative day 5 was sent to a reference laboratory for HIT antibody analysis.

Additional laboratory assessments showed normal chemistry, including urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography revealed no significant abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on postoperative day 7 confirmed thrombocytopenia, with no platelet clumping and no evidence of schistocytes or abnormal ('toxic') white blood cell morphology. HIT screening immunoassay, specifically the PF4/polyanion ELISA, was strongly positive at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units). The confirmatory platelet activation assay, serotonin-release assay (SRA), provided an 'indeterminate' result, which is addressed in more detail later.

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock/critical illness or infection, were considered improbable due to the patient's stable haemodynamic status and negative results from both blood and urine cultures. Uncommon etiologies for post-cardiac surgery thrombocytopenia were also thought to be less likely: post-transfusion purpura was excluded as no transfusions were administered, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura was deemed unlikely based on normal haptoglobin levels and no initial evidence of schistocytes, and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was improbable given that the platelet count decline occurred earlier than expected for any newly introduced postoperative medications (including vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid on the day of surgery, as well as furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine postoperatively).

While the decrease in platelets happened sooner than would be anticipated from intraoperative UFH exposure during CPB, further investigation revealed that UFH had in fact been administered intra-arterially during preoperative heart catheterization. The combination of a thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA indicated that heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was the most likely diagnosis, plausibly initiated by UFH received during the catheterization procedure. To clarify the 'indeterminate' result from the SRA, additional information was sought from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings were thoroughly analyzed. The patient’s POD5 serum demonstrated marked platelet activation, indicative of HIT antibodies, as evidenced by high serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20%), and by absence of serotonin release (0%) when exposed to a supratherapeutic UFH concentration (100 U/mL); this lack of activation at very high heparin levels typifies HIT, as PF4-immune complexes cannot form under such conditions. Nevertheless, two notable observations were made in the SRA data. Firstly, strong serotonin release occurred without UFH present (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; reference range <20%), a pattern typical for aHIT but not for classical, heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, incorporating the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress the serotonin release prompted by patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release remained elevated at 76% despite IV.3, rather than being reduced below 20%, which is customary with HIT antibodies—resulting in an initially classified ‘indeterminate’ SRA. The eventual interpretation was that the patient indeed had aHIT, but that the antibody activity was unusually robust, leading to only partial inhibition by IV.3 rather than the expected near-complete suppression. As far as is known, this unusual SRA reaction pattern has not previously been described in the literature.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and therapeutic argatroban—an agent directly inhibiting thrombin—was initiated at 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. While therapeutic fondaparinux use for HIT is an established off-label option, the hematologist chose argatroban due to its shorter elimination half-life, allowing more flexibility if bleeding should arise, especially considering the patient’s recent stroke and markedly low platelet counts. Furthermore, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin was administered: 100 g on two consecutive days, and a subsequent 100 g dose, due to failure of the platelet count to respond to the first two days of IV Ig therapy. Sequential measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were conducted, intending to monitor the effectiveness of anticoagulation indirectly—rising fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer would indicate successful mitigation of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of treatment, fibrinogen concentrations declined steadily between POD6 and POD17, reaching a lowest point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, while peripheral blood smears revealed red blood cell fragments (schistocytes) from POD9 through POD16. Collectively, these findings indicated a severe and progressive consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. Additionally, despite administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, the patient's platelet count showed only a slight improvement—from 13×10^9/L on POD7 before IV Ig to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14—an increment significantly less than what is typically observed in patients with aHIT who receive high-dose IV Ig (for review).

On postoperative day 14, anticoagulation was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg subcutaneously once daily. Factors influencing this switch included the convenience of fondaparinux, which requires once-daily subcutaneous injections compared to the intravenous administration and PTT-based adjustment necessary for argatroban, in addition to concerns about whether argatroban was adequately controlling the HIT-related hypercoagulable state. These concerns were supported by declining fibrinogen levels, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the emergence of schistocytes observed during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered before initiation of fondaparinux; the antithrombin level prior to infusion was mildly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range: 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet count progressively returned to within normal limits (measured at 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87); neurologically, the patient achieved full recovery. Fondaparinux therapy continued through POD65, after which long-term anticoagulation was transitioned back to apixaban at a dosage of 5 mg orally twice daily. Serial serotonin-release assay (SRA) tests stayed strongly positive for multiple months, with subsequent SRA results displaying patterns more typical for autoimmune HIT (see figure 2). The final blood sample, taken at POD124 after platelet counts had normalized, demonstrated results indicating primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, as evidenced by 15% serotonin release in the absence of unfractionated heparin (UFH) and 100% serotonin release when UFH was present at a concentration of 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient displayed several unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, which were ultimately consistent with a diagnosis of severe autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT). Firstly, the patient's HIT-related decrease in platelet count occurred prior to the typical onset window for HIT—postoperative days 5 to 10—for individuals following cardiac surgery. Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a decline in platelet count before day 5 postoperatively; this early manifestation is associated with recent preoperative exposure to heparin, as was observed in this case.

Secondly, the patient's platelet count decreased to an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by reduced fibrinogen levels, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and evidence of schistocytes during treatment with argatroban. Such features—representing severe HIT-associated hypercoagulability—are characteristic of aHIT.

Third, our patient did not demonstrate the rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia commonly observed after administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin in cases of aHIT; this suggests a particularly severe variant in this instance. Serologically, evidence supporting an atypically severe aHIT subtype was indicated by only partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro in the presence of monoclonal antibody IV.3. To date, this distinctive serological pattern has not been described in connection with aHIT, and recognizing it may aid in preventing overlooked diagnoses of aHIT and their associated clinical risks. Despite this, aHIT was ultimately confirmed in this patient based on three factors. Firstly, testing of additional sera obtained during the extended phase of platelet count normalization revealed some SRA reactivity patterns consistent with aHIT. Secondly, the final serum tested showed a heparin-dependent SRA response typical of classic HIT, suggesting a decline in aHIT antibody levels. Thirdly, no other credible diagnosis could account for the patient's unique clinical trajectory. The patient fortunately experienced complete clinical improvement, without lingering neurological deficits.
