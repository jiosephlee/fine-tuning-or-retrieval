Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient underwent cardiac surgery utilizing heparin for anticoagulation. Postoperatively, his platelet count progressively decreased, reaching 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging identified a parietal lobe stroke. An initial HIT immunoassay revealed a marked positive; the serotonin-release assay showed an unusual result, with marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin and only incomplete suppression by a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Argatroban and high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin were started for anticoagulation. Due to continued hypercoagulability, therapy was switched to fondaparinux, after which platelet counts gradually increased (155×10⁹/L by POD62) and neurological symptoms resolved. Further laboratory assessment demonstrated HIT antibodies exhibiting heparin-independent platelet-activating characteristics, signifying autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to significant thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), distinctive features of this aHIT presentation included unusually early postoperative onset related to heparin exposure prior to surgery and an atypical laboratory pattern partially resistant to Fc receptor blockade.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the primary anticoagulant during cardiac surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), largely due to its numerous benefits, including prevention of artificial surface-induced haemostasis, elimination independent of renal and hepatic pathways, capability for intraoperative anticoagulant assessment via activated clotting time, and low expense. Nevertheless, UFH is associated with a significant immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). The occurrence of HIT following cardiac surgery is relatively frequent, estimated at 0.5%–1.0%, varying according to both the duration and type of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and is notable for its strong relationship with thrombosis, reflected in a relative risk of approximately 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been considered a condition characterized by pronounced platelet activation through clustering of platelet Fc receptors, which results from immune complexes formed between heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies targeting the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) when complexed with polyanionic heparin. More recently, increasing evidence has emerged identifying patients with unusual and frequently severe clinical presentations caused by HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin; this variant has been termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (for review). Here, we report a case of aHIT marked by exceptionally severe, persistent thrombocytopenia, further complicated by thrombotic stroke, in the context of cardiac surgery. This case contributes new understanding to the diversity of aHIT by illustrating an unprecedentedly severe serological profile, partially resistant to Fc receptor blockade. Furthermore, it underscores the complexities encountered in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as the patient’s unusually prompt onset of aHIT was attributable to preoperative UFH exposure. The patient ultimately experienced a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, with a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was newly noted to have a systolic murmur. He was referred for diagnostic cardiac catheterization; however, his apixaban had not been discontinued beforehand. Consequently, the interventional cardiologist opted for a transradial artery access and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulant dose (2000 units of UFH). Angiography revealed 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and a dilated ascending aorta, and he was scheduled for cardiac surgery the following week.

The patient underwent heart surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, and was anticoagulated with 45,000 units of UFH. The operation involved aortic valve replacement using a size 27 bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta with a size 30 Gelweave graft, single-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting with a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The procedure was completed without complications, significant hemorrhage, or need for transfusion, and the patient was weaned from bypass without difficulty and demonstrated stable hemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400×10^9/L). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), the count had decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicating a slight early drop in platelets often seen after cardiac procedures. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, consisting of a subcutaneous injection of 5000 U dalteparin. Subsequent doses of 7500 U dalteparin were given on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, platelet levels fell steadily from POD1 to POD5, reaching a nadir of 50×10^9/L (figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient’s clinical status was stable. Dalteparin administration was halted on POD5; in its place, fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose (2.5 mg) was started (first dose at 18:00 on POD5).

Despite absence of focal neurological deficits, aggravation of postoperative delirium along with further reduction in platelets on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (with repeat measurement at 24×10^9/L) led to the decision to perform a CT scan of the brain. Imaging identified an acute infarction, characterized by a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, which extended into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count revealed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, with a haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L). This haemoglobin result was comparable to the values recorded over the previous three days, which aligns with typical early postoperative blood loss and the absence of continuous bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), and the differential was within normal limits. Reticulocytes measured 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L), considered unremarkable. Haptoglobin levels were elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no evidence of active haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 concentration normalized.

Coagulation tests demonstrated a normal prothrombin time, yielding an international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (RR 25–37 s), and a markedly increased fibrinogen level at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L). The elevated fibrinogen is characteristic of the acute phase reaction observed in the early postoperative period. On POD5, blood was collected and sent for HIT antibody analysis at a reference laboratory.

Additional laboratory assessments (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, bilirubin) were all within normal ranges. Blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography showed no significant abnormalities.

Peripheral blood smear reviewed on POD7 confirmed thrombocytopenia, without platelet aggregates, red cell fragmentation (schistocytes), or abnormal features suggestive of toxic changes in white cells. The PF4/polyanion enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) used for HIT screening returned a strongly positive result with an optical density of 2.2 units (RR <0.4 OD units), though the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (details discussed later).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of significant thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infection, appeared unlikely given both the clinical presentation and laboratory data (with stable haemodynamics and negative blood and urine cultures). Uncommon etiologies, including post-transfusion purpura (no transfusions administered), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (normal haptoglobin levels and an initial absence of schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also considered improbable as the timing of thrombocytopenia did not correspond with any newly introduced postoperative medications—whether intraoperative agents (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or postoperative therapies (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

While the onset of thrombocytopenia appeared too rapid to attribute solely to UFH exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass in surgery, subsequent review of the patient’s recent interventions revealed that UFH was administered intraprocedurally during a preoperative cardiac catheterization. When this exposure was considered alongside the occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was deemed the most plausible explanation, with the most likely trigger being the UFH given at preoperative cardiac catheterization. Further clarification about the ‘indeterminate’ SRA result was requested from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which had performed the test.

The SRA findings underwent careful evaluation. The patient's POD5 serum induced robust platelet activation, evidenced by substantial serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20%), aligning with HIT antibody presence. Notably, no serotonin release occurred at a supratherapeutic UFH concentration (100 U/mL), which is typical in HIT and indicates that PF4-containing immune complexes cannot form at high levels of UFH. 

Two additional aspects of the SRA data were observed. Firstly, the patient's serum provoked pronounced serotonin release even without UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), which is characteristic of aHIT rather than conventional heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, the serotonin release at 0.1 U/mL UFH was only partly inhibited by the addition of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3): serotonin release remained at 76%, instead of dropping below 20% as typically expected with HIT antibodies. This resulted in an initial categorization of the SRA outcome as ‘indeterminate.’ The subsequent interpretation was that the patient had aHIT, but their HIT antibodies displayed unusual reactivity, resulting in only partial inhibition with IV.3. To our knowledge, this distinctive SRA response pattern has not previously been documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosing was stopped, and the patient was started on argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, administered at 0.75 mcg/kg/min aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 s. Although therapeutic doses of fondaparinux are recognized as an effective off-label anticoagulant in HIT, the attending haematologist chose argatroban due to its shorter half-life, considering the patient’s recent stroke and pronounced thrombocytopenia as potential risk factors for bleeding. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (Ig) was administered, beginning with 100 g daily over two days, and a third 100 g dose was given when there was no initial rise in platelet count after the first two days of treatment. To monitor the effectiveness of anticoagulation, repeated fibrinogen and d-dimer measurements were obtained; an increase in fibrinogen and a decrease in d-dimer would indicate that HIT-related hypercoagulability was being managed with anticoagulation.

During the initial phase of therapy, fibrinogen levels steadily declined between postoperative day 6 (POD6) and postoperative day 17 (POD17), reaching a lowest point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer concentrations peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smear revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16. Collectively, these findings indicated significant and progressive consumptive coagulopathy associated with severe HIT. In addition, after administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (Ig), the patient’s platelet count showed only a mild increase, rising from 13×10^9/L on POD7 to a maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14—substantially less than the typical post-intravenous Ig platelet increase observed in patients with aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulant therapy was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg once daily via subcutaneous injection. The rationale for transitioning to fondaparinux included the convenience of once-daily subcutaneous administration compared to argatroban, which requires intravenous dosing adjusted based on PTT, and concerns that argatroban might not adequately suppress HIT-related hypercoagulability, as suggested by declining fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer, and schistocytes noted during argatroban treatment. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, a dose of AT concentrate (1000 units) was administered prior to initiating fondaparinux due to a mildly reduced pre-infusion antithrombin level of 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count progressively returned to normal levels, measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87. Neurological function was also fully restored. Fondaparinux therapy was maintained up to POD65, after which apixaban was re-initiated at a dose of 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial serotonin release assays (SRAs) remained strongly positive over several months, with later SRAs demonstrating a more typical pattern for autoimmune HIT (aHIT) as illustrated in figure 2. The most recent blood sample, drawn on POD124 after platelet normalization, indicated primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies—serotonin release measured at 15% without unfractionated heparin (UFH), but reached 100% in the presence of UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited multiple unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all ultimately consistent with a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Firstly, the reduction in platelet count due to HIT occurred earlier than is typically observed, outside the conventional timeframe for HIT onset—which is between postoperative days 5 and 10 for individuals following cardiac surgery. HIT-associated thrombocytopenia developing before POD5 happens in only approximately 10% of post-cardiac surgery cases, a pattern associated with recent exposure to heparin prior to surgery, which was also noted in this case.

Secondly, the patient's platelet count decreased to a notably low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by declining fibrinogen concentrations, elevated d-dimer measurements, and the presence of schistocytes during treatment with argatroban. This constellation of findings, characteristic of pronounced HIT-related hypercoagulability, reflects the presence of aHIT.

Third, our patient failed to demonstrate a rapid normalization of platelet counts, which is commonly observed when high-dose intravenous Ig is administered to individuals with aHIT; this suggests the patient experienced an exceptionally severe manifestation of this condition. Supporting this, serological findings revealed only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was used, indicating an atypically severe aHIT variant. To our knowledge, this distinctive serologic presentation has not previously been documented in relation to aHIT, and recognizing it may help prevent overlooking an aHIT diagnosis, potentially averting harmful clinical outcomes. However, the diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately established in our case based on three main factors. First, repeat serum samples obtained during the extended period of platelet count improvement displayed SRA activity profiles more typical of aHIT. Second, the final sample tested exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA response consistent with conventional HIT, signaling a decrease in aHIT antibody levels. Third, there were no other credible explanations for the patient’s atypical clinical trajectory. Fortunately, the patient achieved full clinical recovery without persistent neurologic deficits.
