Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin for anticoagulation during cardiac surgery; following the operation, his platelet count progressively declined, falling to 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to investigation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging revealed a parietal lobe infarction. An initial immunoassay test for HIT antibodies showed a marked positive result, and the serotonin-release assay demonstrated an unusual response, characterized by robust serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin that was only partially suppressed by a monoclonal antibody blocking the platelet Fc receptor. The anticoagulation regimen was altered to argatroban along with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing evidence of hypercoagulability led to further transition to fondaparinux, with platelet counts gradually improving to 155×10⁹/L by POD62; neurologic symptoms resolved. Additional laboratory analysis verified the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independent of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Noteworthy features of this case of aHIT, in addition to marked thrombocytopenia (platelet count minimum, 13×10⁹/L), included its early postoperative presentation (prior to POD5) due to preoperative heparin exposure and a distinct laboratory antibody profile indicating partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant anticoagulant during heart surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to several benefits such as suppression of haemostatic activity on artificial surfaces, elimination via non-renal and non-hepatic pathways, the ability to monitor its anticoagulant effect intraoperatively using activated clotting time, and its affordability. Nevertheless, a significant complication of UFH is heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-driven reaction. HIT occurs relatively often after cardiac procedures, with a reported incidence of 0.5%–1.0%, depending upon the postoperative use and type of heparin prophylaxis (UFH vs low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and is notable for its pronounced correlation with thrombotic events, reflected by a relative risk of thrombosis of approximately 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been considered a condition marked by robust platelet activation, instigated via clustering of platelet Fc receptors, which results from immune complexes formed by heparin-dependent antibodies of the immunoglobulin G (IgG) subtype that specifically target platelet factor 4 (PF4) bound to heparin, a polyanionic molecule. Recently, it has become evident that a subset of patients experiences atypical, and frequently severe, clinical manifestations caused by HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin; this form has been termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (for review). The present report details a case of aHIT characterized by unusually profound and persistent thrombocytopenia, complicated by thrombotic stroke subsequent to cardiac surgery. The case sheds light on an exceptionally severe serological profile of aHIT, featuring partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. It further illustrates the diagnostic complexities posed by early postoperative thrombocytopenia, with the patient's unusually rapid onset of aHIT attributable to preoperative UFH administration. The patient's clinical course ultimately concluded with a positive outcome.

Case presentation:

A man in his seventies, who had a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was newly identified as having a systolic murmur. For further evaluation, he underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterisation. Since he did not pause his apixaban prior to the procedure, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery access and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulant dose (only 2000 U of unfractionated heparin). Angiography demonstrated 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta; as a result, he was scheduled for cardiac surgery one week later.

Cardiac surgery was carried out with the use of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), using 45 000 U of unfractionated heparin for anticoagulation. The procedures performed included aortic valve replacement with a #27 bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta using a #30 Gelweave graft, single-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting to the obtuse marginal artery with a saphenous vein graft, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was uneventful, with minimal blood loss and no requirement for transfusion. The patient was weaned from bypass smoothly, maintaining excellent hemodynamic status.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400), and on the first postoperative day (POD1) decreased to 155×10^9/L, representing only a mild early reduction typical for cardiac surgical procedures. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis using low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, beginning with a subcutaneous injection of 5000 U dalteparin, followed by increased dosing (7500 U) administered on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, the platelet count declined steadily from POD1 through POD5, ultimately reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient’s clinical course was unremarkable. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started (first dose given on POD5 at 18:00).

In the absence of localized neurological deficits, progression of postoperative delirium along with a continued decrease in platelets on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (repeated, 24×10^9/L) led to CT brain imaging. The scan demonstrated an acute cerebral infarction, showing a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending up to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On post-operative day 5 (POD5), the complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L and a haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), which was comparable to the haemoglobin levels documented over the preceding three days, consistent with anticipated early post-surgical blood losses and without evidence of continuing haemorrhage. The white blood cell count was measured at 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count was within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating a lack of significant haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 concentrations had normalized.

Coagulation assays indicated a normal prothrombin time and an international normalized ratio (INR) of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), along with a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (RR 25–37 seconds). Fibrinogen was markedly elevated at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), reflecting the expected postoperative acute phase response, as fibrinogen typically increases substantially in the early postoperative period. On POD5, a blood sample was forwarded to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody evaluation.

Additional laboratory studies demonstrated normal results for serum urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography did not reveal significant pathology.

Peripheral blood smear review on POD7 verified thrombocytopenia (with no platelet clumping) and showed no evidence of red cell fragmentation (schistocytes) or abnormal (‘toxic’) white blood cell morphology. Screening immunoassay for HIT—the platelet factor 4 (PF4)/polyanion ELISA—produced a strongly positive result, registering 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units). However, the confirmatory platelet activation assay—the serotonin-release assay (SRA)—produced an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (further discussed below).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of marked thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infection, appeared unlikely based on both clinical presentation and laboratory data (stable haemodynamics; negative results from blood and urine cultures). Unusual causes, including post-transfusion purpura (no transfusions had been administered), post-operative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (normal haptoglobin levels, absence of schistocytes at initial assessment), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also improbable, as the reduction in platelet count manifested too soon after exposure to newly introduced postoperative medications—whether administered intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or during the postoperative phase (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

While the timing of thrombocytopenia was also inconsistent with onset due to unfractionated heparin (UFH) exposure during cardiopulmonary bypass, further review revealed that the patient received UFH during preoperative cardiac catheterisation. Considering the occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was considered the most likely diagnosis, likely precipitated by UFH during preoperative catheterisation. Additional information about the ‘indeterminate’ serotonin release assay (SRA) result was solicited from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which performed the test.

The SRA findings were thoroughly evaluated. Indicating the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s postoperative day 5 (POD5) serum induced pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), and showed no serotonin release (0%) at a supratherapeutic UFH concentration (100 U/mL); this absence of platelet activation at very elevated heparin levels is a hallmark of HIT, reflecting that PF4-containing immune complexes do not form under such high UFH conditions. Yet, two further aspects of the SRA results were observed. Firstly, the patient’s serum prompted marked serotonin release even without UFH present (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%); this is typical for aHIT, rather than classic heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 was added, it did not fully suppress the serotonin release induced by patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: instead, serotonin release remained at 76% in the presence of IV.3, rather than being reduced below 20%, as is commonly expected for HIT antibodies; this led to the original characterization of the SRA result as ‘indeterminate’. The reasoning was that the patient’s condition was aHIT, but with exceptionally reactive HIT antibodies, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3 compared to typical responses. To the best of our knowledge, this unusual SRA pattern has not been previously documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was started for anticoagulation at 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Although therapeutic-dose fondaparinux is effective as an ‘off-label’ option for managing HIT, the haematologist responsible for the case selected argatroban because it has a shorter half-life, which would allow easier management if bleeding developed, particularly given the patient’s recent stroke and severe thrombocytopenia. Additionally, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) was administered at high doses: 100 g on two consecutive days, followed by a third dose of 100 g, because platelet counts did not improve after the first 2 days of IVIg. To assess the efficacy of anticoagulation indirectly, serial measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were performed, since an increase in fibrinogen and a decrease in d-dimer would indicate effective suppression of HIT-related hypercoagulable state.

During the initial phase of management, there was a continuous decline in fibrinogen levels between postoperative day (POD) 6 and POD17, reaching a lowest point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer concentrations peaked around POD12. Additionally, peripheral blood smear showed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16. Collectively, these laboratory findings indicated severe, progressive coagulopathy related to HIT and marked consumption of coagulation factors. Furthermore, following high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin administration, the patient’s platelet count increased only minimally, rising from 13×10^9/L on POD7 (pre-intravenous Ig) to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This degree of thrombocytosis was considerably less than the typical response to high-dose intravenous Ig observed in patients with aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient's anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg daily administered via subcutaneous injection. Factors influencing this adjustment included the convenience of fondaparinux, which permits once-daily subcutaneous administration as opposed to argatroban, which requires intravenous infusion titrated according to PTT, as well as apprehension that argatroban may not have sufficiently managed HIT-associated hypercoagulability. This concern was based on previously noted decreases in fibrinogen, increases in d-dimer, and the presence of schistocytes observed during argatroban therapy. Because fondaparinux acts as an indirect antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, the patient received a 1000 U dose of antithrombin concentrate prior to initiation of fondaparinux. The measured antithrombin level before infusion was mildly decreased at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet count steadily returned to normal, reaching 155×10^9/L by postoperative day (POD) 62 and 202×10^9/L by POD 87; a full neurological recovery was also achieved. Fondaparinux therapy was continued until POD 65, after which apixaban was restarted at 5 mg twice daily by mouth for ongoing anticoagulation. Over several months, serial serotonin-release assay (SRA) results consistently remained strongly positive, with subsequent assays demonstrating SRA patterns more typical for autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT) (see figure 2). The final blood sample, drawn at POD 124 once platelet levels had normalized, revealed findings in line with mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies—there was 15% serotonin release without unfractionated heparin (UFH), but 100% release when UFH was present at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient demonstrated several uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, each of which was ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, the onset of thrombocytopenia related to HIT occurred earlier than the typical time frame for HIT development, which in post-cardiac surgery patients is typically between postoperative days 5 and 10. Fewer than 10% of individuals undergoing cardiac surgery with HIT experience a decline in platelet count before postoperative day 5, and this early onset has been associated with recent preoperative heparin use, as was observed in this case.

Additionally, the patient's platelet count decreased to an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, alongside reductions in fibrinogen, elevations in d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes while on argatroban therapy. These manifestations of pronounced HIT-driven hypercoagulability are indicative of aHIT.

Third, our patient did not demonstrate a rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia, a response that is typically observed when patients with aHIT are treated with high-dose intravenous Ig; this suggests the presence of an especially severe form of aHIT in this case. Notably, the serological findings indicating this unusually severe variant were the only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was used; as far as we are aware, this distinctive serological pattern has not previously been documented in relation to aHIT, and recognizing it could help prevent overlooking the diagnosis, which may lead to unfavorable clinical outcomes. Despite this, the diagnosis of aHIT was ultimately established in our patient based on at least three lines of evidence. First, tests performed on later serum samples, obtained during the extended period of platelet count normalization, revealed SRA activity profiles more typical of aHIT. Second, the final sample demonstrated a heparin-dependent SRA response characteristic of classic HIT, indicative of diminishing aHIT antibodies. Third, no other diagnosis adequately accounted for the patient’s atypical clinical presentation. Encouragingly, the patient experienced a very good clinical recovery and had no remaining neurological deficits.
