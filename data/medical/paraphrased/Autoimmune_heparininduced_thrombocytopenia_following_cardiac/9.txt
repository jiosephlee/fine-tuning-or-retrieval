Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient underwent cardiac surgery utilizing heparin for anticoagulation. Postoperatively, his platelet count progressively decreased, reaching 50×10⁹/L on the fifth day after surgery (POD5), which led to evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies; imaging of the brain identified a parietal stroke. HIT antibody screening by immunoassay was markedly positive; the serotonin-release assay exhibited an unusual pattern, showing strong serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with only partial suppression by a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Anticoagulation therapy was switched to argatroban combined with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Ongoing hypercoagulable state necessitated further transition to fondaparinux, after which the platelet count progressively normalized (155×10⁹/L by POD62), and the patient’s neurological deficits resolved. Further laboratory assessment identified HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independently of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count nadir of 13×10⁹/L), distinctive features of this aHIT case included very early postoperative onset (prior to POD5) due to preoperative heparin exposure, and an atypical laboratory antibody signature characterized by partial resistance to blockade of the Fc receptor.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the predominant anticoagulant during heart surgery involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to its numerous benefits. These include its capacity to inhibit haemostasis triggered by artificial surfaces, elimination pathways that do not rely on the kidneys or liver, the ability to monitor its anticoagulant effect intraoperatively via activated clotting time, and its affordability. Nevertheless, UFH carries a significant risk of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-mediated adverse reaction. The incidence of HIT following cardiac surgery is relatively notable, affecting approximately 0.5%–1.0% of patients, a rate influenced by both the duration and selection of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (comparing UFH and low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)). HIT is particularly significant because of its marked tendency to be associated with thrombosis, conferring an estimated fifteenfold increase in thrombotic risk.

Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) has traditionally been characterized as a condition marked by robust platelet activation, occurring via clustering of platelet Fc receptors due to immune complex formation with heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies that target the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) when it is associated with the polyanion heparin. In recent times, however, it has become increasingly apparent that certain individuals display atypical and sometimes more severe manifestations as a result of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets without the presence of heparin; this variant, known as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT), has been defined in the literature (for review). Here, we report a case of aHIT in which the patient experienced markedly severe and prolonged thrombocytopenia, complicated by thrombotic stroke, following a cardiac surgery. This case illustrates a distinct and severe serological variant of aHIT, notable for partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. Additionally, it underscores the complexities inherent in diagnosing early postoperative thrombocytopenia, as this patient’s abnormally rapid onset of aHIT was attributable to prior exposure to unfractionated heparin (UFH) before surgery. Despite these complications, the patient ultimately achieved a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A man in his seventies, who had been experiencing exertional shortness of breath for two years and had a history of chronic atrial fibrillation managed with apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was discovered to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. Diagnostic cardiac catheterization was performed; however, the patient had not discontinued apixaban prior to the procedure. Therefore, the cardiologist proceeded with the catheterization using a transradial artery approach and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulation dose (only 2000 U of unfractionated heparin). Angiography revealed a 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic valve stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. He was consequently scheduled for cardiac surgery one week later.

Cardiac surgery was conducted utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and anticoagulation was accomplished with the administration of 45,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). The operative procedures involved replacement of the aortic valve with a size 27 bioprosthetic valve, substitution of the ascending aorta using a size 30 Gelweave graft, a solitary coronary artery bypass graft using a saphenous vein to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The intraoperative course was smooth, characterized by minimal blood loss and no requirement for transfusion, and the patient was weaned from CPB without difficulty, displaying stable hemodynamics.

The platelet count measured immediately before surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range (RR) 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the platelet level fell to 155×10^9/L, which signified only a modest drop commonly seen after cardiac surgery. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was started on POD1, consisting of 5000 units of dalteparin administered subcutaneously, followed by increased doses of 7500 units on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, the platelet count continued to decrease each day from POD1 to POD5, ultimately reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Aside from experiencing postoperative delirium, the patient was otherwise recovering appropriately. Dalteparin administration was held on POD5, and prophylactic-dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was initiated instead, with the first dose given at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, progression of the postoperative delirium and a further drop in platelet count on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (confirmed by repeat result of 24×10^9/L) led to a CT scan of the brain being performed. Imaging demonstrated an acute infarct, displaying a region of hypodensity measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm within the left parietal lobe and extending to the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

The complete blood count performed on POD5 revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L and a haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), comparable to the haemoglobin values noted over the prior three days, reflecting anticipated early postoperative blood loss without evidence of continued bleeding. The white blood cell count measured 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) with a normal differential. The reticulocyte count was within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin levels were raised at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), which is indicative of no significant haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 concentrations had normalized.

Coagulation parameters demonstrated a prothrombin time within normal limits, yielding an international normalised ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1). The partial thromboplastin time was also normal at 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds). However, fibrinogen was increased to 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L), which aligns with the expected elevation in the early postoperative period, as fibrinogen serves as an acute-phase protein and frequently rises significantly during this time. On postoperative day 5, a blood sample was forwarded to a reference laboratory for testing of HIT antibodies.

Other blood work included normal chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were drawn. A chest X-ray showed no major abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear from postoperative day 7 showed thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet clumping, red blood cell fragments (schistocytes), or abnormal “toxic” changes in white blood cells. The HIT screening immunoassay—PF4/polyanion ELISA—was markedly elevated, registering 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units), whereas the definitive platelet activation test, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), returned an ‘indeterminate’ outcome (to be detailed later).

Differential diagnosis:

Typical causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock, critical illness, or infection, were considered improbable due to both stable haemodynamic status and negative blood and urine cultures. Less common etiologies, such as post-transfusion purpura—ruled out by the absence of any transfusions—and post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura—excluded by normal haptoglobin levels and an initial lack of schistocytes—were also deemed unlikely. Drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia was considered improbable, as the platelet count decline began too soon for any recently initiated medications, whether administered intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine), to be implicated.

While the decline in platelet count developed earlier than would be expected from the UFH exposure during CPB surgery, additional review revealed that the patient had also received UFH during preoperative cardiac catheterization. Combined with the occurrence of a thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA result, HIT was considered the most likely diagnosis, probably induced by the UFH used at the time of preoperative catheterization. More specific information about the 'indeterminate' SRA was solicited from the reporting institution, the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings underwent thorough evaluation. In line with the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s postoperative day 5 serum induced marked serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL unfractionated heparin (UFH) concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range [RR] <20% release). Conversely, there was an absence of serotonin release (0%) at a supratherapeutic UFH concentration (100 U/mL); this lack of platelet activation at extremely high heparin levels is characteristic of HIT, attributable to the disruption of PF4-containing immune complex formation at these concentrations. Nonetheless, two other aspects of the SRA data were identified. Firstly, the patient's serum elicited robust serotonin release even without UFH present (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), a finding typically observed in autoimmune HIT (aHIT) rather than conventional heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) was introduced, it did not fully suppress the serotonin release detected with patient serum plus 0.1 U/mL UFH: serotonin release persisted at 76% despite IV.3, instead of decreasing to below 20% as is standard with HIT antibodies, which led initially to labeling the SRA outcome as 'indeterminate.' The rationale emerged that the correct diagnosis was aHIT, with the patient possessing unusually reactive HIT antibodies that led to only partial inhibition by IV.3 compared to the expected result. To the best of our knowledge, this distinct SRA reactivity pattern has not been described before.

Treatment:

Prophylactic fondaparinux was stopped, and the patient was started on therapeutic argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, administered at 0.75 mcg/kg/min with an intended PTT range of 55–85 seconds. While fondaparinux at therapeutic levels can be an effective 'off-label' option for HIT, the hematologist managing the case opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which was considered safer should bleeding develop, given the patient’s recent stroke and marked thrombocytopenia. Furthermore, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (Ig) therapy was administered—100 g for two consecutive days initially, then a third 100 g dose—because the platelet count did not increase after the first two days of Ig treatment. Repeated measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were also obtained as indirect markers of anticoagulant activity, with increases in fibrinogen and decreases in d-dimer serving as indicators of improved control of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of therapy, fibrinogen levels progressively declined between POD6 and POD17, reaching a lowest point of 2.49 g/L. Concurrently, d-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 through POD16. These findings collectively indicated a severe and progressive consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. In addition, despite receiving high-dose intravenous Ig, the patient’s platelet count showed only a slight rise, increasing from 13×10^9/L on POD7 (pre-intravenous Ig) to a maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14—an increment notably less than what is typically observed following high-dose intravenous Ig administration in cases of aHIT (see review).

On postoperative day 14, anticoagulation was transitioned from argatroban to therapeutic-dose fondaparinux at 10 mg subcutaneously once per day. This modification was motivated by the relative simplicity of fondaparinux administration—requiring only once-daily subcutaneous injections—compared to the need for continuous intravenous infusion with PTT-based dose adjustments when using argatroban. Another factor prompting the switch was the suspected inadequate management of HIT-associated hypercoagulability by argatroban, evidenced by declining fibrinogen levels, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes during therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, a single dose of antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) was administered beforehand; the antithrombin level prior to infusion measured 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL), indicating a mild decrease.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet count steadily returned to normal, reaching 155×10^9/L on postoperative day (POD) 62 and 202×10^9/L by POD87, accompanied by a full resolution of neurological symptoms. Fondaparinux therapy continued through POD65, after which long-term anticoagulation was initiated with apixaban, administered orally at a dose of 5 mg twice daily. Serial SRA assessments remained strongly positive over several months, with subsequent analyses displaying SRA profiles increasingly typical of atypical HIT (aHIT), as illustrated in figure 2. The final sample, drawn at POD124 after normalization of platelet levels, demonstrated findings most consistent with predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies—15% serotonin release without UFH and 100% release when 0.3 U/mL UFH was present.

Discussion:

Our patient exhibited multiple unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, the development of thrombocytopenia related to HIT occurred earlier than the typical timeframe, which for cardiac surgery patients is generally from postoperative day (POD) 5 to POD 10 (inclusive). Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who experience HIT demonstrate a decline in platelet count before POD 5, a pattern that has been associated with recent exposure to heparin prior to surgery, as also seen in this case.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count dropped to a notably low nadir of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen, increased d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes while on argatroban therapy. These findings, characteristic of severe hypercoagulability associated with HIT, are consistent with a diagnosis of aHIT.

Thirdly, our patient did not demonstrate the rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia typically observed when high-dose intravenous Ig is administered to individuals with aHIT; this suggests the presence of an especially severe manifestation of this condition. Supporting this, serological testing indicated an unusually severe aHIT subtype, as shown by only partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro upon exposure to the monoclonal antibody IV.3. To our knowledge, this distinctive serological profile has not previously been described in aHIT, and increased recognition of this finding may help prevent missed aHIT diagnoses and resultant clinical complications. Nonetheless, the diagnosis of aHIT was eventually substantiated in this patient, based on at least three points. First, analysis of sera obtained during the extended period of platelet count normalization revealed some SRA reactivity patterns that more closely resembled those seen in aHIT. Second, the final serum tested displayed a heparin-dependent SRA result typical of standard HIT, suggesting diminishing aHIT antibodies. Third, no alternative diagnosis adequately accounted for the patient's atypical clinical trajectory. Fortunately, the patient experienced a very favorable recovery, without any lasting neurological deficits.
