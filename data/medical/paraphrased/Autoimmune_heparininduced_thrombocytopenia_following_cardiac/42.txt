Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient underwent cardiac surgery with heparin used for anticoagulation. After surgery, his platelet count progressively decreased, falling to 50×10⁹/L on postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to assessment for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies; imaging of the brain identified a parietal infarct. HIT antibody screening using immunoassay produced a strongly positive result, while the serotonin-release assay for platelet activation displayed an unusual pattern—showing marked serotonin release at heparin concentrations of 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL, with only partial suppression by a platelet Fc receptor–blocking monoclonal antibody. The anticoagulation regimen was altered to argatroban in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to ongoing hypercoagulability, fondaparinux was initiated, and the platelet count gradually recovered (reaching 155×10⁹/L at POD62); neurologic symptoms resolved. Further laboratory studies verified the presence of HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets independent of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to severe thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), distinguishing features in this aHIT case included its early manifestation after surgery—attributed to prior heparin exposure—and a distinct laboratory antibody signature marked by partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the primary anticoagulant during heart surgeries involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), largely due to its numerous benefits, such as suppression of haemostasis induced by artificial surfaces, clearance mechanisms independent of hepatic and renal function, ease of intraoperative monitoring via activated clotting time, and economical cost. Nevertheless, UFH carries a significant risk of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), an immune-mediated complication. HIT occurs with notable frequency after cardiac surgery, estimated between 0.5% and 1.0%, contingent upon the type and length of postoperative heparin thromboprophylaxis (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and is distinguished by its marked association with thrombosis, bearing a relative risk approaching 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been understood as a condition marked by pronounced platelet activation, mediated via platelet Fc receptor aggregation initiated by immune complexes composed of immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies targeting platelet factor 4 (PF4) when combined with polyanionic heparin. More recently, awareness has grown regarding a subset of patients exhibiting atypical and often more severe disease manifestations owing to HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets without heparin. This variant, termed 'autoimmune HIT' (aHIT) (for review), is characterized by unique pathophysiology. Here, we report a case of aHIT presenting with unusually prolonged and severe thrombocytopenia, further complicated by thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. This case offers new perspectives on the range of aHIT by demonstrating a distinctive serological profile, including partial insensitivity to Fc receptor inhibition. Moreover, it underscores the diagnostic complexities of postoperative thrombocytopenia, with the patient’s exceptionally early onset of aHIT attributable to preoperative UFH exposure. The patient ultimately achieved a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his 70s, with a two-year duration of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily, was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. Diagnostic cardiac catheterization was performed without holding his apixaban beforehand; thus, the interventional cardiologist opted for a transradial arterial access and administered a lower-than-standard intraprocedural anticoagulation (2000 U of UFH). Angiography demonstrated a 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal artery, moderate aortic valve stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta, leading to a plan for cardiac surgery in one week.

Cardiac surgery was carried out utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), with anticoagulation provided by 45,000 U of UFH. The procedures completed included an aortic valve replacement with a #27 bioprosthetic valve, replacement of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, single-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting using a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The surgery was uncomplicated, with minimal blood loss, no transfusions required, and separation from bypass occurred without difficulty with stable haemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately before surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), it decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicating only a slight drop in platelets commonly seen after cardiac procedures. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) started on POD1 with a subcutaneous injection of 5000 U dalteparin, and increased doses of 7500 U were given on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, the platelet count steadily fell from POD1 to POD5, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). The patient remained stable apart from experiencing postoperative delirium. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5; instead, fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose (2.5 mg) was initiated (first dose administered at 18:00 on POD5).

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the worsening delirium and further decrease in platelet count on POD6 (26×10^9/L, repeat 24×10^9/L) led to a brain CT scan. Imaging showed an acute cerebral infarction, with a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm by 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending into the cortical area.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count showed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, with haemoglobin measuring 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), closely matching values from the previous three days. This pattern aligns with anticipated early post-surgical blood loss without evidence of persistent bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), displaying a normal differential. Reticulocytes measured 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L), which was unremarkable. Haptoglobin was elevated at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicating no significant haemolysis. Serum vitamin B12 was within normal limits.

Coagulation profiles revealed a normal prothrombin time and corresponding international normalised ratio (INR) of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 seconds (reference range 25–37 seconds), and a markedly increased fibrinogen concentration at 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L). This elevated fibrinogen is characteristic of the acute postoperative phase, as it serves as an acute phase reactant that typically rises significantly in this context. A sample collected on POD5 was forwarded to a reference lab for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody testing.

Additional laboratory studies revealed normal values for urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin. Both blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography did not reveal any significant abnormalities.

Peripheral blood smear review performed on POD7 corroborated thrombocytopenia (with no evidence of platelet aggregation), and demonstrated no schistocytes or abnormal ‘toxic’ white blood cells. HIT screening via PF4/polyanion ELISA resulted in a strongly positive finding, measuring 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units). However, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), a confirmatory platelet activation test, provided an ‘indeterminate’ outcome, further addressed in subsequent discussion.

Differential diagnosis:

Frequent causes of marked thrombocytopenia after cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock, critical illness, or infection, were considered improbable based on the patient's presentation and laboratory findings (hemodynamic status was stable; cultures of blood and urine yielded no growth). Uncommon etiologies for thrombocytopenia in the post-cardiac surgery setting—including post-transfusion purpura (with no transfusions performed), post-surgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (haptoglobin within normal range, no schistocytes detected initially), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia—were also deemed unlikely, given that the thrombocytopenia developed before exposure to any new postoperative medications. The agents administered exclusively on the surgical day (vancomycin, cefazolin, and tranexamic acid), as well as those used in the postoperative period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine), had not been in use long enough to account for the decrease in platelet count.

While the decline in platelet count appeared to arise sooner than expected from the intraoperative UFH delivered during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), additional investigation revealed prior administration of intraprocedural UFH during preoperative cardiac catheterization. In the context of an ischemic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was considered the most likely etiology, presumed secondary to UFH given during the preoperative catheterization. Further clarification about the ‘indeterminate’ SRA was sought from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings underwent thorough evaluation. Reflecting the presence of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient's serum on postoperative day 5 triggered marked serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20%), along with no serotonin release (0%) at supra-therapeutic UFH concentrations (100 U/mL). This lack of platelet activation at very high heparin levels is typical of HIT and indicates the inability to form immune complexes containing PF4 at elevated UFH concentrations. Nonetheless, two notable aspects of the SRA results were observed. First, the patient’s serum induced prominent serotonin release even without UFH (96% release at 0 U/mL heparin; reference range <20%), which is indicative of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) rather than classic heparin-dependent HIT. Second, when the monoclonal antibody IV.3, which blocks Fc receptors, was added, it did not completely suppress the serotonin release from patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH; serotonin release remained at 76%, rather than decreasing to below 20% as usually occurs with HIT antibodies. This led to the initial classification of the SRA as ‘indeterminate.’ The underlying reason was that the patient indeed had aHIT, but their HIT antibodies demonstrated exceptional reactivity, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3 rather than the near-complete blockade typically observed. To the best of our knowledge, this type of atypical SRA response has not been previously described.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosage was stopped, and the patient began treatment with argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55–85 seconds. While fondaparinux used at therapeutic levels is a suitable off-label anticoagulant for HIT, the hematologist managing the case opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, which could be advantageous in the event of bleeding, considering the patient’s recent stroke and unusually profound thrombocytopenia. Furthermore, intravenous immunoglobulin was administered at a high dose, starting with 100 g on two successive days and followed by another 100 g dose, because platelet counts failed to increase after the initial two days of therapy. Serial assessments of fibrinogen and d-dimer were carried out to provide indirect evidence regarding the effectiveness of anticoagulation, with increasing fibrinogen levels and decreasing d-dimer values expected to indicate adequate anticoagulant control over HIT-related hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of treatment, there was a notable decline in fibrinogen levels observed between POD6 and POD17, reaching a minimum of 2.49 g/L. The highest d-dimer concentrations were observed around POD12, and peripheral blood smear revealed the presence of red cell fragments (schistocytes) from POD9 through POD16. Collectively, these three findings indicated a severe and progressive consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. Additionally, after high-dose intravenous Ig therapy, the patient’s platelet count demonstrated only a slight rise—from a baseline of 13×10^9/L (POD7) to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L (POD14)—which is substantially less than the increase typically expected following high-dose intravenous Ig in cases of aHIT (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. This modification took into account the convenience of fondaparinux dosing, which involves once-daily subcutaneous injection as opposed to the intravenous, PTT-adjusted administration required for argatroban. Additionally, there were concerns that argatroban might not have been sufficiently managing the hypercoagulable state associated with HIT, as suggested by decreasing fibrinogen, increasing d-dimer, and the development of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Because fondaparinux acts as an indirect, antithrombin-dependent factor Xa inhibitor, a supplemental dose of AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to initiation; the antithrombin level before infusion was slightly below normal at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet count progressively returned to normal levels, measuring 155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87. Neurologically, the patient achieved full recovery. Fondaparinux therapy continued through POD65, after which apixaban was reintroduced at a dose of 5 mg twice daily by mouth for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial serotonin release assay (SRA) tests remained strongly positive over several months, with some subsequent SRAs exhibiting patterns more typical of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) (figure 2). The most recent test, performed at POD124 after normalization of the platelet count, showed findings indicative of predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, demonstrated by 15% serotonin release without UFH and 100% release in the presence of UFH at a concentration of 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited multiple unusual clinical, laboratory, and serological findings, all of which were ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, his HIT-related thrombocytopenia manifested earlier than the typical window for HIT development, which occurs between postoperative day 5 and day 10 in cardiac surgery patients. Approximately 10% of such individuals experience a HIT-associated decline in platelet count prior to postoperative day 5, often due to preoperative heparin exposure, a circumstance also observed in this patient.

Additionally, the patient’s platelet count dropped to an exceptionally low level of 13×10^9/L, accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and schistocytes observed during argatroban treatment. These severe manifestations of HIT-induced hypercoagulability are characteristic of aHIT.

Thirdly, the patient did not demonstrate a rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia, which is commonly observed in individuals with aHIT receiving high-dose intravenous Ig. This suggests that the patient may have had an exceptionally severe form of the disorder. In fact, serological findings supported this hypothesis, as only partial suppression of platelet activation was observed in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was used. To our knowledge, this distinctive serological profile has not been previously documented in aHIT cases, and recognizing it could help prevent a missed diagnosis and the potential for adverse outcomes. Despite these challenges, the diagnosis of aHIT in this patient was established based on at least three factors. First, subsequent serum samples obtained during the extended period of platelet count recovery revealed SRA reactivity patterns more typical of aHIT. Second, the final sample tested displayed a heparin-dependent SRA response characteristic of classic HIT, indicating a decline in aHIT antibody levels. Third, there was no other reasonable explanation for the patient’s atypical clinical presentation. Ultimately, the patient achieved an excellent recovery, without any lingering neurological deficits.
