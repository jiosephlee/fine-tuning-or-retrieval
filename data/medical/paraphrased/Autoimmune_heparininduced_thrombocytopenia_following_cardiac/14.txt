Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient underwent cardiac surgery using heparin for anticoagulation; after surgery, his platelet levels consistently declined, dropping to 50×10⁹/L on postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to an evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Brain imaging revealed a parietal stroke. The immunoassay screening for HIT antibodies was strongly positive, and the platelet activation (serotonin-release) assay displayed an unusual pattern—marked serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin, with only partial suppression upon adding a platelet Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. Anticoagulation was switched to argatroban and high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to ongoing hypercoagulability, therapy was changed to fondaparinux, which led to a slow recovery in platelet counts (155×10⁹/L by POD62), and the patient’s neurological symptoms resolved. Laboratory work-up verified the presence of HIT antibodies with platelet-activating capacity independent of heparin, supporting a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Notable features of this aHIT case included severe thrombocytopenia (nadir, 13×10⁹/L), unusually early postoperative onset (prior to POD5) attributed to preoperative heparin exposure, and a distinctive laboratory antibody profile showing partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For the past seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the primary anticoagulant during heart operations requiring cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Its widespread use is attributable to several key benefits, including effective inhibition of haemostatic activation on artificial surfaces, elimination that does not rely on hepatic or renal pathways, the ability to monitor anticoagulation intraoperatively via the activated clotting time, and its affordability. Nonetheless, a significant drawback of UFH use is the potential for an immune-driven complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). HIT occurs relatively frequently after cardiac surgery, with an incidence ranging from 0.5% to 1.0% depending on the postoperative heparin regimen used—UFH compared with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)—and is particularly concerning because of its strong link to thrombosis, conferring an approximate 15-fold increased risk.

Historically, HIT has been characterized as a disorder involving pronounced platelet activation via clustering of platelet Fc receptors, a process triggered by immune complexes formed between heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies and the positively charged platelet factor 4 (PF4) when it is complexed with the polyanionic molecule heparin. In recent years, however, recognition has grown that certain patients display atypical and sometimes severe manifestations due to HIT antibodies that possess the ability to activate platelets independent of heparin; this subset is referred to as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (for review). Here, we present a case of aHIT marked by notably severe and persistent thrombocytopenia, complicated by thrombotic stroke following cardiac surgery. This case broadens current understanding of aHIT, demonstrating an exceptionally severe serological profile partially unresponsive to Fc receptor inhibition. Additionally, the report underscores the diagnostic complexities presented by thrombocytopenia occurring early in the postoperative setting, with the unusually rapid development of aHIT in this patient attributable to preoperative exposure to unfractionated heparin (UFH). The patient ultimately achieved a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male in his seventies with a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (maintained on oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily) was discovered to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization without discontinuing apixaban beforehand. As a result, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery access and administered a reduced intraprocedural dose of anticoagulation (restricted to 2000 U of unfractionated heparin). Angiography revealed 60% narrowing of the obtuse marginal branch of the coronary artery, moderate stenosis of the aortic valve, and dilation of the ascending aorta. The patient was subsequently listed for cardiac surgery one week later.

Cardiac surgery was conducted utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), and anticoagulation was maintained with 45,000 units of unfractionated heparin (UFH). The operative procedure involved aortic valve replacement using a #27 bioprosthetic valve, ascending aorta replacement with a #30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary artery bypass employing a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal coronary artery, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was completed without complications, significant hemorrhage, or requirement for blood transfusion. The patient was weaned from CPB smoothly, demonstrating stable and optimal hemodynamics.

The patient's platelet count immediately prior to surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference interval 150–400). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the platelet count had decreased to 155×10^9/L, which is considered a modest early reduction after cardiac surgery. Standard thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was initiated on POD1, with 5000 units of dalteparin administered subcutaneously, and subsequently, a higher dose of 7500 units was given on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Unexpectedly, the platelet count steadily fell from POD1 to POD5, reaching a low of 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). Other than experiencing postoperative delirium, the patient remained clinically stable. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5 and, instead, prophylactic fondaparinux at a dose of 2.5 mg was started, with the initial dose given at 18:00 on POD5.

Despite an absence of focal neurological deficits, the worsening delirium along with a further drop in platelets to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (on repeat, 24×10^9/L) prompted a CT scan of the brain. Imaging demonstrated an acute infarction, with a region of hypodensity measuring 3.2 by 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending to the cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, a haemoglobin concentration of 97 g/L (reference range 135–180 g/L), which was comparable to the haemoglobin measurements from the prior three days—aligning with anticipated early postoperative blood loss and not suggestive of ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (reference range 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) with a normal differential. Reticulocyte count was within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (reference range 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was increased at 3.19 g/L (reference range 0.32–1.97 g/L), a finding not supportive of active haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 concentration had normalized.

Coagulation panel demonstrated a prothrombin time within normal limits, reflected by an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (reference range 0.9–1.1), a normal partial thromboplastin time (PTT) measured at 34 s (reference range 25–37 s), and a markedly increased fibrinogen concentration of 6.59 g/L (reference range 2.00–3.93 g/L). This fibrinogen elevation is characteristic of the early postoperative phase, given its role as an acute phase reactant that typically rises substantially following surgery. On postoperative day 5, a blood specimen was collected and forwarded to a reference laboratory for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody assessment.

Other blood work included normal chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase and bilirubin). Blood and urine cultures were drawn. A chest X-ray showed no major abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear obtained on postoperative day 7 showed thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet aggregation, red cell fragments (schistocytes), or abnormal 'toxic' changes in white blood cells. A PF4/polyanion ELISA was performed as a screening test for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT), producing a strongly positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (reference range <0.4 OD units). However, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), which serves as the confirmatory test for platelet activation, produced an 'indeterminate' result (addressed further below).

Differential diagnosis:

Common causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock/critical illness and infection, appeared improbable based on the clinical scenario and laboratory findings (hemodynamic parameters were stable; blood and urine cultures were negative). Uncommon etiologies, such as post-transfusion purpura (as no transfusions had been administered), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (noted by normal haptoglobin levels and an initial lack of schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also considered unlikely, since the drop in platelet count developed too soon for the implicated postoperative medications—both those administered intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) and those started postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine)—to be causative.

While the decline in platelet count also developed earlier than would be expected from the UFH administered intraoperatively during CPB, further investigation revealed that UFH had been given intraprocedurally during a preoperative cardiac catheterization. In combination with the occurrence of thrombotic stroke and a strong positive result on PF4/polyanion ELISA, HIT was considered the most likely diagnosis, presumably initiated by the UFH used at the time of the preoperative catheterization. Additional information about the ‘indeterminate’ SRA was sought from the reporting laboratory, namely the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

A thorough analysis was performed on the SRA findings. In alignment with the detection of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient's serum from postoperative day 5 elicited pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; with a reference range of less than 20% release), while serotonin release was absent (0%) at a supratherapeutic UFH level of 100 U/mL. The lack of platelet activation at very high doses of heparin is typical of HIT, indicating an inability to form PF4-containing immune complexes under these conditions. Nevertheless, two additional aspects of the SRA results were identified. Firstly, significant serotonin release occurred in the absence of UFH (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; reference range less than 20%), a pattern associated with aHIT but not classic heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, when the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody IV.3 was added, there was not a complete inhibition of serotonin release; specifically, 76% serotonin release persisted at 0.1 U/mL UFH, instead of the expected suppression to below 20% commonly observed with HIT antibodies. This led to the initial classification of the SRA result as ‘indeterminate.’ Ultimately, the findings were explained by the presence of aHIT in this patient, with antibodies exhibiting unusual reactivity, resulting in only partial inhibition by IV.3 rather than the typically near-complete effect. To the best of our knowledge, such an atypical SRA response pattern has not been previously documented.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and the patient was started on argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, at a therapeutic dosage of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, targeting an aPTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Although therapeutic dosing of fondaparinux is recognized as an effective ‘off-label’ anticoagulation strategy in HIT, the hematologist overseeing care opted for argatroban due to its shorter half-life, allowing for easier management should bleeding arise, especially in light of the patient’s recent cerebrovascular event and markedly low platelet count. High-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) was also administered—100 g daily for two days initially, with a third 100 g dose added after the first two days due to persistent thrombocytopenia. Repeated measurements of fibrinogen and d-dimer were obtained to provide indirect markers of anticoagulation efficacy, as increases in fibrinogen coupled with decreases in d-dimer would suggest successful management of the HIT-related prothrombotic state.

During the initial phase of treatment, the patient experienced a continuous reduction in fibrinogen concentration between postoperative day 6 (POD6) and postoperative day 17 (POD17), reaching a lowest value of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of schistocytes from POD9 to POD16. Collectively, these findings indicated a severe and advancing consumptive coagulopathy related to HIT. In addition, after administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), the increase in platelet count was limited, rising from 13×10^9/L prior to IVIg on POD7 to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14, which represents a smaller increment than typically observed following high-dose IVIg therapy for patients with autoimmune HIT, as discussed in the literature.

On postoperative day 14, the patient's anticoagulation regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once daily. Factors influencing this decision included the simpler dosing schedule of fondaparinux, which can be given as a once-daily subcutaneous injection compared to the need for continuous intravenous infusion with partial thromboplastin time (PTT) titration when using argatroban. Another consideration was the potential for inadequate suppression of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT)-related hypercoagulability with argatroban, suggested by declining fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer values, and the development of schistocytes while on this therapy. Because fondaparinux acts as an indirect factor Xa inhibitor that depends on antithrombin (AT), a dose of AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered before starting fondaparinux, given that the patient's AT level before infusion was slightly low at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count steadily returned to within normal limits (155×10^9/L on POD62; 202×10^9/L by POD87), and the patient experienced full neurological restoration. Fondaparinux therapy was maintained up to POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at a dose of 5 mg orally twice daily for extended anticoagulation. Repeated SRA assays continued to yield strong positive results over several months, with subsequent tests demonstrating SRA findings more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The most recent blood sample, collected at POD124 after platelet normalization, produced findings compatible with predominantly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies—exhibiting 15% serotonin release without UFH, but a full 100% release when UFH at 0.3 U/mL was added.

Discussion:

The patient displayed multiple uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, all of which were eventually traced to a diagnosis of severe autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (aHIT). Firstly, his development of HIT-related thrombocytopenia occurred earlier than the typical period for HIT, which, in the context of cardiac surgery, usually falls between postoperative day (POD) 5 and POD10. Fewer than 10% of cardiac surgery patients who go on to develop HIT experience a HIT-related decrease in platelet count before POD5—a pattern associated with recent preoperative heparin administration, as observed in this case.

Secondly, our patient’s platelet count declined to an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L, concurrent with reductions in fibrinogen concentration, elevations in d-dimer, and presence of schistocytes while receiving argatroban therapy. These findings are characteristic of the pronounced hypercoagulable state associated with severe, or autoimmune, HIT.

Third, unlike the typical rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia seen after administration of high-dose intravenous Ig in patients with aHIT, our patient experienced a delayed response, indicating an especially severe manifestation of aHIT. Supporting this severity, serological testing demonstrated only partial suppression of platelet activation in vitro when using monoclonal antibody IV.3. To our knowledge, this distinct serological pattern has not previously been documented in aHIT and increased recognition may help prevent missed diagnoses, which can carry significant clinical risk. However, aHIT was ultimately diagnosed in this case based on at least three key points. First, serial analysis of subsequent serum samples—collected during the extended recovery of the platelet count—revealed some SRA reactivity patterns more typical of aHIT. Second, the final specimen analyzed exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA pattern consistent with standard HIT, indicating the decline of aHIT-related antibodies. Third, no other reasonable alternative diagnosis could account for the patient’s atypical disease trajectory. Fortunately, the patient achieved a full clinical recovery, with no lingering neurological deficits.
