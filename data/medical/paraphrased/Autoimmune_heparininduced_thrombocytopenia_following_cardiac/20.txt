Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received heparin anticoagulation during cardiac surgery; after the procedure, his platelet count progressively declined, reaching 50×10⁹/L by postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led to evaluation for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Neuroimaging revealed a parietal lobe stroke. HIT antibody screening with an immunoassay was strongly positive, and the platelet activation test (serotonin-release assay) displayed an atypical result: robust serotonin release at 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL heparin that was only partially inhibited by an Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody. His anticoagulant regimen was changed to argatroban and high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Due to ongoing hypercoagulability, therapy was transitioned to fondaparinux, which was followed by a gradual improvement in platelet count (155×10⁹/L by POD62); his neurological deficits resolved. Further laboratory studies detected HIT antibodies that activated platelets independent of heparin, confirming a diagnosis of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). This case featured not only marked thrombocytopenia (lowest platelet count, 13×10⁹/L), but also unusual features: onset early in the postoperative period (before POD5) attributable to preoperative heparin exposure, and a distinctive aHIT antibody profile showing partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For over seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has been nearly the sole anticoagulant used during heart operations requiring cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), due to its numerous benefits such as inhibition of artificial surface-associated haemostatic processes, clearance independent of renal and hepatic pathways, intraoperative monitoring using activated clotting time, and affordability. Despite these advantages, UFH is associated with a significant immune-mediated complication: heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). HIT occurs fairly frequently following cardiac procedures, with incidence rates ranging between 0.5% and 1.0%, influenced by the type and duration of postoperative heparin prophylaxis (comparing UFH to low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH)), and is distinguished by its strong correlation with thrombotic events, exhibiting an approximate 15-fold increased risk for thrombosis.

Traditionally, HIT has been characterised by pronounced platelet activation through platelet Fc receptor aggregation, caused by the interaction of immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies with heparin-bound platelet factor 4 (PF4), which forms immune complexes upon contact with polyanionic heparin. However, recent recognition has revealed that a subset of patients experience atypical, often severe, clinical manifestations attributed to HIT antibodies capable of heparin-independent platelet activation; this variant has been termed ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (see review). Herein, we present a case of aHIT distinguished by extreme and persistent thrombocytopenia, further complicated by thrombotic stroke after cardiac surgery. This case offers new perspectives regarding the breadth of aHIT, as it demonstrates an unusually severe serological profile with only partial responsiveness to Fc receptor inhibition. Furthermore, it underscores the diagnostic complexities associated with early postoperative thrombocytopenia, with the patient’s notably rapid aHIT onset attributable to previous exposure to UFH before surgery. The patient ultimately experienced a positive outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, with a two-year history of exertional shortness of breath and chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was recently noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He was scheduled for diagnostic cardiac catheterization, but his apixaban had not been discontinued beforehand. Consequently, the cardiologist opted for a transradial arterial access for the catheterization and administered a reduced dose of intraprocedural anticoagulation (only 2000 U of UFH). Angiography demonstrated 60% stenosis of the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic valve stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta, leading to the patient being booked for cardiac surgery one week later.

Cardiac surgery was carried out with the use of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), employing 45,000 U of UFH for anticoagulation. The operative interventions included replacement of the aortic valve with a size 27 bioprosthesis, ascending aorta replacement using a size 30 Gelweave graft, single coronary artery bypass grafting to the obtuse marginal artery utilizing a saphenous vein, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The procedure was completed without complications, significant bleeding, or need for blood products, and the patient was weaned from CPB without difficulty and demonstrated stable hemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately before surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400×10^9/L). On the first postoperative day (POD1), the platelet count decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicating a modest drop typically seen after cardiac procedures. Standard postoperative thromboprophylaxis with low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) was started on POD1, consisting of 5000 U dalteparin given subcutaneously; this dose was increased to 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. However, from POD1 through POD5, the platelet count continued to fall, reaching 50×10^9/L (see figure 1). The patient’s postoperative course was generally unremarkable, apart from delirium. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5, and prophylactic fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was initiated, with the first dose administered on POD5 at 18:00.

No localizing neurological deficits were present, but as postoperative delirium worsened and platelet counts decreased further to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (with a repeat value of 24×10^9/L), a CT scan of the brain was obtained. Imaging identified an acute cerebral infarct characterized by a region of hypodensity measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending to involve the cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, the complete blood count revealed a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, with haemoglobin measured at 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L), consistent with haemoglobin values documented over the prior three days and aligned with anticipated early postoperative blood loss in the absence of persistent bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), and the differential was within normal limits. Reticulocytes measured 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L), also unremarkable. Haptoglobin was elevated, reaching 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), supporting the lack of significant haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 was noted to have returned to normal levels.

Assessment of coagulation parameters demonstrated a normal prothrombin time and an international normalised ratio (INR) of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), a partial thromboplastin time (PTT) of 34 s (RR 25–37 s), and raised fibrinogen at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), in keeping with the acute phase response commonly seen early after surgery, as fibrinogen rises substantially in the initial postoperative period. A blood specimen taken on POD5 was submitted to a referral lab for HIT antibody testing.

Further laboratory results included normal values for chemistry studies (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin). Both blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography did not reveal any significant pathology.

On postoperative day 7, examination of the peripheral blood film verified thrombocytopenia (absence of platelet clumping), but no schistocytes or abnormal (‘toxic’) white cells were identified. HIT screening by PF4/polyanion ELISA showed a markedly positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units), while the subsequent platelet activation test, the serotonin-release assay (SRA), produced an ‘indeterminate’ finding, which is discussed later.

Differential diagnosis:

Established causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock/critical illness and infection, were considered improbable due to unremarkable haemodynamic status and negative blood and urine cultures. Less common etiologies, such as post-transfusion purpura (no transfusion performed), postsurgical thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (normal haptoglobin and no initial schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia, were also deemed unlikely, since the platelet decline began earlier than would be expected from recently introduced perioperative medications—including agents administered only on the surgery day (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

Although the decrease in platelet count occurred sooner than anticipated for unfractionated heparin (UFH) exposure during intraoperative cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), subsequent evaluation revealed that UFH was administered during a preoperative cardiac catheterisation procedure. In light of the occurrence of a thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was determined to be the most likely diagnosis, probably initiated by preoperative UFH exposure during heart catheterisation. Additional information about the ‘indeterminate’ serotonin-release assay (SRA) was subsequently sought from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which had conducted the test.

A thorough evaluation of the SRA findings was conducted. Indicative of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient’s serum from postoperative day 5 triggered substantial serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range <20%), and showed absence of release (0%) at supratherapeutic UFH concentrations (100 U/mL). The lack of platelet activation at elevated UFH concentrations is a hallmark of HIT, representing the inability to generate PF4-immune complexes at those levels. Nonetheless, two further observations emerged from the SRA data. Firstly, significant serotonin release was detected when UFH was absent (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; RR<20%), which typifies aHIT rather than conventional heparin-dependent HIT. Secondly, the application of the monoclonal antibody IV.3, which blocks Fc receptors, did not fully suppress the serotonin release when patient serum was present at 0.1 U/mL UFH; instead, 76% serotonin release occurred in the presence of IV.3, whereas typical HIT antibodies would reduce release to below 20%. This led to the original classification of an ‘indeterminate’ SRA result. The underlying explanation was that the patient had aHIT, with highly reactive HIT antibodies that caused only partial suppression by IV.3 compared to typical cases. As far as we are aware, this unusual SRA reaction profile has not been documented before.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosage was stopped, and argatroban—a direct thrombin inhibitor—was initiated at 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT between 55 and 85 seconds. Despite the effectiveness of fondaparinux in therapeutic doses as an ‘off-label’ anticoagulant for HIT, the consulting haematologist opted for argatroban, which possesses a shorter half-life, to reduce the risk of bleeding complications given the patient’s recent stroke and unusually severe thrombocytopenia. Additionally, high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin (Ig) was administered: 100 g on each of two consecutive days, and a third 100 g dose followed because the platelet count did not rise after the first two days of intravenous Ig therapy. Repeated assessments of fibrinogen and d-dimer were conducted to indirectly monitor anticoagulant effectiveness, with higher fibrinogen and lower d-dimer values suggesting successful anticoagulant management of HIT-associated hypercoagulable state.

During the initial phase of therapy, the patient's fibrinogen levels steadily declined between postoperative day 6 and day 17, reaching a low point of 2.49 g/L. D-dimer levels peaked around postoperative day 12, and peripheral blood smears identified schistocytes (red cell fragments) from postoperative day 9 to day 16. Collectively, these three parameters indicated the presence of severe, advancing HIT-related consumptive coagulopathy. In addition, following administration of high-dose intravenous Ig, the patient’s platelet count increased only minimally—from 13×10^9/L on postoperative day 7 prior to intravenous Ig, to a temporary peak of 54×10^9/L on postoperative day 14. This platelet response was considerably less than the rise typically observed after high-dose intravenous Ig in cases of aHIT (see review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulant regimen was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once per day. The rationale behind switching to fondaparinux included its simpler administration schedule, allowing for once-daily subcutaneous injections compared to argatroban's requirement for intravenous dosing with PTT-based adjustments. Additionally, there was apprehension that argatroban might not adequately control the hypercoagulability related to HIT, as demonstrated by decreasing fibrinogen levels, increasing d-dimer values, and the presence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect antithrombin-dependent inhibitor of factor Xa, supplemental antithrombin concentrate (1000 U) was administered prior to initiation; the antithrombin level before infusion was slightly below normal at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s platelet count slowly returned to within normal limits (155×10^9/L on POD62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87), accompanied by full neurological restoration. Fondaparinux therapy was continued through POD65, after which apixaban was reintroduced at 5 mg orally twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial SRA assays remained persistently and markedly positive for several months, with later SRA results displaying a pattern more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The final blood specimen, collected at POD124 after normalization of platelet counts, demonstrated findings indicative of chiefly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies, with 15% serotonin release when UFH was absent and 100% release when UFH was added at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited several uncommon clinical, laboratory, and serologic findings, all of which were ultimately ascribed to a diagnosis of severe aHIT. Initially, the onset of thrombocytopenia due to HIT occurred earlier than typically expected; for cardiac surgery patients, the standard timeframe for HIT emergence is between postoperative day 5 and day 10 (inclusive). In contrast, fewer than 10% of such individuals develop a platelet drop associated with HIT prior to postoperative day 5, a scenario related to recent preoperative exposure to heparin—an aspect observed in this case as well.

Secondly, this patient’s platelet count reached an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L. This was accompanied by declining fibrinogen levels, increasing d-dimer concentrations, and the presence of schistocytes on peripheral smear during treatment with argatroban. These manifestations of marked hypercoagulability associated with severe HIT are indicative of aHIT.

Thirdly, our patient failed to demonstrate the rapid improvement in platelet counts typically seen following administration of high-dose intravenous Ig in cases of aHIT, suggesting a particularly severe variant of this condition. Supporting this, serological assessment revealed only partial inhibition of in vitro platelet activation by monoclonal antibody IV.3, an unusual pattern that, to our knowledge, has not been described previously in aHIT. Recognition of this novel serological profile may be important to prevent overlooking aHIT, which could result in unfavorable outcomes. Nonetheless, the diagnosis of aHIT in this patient was substantiated for several reasons. Firstly, testing of additional sera collected during the extended phase of platelet recovery displayed SRA reactivity patterns more consistent with aHIT. Secondly, the most recent sample showed a heparin-dependent SRA response indicative of classic HIT, suggesting that aHIT antibodies were decreasing. Thirdly, alternative diagnoses failed to account for the patient's atypical clinical progression. Encouragingly, the patient achieved full recovery, with no persistent neurological deficits.
