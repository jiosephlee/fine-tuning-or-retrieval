Title: Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An older male patient received cardiac surgery with heparin used as an anticoagulant; in the postoperative period, his platelet count progressively decreased, dropping to 50×10⁹/L by the fifth day after surgery (POD5), which led clinicians to order testing for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Neuroimaging revealed a parietal lobe stroke. An initial immunoassay for HIT antibodies was strongly positive, while the functional platelet activation test (serotonin-release assay) demonstrated an unusual pattern: marked serotonin release at heparin concentrations of 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL, with only incomplete inhibition by a monoclonal antibody targeting the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulation therapy was switched to argatroban along with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Continued signs of hypercoagulability led to a subsequent change to fondaparinux, after which platelets gradually recovered (reaching 155×10⁹/L by POD62); neurological deficits resolved. Laboratory studies confirmed the presence of HIT antibodies that could activate platelets independently of heparin, consistent with autoimmune HIT (aHIT). This case featured, in addition to severe thrombocytopenia (platelet count nadir of 13×10⁹/L), the unusual occurrence of very early postoperative onset (prior to POD5) due to heparin exposure before surgery and an atypical laboratory aHIT antibody profile characterized by partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For nearly seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the primary anticoagulant used during heart operations involving cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), largely because it offers multiple benefits: effective suppression of haemostasis prompted by artificial surfaces, elimination that is independent of renal and hepatic pathways, facilitation of intraoperative anticoagulation assessment via activated clotting time, and affordability. Despite these advantages, UFH carries a significant risk for an immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). After cardiac procedures, HIT occurs relatively frequently, with a reported incidence between 0.5% and 1.0%, which depends on both the postoperative heparin regimen employed (UFH versus low-molecular-weight heparin [LMWH]) and its duration. The condition is particularly notable for its robust relationship with thrombotic events, presenting an approximate 15-fold increased relative risk for thrombosis.

Traditionally, HIT has been perceived as a disorder dominated by intense platelet activation via clustering of platelet Fc receptors, a process initiated by the creation of immune complexes composed of IgG antibodies that recognize platelet factor 4 (PF4) when associated with heparin, a polyanionic molecule. More recently, it has become clear that a subset of patients may experience unusual—and frequently more severe—manifestations due to HIT antibodies that can activate platelets independently of heparin; this variant, referred to as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT), has been reviewed in the literature. The current report presents a case of aHIT marked by pronounced, prolonged thrombocytopenia and complicated by the development of thrombotic stroke in the aftermath of cardiac surgery. This case contributes new perspectives to the understanding of aHIT by documenting an exceptionally severe serological pattern, including partial resistance to blockade of Fc receptors. Additionally, it illustrates the diagnostic complexities associated with early postoperative thrombocytopenia, attributing the patient’s unusually rapid onset of aHIT to preoperative UFH exposure. Ultimately, the patient achieved a positive clinical outcome.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his seventies, who had experienced exertional dyspnea for two years and had chronic atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was noted to have a previously unrecognized systolic murmur. He underwent diagnostic cardiac catheterization without discontinuing apixaban beforehand; as a precaution, the cardiologist opted for a transradial arterial route and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulation dose (2,000 units of UFH). Angiography revealed a 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and an enlarged ascending aorta. He was scheduled for cardiac surgery one week after these findings.

The cardiac operation utilized cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), with a total of 45,000 units of UFH given for anticoagulation. The procedure comprised replacement of the aortic valve with a size 27 bioprosthetic valve, substitution of the ascending aorta using a size 30 Gelweave graft, a single coronary bypass with a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal branch, and excision of the left atrial appendage. The surgery was completed without incident, with minimal blood loss and no requirement for transfusion, and the patient was successfully weaned from CPB with stable hemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery measured 210×10^9/L (reference range 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), this value had decreased to 155×10^9/L, indicating a mild early postoperative drop typical for cardiac surgical procedures. Standard thromboprophylaxis with LMWH was started on POD1, with a 5000 U dose of dalteparin given subcutaneously, followed by increased doses of 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. However, from POD1 through POD5, the platelet count continued to decline, reaching 50×10^9/L by POD5 (see figure 1). Aside from postoperative delirium, the patient otherwise exhibited stable clinical status. Dalteparin was withheld on POD5; prophylactic fondaparinux at 2.5 mg was initiated instead (first administered on POD5 at 18:00).

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the combination of worsening delirium and a further decrease in platelets on POD6 to 26×10^9/L (repeated value, 24×10^9/L) led to ordering a CT scan of the brain. The imaging identified an acute cerebral infarct characterized by a hypodense region measuring 3.2 cm × 1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, extending into the cerebral cortex.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5, a complete blood count revealed a platelet count of 50×10^9/L and a haemoglobin value of 97 g/L (reference range [RR]: 135–180 g/L), which was consistent with haemoglobin measurements from the preceding three days and aligned with expected early postoperative blood loss without evidence of continued bleeding. The white blood cell count was 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L) with a normal differential. Reticulocytes were within the normal range at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was elevated to 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), indicative of no significant haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 level was within the normal limits.

Coagulation parameters included a normal prothrombin time and international normalized ratio of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), and a partial thromboplastin time of 34 s (RR 25–37 s). Fibrinogen concentration was elevated at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), reflecting the acute-phase response commonly seen in the early postoperative period. A sample collected on postoperative day 5 was sent for evaluation of HIT antibodies at a reference laboratory.

Additional laboratory evaluation demonstrated normal renal function, liver transaminases, and bilirubin. Blood and urine cultures were obtained. Chest radiography did not reveal any significant abnormalities.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear on postoperative day 7 confirmed thrombocytopenia, without platelet clumping, red cell fragments (schistocytes), or toxic changes in white cells. The initial screening immunoassay for HIT (PF4/polyanion ELISA) was markedly positive at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units). However, the subsequent serotonin-release assay (SRA), used to confirm platelet activation, yielded an 'indeterminate' result, which is discussed further below.

Differential diagnosis:

Frequent causes of marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, including cardiogenic shock, critical illness, or infection, appeared improbable in this case given stable haemodynamics and negative results for blood and urine cultures. Uncommon etiologies, such as post-transfusion purpura (no history of transfusion), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (normal haptoglobin, absence of schistocytes initially), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia were also unlikely, as the timing of thrombocytopenia onset did not correspond with exposure to any newly initiated postoperative medications—including agents administered intraoperatively (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) and those started postoperatively (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, and midodrine).

Despite the platelet decrease occurring sooner than would be expected from intraoperative UFH administered during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), subsequent review revealed that unfractionated heparin had been used earlier during preoperative cardiac catheterization. Considering the development of a thrombotic stroke and a strongly positive PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) was considered the most plausible explanation, most likely precipitated by UFH exposure during the catheterization procedure. Additional clarification about the ‘indeterminate’ SRA result was sought from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory.

The SRA findings were thoroughly examined. Indicative of platelet-activating HIT antibodies, the patient's serum obtained on postoperative day 5 (POD5) demonstrated marked serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL unfractionated heparin (UFH) concentrations (97% and 99%, respectively; reference range [RR] <20% release). Additionally, there was an absence of serotonin release (0%) at a supratherapeutic UFH level (100 U/mL). This lack of platelet activation at elevated heparin concentrations is a hallmark of HIT and results from the inability to generate immune complexes containing PF4 at such high UFH doses.

Two other notable aspects of the SRA were observed. Firstly, strong serotonin release occurred with the patient’s serum even without any UFH present (96% at 0 U/mL heparin; RR <20%), a finding typically seen in autoimmune HIT (aHIT), rather than the conventional heparin-dependent form of HIT. Secondly, when the monoclonal antibody IV.3, which blocks Fc receptors, was added, it did not fully suppress the serotonin release detected with patient serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH: a 76% release was measured in the presence of IV.3, instead of the expected reduction to less than 20%—a result commonly observed with HIT antibodies. This led to the initial classification of the SRA result as ‘indeterminate.’ The subsequent interpretation was that the patient did, in fact, have aHIT, but their HIT antibodies were unusually potent, resulting in only partial suppression by IV.3, rather than the typical complete inhibition. To date, a similar atypical SRA reaction profile has not been documented in the literature.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at prophylactic dosage was stopped, and argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, was initiated at a therapeutic dose of 0.75 mcg/kg/min, aiming for a PTT range between 55 and 85 s. Though fondaparinux in therapeutic amounts serves as an effective 'off-label' option for managing HIT, the hematologist overseeing care selected argatroban due to its shorter half-life, allowing for quicker reversal if hemorrhage occurred, considering the patient's recent stroke and notably profound thrombocytopenia. Additionally, intravenous immunoglobulin was administered at high doses, starting with 100 g daily over two days, then a third dose of 100 g was given since the platelet count did not improve after the initial 48 hours of IVIG. Serial assessments of fibrinogen and d-dimer levels were conducted as surrogate markers for anticoagulation efficacy, with increased fibrinogen and decreased d-dimer suggestive of successful management of HIT-related hypercoagulability.

The patient's early management was marked by a gradual reduction in fibrinogen levels, starting at postoperative day 6 and reaching a minimum of 2.49 g/L by day 17. D-dimer values peaked around postoperative day 12, while peripheral blood smear analysis between days 9 and 16 revealed the presence of schistocytes. Collectively, these observations indicated a severe and progressive consumptive coagulopathy associated with HIT. Additionally, despite administration of high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin, the platelet count exhibited only a slight increase—from 13×10^9/L at postoperative day 7 to a transient maximum of 54×10^9/L on day 14—which represents a less substantial platelet recovery than typically expected in patients with aHIT receiving high-dose intravenous Ig (for review).

On postoperative day 14, the patient’s anticoagulation therapy was changed from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg once daily administered subcutaneously. Factors influencing the decision included fondaparinux's simpler administration regimen—once-daily subcutaneous injection as opposed to argatroban’s continuous intravenous infusion adjusted by PTT—as well as concerns that argatroban might not have been adequately controlling the hypercoagulability associated with HIT, suggested by decreasing fibrinogen and increasing d-dimer values along with the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban treatment. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect factor Xa inhibitor dependent on antithrombin, AT concentrate (1000 U) was administered before initiating fondaparinux; the antithrombin level prior to infusion was slightly below normal at 0.70 U/mL (reference range 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The platelet count slowly returned to normal levels, measuring 155×10^9/L on postoperative day (POD) 62 and 202×10^9/L on POD87; the patient also achieved full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was continued until POD65, after which apixaban was restarted at an oral dose of 5 mg twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Serial serotonin release assay (SRA) tests remained markedly positive over several months, with subsequent SRAs displaying a profile more typical of autoimmune HIT (aHIT) (see figure 2). The final blood sample collected on POD124, after normalization of the platelet count, demonstrated findings in line with primarily heparin-dependent HIT antibodies—showing 15% serotonin release in the absence of unfractionated heparin (UFH) and 100% release in the presence of UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

This patient exhibited several atypical clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics, all of which were ultimately due to a diagnosis of severe autoimmune HIT (aHIT). Firstly, the development of HIT-related thrombocytopenia in this case occurred prior to the standard window for HIT, which generally ranges from postoperative day (POD) 5 to POD10 in individuals following cardiac surgery. Only approximately 10% of cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a decrease in platelet count before POD5, a pattern often associated with recent preoperative heparin administration, which was evident in our patient.

Secondly, the nadir of this patient’s platelet count reached an exceptionally low value of 13×109/L, accompanied by declining fibrinogen levels, elevated d-dimer, and schistocytes during treatment with argatroban. These manifestations of profound hypercoagulability related to HIT signify the presence of aHIT.

Third, unlike the typical rapid resolution of thrombocytopenia observed in patients with aHIT treated with high-dose intravenous Ig, our patient experienced a notably delayed improvement, suggesting a particularly severe manifestation of this condition. Supporting this, serologic testing indicated a severe aHIT subtype, as only partial inhibition of platelet activation was observed in vitro when monoclonal antibody IV.3 was present; to our knowledge, this distinct serological pattern has not previously been described in the context of aHIT. Recognizing this unique profile may help prevent misdiagnosis and consequent negative outcomes. Despite these atypical findings, aHIT was eventually confirmed in our case for several reasons. First, serum samples obtained later during the patient’s protracted platelet count recovery displayed SRA reactivity patterns more typical of aHIT. Second, the final sample exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA profile consistent with conventional HIT, indicating a decline in aHIT antibody levels. Third, no alternative diagnosis adequately accounted for the patient’s unusual clinical trajectory. Ultimately, the patient achieved a full clinical recovery without persistent neurologic deficits.
