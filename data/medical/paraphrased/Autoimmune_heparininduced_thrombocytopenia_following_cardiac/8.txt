Autoimmune heparin-induced thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery

Rosanne St Bernard, Ishac Nazy, Theodore Earl Warkentin

Abstract:

Summary: An elderly patient underwent cardiac surgery with heparin used for anticoagulation. After surgery, his platelet count consistently decreased, dropping to 50×10⁹/L on postoperative day 5 (POD5), which led clinicians to test for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibodies. Imaging of the brain identified a parietal stroke. Immunoassay screening for HIT antibodies produced a strongly positive outcome, while the serotonin-release assay—a platelet activation test—revealed an unusual pattern: marked serotonin release at heparin concentrations of 0, 0.1, and 0.3 U/mL, with only partial inhibition by a monoclonal antibody that blocks the platelet Fc receptor. Anticoagulation therapy was switched to argatroban, in combination with high-dose intravenous immunoglobulin. Since the patient remained hypercoagulable, fondaparinux was introduced, which correlated with a gradual normalization of the platelet count (155×10⁹/L by POD62), alongside recovery from neurological symptoms. Further laboratory workup identified HIT antibodies capable of activating platelets without heparin, characteristic of autoimmune HIT (aHIT). In addition to marked thrombocytopenia (nadir platelet count 13×10⁹/L), distinctive features of this aHIT case were its rapid postoperative emergence (before POD5) due to prior heparin exposure, and a distinctive laboratory antibody signature that showed partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition.

Background:

For the past seven decades, unfractionated heparin (UFH) has served as the principal intraoperative anticoagulant during heart surgeries utilizing cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), owing to numerous benefits. These include its capability to inhibit haemostatic processes initiated by artificial surfaces, clearance mechanisms that do not rely on renal or hepatic pathways, the feasibility of monitoring intraoperative anticoagulation through activated clotting time, as well as its affordability. Nevertheless, UFH is associated with a significant immune-mediated complication known as heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT). HIT occurs with notable frequency following cardiac surgery—approximately 0.5%–1.0%, contingent upon the length and choice of postoperative heparin-based thromboprophylaxis (comparing UFH to low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH))—and is distinguished by a markedly increased risk of thrombosis, reflected in a relative risk of nearly 15.

Traditionally, HIT has been conceptualized as a condition driven by potent platelet activation due to clustering of platelet Fc receptors. This results from immune complexes formed by heparin-dependent immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies targeting the cationic protein platelet factor 4 (PF4) when it is complexed with the polyanion heparin. More recently, however, it has become evident that certain patients experience atypical and sometimes extremely severe disease courses related to HIT antibodies that can activate platelets independent of heparin presence; this specific subtype is now referred to as ‘autoimmune HIT’ (aHIT) (for review). Here, we present a case of aHIT characterized by exceptionally profound and persistent thrombocytopenia that was further complicated by thrombotic stroke in the aftermath of cardiac surgery. This case expands current understanding of the aHIT spectrum by illustrating a particularly severe serological presentation, which included partial resistance to Fc receptor inhibition. It also underscores the complexities in diagnosing thrombocytopenia soon after surgery, as this patient’s unusually prompt manifestation of aHIT was attributable to UFH exposure prior to the operation. Ultimately, the patient’s clinical course resolved positively.

Case presentation:

A man in his seventies, who had experienced exertional dyspnoea for two years and had a longstanding diagnosis of atrial fibrillation (managed with oral apixaban 5 mg twice daily), was recently identified to have an unrecognized systolic murmur. The patient underwent diagnostic heart catheterisation, during which apixaban therapy had not been discontinued prior to the procedure. Consequently, the cardiologist opted for a transradial artery access and administered a reduced intraprocedural anticoagulant dose (2000 U of UFH). Angiographic assessment revealed 60% stenosis in the obtuse marginal coronary artery, moderate aortic stenosis, and a dilated ascending aorta. The patient was subsequently scheduled for cardiac surgery one week afterwards.

Cardiac surgery was carried out with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), using 45 000 U of UFH to maintain anticoagulation. Surgical procedures included aortic valve replacement utilizing a #27 bioprosthesis, replacement of the ascending aorta with a #30 Gelweave graft, single coronary artery bypass grafting with a saphenous vein graft to the obtuse marginal artery, as well as excision of the left atrial appendage. The operation was uneventful, with no significant blood loss or transfusion requirement, and the patient was weaned from CPB smoothly with stable haemodynamics.

The platelet count immediately prior to surgery was 210×10^9/L (reference range (RR) 150–400). On postoperative day 1 (POD1), it decreased to 155×10^9/L, which is considered a modest initial drop in platelet count for patients undergoing cardiac surgery. Standard thromboprophylaxis with LMWH was initiated on POD1, beginning with 5000 U dalteparin administered subcutaneously, followed by an increased dose of 7500 U on POD2, POD3, and POD4. Contrary to expectations, the platelet count continued to fall, reaching 50×10^9/L by POD5 (see figure 1). Apart from experiencing postoperative delirium, the patient’s clinical status remained stable. On POD5, dalteparin was withheld and prophylactic-dose fondaparinux (2.5 mg) was started, with the first administration at 18:00.

Despite the absence of focal neurological deficits, the progression of postoperative delirium and a further drop in the platelet count to 26×10^9/L on POD6 (with a repeat value of 24×10^9/L) led to the decision to perform CT brain imaging. The scan showed a recent cerebral infarction, characterized by a hypodense area measuring 3.2 cm×1.8 cm in the left parietal lobe, with involvement of the cortical region.

Investigations:

On postoperative day 5 (POD5), the full blood count indicated a platelet level of 50×10^9/L, while the haemoglobin concentration was measured at 97 g/L (reference range [RR] 135–180 g/L). This haemoglobin value was comparable to those from the three preceding days, reflecting anticipated early postoperative blood loss rather than ongoing hemorrhage. The white blood cell count stood at 7.8×10^9/L (RR 4.0–11.0×10^9/L), with a normal differential. Reticulocyte levels were within normal limits at 73×10^9/L (RR 10–100×10^9/L). Haptoglobin was raised to 3.19 g/L (RR 0.32–1.97 g/L), suggesting no evidence of significant haemolysis. The serum vitamin B12 concentration had normalized.

Coagulation profiles were within normal ranges: prothrombin time was normal, corresponding to an international normalized ratio of 1.1 (RR 0.9–1.1), and partial thromboplastin time was 34 s (RR 25–37 s). Fibrinogen was substantially elevated at 6.59 g/L (RR 2.00–3.93 g/L), which aligns with the typical postoperative rise seen as an acute phase reactant. On POD5, a blood sample was submitted to a reference center for heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) antibody testing.

Additional laboratory findings revealed normal results for serum chemistry (urea, creatinine, alanine transaminase, and bilirubin). Both blood and urine cultures were collected. Imaging with chest X-ray did not reveal any significant pathology.

Examination of the peripheral blood smear from POD7 confirmed thrombocytopenia, with no evidence of platelet clumping, red cell fragmentation (schistocytes), or atypical white cell morphology. Screening for HIT using the PF4/polyanion ELISA demonstrated a markedly positive result at 2.2 optical density (OD) units (RR <0.4 OD units), whereas the confirmatory serotonin-release assay (SRA) returned an ‘indeterminate’ result (elaborated on in a later section).

Differential diagnosis:

Conventional causes for marked thrombocytopenia following cardiac surgery, such as cardiogenic shock or critical illness and infection, appeared improbable in view of the patient’s stable haemodynamics and negative results from blood and urine cultures. Similarly, less common etiologies—including post-transfusion purpura (in the absence of any transfusion), postoperative thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (evidenced by normal haptoglobin levels and an initial lack of schistocytes), and drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia—were discounted since the temporal profile of platelet count decline did not coincide with the administration of new medications, whether those administered exclusively on the day of surgery (vancomycin, cefazolin, tranexamic acid) or those introduced in the postoperative period (furosemide, metoprolol, pantoprazole, midodrine).

Despite the platelet decline presenting too rapidly to be attributable to the intraoperative administration of UFH during cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), further history revealed that the patient received UFH during a preoperative cardiac catheterisation. Coupled with the occurrence of a thrombotic stroke and a strong positive result on the PF4/polyanion ELISA, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) emerged as the most likely diagnosis, probably precipitated by the UFH used in the preoperative catheterisation procedure. Additional information about the 'indeterminate' SRA was subsequently sought from the McMaster Platelet Immunology Laboratory, which had issued the report.

A critical evaluation of the SRA findings was undertaken. Supporting the diagnosis of HIT with platelet-activating antibodies, the patient's serum from POD5 induced pronounced serotonin release at both 0.1 and 0.3 U/mL UFH (97% and 99%, respectively; RR<20% release), while serotonin release was absent (0%) at a supratherapeutic UFH concentration (100 U/mL). This absence of platelet activation at markedly elevated heparin levels is indicative of HIT and underscores the disruption of PF4-containing immune complex formation when UFH concentrations are excessively high. Notably, two additional observations were made from the SRA data. First, in the complete absence of UFH (0 U/mL), the patient’s serum still elicited substantial serotonin release (96%; RR<20%), a finding that aligns more closely with aHIT than with classical heparin-dependent HIT. Second, the introduction of the Fc receptor-blocking monoclonal antibody (IV.3) did not fully suppress the serotonin release triggered by the patient’s serum at 0.1 U/mL UFH; serotonin release remained high at 76%, far exceeding the usual inhibition threshold of less than 20% expected with typical HIT antibodies, resulting initially in an 'indeterminate' SRA interpretation. The underlying reason was that although the patient did have aHIT, the HIT antibodies displayed unusual reactivity, such that the typical degree of suppression by IV.3 was only partial. As far as can be ascertained, this atypical SRA pattern has not been previously documented in the literature.

Treatment:

Fondaparinux at a prophylactic dose was stopped, and the patient was started on therapeutic-dose argatroban, a direct thrombin inhibitor, administered at 0.75 mcg/kg/min, with an intended PTT range of 55–85 s. While therapeutic dosing of fondaparinux can serve as an effective ‘off-label’ anticoagulant option for HIT, the managing haematologist chose argatroban due to its shorter half-life, considering the recent onset of stroke and the patient’s markedly severe thrombocytopenia, as a precaution against potential bleeding events. Additionally, high-dose intravenous Ig therapy was initiated, consisting of 100 g administered on two consecutive days, followed by a further 100 g dose after two days without an observed platelet count increase. To monitor the response to anticoagulation, fibrinogen and d-dimer levels were measured serially, with the expectation that increasing fibrinogen and decreasing d-dimer concentrations would suggest adequate anticoagulant suppression of HIT-associated hypercoagulability.

During the initial phase of treatment, there was a marked and progressive reduction in fibrinogen concentrations observed between POD6 and POD17, reaching a lowest level of 2.49 g/L. Concurrently, d-dimer values peaked around POD12, and peripheral blood smears revealed the presence of red cell fragments (schistocytes) from POD9 through POD16. Collectively, these laboratory abnormalities indicated a severe and worsening HIT-associated consumptive coagulopathy. In addition, after administration of high-dose intravenous Ig, the patient’s platelet count increased only modestly—from a baseline of 13×10^9/L on POD7 to a temporary maximum of 54×10^9/L on POD14. This increase was significantly less than the typical platelet response observed following high-dose intravenous Ig in cases of aHIT (see review).

On postoperative day 14 (POD14), the patient's anticoagulant therapy was transitioned from argatroban to fondaparinux at a therapeutic dose of 10 mg administered subcutaneously once per day. This decision was influenced by the convenience of fondaparinux, given its once-daily subcutaneous administration in contrast to the continuous intravenous infusion and PTT-based titration required for argatroban. Additionally, there were concerns that argatroban might not be adequately mitigating the hypercoagulability associated with HIT, as evidenced by decreasing fibrinogen levels, increasing d-dimer values, and the emergence of schistocytes during argatroban therapy. Since fondaparinux acts as an indirect inhibitor of factor Xa through antithrombin (AT) dependency, 1000 units of AT concentrate were administered prior to initiating fondaparinux; the patient's AT activity before the infusion was modestly below the reference range at 0.70 U/mL (RR 0.80–1.40 U/mL).

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's platelet count progressively returned to the normal range (155×10^9/L at POD62 and 202×10^9/L at POD87), accompanied by full neurological recovery. Fondaparinux therapy was continued up to POD65, after which apixaban was reintroduced at an oral dose of 5 mg twice daily for ongoing anticoagulation. Repeated SRA tests remained strongly positive over several months, and later SRA patterns exhibited features more typical of aHIT (see figure 2). The most recent sample, collected at POD124 after normalization of the platelet count, demonstrated results indicative of mainly heparin-dependent HIT antibodies: serotonin release was 15% without UFH, but reached 100% in the presence of UFH at 0.3 U/mL.

Discussion:

The patient exhibited a range of atypical clinical, laboratory, and serological characteristics that were all ultimately explained by a diagnosis of severe aHIT. To begin with, the onset of thrombocytopenia linked to HIT occurred earlier than the typical window, which for individuals undergoing cardiac surgery generally falls between POD5 and POD10 (inclusive). In fact, only around 10% of post-cardiac surgery patients who develop HIT experience a drop in platelet count before POD5—a scenario that has been associated with recent preoperative exposure to heparin, a situation also confirmed in this case.

Furthermore, the patient's platelet count reached an exceptionally low nadir of 13×10^9/L and this was accompanied by decreasing fibrinogen, elevated d-dimer concentrations, and evidence of schistocytosis during anticoagulation with argatroban. These findings are consistent with the pronounced hypercoagulable state observed in severe HIT, serving as markers suggestive of aHIT.

Thirdly, our patient did not demonstrate the rapid improvement in thrombocytopenia that is commonly seen in individuals with aHIT treated with high-dose intravenous Ig; this suggests the presence of an especially severe variant of the disorder. In fact, serological assessment indicated a particularly severe form of aHIT, as evidenced by only partial inhibition of platelet activation in vitro in the presence of the monoclonal antibody IV.3. To the best of our knowledge, this distinct serological pattern has not previously been described in association with aHIT. Recognition of this finding may help prevent underdiagnosis of aHIT, which could otherwise lead to unfavorable clinical outcomes. Despite these challenges, a diagnosis of aHIT in our patient was established on the basis of at least three key points. First, subsequent sera collected during the extended course of platelet recovery displayed some SRA reactivity profiles aligning with those typical of aHIT. Second, the final tested sample exhibited a heparin-dependent SRA response consistent with classical HIT, indicating that the aHIT antibody response was diminishing. Third, alternative explanations for the patient's atypical clinical presentation were lacking. Ultimately, the patient experienced a full clinical recovery and remained free of lasting neurological deficits.
