Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Overview: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic form of diabetes inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. This case report documents a patient with HNF1A-MODY caused by a newly identified heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) gene. The individual was found to have elevated blood glucose and glycosuria, while diabetic ketoacidosis was absent. The patient's family history revealed instances of diabetes developing at an early age. Genetic analysis identified a pathogenic HNF1A variant not previously reported. Initiation of therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist along with metformin lowered the patient's glycated haemoglobin levels from 10.2% to 6.0% over a three-month period. This case underscores the value of genetic testing in individuals with early-onset diabetes, which is commonly mistaken for type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and broadens the catalogue of HNF1A mutations pertinent to precision medicine.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetes conditions caused by an intrinsic defect in the formation and activity of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders commonly present, and are diagnosed, in individuals younger than 25 years. MODY accounts for roughly 1%–6% of diabetes cases with early onset. The frequency of MODY may differ among distinct ethnic and racial populations, and its prevalence is possibly underreported. Existing investigations have primarily focused on White European cohorts, though newer research has evaluated MODY occurrence in Asian and Australian populations. Reported prevalence rates for MODY vary widely, which is largely attributed to the fact that genetic testing is frequently restricted to those with particular clinical features, even in population-based studies.

More than 15 genes have been implicated in the monogenic form of MODY, with each gene mutation associated with distinct treatment approaches. Most cases across various MODY subtypes are predominantly caused by mutations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta.

HNF1A-MODY is the most common variant, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses, and displays a pronounced autosomal dominant pattern of symptomatic diabetes within families. During early adolescence, individuals may not display symptoms, and hyperglycemia is often discovered incidentally during routine blood assessments, usually without signs of insulin resistance. Measurement of postprandial glucose or performing a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal marked elevation of glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also occur, reflecting a lowered renal threshold for glucose due to impaired glucose handling in the renal tubules. In advanced disease stages, classic symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight loss, akin to those found in type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D), may develop. As a result, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D remains challenging, given the similarity in clinical presentations.

Several diagnostic strategies for MODY have been described in the literature. Following a physical exam and collection of family history indicating MODY phenotype, and confirmation of negative autoantibody status for type 1 diabetes—specifically for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—in addition to pertinent biochemical evaluations, molecular genetic analysis assumes a central importance in confirming diagnosis. Accurate identification of MODY subtypes is critical for selecting appropriate therapies, predicting risk of complications, evaluating long-term outlook, and providing family guidance. Recent research has reported hundreds of distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene related to HNF1A-MODY.

In this report, genetic analysis performed using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel revealed a previously unidentified heterozygous nucleotide change, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY. Pathogenicity assessment was conducted according to the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) variant classification guidelines. This particular variant had not been documented in prior studies, nor was it found in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. Written informed consent for publication was obtained directly from the patient.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment of elevated blood glucose and the presence of glucose in urine detected at his routine yearly checkup. His physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, blood pressure measuring 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse rate of 78 bpm. No signs of acanthosis nigricans were seen on examination. The patient's family history was significant for diabetes appearing at a young age; specifically, his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. None of these relatives with diabetes had any documented history of early-onset cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

His glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting plasma glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. At 2 hours following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), his glucose was 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described increased thirst, enhanced appetite, and unintentional recent weight reduction. Lipid assessment showed a high-density lipoprotein of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings consistent with a possible metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). There was no notable history of past medical or surgical conditions.

A glucagon stimulation test demonstrated a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L after 6 minutes, reflecting decreased insulin production. This test assesses remaining β-cell function by measuring stimulated C-peptide response to glucagon. Typically, a stimulated C-peptide greater than 0.60 nmol/L reflects ongoing endogenous insulin release, while lower values point to β-cell impairment. Nonetheless, this test serves as an adjunct rather than a diagnostic gold standard, particularly when islet autoantibodies are negative or the phenotype is unclear. Microalbuminuria screening was negative. Evaluation of sudomotor function using electrochemical skin conductance (such as Sudoscan) revealed a marked reduction in the hands, indicating significant autonomic involvement. Testing for GADA and IA-2A antibodies returned negative. A genetic test for MODY, conducted one month after diagnosis, identified a heterozygous pathogenic HNF1A variant (p.N270S; HNF1A:c.809A>G), establishing a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose measurements. The initial therapeutic approach comprised basal insulin degludec at eight units daily, together with metformin at a dose of 1500 mg/day and saxagliptin at 5 mg/day, reflecting an early suspicion of T2D. This regimen was intended to swiftly stabilize blood glucose while targeting probable insulin resistance.

Alongside medications, the patient was counseled to follow a regulated dietary regimen and maintain consistent physical activity, forming part of an integrated diabetes management plan. Follow-up assessments took place in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to track glycaemic status and evaluate response to therapy.

Despite moderate yet suboptimal β-cell function demonstrated on a glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a significant family history of early-onset diabetes, suspicion for MODY remained elevated.

Treatment was initiated before genetic testing outcomes became available, relying on the patient's clinical features and diagnostic laboratory results at presentation.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month later, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. After confirming the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY through genetic testing and taking into account the patient’s moderately preserved but insufficient β-cell activity—as well as the improved HbA1c—insulin was stopped and dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) was started to aid in glycaemic regulation and support pancreatic function. At the 6-month mark, HbA1c had further decreased to 6.0%, reflecting marked improvement. With glycaemic control achieved, the treatment plan was transitioned to a simplified regimen of metformin (1500 mg daily) plus saxagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg daily) for long-term maintenance. The patient is consistently monitored, and HbA1c continues to remain below 6.5%. Thus far, there is no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and vigilance for chronic complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, a transcription factor situated at chromosome 12q24, contains 10 exons extending over 23,790 base pairs. This gene produces a protein consisting of 631 amino acids that is found in several organs, such as the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Mutations in HNF1A result in a gradual loss of β-cell function, demonstrated by decreased expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes, and diminished pyruvate kinase activity. The ultimate consequence is lower insulin release and impaired glucose tolerance.

In this case, the diagnosis process followed established MODY criteria, which include clinical, biochemical, and genetic indicators: onset of diabetes at a young age, a notable family history, postprandial hyperglycemia, and absence of diabetes-related autoantibodies. Maintenance of fasting C-peptide above 0.6 ng/mL supports a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes. Nevertheless, repeated measurement of C-peptide is important, as values may remain within the normal range in early type 1 diabetes. A major challenge in recognizing MODY is limited physician awareness and diagnostic suspicion, leading to frequent misclassification as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The Exeter MODY probability calculator offers clinicians a tool to select patients for genetic testing, though it was validated using White European data and may overstate MODY likelihood in other ethnic groups. Inputting our patient's information into this calculator yielded an estimated 62.4% likelihood (equivalent to a 1 in 1.6 chance) of MODY. While most MODY patients are negative for autoantibodies, case reports have documented autoantibody positivity in some individuals with HNF1A variants. Earlier data indicate lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome in MODY compared to type 2 diabetes. However, as obesity prevalence grows, BMI and metabolic syndrome are becoming less helpful in differentiating between MODY and T2DM, which may partly account for the elevated BMI seen in this patient. Furthermore, although family history is valuable in assessing for MODY, its reliability is hampered by factors such as inaccurate reporting, occurrence of de novo mutations, and high rates of type 2 diabetes within families.

Therapeutic decisions for HNF1A-MODY are based on the patient's age and HbA1c measurements. Initial management typically consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release by targeting potassium-sensitive ATP channels. As β-cell capacity diminishes in HNF1A-MODY and the risk of hypoglycaemia increases, GLP-1 receptor agonists may provide a safer and more efficacious option than sulfonylureas. Evidence from a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study indicates that liraglutide and glimepiride comparably lower fasting and postprandial glucose in this population, without statistically significant differences in efficacy. Importantly, the incidence of mild hypoglycaemia is reduced with GLP-1 receptor agonists relative to glimepiride. In our case, genetic confirmation allowed for cessation of insulin therapy once glycaemic targets were met, with subsequent transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist regimen, resulting in sustained glycaemic control. This case underscores the essential role of genetic testing in informing individualized therapy. Glycosuria observed in HNF1A-MODY results from diminished SGLT2 expression, as SGLT2 is transcriptionally regulated by HNF1A. Literature has described instances of pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis in HNF1A-MODY patients related to excessive inhibition of remaining SGLT-2 activity. Consequently, the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors in this subgroup may present heightened risks—including dehydration, vascular instability, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—that could surpass their therapeutic advantages. In the present case, treatment with liraglutide in combination with metformin proved to be both effective and well-tolerated, in alignment with prior reports.

Precision medicine plays a pivotal role in MODY management, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY may not consistently meet established diagnostic criteria despite adherence to clinical guidelines. At present, no reliable biomarkers exist to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from either type 1 diabetes or T2D. Chen et al conducted a study involving 2429 young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D and found a MODY prevalence of 1.5%, with 0.74% carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. In another investigation, Møller et al determined that among Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes in a large population-based cohort, 10% of the 39 patients lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes harbored HNF1A mutations. A recent Russian study also screened 506 subjects suspected of hereditary diabetes without autoimmune features—defined by negative results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at onset, preserved C-peptide, low insulin needs within five years, and absence of severe obesity. Among these, 46.4% were found to carry pathogenic or LP variants, with HNF1A being frequently implicated. The confirmation of MODY diagnosis requires DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables simultaneous analysis of extensive patient DNA, making MODY screening more practical. The advent of NGS has facilitated the discovery of numerous novel genetic variants. Guidelines by the ACMG are used to assess the pathogenicity of these variants. Databases such as ClinVar contain records of over 1000 unique HNF1A variants, spanning pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In the present case, the novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not listed in ClinVar or other standard variant databases, confirming its novelty rather than calling its clinical significance into question. Notably, the variant alters an amino acid adjacent to established pathogenic residues, possesses a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and demonstrates familial segregation with diabetes, all fulfilling ACMG guidelines for a likely pathogenic classification. Therefore, although ClinVar is invaluable for documenting known variants, multiple independent genetic and clinical findings beyond these databases underpin the pathogenic nature of this variant.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, whereas mutations are less frequent in the transactivation domain. The novel variant identified in this case is also located within the DNA-binding domain. Certain studies indicate that HNF1A mutations, such as P, exert minimal influence on the functionality of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, some HNF1A variants that are not sufficient to cause MODY independently may nonetheless contribute to a greater risk of developing T2D.

To summarize, this case underscores the significance of precise diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY and its role in guiding tailored treatment approaches. Ongoing research will aim to establish enhanced NGS screening methods to raise MODY detection rates, resulting in improved cost-effectiveness and clinical outcomes.
