Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a monogenic, autosomal dominant subtype of diabetes. This case report details a patient with hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A)-MODY resulting from a newly identified heterozygous mutation in HNF1A. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. Notably, the family history included several cases of early-onset diabetes. Molecular analysis revealed a pathogenic HNF1A variant that has not been previously described. Initiation of therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist in conjunction with metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a three-month period. This case underscores the value of conducting genetic testing in patients with diabetes manifesting at a young age—often incorrectly classified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and broadens the known spectrum of HNF1A mutations pertinent to tailored medical interventions.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes characterized by an inherent impairment in pancreatic β-cell formation and activity. Presentation and diagnosis generally occur before 25 years of age. MODY accounts for around 1% to 6% of cases of diabetes presenting in young individuals. The frequency of MODY differs among various ethnic and racial populations, and actual rates may be underreported. Historically, most investigations have focused on populations of White European descent. More recently, the frequency of MODY has been assessed in Asian and Australian cohorts. Reported rates of MODY show substantial variability, largely due to the fact that genetic testing is primarily offered to subjects selected for specific clinical features, even when conducted within population-based cohorts.

More than 15 genes harboring relevant mutations have been implicated in the monogenic MODY phenotype, with each genetic subtype necessitating distinct treatment approaches. The bulk of documented MODY cases are primarily attributed to mutations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β.

Of these, HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent form, accounting for roughly 30%–65% of MODY diagnoses, and is characterized by a clear autosomal dominant pattern of symptomatic diabetes transmission within affected families. In the early teen years, clinical manifestations often remain absent, and hyperglycemia is frequently discovered incidentally during routine screening, typically without evidence of insulin resistance. Measurement of postprandial blood glucose or administration of a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal substantial elevations in plasma glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Furthermore, glycosuria can occur, which is related to a lowered renal threshold for glucose due to impaired glucose reabsorption in the renal tubules. As the disease advances, symptoms like increased thirst, frequent urination, excessive hunger, and unintended weight loss—similar to those found in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes—can present. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes poses a diagnostic challenge, given their overlapping clinical characteristics.

At present, multiple diagnostic pathways for MODY have been outlined in the literature. When clinical examination and evaluation of family history point toward a likely MODY phenotype, and type 1 diabetes-associated autoantibodies—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 antibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, supplemented by additional relevant biochemical assessments, molecular genetic analysis plays a vital role in establishing the diagnosis. Accurate identification is essential to guide therapeutic strategies, anticipate the risk of complications, inform prognosis, and support family counseling. Recent research indicates that numerous distinct mutations within the HNF1A gene contribute to HNF1A-MODY.

In the patient discussed in this report, a previously undescribed heterozygous change, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), was detected in exon 4 of HNF1A through next-generation sequencing (NGS) gene panel testing, and its pathogenic potential was assessed following the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria for variant classification. This particular variant was not documented in the literature, nor found in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for publication of this case.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment after elevated blood glucose and urinary glucose were detected during his routine annual checkup. Upon physical evaluation, his BMI measured 24.6 kg/m^2, blood pressure was 112/70 mmHg, and pulse rate was 78 bpm. No evidence of acanthosis nigricans was found on examination. The family history revealed several relatives with early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother developed the condition in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. There was no reported history of premature cardiovascular disease among the family members with diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. Following administration of a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), his 2-hour glucose was 19.8 mmol/L. No evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis was present. The patient described experiencing polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent weight reduction. Lipid panel showed a high-density lipoprotein cholesterol of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings suggestive of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He denied any significant medical or surgical antecedents.

A glucagon stimulation test revealed a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, reflecting inadequate insulin production. This assessment measures residual β-cell activity by quantifying C-peptide levels after glucagon administration. In routine settings, a stimulated C-peptide exceeding 0.60 nmol/L indicates intact endogenous insulin secretion, whereas values below this threshold point to β-cell insufficiency. Nevertheless, the glucagon stimulation test is considered a supportive tool, particularly when autoantibody findings are inconclusive or when clinical features are unusual. Testing did not identify microalbuminuria. Sudomotor testing, performed using an electrochemical skin conductance device (such as Sudoscan), found markedly decreased sudomotor function in the hands, signifying substantial autonomic impairment. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) tested negative. Genetic testing for maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY), conducted one month following diagnosis, identified a heterozygous pathogenic (P) HNF1A variant—p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)—confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. Initial management included basal insulin degludec at a dosage of eight units per day, alongside metformin at 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin at 5 mg daily, reflecting an initial assumption of type 2 diabetes. This regimen was selected to quickly optimize glycaemic control and mitigate possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient received recommendations to follow a structured diet and maintain consistent physical exercise as part of an integrated approach to diabetes care. Ongoing assessments took place at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient service, with the goals of monitoring glycaemic status and evaluating the effectiveness of therapy.

Nonetheless, due to the patient’s moderate yet inadequate β-cell reserve demonstrated by glucagon-stimulated C-peptide (0.83 nmol/L), the absence of islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced family history of diabetes manifesting at a young age, a strong clinical suspicion for MODY was raised.

Initial treatment was started prior to the availability of genetic testing results, based on the clinical presentation and laboratory findings at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c was reduced to 9.1%. Given the genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and assessment of the patient’s moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell function, coupled with the improved HbA1c, insulin therapy was discontinued in favor of dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg weekly) to optimize glycemic control and help maintain pancreatic function. At the six-month follow-up, HbA1c had further declined to 6.0%, reflecting notable improvement. With stabilized glycemic parameters, the treatment plan was adjusted to metformin (1500 mg/day) in conjunction with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day) for long-term management. The patient continues to be monitored regularly, maintaining HbA1c levels consistently under 6.5%. Thus far, microalbuminuria and diabetic retinopathy have not developed, and continued monitoring for potential long-term complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor situated on chromosome 12q24. It consists of 10 exons covering 23,790 base pairs and produces a protein with 631 amino acids, which is found in multiple organs, such as the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Alterations in HNF1A are associated with a gradual reduction in β-cell functionality, characterized by diminished insulin and GLUT2 gene expression and lower activity of pyruvate kinase. These changes ultimately cause a reduction in insulin release and impaired glucose tolerance.

Diagnosis in this case was performed following established MODY criteria, integrating clinical presentation, biochemical markers, and molecular findings. Criteria include early diabetes onset, strong familial aggregation, postprandial hyperglycemia, and the absence of autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide above 0.6 ng/mL points towards non–type 1 diabetes; nevertheless, repeated C-peptide measurement is important since early type 1 diabetes can occasionally show normal levels. A frequent challenge in diagnosing MODY is limited awareness and clinical suspicion, often resulting in mistaken diagnoses as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator from the Exeter group can assist healthcare professionals in selecting patients suitable for genetic analysis. It should be noted that this calculator is based on data from a White European cohort and may provide inflated MODY probabilities for individuals from other ethnic backgrounds. When our patient's data were analyzed using this calculator, the likelihood of MODY was estimated to be 62.4% (equivalent to 1 in 1.6 chance). Various case reports have documented MODY patients presenting with autoantibodies. While a minority of individuals with HNF1A mutations test positive for autoantibodies, most with MODY lack them. Previous research indicates MODY is associated with lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to type 2 diabetes, but growing obesity prevalence has decreased the discriminative value of BMI and metabolic syndrome, possibly accounting for the elevated BMI observed in this case. Furthermore, though family history contributes significantly to MODY assessment, its reliability may be reduced due to factors like incomplete reporting, de novo mutations, and the widespread occurrence of type 2 diabetes in families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on patient age and HbA1c measurements. Low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels, are typically the initial therapy. As β-cell function deteriorates—especially in those susceptible to hypoglycaemia—GLP-1 receptor agonists may provide a more favorable and safer alternative compared with sulfonylureas. A small, randomized, double-blind, crossover study found that both liraglutide and glimepiride achieved significant reductions in fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glycaemic excursions in individuals with HNF1A-MODY, with no meaningful differences observed between treatments. Notably, therapy with GLP-1 receptor agonists resulted in fewer mild hypoglycaemic events compared to glimepiride. In this case, genetic confirmation enabled cessation of insulin following achievement of glycaemic targets, permitting the initiation of GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which successfully maintained glycaemic control. This underscores the substantial importance of genetic evaluation in tailoring management. Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY results from decreased SGLT2 expression due to HNF1A regulatory activity. There are reports of severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis in individuals with HNF1A-MODY linked to heightened SGLT-2 inhibition. Thus, potential adverse effects of SGLT-2 inhibitors in this population—including dehydration, risk of vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may exceed the expected therapeutic gains. In the described patient, a combination therapy of liraglutide and metformin proved both safe and effective, in alignment with prior research findings.

Tailoring medical management to the individual is essential in cases of MODY, as many individuals with HNF1A-MODY do not fully satisfy established diagnostic standards, even with the guidance of current clinical protocols. At present, no reliable biomarkers are available to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or type 2 diabetes (T2D). Chen et al investigated 2,429 young Chinese adults with T2D diagnosed at an early age and found a MODY prevalence of 1.5%, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) variants associated with MODY. In another study, Møller et al observed that within a broad, population-based sample of Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 individuals lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes carried HNF1A mutations. Likewise, recent research from Russia examined 506 people suspected of hereditary diabetes without autoimmune markers—characterized by the absence of IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at onset, preservation of C-peptide, minimal insulin needs over five years, and no severe obesity. That analysis found P or LP variants in 46.4% of cases, with HNF1A among the most frequently implicated genes. Confirmatory diagnosis of MODY depends on DNA sequencing. The advent of next-generation sequencing (NGS) facilitates analysis of extensive patient DNA in a single assay, increasing the practicality of broad MODY screening. As NGS technology has expanded, many new variants have been identified. The ACMG has issued recommendations for determining variant pathogenicity. Over 1,000 unique HNF1A variants are now catalogued in resources such as ClinVar, spanning P, LP, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In our patient, a previously undescribed heterozygous HNF1A change (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or other commonly referenced databases, reflecting its novelty rather than casting doubt on its significance. Notably, this mutation changes an amino acid adjacent to established P residues, has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes within the family, satisfying ACMG requirements for a 'LP' variant designation. Therefore, while ClinVar is an important repository for known variants, multiple independent genetic and clinical findings substantiate the potential pathogenic nature of this variant beyond its absence from established databases.

Research has demonstrated that most mutations detected are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a comparatively lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is also positioned in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations suggest that specific HNF1A mutations, while present, exert only a limited influence on HNF1A protein function. Furthermore, there are HNF1A variants that, although insufficient to cause MODY on their own, may contribute to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the necessity for precise identification of HNF1A-MODY, as it significantly influences patient management. Ongoing studies will aim to refine NGS-based screening protocols, which should enhance the diagnostic yield for MODY and facilitate improved cost control and clinical outcomes.
