Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic diabetes variant transmitted in an autosomal dominant fashion. This case concerns an individual with HNF1A-MODY arising from a newly identified heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without developing diabetic ketoacidosis. The familial background revealed early-onset diabetes among relatives. Genetic analysis detected a previously undescribed pathogenic mutation in HNF1A. Management with a GLP-1 receptor agonist alongside metformin lowered the patient's glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a period of 3 months. This report underscores the value of genetic evaluation in early-onset diabetes, commonly mistaken for type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and broadens the known spectrum of HNF1A mutations that inform precision-based therapy.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a spectrum of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes characterized by an inherent defect in pancreatic β-cell maturation and functionality. These disorders usually present before 25 years of age. MODY accounts for an estimated 1%–6% of diabetes diagnosed in youth. The prevalence of MODY differs among diverse racial and ethnic populations and may be underreported. To date, research has primarily targeted White Europeans, although more recent investigations have assessed MODY prevalence in Asian and Australian settings. Prevalence figures for MODY demonstrate substantial variability, frequently attributable to the practice of limiting genetic testing to those selected by clinical features, even within broader population-based studies.

Mutations in more than 15 distinct genes have been associated with the monogenic MODY phenotype, each guiding tailored treatment approaches. Most MODY cases are attributed to mutations in specific genes, including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. HNF1A-MODY is the most frequent form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of MODY diagnoses, and shows a discernible autosomal dominant inheritance pattern with symptomatic diabetes manifesting across generations. In the early adolescent years, clinical manifestations are often absent, and hyperglycemia is commonly discovered incidentally through routine blood analyses, typically in the absence of signs indicating insulin resistance. Postprandial glucose elevations (≥11.1 mmol/L) may be uncovered by monitoring blood glucose after eating or by performing a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Glycosuria may also be present due to a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, which results from impaired glucose handling in the renal tubules. As the disorder advances, patients can develop symptoms like polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight loss, which are akin to those seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 or T2D is challenging due to considerable overlap in clinical features.

Multiple diagnostic protocols for MODY have been defined in the literature. Following a clinical evaluation and review of family history suggesting features consistent with MODY, and confirmation of negative autoantibodies typical for type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—in addition to relevant laboratory investigations, molecular genetic analysis becomes a key step in confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification of MODY is essential for selecting appropriate therapeutic strategies, anticipating complications, evaluating prognosis, and enabling effective genetic counselling for families. Recent research has identified numerous distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene contributing to HNF1A-MODY.

In the present case, we detected a previously undescribed heterozygous mutation, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A, in a patient with HNF1A-MODY through next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel testing. The variant’s pathogenic potential was assessed in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) recommendations for variant classification. This alteration had not been previously reported in the literature, nor was it present in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment following detection of elevated blood glucose and glycosuria at his routine yearly health check. On physical examination, his BMI measured 24.6 kg/m^2, blood pressure was recorded at 112/70 mmHg, and his heart rate was 78 beats per minute. There was no evidence of acanthosis nigricans on examination. His family history was significant for early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at 20 years of age. No cases of early-onset cardiovascular disease had been reported among the relatives with diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was elevated at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting plasma glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, the 2-hour glucose measurement reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no clinical or laboratory evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. Symptomatically, the patient noted increased thirst, polyphagia, and had experienced recent weight loss. Lipid analysis demonstrated a high-density lipoprotein of 1.01 mmol/L and triglyceride concentration of 4.31 mmol/L, findings suggestive of possible metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). His past medical and surgical history was unremarkable.

During a glucagon stimulation test, serum C-peptide measured 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L after 6 minutes, reflecting impaired insulin secretory capacity. This test is utilized to determine remaining β-cell function, as it assesses the stimulated C-peptide response post-glucagon administration. In routine settings, a stimulated C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L is considered to reflect retained endogenous insulin production, while lower readings point toward β-cell insufficiency. Nonetheless, the glucagon stimulation test should serve as adjunctive evidence, especially if autoantibody assays are inconclusive or the case does not align with typical presentations. No microalbuminuria was found. Assessment of sudomotor activity revealed markedly diminished function in the hands. Sudomotor assessment was performed using electrochemical skin conductance measurements (for example, with a Sudoscan device), a non-invasive technique that gauges small fiber autonomic nerve activity. In this patient, hand conductance was markedly decreased, signifying substantial autonomic impairment. Testing for antibodies, including GAD antibody (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibody (IA-2A), yielded negative results. A genetic test for MODY performed one month after diagnosis identified a heterozygous pathogenic (P) variant in HNF1A (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), supporting a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The initial therapeutic approach included basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, along with metformin at a total daily dose of 1500 mg and saxagliptin at 5 mg per day, based on an initial assessment suggestive of T2D. This regimen was selected to facilitate prompt glycaemic stabilization and to counteract potential insulin resistance.

Alongside pharmacologic intervention, the patient received recommendations to follow a regulated diet and maintain consistent physical activity, forming part of an integrated diabetes care plan. Subsequent follow-up visits were scheduled at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient service to track glycaemic indices and evaluate the effectiveness of therapy.

Nonetheless, the findings of moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell function on glucagon stimulation testing (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a notable family history of diabetes diagnosed at a young age raised significant clinical suspicion for MODY.

Initial treatment was started prior to the availability of genetic testing results, based on the clinical presentation and laboratory findings at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. Once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed genetically and taking into account the patient’s moderately preserved, though inadequate, β-cell function along with the HbA1c improvement, insulin therapy was stopped. The patient was transitioned to dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg administered weekly) to enhance glycaemic regulation and help maintain pancreatic function. Six months into this regimen, HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, reflecting notable glycaemic improvement. With sustained stabilization, the treatment plan was adjusted to long-term maintenance consisting of metformin (1500 mg per day) and saxagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg per day). The patient continues with regular follow-up visits, maintaining HbA1c below 6.5%. Thus far, no microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy has developed, and ongoing monitoring for potential chronic complications continues.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, located in the q24 region on chromosome 12, acts as a transcription factor and consists of 10 exons spanning 23,790 base pairs. It codes for a 631-amino-acid protein expressed in tissues such as the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Variants in HNF1A result in a gradual loss of β-cell functionality, reflected by decreased expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes along with diminished pyruvate kinase activity, ultimately leading to compromised insulin release and impaired glucose tolerance.

In this case, the diagnostic approach followed established MODY criteria, incorporating clinical features, laboratory data, and genetic confirmation—such as early diabetes onset, notable familial clustering, post-meal hyperglycemia, and absence of autoantibodies. Fasting C-peptide above 0.6 ng/mL supports non-type 1 diabetes, though follow-up C-peptide assessment remains necessary as normal values can occur early in type 1 diabetes. One of the main obstacles to diagnosing MODY is limited awareness and suspicion among clinicians, with frequent misclassification as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator, developed by the Exeter group, assists providers in determining the likelihood of MODY and indicating genetic testing; however, as this calculator is based on data from White European cohorts, it may overstate MODY likelihood for patients of other backgrounds. Application of our patient's clinical data to this calculator indicated a 62.4% likelihood (1 in 1.6 chance) of MODY. Various reports have documented autoantibody presence in some patients with MODY, and although a minority of individuals harboring HNF1A mutations may test positive, most MODY cases are autoantibody negative. Previous investigations indicate lower frequencies of obesity and metabolic syndrome in MODY compared with type 2 diabetes, but rising obesity has made it increasingly difficult to separate MODY from T2D based solely on BMI or metabolic syndrome features—a possible factor in the increased BMI observed in our patient. Moreover, while a family history supports MODY identification, its diagnostic utility is often constrained due to incomplete reporting, de novo mutations, or the high background rate of T2DM in relatives.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on the patient's age and HbA1c measurements. Low-dose sulfonylureas are recommended as initial therapy due to their action on potassium-sensitive ATP channels, increasing insulin release. For patients with declining β-cell function or heightened susceptibility to hypoglycaemia, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer and potentially more efficacious substitute for sulfonylureas. In a small randomized, double-blind crossover study, both liraglutide and glimepiride were shown to significantly decrease fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose excursions, with comparable efficacy in HNF1A-MODY subjects. Importantly, GLP-1 receptor agonist use carried a reduced risk of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic confirmation of the diagnosis allowed us to discontinue insulin after stabilizing glucose levels and to switch the patient to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which provided continued glycaemic control. This case underscores the importance of genetic testing in informing therapeutic approaches. The presence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is linked to diminished SGLT2 expression, regulated by HNF1A transcription. Previous reports have noted that patients with HNF1A-MODY may develop severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis, attributed to marked inhibition of any remaining SGLT-2 function. The hazards of prescribing SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY patients—including higher risks of dehydration, vascular compromise, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may surpass their therapeutic benefits. In this patient, the combination of liraglutide and metformin has proven both effective and safe, aligning with earlier research findings.

Precision medicine plays a vital role in the management of MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY may not fully satisfy established diagnostic criteria despite the presence of current guidelines. At present, there are no specific biomarkers available to reliably distinguish HNF1A-MODY from either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Chen et al demonstrated that, among 2429 young adults with early-onset T2D in China, MODY prevalence was 1.5%, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY-associated variants. In addition, Møller et al reported that in a large Danish Caucasian cohort with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 individuals who lacked high-risk HLA haplotypes carried HNF1A gene mutations. Likewise, a more recent Russian population-based investigation evaluated 506 patients suspected of hereditary diabetes who presented with negative autoimmune markers (IAA, ICA, GADA, ZnT8 antibodies) at diagnosis, maintained C-peptide production, required low doses of insulin for up to 5 years, and did not have severe obesity. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were detected in 46.4% of this cohort, with HNF1A being among the most prevalent affected loci. Confirming clinical suspicion of MODY necessitates DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables the simultaneous analysis of large segments of genomic DNA, streamlining MODY diagnostics. The introduction of NGS has led to the identification of numerous novel genetic changes. The ACMG has published protocols for assessing whether these variants qualify as pathogenic. More than 1000 unique HNF1A alterations, spanning the spectrum from pathogenic to likely benign or variants of uncertain significance, are catalogued in resources such as ClinVar. In our reported patient, the new heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not identified in ClinVar or other routine variant repositories, marking it as novel but not diminishing its potential clinical importance. Of note, this mutation involves an amino acid neighboring established pathogenic sites, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense prediction score (0.995), and cosegregates with diabetes within the pedigree—fulfilling ACMG’s standards for an LP designation. Thus, while variant databases like ClinVar offer important data on known mutations, multiple independent genetic and clinical findings in this case corroborate the pathogenicity of this variant, regardless of its absence from such registries.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is located in the DNA-binding domain. According to some reports, certain HNF1A mutations, though present, exert limited impact on the function of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, there are HNF1A variants that do not independently lead to MODY but may nonetheless elevate the risk of developing T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the critical role of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY in guiding individualized therapy. Ongoing studies will emphasize refining NGS-based screening approaches to enhance the diagnosis of MODY, thereby optimizing resource allocation and improving clinical outcomes.
