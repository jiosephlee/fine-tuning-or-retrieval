Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic form of diabetes with autosomal dominant inheritance. This case report details an individual with HNF1A-MODY resulting from a novel heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The patient was found to have hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. There was a family history of diabetes manifesting at an early age. Genetic analysis revealed a pathogenic HNF1A variant that had not been previously described. The patient's management with a GLP-1 receptor agonist combined with metformin led to a reduction in glycated haemoglobin levels from 10.2% to 6.0% over a period of 3 months. This case underscores the value of genetic testing in patients with early-onset diabetes—often wrongly identified as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and broadens the known range of HNF1A mutations relevant to individualized medicine approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes resulting from an inherent defect in the formation and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders commonly arise and are identified before the age of 25. MODY accounts for roughly 1%–6% of diabetes cases with onset at a young age. Its prevalence differs across ethnicities and racial backgrounds and may frequently be underreported. Most existing research has focused on White European populations, though recent investigations have expanded to Asian and Australian cohorts. Prevalence estimates for MODY fluctuate widely, often because genetic testing is confined to patients meeting certain clinical criteria, even within population-based studies.

More than 15 gene mutations have been identified as causative for the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with each genetic variant potentially requiring distinct management approaches. Most cases across different MODY subtypes are mainly associated with mutations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. HNF1A-MODY accounts for the largest proportion of MODY, representing approximately 30%–65% of cases, and demonstrates an unmistakable autosomal dominant inheritance of clinically evident diabetes within families. During early adolescence, patients often lack noticeable symptoms, and hyperglycemia is often incidentally detected on routine laboratory assessment, commonly in the absence of clinical signs of insulin resistance. Measuring postprandial blood glucose or performing a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may uncover marked postprandial hyperglycemia (≥11.1 mmol/L). Furthermore, glycosuria may occur due to a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, as a result of impaired tubular glucose transport. At more advanced disease stages, manifestations such as excessive thirst, increased urination, heightened appetite, and unintentional weight loss—mirroring symptoms observed in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—can emerge. Thus, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D presents a significant diagnostic challenge due to their overlapping presentations.

Multiple studies have established various diagnostic protocols for MODY. Once physical examination and evaluation of family history raise suspicion for a clinical phenotype of MODY and tests for type 1 diabetes-related autoantibodies—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma-associated antigen 2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are negative, alongside other corroborating laboratory studies, molecular genetic analysis becomes pivotal in confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification is fundamental for selecting appropriate therapy, anticipating disease complications, determining prognosis, and enabling genetic counseling for families. Recent research has identified hundreds of distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene underlying HNF1A-MODY.

In the present report, we detected a previously unreported heterozygous missense variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient with HNF1A-MODY, through next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel analysis. The pathogenicity of this mutation was assessed using the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) variant interpretation criteria. This particular variant was absent from the existing medical literature as well as the VarSome and ClinVar databases. Informed written consent for publication of this case report was obtained directly from the patient.

Case presentation:

A young adult presented after being identified with elevated blood glucose levels and glycosuria during a routine annual checkup. Upon assessment, his body mass index measured 24.6 kg/m2, his blood pressure was 112/70 mmHg, and his pulse rate was 78 beats per minute. No signs of acanthosis nigricans were detected on physical examination. The patient’s family history was significant for diabetes diagnosed at an early age: the maternal grandmother was affected in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at 20 years old. There was no documented occurrence of early-onset cardiovascular disease among relatives with diabetes.

Investigations:

His glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. After 2 hours following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), glucose was 19.8 mmol/L. No evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis was observed. The patient described experiencing increased thirst and appetite alongside recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid studies showed a high-density lipoprotein concentration of 1.01 mmol/L and triglyceride level of 4.31 mmol/L, findings that raise suspicion for metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He denied any notable past medical or surgical conditions.

The glucagon stimulation test identified a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, findings that are consistent with inadequate insulin production. This diagnostic method measures C-peptide response following glucagon administration to estimate remaining β-cell capacity. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide value above 0.60 nmol/L typically reflects maintained endogenous insulin output, whereas lower results indicate β-cell impairment. Despite its utility, the glucagon stimulation test serves as a supplementary assessment, especially when autoantibody findings are equivocal or the case features are not classic. No microalbuminuria was detected. Testing of sudomotor activity via electrochemical skin conductance (such as Sudoscan), a non-invasive measure of small fibre autonomic nerve involvement, revealed a marked reduction in conductance in the hands, signifying severe autonomic dysfunction. Autoantibody testing for both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) returned negative. Genetic testing for maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY), conducted one month after diagnosis, identified a heterozygous pathogenic (P) variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), diagnostic for HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose values. The first therapeutic approach included basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, together with metformin 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin 5 mg daily, indicating an initial assumption of type 2 diabetes. This regimen was selected to facilitate swift glycaemic stabilization while mitigating potential insulin resistance.

Beyond pharmacologic intervention, recommendations were provided for the patient to follow a structured dietary protocol and participate in consistent physical activity, forming an integral part of an overall diabetes care strategy. Subsequent assessments took place at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient service, with ongoing monitoring of glycaemic status and evaluation of the response to treatment.

Nevertheless, considering the patient’s moderately maintained, though inadequate, β-cell activity as shown by the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and substantial familial history of diabetes manifesting at an early age, there was considerable clinical suspicion for MODY.

Therapy was initiated prior to receipt of genetic testing data, relying on the patient’s clinical features and laboratory results available at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c had dropped to 9.1%. Once HNF1A-MODY was genetically confirmed, and given the patient’s moderately retained yet inadequate β-cell function as well as the improvement in HbA1c to 9.1%, insulin was discontinued. The patient was initiated on dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) to enhance glycemic control and help preserve pancreatic function. Six months later, HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, reflecting substantial progress. With glycemic stability established, the regimen was streamlined to metformin (1500 mg daily) combined with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg daily) as a maintenance strategy. The patient continues with regular follow-up, maintaining HbA1c levels consistently below 6.5%. No evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy has been observed thus far, and the patient remains under ongoing monitoring for potential long-term complications.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor situated on chromosome 12 at the q24 locus. It consists of 10 exons over 23,790 base pairs and produces a protein made up of 631 amino acids, with expression found in organs such as the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Genetic alterations in HNF1A cause progressive deterioration of β-cell function, evidenced by reduced insulin and GLUT2 gene expression along with diminished activity of pyruvate kinase. This cascade results in decreased insulin release and impaired glucose tolerance.

For this patient, the diagnostic approach followed established MODY criteria, which include clinical, biochemical, and genetic markers such as early onset diabetes, a strong familial pattern, postprandial glucose intolerance, and negative autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide level above 0.6 ng/mL supports a diagnosis outside of type 1 diabetes; nonetheless, ongoing monitoring of C-peptide is warranted since normal values can be found in early type 1 diabetes. A major challenge in identifying MODY is insufficient awareness and suspicion in clinical settings, leading to frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator created by the Exeter group assists in recognizing candidates for genetic testing, but its calibration is based on White European populations and may overestimate MODY likelihood in other ethnic groups. Upon entering this patient’s data, the tool yielded a 62.4% estimated MODY probability (or roughly a 1 in 1.6 chance). Case reports have shown some MODY patients have detectable autoantibodies, with individuals bearing HNF1A mutations occasionally presenting autoantibodies, although most MODY cases are seronegative. Previous research indicated lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome among MODY patients compared to those with T2D; however, rising obesity trends have diminished the effectiveness of BMI or metabolic syndrome for differentiating MODY from T2DM, potentially explaining the elevated BMI observed in this instance. Moreover, while family history can be informative for MODY, its value may be limited due to factors such as under-reporting, spontaneous mutations, and the widespread occurrence of T2DM within families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on the patient’s age and HbA1c values. Low-dose sulfonylureas are typically the initial therapy, working to stimulate insulin release via potassium-sensitive ATP channels. As β-cell function deteriorates in HNF1A-MODY, GLP-1 receptor agonists may be a more favorable and safer option, especially in individuals susceptible to hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small, randomized, double-blind, crossover study demonstrated that liraglutide and glimepiride both significantly decreased fasting plasma glucose and mitigated postprandial glucose variability in HNF1A-MODY subjects, with no marked difference in efficacy between the two agents. However, GLP-1 receptor agonists were associated with a reduced risk of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. In this case, genetic testing enabled the cessation of insulin once glycaemic control was achieved, allowing transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which continued to maintain optimal glycaemic outcomes. This underscores the importance of genetic analysis in directing therapeutic strategies. Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY arises from diminished SGLT2 expression, which is regulated by HNF1A transcription. One publication reported that HNF1A-MODY patients could develop pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to heightened suppression of residual SGLT-2 activity. The hazards of SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY—including increased dehydration risk, possible vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may outweigh their prospective advantages. In the present case, the combination therapy with liraglutide and metformin proved to be both safe and effective, aligning with previously published findings.

The application of precision medicine is particularly important in the context of MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy all conventional diagnostic requirements outlined in clinical protocols. At present, specific biomarkers for distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes and T2D are lacking. Chen et al reported that in a cohort of 2429 Chinese young adults presenting with early-onset T2D, MODY was identified in 1.5% of cases, with 0.74% found to have pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al’s work demonstrated that in a broad population-based cohort of Danish Caucasians diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, 10% (4 of 39) of patients who did not possess any high-risk HLA haplotypes carried HNF1A gene mutations. Similarly, a recent Russian population study evaluated 506 individuals presumed to have hereditary diabetes forms without autoimmune features—characterized by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, sustained C-peptide levels, minimal insulin needs over five years, and no severe obesity—and found P or LP variants in 46.4% of cases, with HNF1A among the most frequently implicated genes. Clinical suspicion of MODY requires confirmation via DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables comprehensive evaluation of extensive patient DNA within a single process, enhancing the practicality of MODY detection. Advances in NGS have led to the identification of numerous novel genetic variants. The ACMG provides a framework for assessing whether these variants are pathogenic. More than 1000 unique HNF1A variants are catalogued in databases such as ClinVar, encompassing pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and uncertain significance classifications. In our patient, the newly discovered heterozygous HNF1A mutation (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or other widely utilized variant repositories, establishing its novelty but not its clinical relevance. Notably, this variant modifies an amino acid residue contiguous to established pathogenic sites, yields a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and exhibits familial co-segregation with diabetes, which collectively satisfy ACMG criteria for a ‘likely pathogenic’ designation. Therefore, although ClinVar is an important tool for variant reporting, the pathogenic nature of this mutation is corroborated by multiple independent genetic and clinical findings, irrespective of its presence in database records.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain displays a lower frequency of mutations. The variant described in this report resides within the DNA-binding domain. Certain studies suggest that HNF1A mutations, while P, exert only limited influence on the function of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, some HNF1A variants that are insufficient to independently cause MODY may be linked to a heightened susceptibility to T2D.

To summarize, this case underscores the necessity of precise HNF1A-MODY diagnosis and its significance in guiding individualized therapy. Future investigations will aim to establish improved NGS-based screening protocols to enhance MODY detection rates, thereby promoting superior cost effectiveness and clinical outcomes.
