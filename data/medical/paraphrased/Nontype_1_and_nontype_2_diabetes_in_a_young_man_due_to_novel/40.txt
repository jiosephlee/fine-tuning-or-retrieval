Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic type of diabetes inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. This case report details a patient with HNF1A-MODY resulting from a novel heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. There was a significant family history of diabetes diagnosed at an early age. Genetic analysis identified a pathogenic HNF1A variant that had not been previously documented. The combination therapy of a GLP-1 receptor agonist with metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin levels from 10.2% to 6.0% over a three-month period. This case underscores the critical role of genetic testing in cases of early-onset diabetes, which are often incorrectly classified as type 1 or type 2, and further broadens the range of HNF1A mutations pertinent to individualized medical approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) comprises a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes marked by an intrinsic abnormality in the formation and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders generally present and are identified prior to 25 years of age. MODY accounts for roughly 1% to 6% of cases of diabetes with onset in youth. Prevalence rates of MODY may differ across diverse ethnic and racial populations, and its occurrence may be underreported. To date, investigations have largely focused on populations of White European descent. More recent research has begun to assess MODY prevalence within Asian and Australian cohorts. Reported estimates of MODY prevalence demonstrate wide variability, often attributed to the restriction of genetic testing to individuals selected based on specific clinical criteria, even in studies that sample broader populations.

More than 15 genes have been identified as having pathogenic variants associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with each gene relating to distinct management approaches. Most cases reported across the various MODY subtypes stem from mutations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. HNF1A-MODY is the most frequently occurring subtype, accounting for approximately 30% to 65% of all MODY diagnoses and demonstrating a well-defined autosomal dominant inheritance pattern for symptomatic diabetes within affected families. During early adolescence, clinical symptoms often remain absent, and hyperglycemia is usually detected incidentally during routine blood work, generally in the absence of signs indicative of insulin resistance. Assessment of blood glucose following meals or performance of a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal a marked postprandial glycemic increase (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be present and is attributed to a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired tubular glucose transport. At more advanced disease stages, classic symptoms such as excessive thirst, frequent urination, increased appetite, and unintended weight loss may appear, mirroring the presentation seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). As a result, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D can be challenging due to similar clinical characteristics.

Multiple diagnostic protocols for MODY have been described in the literature. Once physical examination and family history indicate a MODY phenotype, and autoantibodies related to type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibody (GADA), insulinoma-associated antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, along with corroborating biochemical assays, molecular genetic evaluation plays an essential part in confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification of MODY is vital for appropriate therapeutic decisions, predicting possible complications, determining prognosis, and supporting genetic counselling within families. Recent investigations have documented that several hundred distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene account for HNF1A-MODY.

The patient described herein was found, via next-generation sequencing (NGS) gene panel testing, to have a previously unreported heterozygous nucleotide change—p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)—located in exon 4 of HNF1A. We evaluated its pathogenicity according to the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant assessment. This particular mutation was absent in published literature, the VarSome database, and the ClinVar database. Written informed consent for publication of the case report was obtained directly from the patient.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment of elevated blood glucose levels and urinary glucose detected at his routine annual check-up. On physical examination, his body mass index measured 24.6 kg/m2, blood pressure was 112/70 mmHg, and pulse rate was 78 beats per minute. No signs of acanthosis nigricans were found. The family history revealed multiple cases of early-onset diabetes: the maternal grandmother developed diabetes in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the younger brother at age 20. There was no reported incidence of premature cardiovascular disease among diabetic relatives.

Investigations:

His HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. At 2 hours following a 75 g OGTT, glucose was 19.8 mmol/L. He exhibited no features of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described polydipsia, increased appetite, and recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid analysis showed an HDL concentration of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings which suggest possible metabolic syndrome (see tables 1 and 2). He did not have a notable past medical or surgical history.

Glucagon stimulation testing yielded C-peptide levels of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, demonstrating deficient insulin production. This test is used to assess residual β-cell capability by measuring C-peptide response after glucagon administration. In general, a stimulated C-peptide greater than 0.60 nmol/L is considered to reflect ongoing endogenous insulin production; lower values are consistent with β-cell impairment. Despite this, the glucagon stimulation test is best viewed as adjunctive, rather than conclusive, especially in the setting of unclear antibody status or atypical symptoms. Microalbuminuria screening was negative. Electrochemical skin conductance (e.g., Sudoscan) was used to evaluate sudomotor function, revealing pronounced reduction in sweat gland activity over the hands and confirming marked small-fiber autonomic neuropathy. Both GADA and IA-2A antibody assays returned negative results. One month following diagnosis, molecular testing for MODY detected a heterozygous, pathogenic (P) variant in HNF1A (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), establishing the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The initial therapeutic approach comprised basal degludec insulin at eight units per day, combined with metformin at 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin at 5 mg daily, reflecting preliminary management consistent with type 2 diabetes. This regimen was selected to facilitate prompt glycaemic stabilization and address possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient received guidance to follow a structured nutrition plan and participate in routine physical exercise as elements of an integrated diabetes management strategy. Subsequent assessments were performed in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to track glycaemic regulation and evaluate treatment efficacy.

Nevertheless, due to the patient’s moderately maintained, though inadequate, β-cell capacity observed in the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), negative islet autoantibody status, and a prominent family history of early-onset diabetes, there was substantial clinical suspicion for MODY.

Therapy was initiated prior to receiving genetic test results, relying on the clinical picture and laboratory parameters observed at presentation.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient's HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. With genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and acknowledging the patient’s still moderately retained but inadequate β-cell function, along with the reduction in HbA1c to 9.1%, insulin treatment was discontinued and dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg administered weekly) was initiated to enhance glycemic management and conserve pancreatic function. Six months into therapy, the patient’s HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, demonstrating marked improvement. Once glycemic levels stabilized, the therapeutic regimen was streamlined to include metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day) for ongoing maintenance. The patient continues regular follow-up appointments, with HbA1c maintained consistently under 6.5%. Up to the present time, there has been no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and regular monitoring for chronic complications remains underway.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, a transcription factor situated at chromosome 12q24, consists of 10 exons over a stretch of 23,790 base pairs, coding for a protein composed of 631 amino acids. This protein is found in multiple tissues, such as the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Variants in HNF1A cause a gradual loss of β-cell function, resulting from disrupted expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes and diminished pyruvate kinase activity. These genetic alterations ultimately contribute to lower insulin production and glucose intolerance.

For this case, diagnostic assessment followed established MODY criteria, encompassing clinical, biochemical, and genetic parameters: early diabetes onset, marked familial prevalence, intolerance to postprandial blood glucose, and absence of autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide measurement above 0.6 ng/mL favors non-type 1 diabetes, but ongoing C-peptide surveillance is warranted, since initial readings can appear normal in the early phase of type 1 diabetes. MODY identification frequently faces hurdles, notably limited awareness and low suspicion among clinicians; affected individuals are often misclassified as having type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The Exeter group’s MODY probability calculator assists in flagging patients for genetic screening, although it is based on White European population data and may inflate MODY likelihood in other ethnicities. In this instance, entering the patient’s data into the calculator yielded a 62.4% estimated probability (1 in 1.6 odds) of MODY. Reports exist of MODY patients with autoantibodies; while most individuals with HNF1A mutations are autoantibody-negative, some cases are positive. Earlier literature indicates that MODY cases present lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to T2D. Nonetheless, the increasing prevalence of obesity complicates the use of BMI and metabolic syndrome to differentiate MODY from T2DM, potentially accounting for the elevated BMI observed here. While a positive family history is a key component of MODY diagnosis, it can be less reliable due to factors such as under-reporting, sporadic mutations, and high rates of T2DM within families.

The management of HNF1A-MODY depends on the patient's age and HbA1c. Initial therapy typically consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function diminishes, GLP-1 receptor agonists may provide a safer and more efficacious option compared to sulfonylureas, especially for individuals at higher risk of hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small randomized, double-blind, crossover study showed that both liraglutide and glimepiride significantly lower fasting and postprandial plasma glucose levels in HNF1A-MODY patients, without notable differences in efficacy between the two agents. Moreover, therapy with GLP-1 receptor agonists is associated with a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. By confirming the genetic diagnosis, insulin therapy was withdrawn following the establishment of adequate glycaemic control, and the patient was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, which sustained optimal glycaemic regulation. This underscores the essential role of genetic screening in formulating treatment strategies. Glycosuria seen in HNF1A-MODY results from decreased SGLT2 expression regulated by HNF1A. There is documentation of HNF1A-MODY individuals developing significant dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to substantial inhibition of residual SGLT-2 function. The adverse effects linked to SGLT-2 inhibitor use in HNF1A-MODY, including a higher risk of dehydration, potential for vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, may outweigh the advantages offered by these agents. In this case, combining liraglutide with metformin yielded safe and effective glycaemic control, in accordance with earlier reports.

Precision medicine plays a pivotal role in MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all standard diagnostic criteria despite existing clinical guidelines. At present, there are no effective biomarkers capable of distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Chen et al reported that in a cohort of 2429 young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D, MODY was present in 1.5%, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY gene variants. Research by Møller et al demonstrated that within a sizable Danish Caucasian population sample diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 patients lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes were identified with mutations in HNF1A. Likewise, a recent population-based study from Russia examined 506 subjects suspected of having hereditary diabetes without evidence of autoimmunity, based on negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at onset, maintained C-peptide production, low insulin requirements for up to 5 years, and no severe obesity. This investigation detected P or LP variants in 46.4% of patients, with HNF1A as one of the most frequently implicated genes. Confirmation of MODY clinical suspicion relies on DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) facilitates the simultaneous analysis of extensive portions of patient DNA, making MODY screening more practical. The advent of NGS has led to the identification of numerous new genetic variants. The ACMG offers guidelines to determine the pathogenicity of these variants. Over 1000 unique HNF1A variants, including P, LP, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance, are catalogued in databases such as ClinVar. In the present case, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not found in ClinVar or other major variant repositories, underscoring its originality rather than diminishing its clinical significance. Notably, this variant impacts an amino acid residue next to established P sites, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes within the family, collectively satisfying ACMG criteria for an LP classification. Therefore, while ClinVar serves as a comprehensive catalogue of documented variants, the pathogenic status of this variant is supported by independent genetic and clinical evidence in addition to database listings.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits fewer mutations. The novel variant described in this report is positioned in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations suggest that HNF1A mutations, such as P, exert only minor influence on HNF1A protein functionality. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants that do not independently cause MODY may nonetheless contribute to an increased susceptibility to type 2 diabetes.

To summarize, this case underscores the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its implications for personalized therapy. Future studies will aim to establish more efficient next-generation sequencing screening approaches to enhance the MODY detection rate, thereby improving cost-effectiveness and clinical outcomes.
