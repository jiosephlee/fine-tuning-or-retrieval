Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a monogenic form of diabetes inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. This case report details an instance of HNF1A-MODY caused by a newly identified heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose levels and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history revealed cases of diabetes with early onset. Genetic analysis detected a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A not previously described. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist in combination with metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a three-month period. This report underscores the significance of genetic testing in cases of early-onset diabetes—frequently mistaken for either type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and broadens the known array of HNF1A mutations significant for precision medicine.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a set of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes, which are marked by an intrinsic defect in both the formation and functionality of pancreatic β-cells. These syndromes usually arise and are detected prior to the age of 25. MODY is responsible for approximately 1%–6% of diabetes cases that present in youth. Prevalence rates for MODY can differ among various ethnic and racial groups and may frequently be underrecognized. The majority of existing research has primarily targeted populations of White European descent. However, recent investigations have also addressed MODY prevalence within Asian and Australian contexts. There is considerable heterogeneity in reported prevalence figures for MODY, a discrepancy that often stems from the fact that genetic screening tends to be restricted to individuals selected on the basis of their clinical profiles, even in studies that survey broader populations.

More than 15 distinct genes have been implicated in the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with each gene mutation necessitating a unique therapeutic approach. Most MODY cases are attributed to mutations in hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. Among these, HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses. It is characterized by a well-defined autosomal dominant inheritance of symptomatic diabetes within affected families. In the early adolescent period, individuals typically remain asymptomatic, and hyperglycemia is often incidentally discovered during routine screenings, with no overt signs of insulin resistance. Assessment of postprandial glucose levels or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal pronounced post-meal hyperglycemia (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be observed, resulting from a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired tubular glucose transport. Nevertheless, as the disease progresses, classic symptoms such as excessive thirst, frequent urination, increased appetite, and weight loss—mirroring those of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—can emerge. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D presents significant diagnostic challenges given their similar clinical presentations.

A variety of diagnostic protocols for MODY have been delineated in previous research. When clinical evaluation, including both physical examination and assessment of family medical background, indicates the possibility of MODY, and when autoantibody screens for type 1 diabetes—specifically for glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—yield negative results, coupled with corroborative biochemical analyses, molecular genetic testing becomes indispensable for confirming the diagnosis. Achieving an accurate diagnosis is essential for selecting the most appropriate therapeutic strategy, predicting likely disease outcomes, gauging prognosis, and providing effective genetic counseling for families. Recent research has identified numerous distinct pathogenic variants in the HNF1A gene associated with HNF1A-MODY.

Within the present case study, we detected a previously unreported heterozygous missense variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient with HNF1A-MODY, utilizing a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel for genetic analysis and assessed its pathogenic potential based on the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria for variant classification. This particular mutation has not been described in published literature, nor is it recorded in either the VarSome or ClinVar databases. Informed written consent was obtained directly from the patient to permit publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult presented for assessment after routine screening revealed hyperglycaemia and glycosuria. Physical examination showed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure reading of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. No acanthosis nigricans was detected. The family history revealed several cases of early-onset diabetes: the maternal grandmother had been diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the younger brother at the age of 20. There were no reports of premature cardiovascular disease in family members affected by diabetes.

Investigations:

The patient's glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), while his fasting plasma glucose was elevated at 12.4 mmol/L. After a 75 g OGTT, the 2-hour glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. There were no clinical or laboratory indicators of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described symptoms of polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent, unexplained weight loss. Laboratory evaluation of his lipid profile showed high-density lipoprotein at 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings that may indicate an underlying metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He denied any relevant prior medical or surgical conditions.

The glucagon stimulation test yielded serum C-peptide levels of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes post-injection, pointing to suboptimal insulin secretory capacity. This test assesses the functional reserve of pancreatic β-cells via measurement of glucagon-stimulated C-peptide. Clinically, a post-stimulation C-peptide exceeding 0.60 nmol/L is generally considered to reflect intact endogenous insulin production, while lower values are indicative of β-cell insufficiency. Despite its utility, the glucagon stimulation test should be interpreted as supplementary, especially if autoantibody analysis is inconclusive or if the clinical features are atypical. There was no evidence of microalbuminuria. Sudomotor evaluation, performed by measuring electrochemical skin conductance (for example, using Sudoscan), revealed markedly diminished sudomotor function in the hands, highlighting substantial small fibre autonomic neuropathy. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibody (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibody (IA-2A) assays returned negative. One month following diagnosis, genetic screening for MODY was conducted and identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), thereby confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased levels of HbA1c and plasma glucose. The initial therapeutic approach involved basal insulin degludec at a dose of eight units per day, together with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), indicative of a provisional working diagnosis of T2D. This regimen was selected to ensure prompt glycaemic stabilization while also considering underlying insulin resistance.

Alongside pharmacological interventions, the patient received recommendations to maintain a regulated diet and participate in consistent physical exercise, forming an integral component of holistic diabetes care. Subsequent assessments took place at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to track glycaemic parameters and evaluate treatment effectiveness.

Nonetheless, due to the combination of moderately retained yet suboptimal β-cell reserve as demonstrated by the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), the absence of islet autoantibodies, and a notable familial aggregation of early-onset diabetes, clinicians maintained a heightened suspicion for MODY.

Therapy was initiated prior to receiving results from genetic analysis, relying instead on the presenting clinical features and laboratory data available at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c was reduced to 9.1%. With genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and in light of the patient’s partial but insufficient β-cell function, as well as the observed HbA1c improvement, insulin therapy was discontinued. Instead, dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) was initiated to enhance glycaemic control and preserve residual pancreatic function. Over a 6-month period, the patient’s HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, demonstrating marked glycaemic improvement. Upon achieving stable glycaemic control, the treatment regimen was adjusted to a maintenance therapy consisting of metformin (1500 mg/day) combined with saxagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day). The patient continues to be monitored routinely, with HbA1c values consistently remaining below 6.5%. Thus far, there has been no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and vigilant monitoring for chronic complications continues.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, situated on chromosome 12 at the q24 locus, functions as a transcription factor. It contains 10 exons distributed over 23,790 base pairs and codes for a 631-amino acid protein that is present in multiple tissues such as the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Mutations within HNF1A result in the progressive loss of β-cell activity, evidenced by diminished expression of both insulin and GLUT2 genes and lowered pyruvate kinase function. This leads to impaired insulin secretion and subsequent glucose intolerance.

In this instance, the approach to diagnosis was consistent with established MODY diagnostic standards, which integrate clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and genetic confirmation. These standards include an early age of diabetes onset, strong familial clustering, postprandial glucose intolerance, and negative tests for diabetes-associated autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide measurement above 0.6 ng/mL points towards non-type 1 diabetes. Nonetheless, regular follow-up C-peptide assessments remain important, as early-phase type 1 diabetes can initially present with normal values. A key obstacle to MODY recognition is limited awareness and low clinical suspicion, often resulting in misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. To aid diagnosis, the Exeter group has developed a MODY probability calculator, which assists clinicians in determining the likelihood that a patient is an appropriate candidate for genetic testing. However, as the tool is based on data from White European individuals, it may yield inflated MODY probabilities when applied to non-White populations. For our patient, entering clinical information into this calculator indicated a 62.4% probability (approximately 1 in 1.6 likelihood) of MODY. The literature also contains reports of MODY cases with detectable autoantibodies; while most individuals with MODY lack autoantibodies, some patients harboring HNF1A mutations do present with them. Historically, individuals with MODY have exhibited lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome relative to those with type 2 diabetes, but increasing population-level obesity has reduced the discriminatory utility of BMI and metabolic syndrome in differentiating MODY from T2DM—potentially accounting for the higher BMI observed in our patient. Furthermore, while a family history of diabetes can be informative for MODY diagnosis, its diagnostic value may be diminished by factors such as under-reporting, the emergence of de novo mutations, and the broad prevalence of type 2 diabetes within families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on the patient’s age and HbA1c measurements. The initial recommended approach utilizes low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin release by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function diminishes over time, GLP-1 receptor agonists are recognized as a safer and potentially more efficacious substitute to sulfonylureas, especially for patients at heightened risk of hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small randomized, double-blind, crossover study indicates that liraglutide and glimepiride both successfully lower fasting and postprandial glucose concentrations, without notable differences in efficacy among those with HNF1A-MODY. However, treatment with GLP-1 receptor agonists carries a reduced likelihood of mild hypoglycaemia compared with glimepiride. Genetic diagnosis enabled cessation of insulin therapy after the patient’s glycaemic control was stabilized, facilitating a transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which continued to provide adequate glucose regulation. This underscores the pivotal role of genetic testing in selecting appropriate treatments. Glycosuria in individuals with HNF1A-MODY arises from decreased SGLT2 expression, since SGLT2 transcription is regulated by HNF1A. One study noted that patients with HNF1A-MODY could face severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis as a consequence of pronounced inhibition of remaining SGLT-2 activity. Therefore, the disadvantages of SGLT-2 inhibitors—such as elevated risks of dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may outweigh their therapeutic advantages for this population. In the present case, combination therapy with liraglutide and metformin was both effective and well tolerated, corroborating existing literature.

The application of precision medicine is particularly important for managing MODY, given that individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all established diagnostic criteria, even when adhering to current clinical protocols. At present, there remains a lack of reliable biomarkers to clearly distinguish HNF1A-MODY from both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. In a cohort study conducted by Chen et al, involving 2429 young Chinese adults with early-onset type 2 diabetes, MODY was found to have a prevalence rate of 1.5%, and 0.74% of participants carried pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY-associated variants. Complementary findings by Møller et al demonstrated that within a population-based sample of Danish Caucasian patients diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, 10% of a subset of 39 individuals without high-risk HLA haplotypes were found to possess mutations in the HNF1A gene. Another recent study from Russia undertook genetic screening in 506 patients suspected of hereditary diabetes without autoimmune manifestations—defined by negative tests for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at onset, sustained C-peptide production, minimal insulin requirements within five years, and lack of significant obesity. This investigation identified P or LP variants in 46.4% of subjects, with HNF1A being among the most frequently involved genes. Molecular confirmation of MODY relies on DNA sequencing. The advent of NGS has made large-scale screening for MODY more practical, enabling simultaneous analysis of extensive patient genomic data. Advances in NGS technology have facilitated the discovery of numerous novel genetic variants. The ACMG has established recommendations to assess the pathogenic nature of such variants. To date, databases like ClinVar catalogue more than 1000 unique HNF1A variants, spanning classifications from P and LP to benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In this study, the heterozygous HNF1A variant identified (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not listed in ClinVar or similar widely utilized variant repositories, underscoring its novelty rather than detracting from its clinical significance. Notably, the variant alters an amino acid adjacent to previously documented P residues, has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and shows familial segregation with diabetes, collectively satisfying ACMG standards for a LP’ categorization. Hence, although ClinVar serves as a critical compendium of documented variants, the pathogenic potential of this novel variant is substantiated through independent genetic and clinical lines of evidence in addition to the absence of prior database annotations.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the frequency of mutations in the transactivation domain is relatively low. The novel variant identified in the present case also resides within the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations suggest that, while some HNF1A mutations—specifically, P variants—exert limited influence on the function of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, some HNF1A variants, though insufficient on their own to cause MODY, may nonetheless contribute to a heightened susceptibility to T2D.

To summarize, this report underscores the critical role of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY in guiding personalized therapeutic interventions. Ongoing research efforts will prioritize the advancement of more robust NGS screening approaches to enhance the accuracy of MODY detection, thereby facilitating improved cost-effectiveness and better clinical outcomes.
