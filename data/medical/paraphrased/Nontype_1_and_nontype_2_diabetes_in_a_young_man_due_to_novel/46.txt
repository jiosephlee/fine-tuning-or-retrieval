Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a monogenic diabetes type with autosomal dominant inheritance. This case report details a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY attributed to a novel heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history revealed multiple members with early-onset diabetes. Genetic analysis identified a previously unrecognized pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Initiation of therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist combined with metformin resulted in a decrease in HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% over 3 months. This report underscores the critical role of genetic evaluation in cases of early-onset diabetes—often inaccurately classified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and broadens the known range of HNF1A mutations pertinent to individualized medical care.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes that result from inherent abnormalities in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders generally present and are identified prior to 25 years of age. MODY accounts for roughly 1%–6% of diabetes diagnoses among young individuals. Its prevalence differs across ethnic and racial populations and may be underrecognized in some groups. Historically, studies have focused predominantly on populations of White European descent. More recent investigations have assessed the occurrence of MODY in Asian and Australian cohorts. Reported prevalence rates for MODY display substantial variability, largely attributable to genetic testing being restricted to patients exhibiting particular clinical features, even in broader population-based research.

More than 15 gene mutations have been associated with the monogenic MODY phenotype, each offering distinct treatment approaches. The bulk of MODY cases across its various subtypes stem primarily from mutations in genes such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta.

HNF1A-MODY is the most frequent form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses, and is characterized by an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, with affected families showing a clear pattern of symptomatic diabetes transmission. During early adolescence, patients often lack overt symptoms; elevated blood glucose concentrations are frequently detected incidentally during routine laboratory screenings, usually in the absence of signs indicating insulin resistance. Assessment of postprandial blood glucose or administration of a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal marked increases in glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Furthermore, glycosuria may be noted, resulting from a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired glucose transport within the renal tubules. At advanced stages, patients may develop symptoms including polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintended weight loss—manifestations similar to those found in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Because MODY can share clinical characteristics with type 1 and T2D, distinguishing it from these more common forms of diabetes poses ongoing diagnostic challenges.

Several diagnostic protocols for MODY have been outlined in the literature. Following clinical evaluation, including physical examination and assessment of family history that indicate a phenotype consistent with MODY, and demonstration of absence of autoantibodies typically associated with type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—in addition to supporting biochemical investigations, molecular genetic analysis is pivotal in establishing the diagnosis. Accurate identification of the MODY subtype is essential for guiding optimal treatment, predicting possible complications, determining prognosis, and providing genetic counseling to the family. Recent research has revealed hundreds of distinct HNF1A gene mutations associated with HNF1A-MODY.

In the present case, genetic screening through a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel identified a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient with HNF1A-MODY. The pathogenicity of this alteration was assessed following the guidelines for variant interpretation provided by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG). This specific mutation was not documented in published literature, nor listed in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient granted written informed consent for the publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment of elevated blood glucose and presence of glucose in urine, which were detected during his routine annual check-up. Upon physical examination, his body mass index measured 24.6 kg/m^2, blood pressure registered at 112/70 mmHg, and his pulse rate was 78 beats per minute. No signs of acanthosis nigricans were identified on exam. The patient’s family history revealed multiple cases of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at 20 years old. There was no reported incidence of premature cardiovascular disease among diabetic relatives.

Investigations:

The patient's HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol) and fasting glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. After a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, the 2-hour glucose value reached 19.8 mmol/L. No evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis was observed. The individual described polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent weight loss. Lipid analysis showed a high-density lipoprotein of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings consistent with potential metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). There was no notable prior medical or surgical background.

A glucagon stimulation test yielded serum C-peptide concentrations of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, suggesting inadequate insulin production. This assessment is employed to estimate remaining β-cell activity through the measurement of stimulated C-peptide after administration of glucagon. In clinical settings, stimulated C-peptide values above 0.60 nmol/L generally indicate preservation of endogenous insulin secretion, whereas lower results point to compromised β-cell function. Nevertheless, the glucagon stimulation test serves mainly as an adjunct tool, especially when autoantibody testing is indeterminate or clinical findings are unusual. No microalbuminuria was found. Evaluation of sudomotor activity using electrochemical skin conductance (such as Sudoscan) — a non-invasive approach for assessing small fiber autonomic nerves — demonstrated severely diminished function in the hands, indicative of marked autonomic impairment. Testing for autoantibodies, specifically GADA and IA-2A, returned negative results. Genetic screening for MODY was conducted one month following diagnosis and detected a heterozygous pathogenic (P) mutation in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose values. The first-line therapeutic approach included a basal regimen of insulin degludec (eight units daily), along with metformin (1500 mg daily) and saxagliptin (5 mg daily), based on the provisional assessment of T2D. This protocol was selected to enable prompt glycaemic stabilization and target possible insulin resistance.

Alongside pharmacologic intervention, recommendations were given for the patient to follow a structured diet and participate in routine exercise as elements of comprehensive diabetes care. Ongoing follow-up occurred in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient setting, with the goal of tracking glycaemic outcomes and evaluating therapeutic effectiveness.

Nevertheless, moderate but inadequate β-cell activity on glucagon stimulation testing (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a strong family history of early-onset diabetes raised substantial suspicion for MODY.

Treatment was initiated based on clinical findings and laboratory data prior to receiving results from genetic testing.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. In light of the genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and evaluation of the patient’s partially maintained but still inadequate β-cell activity, combined with the observed HbA1c improvement to 9.1%, insulin was discontinued and substituted with dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, administered at 1.5 mg once weekly) to help achieve glycaemic targets and sustain pancreatic function. Six months after initiating dulaglutide, HbA1c further decreased to 6.0%, reflecting notable glycaemic enhancement. Once control stabilized, treatment was streamlined to include metformin (1500 mg daily) along with saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg daily) for ongoing maintenance. The patient continues to attend scheduled follow-ups, with HbA1c levels consistently maintained under 6.5%. There have been no indications of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and monitoring for potential long-term complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene codes for a transcription factor found in the q24 region on chromosome 12. It contains 10 exons distributed across 23,790 base pairs and produces a protein consisting of 631 amino acids, which is present in multiple organs, such as the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Mutations in HNF1A cause a gradual loss of β-cell activity, characterized by reduced insulin and GLUT2 gene expression, along with diminished pyruvate kinase function. This progressive dysfunction leads to reduced insulin output and impaired glucose tolerance.

For this case, diagnosis followed established MODY criteria, incorporating clinical, laboratory, and genetic data. These include diabetes developing early in life, a strong familial background, abnormal postprandial glucose levels, and absence of autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide concentration exceeding 0.6 ng/mL supports a non-type 1 diabetes diagnosis, but ongoing assessment of C-peptide remains important, as early type 1 diabetes can present with normal values. One major challenge in diagnosing MODY is low awareness and insufficient clinical suspicion, resulting in frequent misclassification as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator from the Exeter group can assist clinicians in determining patients suitable for genetic evaluation. Nevertheless, as this calculator was developed using data from White European cohorts, it may produce inflated MODY probabilities among individuals from other ethnic backgrounds. When our patient's data were entered into the calculator, MODY risk was calculated at 62.4% (equivalent to 1 in 1.6). Multiple case reports have noted the presence of autoantibodies in MODY patients, and some with HNF1A variants may test positive, although most MODY cases are autoantibody negative. Previous research indicates that MODY patients tend to have lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to those with type 2 diabetes. However, with increasing obesity rates, distinguishing MODY from T2DM using BMI or metabolic syndrome is less reliable, potentially accounting for the elevated BMI observed in our patient. Although family history plays a significant role in MODY diagnosis, its effectiveness can be affected by factors such as under-reporting, de novo mutations, and the high incidence of T2DM in families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on the patient’s age and HbA1c values. Low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin secretion via modulation of potassium-sensitive ATP channels, are the preferred initial therapy. As β-cell function declines, GLP-1 receptor agonists may offer a safer and more efficacious option compared to sulfonylureas, especially in individuals susceptible to hypoglycaemia. A randomised, double-blind, crossover study found both liraglutide and glimepiride significantly decreased fasting plasma glucose and mitigated postprandial glucose excursions, with no notable differences in effectiveness between the agents in patients with HNF1A-MODY. GLP-1 receptor agonists also demonstrated a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Following genetic confirmation, we were able to halt insulin therapy once glycaemic control was achieved and successfully switch the patient to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, maintaining stable glucose levels. This underscores the importance of genetic testing in directing clinical management. Glycosuria observed in HNF1A-MODY is a result of diminished SGLT2 expression regulated by HNF1A transcription. Previous reports have suggested that individuals with HNF1A-MODY might be at risk for severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to pronounced inhibition of remaining SGLT-2 function. The adverse effects of SGLT-2 inhibitors in this population—including increased likelihood of dehydration, possible vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may surpass their therapeutic advantages. In the present case, treatment with liraglutide and metformin proved both efficacious and safe, corroborating earlier research findings.

Precision medicine is vital for the management of MODY, particularly as individuals with HNF1A-MODY may not fully satisfy conventional diagnostic criteria, even when existing clinical protocols are applied. At present, there is a lack of reliable biomarkers for distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Chen et al reported that in a group of 2,429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D, MODY was identified in 1.5%, with 0.74% possessing pathogenic or likely pathogenic MODY-related genetic variants. Møller et al demonstrated that, within a sizable population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian type 1 diabetes patients, 10% of 39 individuals without high-risk HLA haplotypes had HNF1A gene mutations. Additionally, a recent study in Russia evaluated 506 patients suspected of monogenic forms of diabetes based on negative tests for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, preserved C-peptide secretion, minimal insulin requirements in the first five years, and no significant obesity. This investigation detected pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in 46.4% of participants, with HNF1A being among the frequently implicated genes. Confirming clinical suspicion of MODY requires DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) offers the capability to analyze extensive genetic material from patients in a single assay, enhancing the practicality of MODY testing. Advances in NGS technology have facilitated the discovery of numerous genetic variants. The ACMG has established protocols to determine the pathogenic nature of these variants. In databases like ClinVar, over 1,000 unique HNF1A variants have been catalogued, classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, or of uncertain significance. In the present case, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A mutation (c.809A>G, p.N270S) is not documented in ClinVar or other popular variant repositories, underscoring its novelty rather than challenging its clinical importance. Notably, this mutation affects an amino acid adjacent to previously established pathogenic sites, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and is inherited among affected family members, all meeting ACMG criteria for likely pathogenic classification. Therefore, although ClinVar is instrumental in tracking reported variants, the pathogenic status of this particular mutation is corroborated by multiple independent genetic and clinical factors in addition to database annotation.

Research has demonstrated that most observed mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the mutation frequency is lower in the transactivation domain. The novel variant described in this report resides in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have noted that HNF1A mutations, such as P, exert only minor influences on the functional activity of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants that do not independently induce MODY may still confer a higher susceptibility to T2D.

To summarize, this case underscores the critical role of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its influence on patient-specific therapeutic approaches. Ongoing studies aim to establish improved NGS screening methods to enhance MODY detection, ultimately facilitating more effective resource allocation and improved clinical outcomes.
