Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents an autosomal dominant, single-gene form of diabetes. This case report presents an individual with HNF1A-MODY resulting from a newly identified heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha gene. The patient was found to have elevated blood glucose and glycosuria, but did not exhibit diabetic ketoacidosis. A significant family history of diabetes diagnosed at a young age was observed. Molecular analysis identified a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Initiation of a GLP-1 receptor agonist combined with metformin lowered the patient’s HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. The case underscores the value of genetic evaluation in individuals with early-onset diabetes, a condition often inaccurately classified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and adds a new HNF1A mutation to those relevant for individualized treatment approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes resulting from a primary impairment in the development and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders usually present and are identified before age 25. MODY accounts for roughly 1% to 6% of diabetes cases with early onset. The frequency of MODY differs across ethnicities and racial backgrounds and may be underreported. Most existing studies have focused predominantly on populations of White European descent, while recent research has started to evaluate MODY prevalence in Asian and Australian regions. Reported prevalence rates for MODY show wide variation, often attributable to the practice of restricting genetic testing to individuals selected on clinical grounds, including in population-based studies.

Pathogenic mutations in more than 15 different genes have been associated with the monogenic presentation of MODY, with each genetic variant linked to distinct treatment approaches. Most reported cases across the various MODY forms are attributed to alterations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. HNF1A-MODY accounts for the largest proportion of MODY cases, comprising approximately 30%–65% of all identified cases, and is marked by a clear autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of symptomatic diabetes within families. During early adolescence, the condition often lacks obvious symptoms, and hyperglycemia is frequently detected incidentally during routine screening, in the absence of any overt clinical signs of insulin resistance. Measurement of blood glucose following meals, or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), can reveal marked elevations in postprandial glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be observed, which results from a lowered renal threshold for glucose due to impaired reabsorption within the renal tubules. As the disease advances, classic symptoms such as excessive thirst, frequent urination, increased appetite, and unintentional weight loss—similar to clinical presentations of type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may emerge. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 diabetes and T2D remains challenging due to these shared clinical manifestations.

Various diagnostic protocols for MODY have been delineated in the literature. When clinical assessment and family history indicate a MODY phenotype, and autoantibodies typical for type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, together with corroborating biochemical evaluations, molecular genetic testing becomes essential in establishing the diagnosis. Accurate identification of the specific MODY subtype guides appropriate therapeutic management, informs the risk of complications, aids in prognostication, and supports genetic counselling for affected families. Recent research has revealed that hundreds of unique mutations in the HNF1A gene can cause HNF1A-MODY.

In this reported case, genetic analysis using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel uncovered a novel heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY. Pathogenicity was evaluated in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation. This particular mutation has not previously been described in the literature, nor is it listed in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment due to the detection of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria at his routine annual checkup. Physical examination revealed a body mass index of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure reading of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. There were no findings of acanthosis nigricans. His family history was significant for early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother had been diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. There was no reported family history of premature cardiovascular disease in those relatives affected by diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting plasma glucose was recorded as 12.4 mmol/L. Two hours following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. No evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis was identified. The patient described polydipsia, increased hunger, and recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid studies showed an HDL cholesterol of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides measured at 4.31 mmol/L, pointing to a likely metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). There was no relevant past medical or surgical history.

Upon glucagon stimulation testing, the serum C-peptide was 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and rose to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, reflecting inadequate insulin production. This assessment gauges β-cell reserve by quantifying the C-peptide response after glucagon administration. Clinically, stimulated C-peptide values above 0.60 nmol/L are indicative of continued endogenous insulin output, while readings below this threshold signify β-cell impairment. Nevertheless, interpretation of the glucagon stimulation test should be supplementary and not conclusive, especially where autoantibody findings are ambiguous or the clinical scenario is uncharacteristic. Microalbuminuria testing yielded negative results. Sudomotor evaluation indicated severely diminished sudomotor activity in both hands. This was conducted using electrochemical skin conductance assessment (e.g., Sudoscan), a non-invasive approach measuring small fiber autonomic function. Markedly decreased conductance in the hands was identified, highlighting pronounced autonomic neuropathy. Serologic assays for GAD antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) were both negative. Genetic evaluation for MODY, performed one month after diagnosis, detected a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), confirming HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly raised HbA1c and plasma glucose values. The initial therapeutic approach involved administration of basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, alongside metformin at 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin at 5 mg daily, reflecting an initial assessment consistent with T2D. The intention of this regimen was to swiftly normalize blood glucose while targeting possible insulin resistance.

Alongside medications, the patient was counseled to maintain a structured diet and participate in routine physical exercise as components of a holistic diabetes management strategy. Subsequent assessments were performed at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to evaluate glycaemic stability and treatment efficacy.

Despite these measures, due to moderately retained but inadequate β-cell function evidenced by glucagon stimulation testing (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a significant family history of early-onset diabetes, clinicians maintained a strong suspicion for MODY.

Therapy was initiated prior to receiving genetic testing outcomes, based upon the patient’s clinical features and laboratory results at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c was reduced to 9.1%. With the genetic diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY established and taking into account the patient's moderately preserved but still insufficient β-cell reserve, as well as the observed HbA1c reduction, insulin therapy was stopped and dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg administered weekly) was initiated to enhance glycaemic regulation and protect residual pancreatic function. At the six-month mark, HbA1c further improved to 6.0%, reflecting marked glycaemic benefit. Once glycaemic control stabilized, the treatment approach was transitioned to a simpler regimen comprising metformin (1500 mg/day) combined with saxagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day) for sustained management. The patient continues to attend routine follow-up appointments, maintaining HbA1c levels below 6.5%. Currently, there are no indications of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and ongoing monitoring for chronic complications is maintained.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, situated on chromosome 12 at q24, functions as a transcription factor. It contains 10 exons over a span of 23,790 base pairs, coding for a protein comprised of 631 amino acids. Expression of this protein occurs in several organs, such as the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Mutations in HNF1A result in a gradual loss of β-cell activity, characterized by decreased expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes and diminished pyruvate kinase activity. This process leads to reduced insulin production and impaired glucose tolerance.

The diagnostic approach for this case followed established MODY criteria, which include clinical, biochemical, and genetic components, such as an early age at diabetes onset, notable family history, postprandial glucose intolerance, and negative diabetes autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide level above 0.6 ng/mL supports a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes. Continued C-peptide measurement remains necessary, as values may be normal in the early phases of type 1 diabetes. Insufficient recognition and clinical suspicion create obstacles to MODY identification, with affected individuals frequently diagnosed with type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator, developed by the Exeter group, assists clinicians in selecting patients for genetic evaluation, although it was based on data from a White European population and may produce overestimated MODY probabilities in non-White populations. Entry of our patient’s clinical data into this calculator yielded an estimated MODY probability of 62.4% (equivalent to a 1 in 1.6 chance). Multiple case reports have documented autoantibody presence in MODY patients, with some individuals carrying HNF1A mutations presenting with autoantibodies, although most MODY cases lack autoantibodies. Earlier research indicates lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome among MODY patients compared with those who have type 2 diabetes. However, due to increasing obesity prevalence, BMI and metabolic syndrome are less reliable for differentiating MODY from T2DM, which may account for the elevated BMI observed in this instance. Moreover, while family history contributes to MODY diagnosis, its utility is restricted by factors such as under-reporting, occurrence of novel mutations, and the widespread existence of T2DM within families.

Therapeutic management for HNF1A-MODY depends on the patient’s age and HbA1c values. Sulfonylureas at low doses are considered the primary option; they promote insulin secretion by targeting ATP-dependent potassium channels. As β-cell function deteriorates in HNF1A-MODY and in patients vulnerable to hypoglycaemia, GLP-1 receptor agonists may provide a safer and potentially superior alternative to sulfonylureas. A small randomized, double-blind, crossover study demonstrated that both liraglutide and glimepiride decrease fasting plasma glucose and control postprandial glucose excursions with comparable efficacy among individuals with HNF1A-MODY. Furthermore, GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a decreased incidence of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic diagnosis allowed for the cessation of insulin after achieving glycaemic control, enabling the patient’s transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which sustained adequate glucose regulation. This case underscores the essential role of genetic testing for therapeutic guidance. Glycosuria observed in HNF1A-MODY stems from diminished SGLT2 expression, regulated transcriptionally by HNF1A. A published report noted that patients with HNF1A-MODY can develop pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis as a result of pronounced inhibition of the remaining SGLT-2 activity. The complications linked to SGLT-2 inhibitor use in HNF1A-MODY—such as heightened dehydration risk, possible vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may surpass the anticipated benefits of these drugs. In the presented scenario, the combination of liraglutide and metformin achieved safe and effective glycaemic control, aligning with findings from earlier investigations.

The importance of precision medicine for MODY is underscored by the fact that individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all diagnostic criteria outlined in current clinical guidelines. At present, there are no adequate biomarkers capable of distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Chen et al demonstrated that MODY accounted for 1.5% among 2429 young Chinese adults presenting with early-onset T2D, with 0.74% carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Additionally, Møller et al reported that in a population-based study involving Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 patients who lacked high-risk HLA haplotypes harbored HNF1A mutations. Another recent population-based investigation in Russia analyzed 506 individuals suspected to have hereditary diabetes without autoimmune markers—defined by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, continued C-peptide production, minimal insulin needs within the first five years, and absence of marked obesity. Pathogenic or LP variants were found in 46.4% of participants, with HNF1A being one of the most frequently implicated genes. Confirming a MODY diagnosis relies on DNA sequencing, which is indispensable for supporting clinical suspicion. The application of next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables comprehensive analysis of patient DNA in a single assay, facilitating broader MODY screening. NGS has led to the identification of numerous novel genetic variants, and the ACMG offers recommendations for determining variant pathogenicity. Databases such as ClinVar have documented over 1000 unique HNF1A variants, encompassing pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In our patient, a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not listed in ClinVar or other widely used variant databases, emphasizing its novelty rather than questioning its clinical significance. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid residue adjacent to recognized pathogenic positions, has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and demonstrates familial segregation with diabetes, all of which correspond with ACMG guidelines for a likely pathogenic classification. Therefore, although ClinVar is instrumental in cataloguing known variants, the evidence supporting the pathogenic nature of this novel variant derives from multiple independent genetic and clinical findings beyond the confines of existing databases.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are concentrated in the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant identified in this patient is also localized to the DNA-binding domain. Certain studies suggest that some HNF1A mutations, though present, exert only limited influence on HNF1A protein functionality. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants that do not independently result in MODY may be linked to a heightened risk of developing T2D.

To summarize, this case underscores the necessity for precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its significance for tailoring individual therapy. Ongoing investigations aim to establish enhanced NGS-based screening methods in order to increase MODY detection rates, optimize healthcare expenditures, and maximize clinical outcomes.
