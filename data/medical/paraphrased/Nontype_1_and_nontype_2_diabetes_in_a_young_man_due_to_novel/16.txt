Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a monogenic, autosomal dominant type of diabetes. This report details a case involving HNF1A-MODY resulting from a novel heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. A significant family history of early-onset diabetes was present. Genetic analysis identified a pathogenic HNF1A variant not previously reported. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist combined with metformin achieved a reduction in glycated haemoglobin levels from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This case underscores the critical role of genetic evaluation in early-onset diabetes, a condition often misclassified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and broadens the known spectrum of HNF1A mutations with relevance to precision medicine.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) comprises a set of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes marked by a primary abnormality in pancreatic β-cell formation and function. The onset and diagnosis of these disorders usually occur before 25 years of age. MODY accounts for approximately 1%–6% of diabetes diagnosed at a young age. Prevalence rates can differ among various racial and ethnic populations and may be underestimated. Historically, most investigations have focused on White European cohorts. More recently, the frequency of MODY has also been explored in populations from Asia and Australia. Reported prevalence rates for MODY vary widely, a difference that often arises because genetic testing is commonly restricted to individuals meeting certain clinical criteria, even within population-based studies.

Pathogenic variants in more than 15 genes have been associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, and each gene alteration guides specific therapeutic approaches. The majority of diagnosed MODY cases stem from mutations in genes such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. HNF1A-MODY is the most common MODY subtype, accounting for approximately 30% to 65% of all cases. This form displays a clear autosomal dominant pattern of symptomatic diabetes within affected families. In early adolescence, clinical signs are often lacking, and elevated blood glucose is commonly discovered incidentally through standard blood tests, typically without evidence of insulin resistance. Monitoring postprandial blood glucose or administering a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal a marked elevation in post-meal glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria can also occur, explained by a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption owing to impaired renal tubular glucose transport. Nonetheless, as the condition progresses, patients may develop symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss, paralleling manifestations seen in type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 or T2D remains challenging due to significant clinical overlap.

Multiple diagnostic pathways for MODY have been described in recent research. When clinical evaluation, family history, and physical examination raise suspicion for MODY, and autoantibodies commonly associated with type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are negative, molecular genetic analysis becomes an important tool in confirming the diagnosis, especially alongside corroborating biochemical investigations. Establishing an accurate diagnosis is essential to guide therapeutic decisions, anticipate potential complications, provide prognostic information, and support genetic counseling for families. Recent data have indicated that mutations numbering in the hundreds within the HNF1A gene account for HNF1A-MODY.

In the present case, we detected a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene, in a patient with HNF1A-MODY through next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel analysis. Pathogenicity was evaluated based on the criteria set forth by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) for variant interpretation. This particular mutation has not been documented in published literature nor referenced in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was referred after detection of hyperglycemia and glycosuria at his yearly health check. On physical assessment, his body mass index measured 24.6 kg/m², his blood pressure was 112/70 mmHg, and his pulse was 78 beats per minute. No evidence of acanthosis nigricans was found on physical examination. The family history revealed several cases of diabetes with early onset: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at the age of 20. There was no reported incidence of premature cardiovascular disease in those family members with diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g OGTT, his 2-hour glucose was 19.8 mmol/L. Diabetic ketoacidosis was absent. The patient described experiencing polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent unintended weight reduction. Lipid analysis demonstrated an HDL level of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, which are compatible with possible metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He had no notable past medical or surgical history.

A glucagon stimulation test revealed serum C-peptide concentrations of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L after 6 minutes, reflecting inadequate endogenous insulin production. This assessment measures residual β-cell function by evaluating C-peptide levels following glucagon administration. Clinically, stimulated C-peptide exceeding 0.60 nmol/L suggests retained endogenous insulin secretion; values below this threshold imply β-cell impairment. However, the glucagon stimulation test is considered adjunctive, particularly in cases with equivocal autoantibody status or non-classical disease manifestation. No microalbuminuria was present. Electrochemical skin conductance testing (such as Sudoscan) was utilized to assess sudomotor function, demonstrating profound reduction in the hands, consistent with marked autonomic neuropathy. Testing for GADA and IA-2A antibodies was negative. Genetic testing for MODY conducted one month after diagnosis identified a heterozygous, pathogenic (P) variant in HNF1A—specifically, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)—indicative of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The initial therapeutic approach included basal insulin degludec at a dose of eight units per day, in addition to metformin 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin 5 mg daily, consistent with an initial working diagnosis of T2D. This regimen was selected to quickly establish glycaemic control and to target possible insulin resistance.

Alongside medication, the patient received recommendations to follow a regulated diet and incorporate consistent physical exercise, as components of an all-encompassing diabetes care plan. Follow-up appointments at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic were scheduled to evaluate glycaemic parameters and monitor the effectiveness of the chosen therapy.

Nonetheless, the observation of moderately retained but inadequate β-cell capacity during the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), in combination with the absence of islet autoantibodies and a notable family history of early-onset diabetes, resulted in a strong suspicion for MODY.

Initial treatment was started prior to the availability of genetic testing results, based on the clinical presentation and laboratory findings at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. Genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and the finding of partially retained but inadequate β-cell function, combined with the HbA1c improvement to 9.1%, led to discontinuation of insulin therapy in favor of dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg weekly), with the goal of enhancing glycemic control and preserving pancreatic function. At the 6-month mark, HbA1c had further declined to 6.0%, demonstrating marked glycemic improvement. With stabilization of glycemic control, the treatment regimen was adjusted to metformin (1500 mg/day) combined with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day) for ongoing maintenance. The patient continues to be monitored regularly, and HbA1c has consistently stayed below 6.5%. There is no current evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and routine screening for chronic complications remains in place.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, positioned at the q24 region on chromosome 12, encodes a transcription factor consisting of 10 exons across 23,790 base pairs, yielding a protein made up of 631 amino acids. This protein is expressed in multiple tissues such as the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Alterations in HNF1A result in a gradual deterioration of β-cell function, demonstrated by decreased activity of pyruvate kinase and compromised expression of both insulin and GLUT2 genes. This sequence leads to impaired insulin production and eventual glucose intolerance.

The assessment in this case followed established diagnostic criteria for MODY, which integrate clinical, biochemical, and genetic factors. These include early diabetes onset, a robust familial pattern, evidence of postprandial hyperglycemia, and negative autoimmune markers. A fasting C-peptide concentration above 0.6 ng/mL indicates the likelihood of a non-type 1 diabetes diagnosis; nevertheless, continued surveillance of C-peptide is vital, since normal values might appear during the early course of type 1 diabetes. Diagnosis of MODY is frequently hindered by limited awareness and suspicion among clinicians, resulting in frequent misclassification as either type 1 diabetes or T2D. To aid in identifying suitable patients for genetic evaluation, the Exeter group created the MODY probability calculator. However, since this tool was derived from White European populations, it may inflate the likelihood of MODY in patients from other ethnicities. Applying the patient's clinical data to this calculator yielded a MODY probability of 62.4% (equivalent to a 1 in 1.6 likelihood). Although most MODY patients do not have autoantibodies, reports exist of MODY individuals—including those with HNF1A variants—who present with positive autoantibodies. Historically, lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome have been observed in MODY in contrast to T2D, but increasing prevalence of obesity has reduced the discriminative ability of BMI and metabolic syndrome status, possibly contributing to the higher BMI in this patient. Furthermore, although a positive family history is a key MODY criterion, its diagnostic value is sometimes restricted by factors such as under-reporting, spontaneous mutations, and the widespread occurrence of T2D within families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by the patient's age and glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) measurements. Initial treatment typically includes low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin release by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function deteriorates in HNF1A-MODY individuals, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists represent a viable and potentially safer substitute for sulfonylureas, particularly for those with a higher risk of hypoglycaemia. Findings from a small, randomized, double-blind, crossover study showed that both liraglutide and glimepiride significantly decreased fasting and postprandial plasma glucose variations, with no marked difference in effectiveness between them in patients with HNF1A-MODY. Notably, GLP-1 receptor agonist use was associated with fewer episodes of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. In our patient, genetic confirmation of the diagnosis enabled the withdrawal of insulin following achievement of adequate glycaemic control, after which therapy was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist with continued effective glucose regulation. This underscores the pivotal role of genetic testing in selecting appropriate therapy. The presence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY stems from decreased sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) expression, regulated by HNF1A-dependent transcription. Published data indicate that individuals with HNF1A-MODY may be at risk of severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis owing to marked inhibition of residual SGLT-2 function. Therefore, the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors in these patients may present significant hazards, including exacerbated dehydration, risk of vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, which could surpass any potential benefit. In this instance, the combination of liraglutide and metformin resulted in safe and effective glycaemic control, in line with prior reports.

Tailored therapeutic approaches are vital in MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all guideline-based diagnostic parameters. At present, there are no established biomarkers capable of reliably distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes (T2D). Chen et al conducted a study involving 2429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D and found that MODY was present in 1.5% of the cohort, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY-associated variants. In a large, population-based sample of Danish Caucasians with type 1 diabetes, Møller et al demonstrated that, among 39 patients who lacked high-risk HLA haplotypes, 10% carried HNF1A gene mutations. Likewise, a recent population-based investigation in Russia assessed 506 patients with presumed hereditary diabetes without autoimmune markers (with negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibody results at diagnosis, sustained C-peptide secretion, reduced insulin requirements for up to five years, and the absence of severe obesity). This analysis detected P or LP variants in 46.4% of participants, with HNF1A ranking among the most prevalent genes involved. Genetic sequencing is necessary to validate MODY diagnoses. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) makes it possible to process extensive segments of patient DNA in one procedure, enhancing the practicality of MODY identification. The advent of NGS has led to the identification of numerous previously unreported genetic changes. The ACMG sets forth standards to ascertain whether such variants should be classified as P. Databases such as ClinVar list more than 1000 unique HNF1A variants encompassing P, LP, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In this report, we identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) which was not represented in ClinVar or other widely referenced databases, indicating its novel status as opposed to undermining its clinical importance. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid position adjacent to established P sites, possesses a high AlphaMissense predictive score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes within the family, all findings consistent with an ACMG-based LP classification. Consequently, even though repositories such as ClinVar are instrumental for variant annotation, the likelihood of pathogenicity for this mutation is further substantiated by separate genetic and phenotypic evidence apart from database listings.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a comparatively lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is also located in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have indicated that some HNF1A mutations, specifically the P type, exert limited influence on the functionality of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants that do not independently cause MODY may nonetheless be linked to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case emphasizes the critical role of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and how it informs personalized treatment decisions. Ongoing research will be directed toward the development of more efficient NGS-based screening approaches, aiming to enhance the detection rate of MODY while also improving cost efficiency and clinical outcomes.
