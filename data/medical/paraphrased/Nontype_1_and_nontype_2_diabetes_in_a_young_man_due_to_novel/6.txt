Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a single-gene, autosomal dominant type of diabetes. This paper presents a patient diagnosed with hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A)-MODY resulting from a previously unidentified heterozygous mutation in HNF1A. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose and urinary glucose, but without diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient's family history revealed multiple cases of early-onset diabetes. Molecular analysis revealed a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A. The initiation of therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist combined with metformin led to a decline in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over 3 months. This report underscores the value of molecular genetic testing in cases of early-onset diabetes—which are often incorrectly diagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and contributes new data regarding HNF1A variants that are significant for precision medicine approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a set of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes that stem from intrinsic defects in the formation and operation of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders usually present themselves and are identified before individuals reach 25 years of age. MODY accounts for about 1%–6% of diabetes cases arising in younger individuals. The frequency of MODY may differ across various ethnic and racial groups, with actual rates likely being higher than currently reported. Investigations have so far primarily targeted White European cohorts, while more recent efforts have extended to populations in Asian and Australian settings. Reported prevalence figures for MODY are highly variable, which is frequently attributed to the fact that genetic testing is often restricted to those selected on the basis of particular clinical criteria, even in studies drawing on broader population samples.

More than 15 genes have been associated with the development of the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with mutations in each gene leading to distinct therapeutic approaches. The bulk of MODY cases reported can be attributed to alterations in key genes, including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. Among these, HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent subtype, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of MODY diagnoses, and is characterized by an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern with overt diabetes symptoms present across multiple family members. During early adolescence, clinical manifestations often remain absent, and hyperglycemia is frequently discovered incidentally during routine screenings, typically in the absence of evidence for insulin resistance. Measurement of postprandial glucose levels or performing a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may uncover a marked elevation in post-meal plasma glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Furthermore, glycosuria may be observed, attributed to a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired tubular glucose transport. In advanced disease stages, classical symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss can emerge, mirroring those seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). This substantial clinical overlap complicates the differential diagnosis between MODY, type 1 diabetes, and T2D.

Multiple diagnostic strategies for MODY have been delineated in the literature. When physical evaluation and review of familial health patterns raise suspicion for a MODY phenotype, and when autoimmune markers typically present in type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, further biochemical investigations provide additional confirmation. At this juncture, molecular genetic analysis becomes indispensable for establishing a definitive diagnosis. Accurate identification of MODY subtypes is essential for selecting an appropriate therapeutic regimen, predicting disease progression, estimating risk for complications, and enabling targeted family counseling. Recent investigations have reported the identification of hundreds of mutations in the HNF1A gene as underlying causes of HNF1A-MODY.

In the present case, we detected a previously unreported heterozygous missense variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A, in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY through next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel testing. The pathogenicity of this alteration was assessed based on the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) standards for variant classification. This specific mutation has not been documented in existing scientific publications, nor is it present in either the VarSome or ClinVar databases. Written informed consent was secured from the patient to publish this case study.

Case presentation:

A young adult presented for assessment following the detection of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria at his routine annual health screening. Physical evaluation revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. The physical examination did not show any signs of acanthosis nigricans. His family history indicated multiple cases of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at the age of 20. No relatives with diabetes had a documented history of premature cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

The patient's glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), while fasting plasma glucose was found to be 12.4 mmol/L. Following administration of a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), the 2-hour glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. No clinical features of diabetic ketoacidosis were observed. He described symptoms including heightened thirst and appetite as well as recent weight reduction. Analysis of the lipid profile showed a high-density lipoprotein level of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, suggestive of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). The patient did not report any relevant past medical or surgical issues.

A glucagon stimulation test revealed a serum C-peptide concentration of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline (0 min) and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 min, indicating diminished insulin production. This test measures the ability of residual pancreatic β-cells to secrete insulin in response to glucagon, as assessed by stimulated C-peptide concentrations. In clinical application, stimulated C-peptide values exceeding 0.60 nmol/L typically reflect the preservation of endogenous insulin secretion, whereas lower results are indicative of β-cell impairment. It is important to note that the glucagon stimulation test should be interpreted as adjunctive evidence, particularly when autoantibody status is unclear or clinical findings are atypical. No microalbuminuria was identified. Sudomotor evaluation, performed through electrochemical skin conductance (for instance, using Sudoscan), demonstrated a marked reduction in sudomotor function in the hands, signifying significant autonomic dysfunction. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) were undetectable. At one month after diagnosis, molecular genetic testing for MODY identified a heterozygous, pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased levels of HbA1c and plasma glucose. The first-line therapeutic approach included basal insulin degludec at a dosage of eight units per day, along with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), corresponding to an initial working diagnosis of T2D. The primary objective was to promptly normalize glycaemic levels while also mitigating suspected insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient received recommendations to follow a regimented diet and to participate in consistent physical exercise, forming a holistic strategy for diabetes management. Ongoing assessments were performed in the endocrinology and metabolism clinic to track glycaemic outcomes and evaluate the effectiveness of therapy.

Nonetheless, due to only moderate but insufficient β-cell reserve observed during the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a notable family history of early-onset diabetes, there remained a considerable clinical likelihood of MODY.

The choice to initiate treatment was made before genetic testing results were available, relying on the clinical features and laboratory data collected at presentation.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient's HbA1c was reduced to 9.1%. In light of the confirmed diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY through genetic testing, the observation of moderately sustained, albeit insufficient, β-cell activity, and the improvement in HbA1c, insulin therapy was discontinued. Dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist administered at 1.5 mg weekly, was introduced with the aim of enhancing glycaemic control and preserving pancreatic function. Six months into this treatment, the patient’s HbA1c had declined further to 6.0%, demonstrating marked clinical improvement. With stabilization of glycaemic levels, the therapeutic regimen was transitioned to a maintenance protocol comprising metformin (1500 mg/day) alongside saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day). The patient continues to be monitored regularly, consistently displaying HbA1c levels below 6.5%. At present, there is no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and surveillance for chronic diabetes complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene codes for a transcription factor positioned in the q24 segment of chromosome 12. It consists of 10 exons that collectively cover 23,790 base pairs and produce a protein comprising 631 amino acids. This protein is found in multiple tissues, most notably the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Variations in the HNF1A gene contribute to a gradual deterioration of β-cell function, characterized by diminished expression of the insulin and GLUT2 genes and lower activity of pyruvate kinase. These defects lead to a reduction in insulin release and subsequently impair glucose tolerance.

The diagnostic evaluation in the present case was in line with the established criteria for MODY, integrating clinical presentation, biochemical assessments, and genetic analysis. Criteria considered included early diabetes onset, a marked family history of the disease, postprandial hyperglycemia, and the absence of diabetes-associated autoantibodies. Retained fasting C-peptide concentrations above 0.6 ng/mL are indicative of a diabetes type other than type 1, though ongoing C-peptide surveillance is crucial since values may remain within normal ranges during the initial phase of type 1 diabetes. A substantial obstacle to MODY diagnosis is insufficient recognition and lack of clinical suspicion, resulting in frequent misclassification as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes (T2D). The Exeter group has designed a MODY probability calculator to support clinicians in pinpointing individuals appropriate for genetic evaluation. Nevertheless, this model is based on data primarily from White European cohorts, and its predictive accuracy may be compromised in non-White populations, leading to possible overestimation of MODY risk. When applied to our patient's data, the calculator yielded an estimated MODY probability of 62.4% (approximately a 1 in 1.6 likelihood). Case reports have documented the presence of autoantibodies in certain MODY patients. Although autoantibody positivity can occur in individuals with HNF1A mutations, it remains uncommon in MODY overall. Historical research suggests that MODY patients experience lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome than those with T2D. However, given the rising prevalence of obesity, BMI and metabolic syndrome have become less reliable for differentiating between MODY and T2DM, potentially explaining the elevated BMI observed in our patient. Moreover, while a positive family history is traditionally valuable for diagnosing MODY, its reliability may be hindered by under-reporting, de novo mutations, or the widespread occurrence of T2DM among relatives.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on both patient age and HbA1c measurements. The preferred initial therapy is typically a low-dose sulfonylurea, which promotes insulin release via modulation of ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function diminishes in HNF1A-MODY patients, GLP-1 receptor agonists emerge as a safer and potentially superior option compared to sulfonylureas, particularly for those susceptible to hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small randomised, double-blind, crossover study indicates that liraglutide and glimepiride are equally effective in lowering both fasting and postprandial glucose concentrations among individuals with HNF1A-MODY, with no statistically significant differences detected between these therapies. Importantly, the likelihood of mild hypoglycaemic episodes appears reduced with GLP-1 receptor agonist use compared to glimepiride. Genetic confirmation of diagnosis allowed us to successfully withdraw insulin therapy after achieving glycaemic stability, subsequently switching the patient to GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment, which continued to provide stable glycaemic control. This underscores the essential role of genetic testing in personalising therapeutic strategies. Glycosuria associated with HNF1A-MODY is due to decreased SGLT2 expression, which is transcriptionally regulated by HNF1A. Reports have suggested that individuals with HNF1A-MODY might face an elevated risk of severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis as a result of significant inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. The potential adverse effects linked to SGLT-2 inhibitor use in HNF1A-MODY, including an increased propensity for dehydration, possible vascular instability, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, could outweigh their therapeutic benefits in this population. In the present case, the combined regimen of liraglutide and metformin provided both effective glycaemic control and a favourable safety profile, which is consistent with findings from prior research.

Precision medicine is particularly important in the context of MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfil all established diagnostic requirements, even though clinical protocols exist. Presently, there is a lack of reliable biomarkers capable of distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from either type 1 diabetes or T2D. Chen et al demonstrated that among 2429 young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D, MODY accounted for 1.5% of cases, with 0.74% identified as having pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Meanwhile, Møller et al’s research, focusing on a substantial population-based cohort of Danish Caucasians diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, revealed that 10% of 39 patients lacking any high-risk HLA haplotypes carried mutations in the HNF1A gene. Similarly, a recent large-scale study conducted in Russia analyzed 506 subjects presumed to have hereditary diabetes forms, characterized by negative results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 autoantibodies at diagnosis, ongoing C-peptide production, minimal insulin needs within 5 years, and no severe obesity. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified in 46.4% of these participants, with HNF1A among the genes most frequently implicated. Confirmatory diagnosis of MODY depends on DNA sequencing, with NGS technologies facilitating the simultaneous evaluation of extensive regions of patient DNA, thereby streamlining MODY detection. NGS advancements have also led to the discovery of numerous novel genetic variants. According to guidelines issued by the ACMG, these variants can be systematically assessed for pathogenicity. Databases such as ClinVar have catalogued over 1000 unique HNF1A variants—including pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In this report, the previously undescribed heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not recorded in ClinVar or in other commonly referenced variant databases, underscoring its novelty rather than diminishing its clinical importance. Notably, this variant changes an amino acid adjacent to established pathogenic residues, presents with a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and is inherited alongside diabetes within the family, meeting the ACMG’s criteria for a LP classification. Consequently, while databases like ClinVar provide valuable variant catalogues, the pathogenic role of this variant is corroborated by various independent genetic and clinical data, extending beyond the information contained in these resources.

Research has demonstrated that most mutations detected are concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain tends to exhibit fewer mutations. The novel variant described in this report is also located in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations suggest that some HNF1A mutations, despite being present, exert minimal influence on the protein’s activity. Moreover, there are HNF1A variants that, while not independently sufficient to induce MODY, may still be linked to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

To summarize, this case underscores the critical role of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY in guiding individualized therapeutic strategies. Ongoing studies will prioritize the optimization of NGS screening methodologies to enhance MODY detection rates, thereby improving both the cost-effectiveness and the clinical outcomes of patient care.
