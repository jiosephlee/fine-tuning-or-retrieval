Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a monogenic form of diabetes inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. This case report details a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY associated with a novel heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history revealed cases of diabetes with early onset. Genetic analysis identified a previously unreported pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Following initiation of a GLP-1 receptor agonist alongside metformin, the patient’s glycated haemoglobin decreased from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month interval. This case underscores the critical role of genetic testing in the evaluation of early-onset diabetes, commonly mistaken for type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and adds to the known diversity of HNF1A mutations significant for individualized treatment approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a set of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetes conditions marked by intrinsic abnormalities in pancreatic β-cell growth and function. These disorders generally appear and are diagnosed before the age of 25. MODY accounts for approximately 1%–6% of all diabetes cases in younger individuals. Its frequency may differ across various racial and ethnic populations, and rates may actually be higher than reported. Most investigations to date have focused on White European groups, while more recent efforts have begun to assess MODY occurrence in Asian and Australian populations. Reported prevalence figures for MODY are highly variable, in part because genetic testing is often restricted to those with particular clinical features, even when larger population studies are conducted.

Pathogenic variants in more than 15 genes have been associated with the monogenic diabetes phenotype known as MODY, with each gene tied to specific treatment approaches. Most reported instances of various MODY subtypes stem primarily from mutations in hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta (HNF1B) genes. 

HNF1A-MODY constitutes the most common form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of MODY cases, and is characterized by a clear autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of symptomatic diabetes within affected families. During early adolescence, individuals may not display symptoms, and increased blood glucose is often discovered incidentally during routine screening, generally in the absence of clinical signs of insulin resistance. Postprandial glucose testing or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal marked elevations in glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be present, attributed to a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired transport in the renal tubules. As the disease progresses, classical symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss can emerge, resembling presentations typical of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D can be challenging given the shared clinical features among these conditions.

Multiple diagnostic strategies for MODY have been described in recent literature. Following physical examination and evaluation of family history that indicate MODY, together with negative results for autoantibodies typically associated with type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—as well as other relevant biochemical tests, genetic analysis becomes pivotal in confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification of the MODY subtype is essential for selecting optimal therapy, predicting possible complications, gauging prognosis, and supporting family counselling efforts. Recent investigations have identified a wide spectrum of HNF1A gene mutations implicated in HNF1A-MODY.

In the patient described herein, we discovered a previously unreported heterozygous nucleotide change, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A, by employing a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel and evaluating pathogenicity based on the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria for variant interpretation. This specific mutation was absent from published literature as well as the VarSome and ClinVar databases. Written informed consent for publication of this case report was obtained from the patient.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment after hyperglycaemia and glycosuria were detected during his routine annual check-up. Upon evaluation, his BMI measured 24.6 kg/m², blood pressure was recorded at 112/70 mmHg, and his heart rate was 78 beats per minute. Physical examination revealed no evidence of acanthosis nigricans. The patient’s family history showed multiple cases of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at the age of 20. No family members diagnosed with diabetes were reported to have developed early-onset cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

The patient’s HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol) and fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. Two hours following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), his glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. There were no indications of diabetic ketoacidosis. He described increased thirst, heightened hunger, and unintentional recent weight reduction. Lipid panel demonstrated an HDL cholesterol of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L, findings that raise suspicion for metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He did not have any notable medical or surgical background.

Results of the glucagon stimulation test showed a baseline C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L and a value of 0.83 nmol/L after 6 minutes, which points to impaired insulin secretion. The glucagon stimulation test measures the ability of β-cells to secrete insulin through C-peptide quantification post-glucagon administration. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide greater than 0.60 nmol/L is commonly considered to reflect preserved endogenous insulin production, whereas lower readings are consistent with β-cell impairment. Still, this test serves a supplemental rather than definitive diagnostic role, especially in situations where autoantibody testing is inconclusive or the clinical picture is unclear. No microalbuminuria was observed. Sudomotor assessment via electrochemical skin conductance (e.g., Sudoscan) revealed profound impairment in the hands, indicative of marked autonomic neuropathy involving small fibres. Both GAD antibody (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibody (IA-2A) assays were negative. At one month after diagnosis, genetic analysis for MODY identified a heterozygous, pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), diagnostic for HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly elevated HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The first therapeutic approach involved basal insulin degludec at a dose of eight units per day, along with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), reflecting an initial assessment consistent with T2D. This regimen sought to rapidly improve glycaemic control while also targeting possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient was instructed to follow a structured nutritional program and incorporate consistent physical activity, as components of an all-encompassing diabetes care plan. Follow-up visits were arranged at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient facility to observe glycaemic status and evaluate treatment effectiveness.

Nevertheless, the patient’s moderately preserved but suboptimal β-cell function as demonstrated by the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and notable family history of early-onset diabetes raised considerable suspicion for MODY.

The initial management was implemented before genetic testing results were available, relying upon the patient’s clinical profile and laboratory findings at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c reduced to 9.1%. Upon genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY, and in light of the patient’s moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell function and the noted improvement in HbA1c, insulin therapy was discontinued. Dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist at a dose of 1.5 mg once weekly, was initiated to aid glycaemic management and protect pancreatic function. Six months following this therapeutic adjustment, HbA1c dropped further to 6.0%, reflecting marked glycaemic improvement. With stabilization of glycaemic control, the treatment plan was streamlined to long-term maintenance with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin, a DPP-4 inhibitor (5 mg/day). The patient continues to undergo regular monitoring, with HbA1c levels consistently below 6.5%. Thus far, no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy has been observed, and surveillance for chronic complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene codes for a transcription factor that resides in chromosome 12’s q24 region. It contains 10 exons over 23,790 base pairs and produces a protein with 631 amino acids, which is found in multiple tissues, such as the pancreas, kidneys, and liver. Alterations in the HNF1A gene result in a gradual deterioration of β-cell activity, characterized by diminished insulin and GLUT2 gene expression and decreased pyruvate kinase function. This process culminates in lower insulin production and impaired glucose regulation.

In this instance, diagnosis was based on contemporary criteria for MODY, integrating clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and genetic confirmation, such as diabetes onset at an early age, a pronounced familial pattern, difficulties with postprandial glycemic control, and absence of diabetes-associated autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide concentration above 0.6 ng/mL supports a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes. Nevertheless, continued C-peptide assessment is required, since early type 1 diabetes can also demonstrate normal C-peptide levels. A major obstacle in identifying MODY is insufficient knowledge and suspicion within clinical practice; patients may often be incorrectly labeled as having type 1 diabetes or T2D. The Exeter group’s MODY probability calculator assists clinicians in determining which individuals should undergo genetic analysis, though its applicability is limited since it was derived from a White European dataset and may over-predict MODY likelihood in other populations. Application of our patient's clinical details yielded an estimated MODY probability of 62.4% (corresponding to 1 in 1.6 odds) using this calculator. Various case series have reported the occurrence of autoantibodies in some MODY patients. Individuals carrying HNF1A mutations can possess autoantibodies, but most MODY patients test negative. Previous data indicate that prevalence rates of metabolic syndrome and obesity are lower in those with MODY than among people with T2D. However, increased obesity rates have diminished the utility of BMI and features of metabolic syndrome in differentiating MODY from T2DM, which might account for our patient’s elevated BMI. Furthermore, while family history contributes to the assessment for MODY, its value can be reduced by under-recognition, the presence of new mutations, or the frequent occurrence of T2D in relatives.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on both patient age and HbA1c measurements. Initial therapy typically consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin secretion by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function decreases in individuals with HNF1A-MODY—especially those at heightened risk for hypoglycaemia—GLP-1 receptor agonists may offer a safer and more efficacious alternative to sulfonylureas. Evidence from a small randomized, double-blind, crossover study found liraglutide and glimepiride to be equally effective in lowering fasting plasma glucose and reducing postprandial glucose variability, with no significant difference between these agents among HNF1A-MODY subjects. Furthermore, treatment with GLP-1 receptor agonists is associated with a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic confirmation enabled insulin discontinuation in this case, with sustained glycaemic control following transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, underscoring the pivotal role of genetic diagnostics in informing therapeutic strategy. The presence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY can be explained by decreased expression of SGLT2, regulated by HNF1A transcription. A prior report suggested that individuals with HNF1A-MODY may develop marked dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis as a result of excessive inhibition of the remaining SGLT-2 function. Potential adverse effects linked to SGLT-2 inhibitor use in HNF1A-MODY—including a greater likelihood of dehydration, possible vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may outweigh any therapeutic advantage. In the presented case, a regimen of liraglutide and metformin achieved effective and safe glycaemic control, in keeping with existing literature.

Tailored approaches are imperative in the management of MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill every diagnostic parameter outlined in established clinical protocols. At present, appropriate biomarkers to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from both type 1 diabetes and T2D are lacking. Chen et al’s investigation involving 2429 Chinese youths with early-onset T2D found the MODY prevalence to be 1.5%, with 0.74% identified as having pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. In the work of Møller et al, it was demonstrated that among a substantial cohort of Danish Caucasian patients diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, 10% of the 39 individuals lacking any high-risk HLA haplotypes possessed mutations within the HNF1A gene. Likewise, a contemporary Russian population-based survey assessed 506 participants suspected of hereditary diabetes who lacked autoimmune characteristics—including negative results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at onset, sustained C-peptide, minimal insulin demand within 5 years, and no marked obesity. In this group, 46.4% were found to carry P or LP variants, with HNF1A representing one of the most frequently impacted genes. Confirmation of MODY suspicions necessitates DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) facilitates the simultaneous analysis of extensive patient DNA samples, enhancing practicality for MODY detection. The adoption of NGS has led to the identification of numerous novel genetic variants. The ACMG has established standards for determining pathogenic status. Databases such as ClinVar now include over 1000 unique HNF1A variants, ranging from P and LP to benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In our report, the novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or other widely referenced variant databases, underscoring its uniqueness rather than questioning clinical importance. This variant alters an amino acid next to previously documented P residues, exhibits a high AlphaMissense score of 0.995, and co-segregates with diabetes in the family, collectively fulfilling ACMG criteria for classification as LP’. Therefore, although ClinVar serves as a comprehensive repository for described variants, the pathogenic nature of this variant is substantiated through several separate genetic and clinical sources in addition to database listings.

Research has shown that most detected mutations are concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a comparatively lower mutation frequency. The novel variant described in this report is located in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations suggest that specific HNF1A mutations, while present, exert only limited effects on HNF1A protein activity. Moreover, some HNF1A variants that do not independently result in MODY may nonetheless confer a heightened susceptibility to T2D.

To summarize, this case underlines the necessity for precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and emphasizes its relevance for personalized therapy. Future studies will aim to devise improved NGS-based screening methods to raise the detection rate of MODY, thereby optimizing healthcare expenditures and clinical outcomes.
