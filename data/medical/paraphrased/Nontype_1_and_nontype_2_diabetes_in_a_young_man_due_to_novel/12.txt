Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic form of diabetes inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. This case details an individual diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY resulting from a previously undescribed heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha gene. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis, and had a significant family history of early-onset diabetes. Genetic analysis identified a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Initiation of therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist alongside metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This report underscores the value of genetic evaluation in cases of early-onset diabetes, as such cases are often mistaken for type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and it also broadens the known range of HNF1A mutations pertinent to individualized treatment approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes marked by a fundamental abnormality in pancreatic β-cell formation and function. These syndromes generally appear and are diagnosed prior to age 25. MODY accounts for about 1%–6% of diabetes with onset in youth. Prevalence of MODY may differ across diverse racial and ethnic populations and is likely to be underestimated. Historically, most research efforts have targeted White European cohorts. More recently, investigations into MODY prevalence have extended to populations in Asia and Australia. Reported prevalence rates of MODY differ widely, with much of this disparity attributed to the fact that genetic testing tends to be conducted selectively in individuals who exhibit certain clinical features, including in studies performed in broad population samples.

Mutations in more than 15 distinct genes have been associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, each mutation suggesting unique treatment options. The bulk of reported MODY cases stem from alterations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. Among these, HNF1A-MODY is the most frequently encountered subtype, accounting for approximately 30% to 65% of all MODY cases. This form demonstrates a prominent autosomal dominant inheritance pattern for symptomatic diabetes within affected families. In early adolescence, affected individuals may lack overt symptoms, with hyperglycemia often discovered incidentally during standard laboratory evaluation, and in the absence of clinical evidence for insulin resistance. Measurement of postprandial glucose or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal considerable elevations in plasma glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria can also be present, resulting from a diminished renal threshold for glucose due to impaired tubular glucose handling. Nevertheless, as the disease progresses, patients may develop symptoms such as excessive thirst, urination, hunger, and weight loss, paralleling those seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, clinical similarities between MODY and both type 1 and T2D frequently present significant challenges for accurate diagnosis.

Multiple diagnostic strategies for MODY have been described in the literature. Once physical examination and evaluation of family history point toward a possible clinical phenotype of MODY, and autoimmune markers associated with type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are negative, further biochemical testing and, importantly, molecular genetic analysis become integral to confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification is essential for guiding therapeutic decisions, estimating risk of complications, prognostic assessment, and providing genetic counseling to relatives. Recent studies have documented several hundred distinct mutations within the HNF1A gene as causative for HNF1A-MODY.

In the patient described in this report, genetic testing with a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel revealed a novel heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene. Pathogenicity of the variant was evaluated in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria for variant classification. This specific mutation has not previously been described in published reports, nor is it listed in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent directly for the publication of this case.

Case presentation:

A young adult was referred for assessment of elevated blood glucose and the presence of glucose in the urine detected during his routine annual check-up. Physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m^2, a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse rate of 78 beats per minute. No evidence of acanthosis nigricans was found on inspection. The family history revealed several cases of diabetes diagnosed at an early age: the maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at 20 years old. None of the diabetic relatives had a documented history of early-onset cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

The patient's glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. Two hours following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), the glucose level was 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. He described experiencing increased thirst, heightened appetite, and recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid analysis showed high-density lipoprotein at 1.01 mmol/L and triglyceride concentration at 4.31 mmol/L, raising suspicion for metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). His past medical and surgical histories were unremarkable.

During a glucagon stimulation test, the serum C-peptide level was 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and rose to 0.83 nmol/L after 6 minutes, consistent with impaired insulin secretion. This assessment measures residual β-cell capacity through stimulated C-peptide measurement following glucagon administration. In clinical practice, a stimulated C-peptide exceeding 0.60 nmol/L typically suggests remaining endogenous insulin production, while lower values indicate compromised β-cell activity. Nonetheless, the glucagon stimulation test is regarded as adjunctive rather than definitive, especially in scenarios involving ambiguous autoantibody results or unusual clinical presentations. Testing for microalbuminuria was negative. Sudomotor assessment via electrochemical skin conductance testing (such as Sudoscan), a non-invasive method evaluating small fibre autonomic function, indicated severely diminished sudomotor activity in the hands, signifying substantial autonomic involvement. Screening for both GADA and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) yielded negative results. Genetic evaluation for MODY conducted one month after diagnosis identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. Initial management involved basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, combined with metformin 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin 5 mg daily, in line with an early consideration of type 2 diabetes. This regimen was intended to promptly improve glycaemic control and mitigate possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medications, the patient was also instructed to follow a structured diet and engage in consistent physical exercise as integral components of an all-encompassing diabetes care plan. Subsequent follow-up visits took place at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic for ongoing assessment of glycaemic regulation and evaluation of therapeutic efficacy.

Nonetheless, the presence of only moderately maintained but inadequate β-cell activity on glucagon stimulation (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absent islet autoantibodies, and a prominent family history of early-onset diabetes raised a strong clinical suspicion for MODY.

Initial treatment was started prior to the availability of genetic testing results, based on the clinical presentation and laboratory findings at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. With genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and evaluation showing partially preserved yet inadequate β-cell function, along with the improved HbA1c of 9.1%, insulin treatment was stopped and dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) was initiated to aid glycaemic control and help maintain pancreatic function. Six months after beginning this therapy, the HbA1c further reduced to 6.0%, reflecting notable improvement. As glycaemic stability was achieved, the treatment approach was simplified to metformin (1500 mg per day) combined with saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg per day) for long-term management. The patient continues to be monitored regularly, with HbA1c persistently under 6.5%. There have been no indications of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy so far, and surveillance for chronic complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, a transcription factor situated on chromosome 12 at the q24 locus, consists of 10 exons covering 23,790 base pairs and encodes a 631-amino-acid protein expressed in multiple tissues, such as the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Alterations in HNF1A result in a gradual reduction of β-cell performance, reflected by diminished insulin and GLUT2 gene expression and lowered pyruvate kinase activity. These changes culminate in reduced insulin output and impaired glucose tolerance.

Diagnosis in this case followed established MODY diagnostic guidelines, which include clinical presentation, biochemical findings, and confirmation by genetic testing, such as early-onset diabetes, a pronounced familial pattern, postprandial hyperglycemia, and the absence of diabetes-related autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide measurement exceeding 0.6 ng/mL supports non-type 1 diabetes, though repeat assessment is critical as early type 1 diabetes can also present with normal values. Diagnostic challenges include insufficient awareness and suspicion for MODY, leading to frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator from the Exeter group can assist clinicians in selecting patients for genetic evaluation, but it was developed with data predominantly from White European cohorts and may overestimate risk in non-White populations. Application of this calculator to our patient yielded a 62.4% chance (1 in 1.6) of MODY. Literature describes rare instances of autoantibodies among individuals with MODY; while most cases with HNF1A mutations lack autoantibodies, there are exceptions. Initial research indicates that obesity and metabolic syndrome are less prevalent in MODY compared with type 2 diabetes, but as general rates of obesity have climbed, differentiation between MODY and T2DM using BMI or features of metabolic syndrome has become less reliable, possibly accounting for the elevated BMI observed in our case. Moreover, while family history contributes significantly to MODY assessment, its reliability can be compromised by factors such as under-reporting, de novo mutations, or widespread familial occurrence of T2DM.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is based on the patient's age and HbA1c levels. The initial approach typically utilizes low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin release via potassium-sensitive ATP channels. As β-cell function deteriorates, GLP-1 receptor agonists may provide a safer and more efficacious option for those with HNF1A-MODY, particularly for patients at elevated risk of hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small randomized, double-blind, crossover study revealed that liraglutide and glimepiride both significantly decreased fasting and postprandial plasma glucose levels, showing no meaningful difference between the therapies in those with HNF1A-MODY. However, GLP-1 receptor agonists carry a reduced risk of mild hypoglycaemia compared with glimepiride. In the present case, genetic confirmation allowed for insulin cessation after glycaemic targets were reached, and the patient was subsequently managed with a GLP-1 receptor agonist, which sustained glycaemic control. This underscores the essential role of genetic testing in informing therapeutic choices. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria results from diminished SGLT2 expression regulated by HNF1A. One report has described that patients with HNF1A-MODY could suffer from pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to the substantial inhibition of remaining SGLT-2 function. The hazards of SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy in this context—including increased dehydration, possible vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may surpass any therapeutic advantages. For this patient, treatment with liraglutide and metformin proved both effective and safe, aligning with prior research.

Personalized medicine holds significant importance in the context of MODY, since individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all diagnostic benchmarks even when current clinical protocols are applied. Presently, there is a lack of reliable biomarkers to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Chen et al reported that, among 2,429 Chinese young adults presenting with early-onset type 2 diabetes, the prevalence of MODY was 1.5%, with 0.74% carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Another study conducted by Møller et al found that, within a large cohort of Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of the 39 individuals without high-risk HLA haplotypes carried HNF1A gene mutations. Additionally, a recent population-based investigation from Russia assessed 506 patients suspected of having monogenic forms of diabetes, based on clinical features including negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at disease onset, sustained C-peptide levels, minimal insulin requirements within five years, and no severe obesity. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were detected in 46.4% of cases, with HNF1A being among the genes most frequently implicated. DNA sequencing is critical for confirming clinical suspicions of MODY. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables the assessment of substantial portions of DNA from a single patient in one assay, thereby facilitating more accessible MODY screening. With advancements in NGS technology, numerous novel genetic variants have been identified. The ACMG offers recommendations for interpreting the pathogenicity of these newly described variants. Databases such as ClinVar contain reports of over 1,000 distinct HNF1A variants, encompassing those classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, or of uncertain significance. In our report, the heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was novel, not appearing in ClinVar or other major variant repositories, indicating its originality rather than diminishing its clinical import. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid next to previously established pathogenic sites, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense probability (0.995), and is observed to co-segregate with diabetes in affected family members—all satisfying ACMG criteria for a likely pathogenic designation. Therefore, although resources such as ClinVar are indispensable for documenting genetic alterations, the evidence supporting this variant’s pathogenicity is corroborated by robust genetic and phenotypic findings in addition to absence in databases.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is also positioned within the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have revealed that some HNF1A mutations, despite their presence, exert little influence on the function of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, particular HNF1A variants that are not sufficient on their own to cause MODY may nonetheless be linked to an elevated risk of developing type 2 diabetes.

In summary, this case underscores the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its implications for tailored therapy. Ongoing studies aim to develop more efficient next-generation sequencing (NGS) screening methods to enhance MODY diagnostic rates, thereby optimizing both cost efficiency and clinical outcomes.
