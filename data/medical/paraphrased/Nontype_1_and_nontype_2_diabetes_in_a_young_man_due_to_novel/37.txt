Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is an inherited, autosomal dominant single-gene disorder causing diabetes. This case report presents an individual with HNF1A-MODY linked to a previously unidentified heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The patient exhibited elevated blood glucose levels and glycosuria but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history revealed diabetes occurring at a young age. Genetic analysis identified a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist in combination with metformin lowered the patient's glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over 3 months. This case underscores the significance of genetic evaluation in the setting of early-onset diabetes, which is often incorrectly classified as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and broadens the known array of clinically relevant HNF1A mutations for targeted therapeutic approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of dominantly inherited diabetes syndromes that are not autoimmune in nature and stem from intrinsic defects in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders generally present and are identified in patients younger than 25 years. MODY accounts for approximately 1% to 6% of diabetes cases with early onset. The frequency of MODY may differ among distinct ethnic and racial populations and may be underrecognized. Investigations to date have predominantly focused on individuals of White European descent, while recent research has begun to examine its prevalence in Asian and Australian contexts. Reported prevalence rates for MODY differ considerably, a discrepancy largely attributable to the practice of restricting genetic testing to persons with specific clinical profiles, even in population-based studies.

More than 15 gene mutations have been associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, each linked to unique treatment approaches. Most MODY cases are attributed to variants in genes such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. Among these, HNF1A-MODY is the most common subtype, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY cases, and is characterized by autosomal dominant inheritance of diabetes within families. During early adolescence, clinical signs are often absent, and hyperglycemia is commonly discovered incidentally through routine laboratory screening, generally without evidence of insulin resistance. Elevated postprandial blood glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L) may be detected through measurement after meals or via a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Glycosuria may also be present, resulting from a lowered renal threshold for glucose, secondary to impaired glucose transport in the renal tubules. In advanced disease, manifestations such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintended weight loss may occur, resembling symptoms typical of type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 diabetes and T2D is often challenging due to shared clinical presentations.

Multiple diagnostic protocols for MODY have been outlined in the literature. If the patient's physical examination and family history point toward MODY, and autoantibodies commonly seen in type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are negative, further biochemical testing can support the diagnosis. Molecular genetic analysis plays a pivotal role in confirming MODY. Establishing an accurate diagnosis is essential for selecting appropriate therapies, predicting complications, evaluating prognosis, and conducting family counseling. Recent investigations have identified numerous distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene underlying HNF1A-MODY.

In this report, we detected a previously undescribed heterozygous missense variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), in exon 4 of HNF1A from a patient with HNF1A-MODY by applying a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel, and evaluated its pathogenicity using the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria. This variant has not been documented in published literature, the VarSome database, or ClinVar database. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment of elevated blood glucose and glycosuria discovered during his routine annual check-up. On physical examination, his BMI measured 24.6 kg/m2, blood pressure recorded at 112/70 mmHg, and pulse rate was 78 beats per minute. No signs of acanthosis nigricans were detected. The family history revealed instances of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. None of the affected relatives had a reported history of premature cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

The patient's glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol) and fasting blood glucose was found to be 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), the 2-hour post-load glucose concentration reached 19.8 mmol/L. There were no clinical indications of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described increased thirst and appetite, as well as recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid panel results showed high-density lipoprotein at 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, which may be indicative of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He did not have any notable previous medical conditions or surgical interventions.

A glucagon stimulation test revealed a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, denoting deficient insulin release. This test assesses β-cell reserve through measurement of glucagon-stimulated C-peptide. Clinically, a post-stimulation C-peptide result exceeding 0.60 nmol/L typically signifies maintained endogenous insulin production; values below this threshold are suggestive of impaired β-cell function. Nonetheless, the glucagon stimulation assay is considered a supportive measure and not definitive, particularly when autoantibody status is unclear or the clinical presentation deviates from classic patterns. Testing did not reveal microalbuminuria. Sudomotor assessment using electrochemical skin conductance (such as Sudoscan) indicated marked reduction in sudomotor function in the hands, suggesting prominent autonomic impairment. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) yielded negative results. At one month after diagnosis, genetic testing for MODY identified a heterozygous, pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), which is diagnostic for HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose measurements. The first line of therapy included basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, paired with metformin at 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin at 5 mg daily, aligning with an initial hypothesis of T2D. This approach was selected to quickly lower blood glucose levels and address possible insulin resistance.

Alongside medical management, the patient received recommendations to follow a regimented meal plan and participate in consistent exercise, incorporating lifestyle modifications into the overall diabetes care strategy. Glycaemic control and therapeutic efficacy were monitored during subsequent visits to the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic.

Nevertheless, a glucagon stimulation test showed the patient maintained moderate yet suboptimal β-cell function (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), had absent islet autoantibodies, and possessed a significant family history of early-onset diabetes, raising strong clinical suspicion for MODY.

The initial treatment plan was initiated prior to receiving genetic testing results, relying instead on the patient’s presenting symptoms and diagnostic laboratory data.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month afterward, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. Genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY was obtained, and due to the patient’s moderately preserved but insufficient β-cell function as well as the improvement in HbA1c to 9.1%, insulin was discontinued. Dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist (1.5 mg weekly), was initiated to promote glycaemic control and help maintain pancreatic function. After 6 months on this regimen, the patient’s HbA1c fell to 6.0%, showing substantial progress. With glycaemic control stabilizing, the treatment approach was further adjusted to a maintenance regimen consisting of metformin (1500 mg/day) and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day). The patient continues to be followed regularly, and HbA1c has remained consistently below 6.5%. No evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy has been observed thus far, and ongoing monitoring for potential long-term complications continues.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, found on chromosome 12q24, encodes a transcription factor composed of 10 exons and spanning 23,790 base pairs. This gene produces a protein with 631 amino acids that is present in multiple tissues, such as the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Mutations in HNF1A contribute to a gradual impairment of β-cell functionality, resulting from decreased expression of the insulin and GLUT2 genes as well as lower pyruvate kinase activity. The culmination of these effects is diminished insulin release and reduced glucose tolerance.

For this patient, the diagnostic approach was consistent with established MODY criteria, requiring integration of clinical, biochemical, and genetic findings. These criteria include diabetes with early onset, significant family history, impaired glucose tolerance after meals, and absence of autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide concentration above 0.6 ng/mL indicates preserved β-cell function and supports the diagnosis of a non-type 1 diabetes. Nevertheless, C-peptide should be monitored over time since normal results may also be found in the initial phase of type 1 diabetes. A major diagnostic challenge for MODY arises from limited clinical awareness, often leading to misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator, designed by the Exeter group, is a resource to guide clinicians regarding genetic testing; however, its performance is best established in White European populations, and it could overestimate MODY likelihood in individuals from other backgrounds. Applying this tool to our patient’s data yielded an estimated 62.4% probability (or a 1 in 1.6 chance) of MODY. Reports in the literature have documented the presence of autoantibodies in some individuals with MODY, and certain cases of HNF1A mutations have shown autoantibody positivity, though most are negative. Previous research has indicated that metabolic syndrome and obesity are less prevalent in MODY than type 2 diabetes. Still, rising obesity rates have rendered BMI and metabolic syndrome less useful in distinguishing MODY from type 2 diabetes, possibly accounting for the increased BMI observed here. While a positive family history remains an important criterion for MODY, its diagnostic utility may be weakened by issues such as unrecognized ancestry, de novo mutations, or the frequent occurrence of T2DM within families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on patient age and HbA1c concentrations. The primary approach is low-dose sulfonylurea therapy, which promotes insulin release by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. For individuals with HNF1A-MODY, especially those susceptible to hypoglycaemia or experiencing diminished β-cell activity, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer and potentially more efficacious option compared to sulfonylureas. Evidence from a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study showed that liraglutide and glimepiride were equally effective in lowering fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose variability in HNF1A-MODY patients, with no notable differences between the treatments. GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycaemia relative to glimepiride. By confirming the diagnosis genetically, insulin therapy was discontinued after achieving glycaemic stability, and the patient was successfully switched to GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment, which continued to maintain glycaemic control. This underscores the importance of genetic testing for selecting appropriate therapies. Glycosuria observed in HNF1A-MODY results from diminished SGLT2 expression, regulated by HNF1A transcriptional activity. One report described that patients with HNF1A-MODY may develop severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis as a consequence of profound inhibition of residual SGLT-2 function. Potential adverse effects from SGLT-2 inhibitors in this population—including increased dehydration risk, vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—could outweigh the benefits these agents might provide. In this instance, the combination of liraglutide and metformin proved to be both effective and well-tolerated, aligning with previous research findings.

Tailored therapeutic approaches are essential in MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy every diagnostic criterion outlined in current clinical protocols. At present, there are no effective biomarkers for distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or type 2 diabetes. Chen et al reported that, in a cohort of 2,429 young Chinese adults presenting with early-onset T2D, MODY was identified in 1.5% of cases, with 0.74% possessing pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY gene variants. Work by Møller et al demonstrated that, among a sizable population-based group of Danish Caucasian type 1 diabetes patients, mutations in the HNF1A gene were detected in 10% of 39 individuals lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes. Along similar lines, a recent Russian population study screened 506 subjects who were considered to have hereditary diabetes in the absence of autoimmune markers—criteria included negative results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at onset, intact C-peptide production, minimal insulin needs within five years, and no marked obesity. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were discovered in 46.4% of this cohort, with HNF1A among the genes most frequently affected. Genetic testing via DNA sequencing remains the definitive method for confirming suspected MODY cases. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables examination of extensive patient DNA in a single assay, making MODY screening more accessible. As NGS technology advances, a greater number of genetic alterations have come to light. The ACMG provides standards for assessing variant pathogenicity. Databases such as ClinVar list over 1,000 unique HNF1A gene variants, encompassing pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of unknown significance. In this report, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A mutation (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or other standard variant repositories, emphasizing its uniqueness rather than undermining its potential clinical significance. Notably, this mutation alters an amino acid residue located next to established pathogenic sites, carries a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and segregates with diabetes within the family, fulfilling ACMG criteria for a likely pathogenic designation. Although ClinVar serves as a key reference for variant annotation, the case for pathogenicity of this variant is substantiated by several independent genetic and clinical findings beyond those documented in public databases.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is positioned within the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations suggest that some HNF1A mutations, although present, exert only a modest influence on HNF1A protein activity. Furthermore, specific HNF1A variants that are insufficient to independently trigger MODY may nonetheless contribute to an elevated risk of T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its implications for personalized treatment. Ongoing studies will aim to establish more efficient NGS-based screening protocols to enhance the detection rate of MODY, thereby optimizing both costs and clinical outcomes.
