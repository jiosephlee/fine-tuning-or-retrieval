Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Overview: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is characterized as a monogenic, autosomal dominant form of diabetes. This case report details an instance of HNF1A-MODY associated with a newly identified heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) gene. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. Notably, the family history included several cases of early-onset diabetes. Molecular analysis revealed a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist combined with metformin resulted in a decrease of glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a three-month period. This report emphasizes the value of genetic evaluation in cases of early-onset diabetes, a condition often mistakenly diagnosed as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and further broadens the known spectrum of HNF1A mutations pertinent to precision medical approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes disorders stemming from a primary impairment in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These diseases usually appear and are identified prior to 25 years of age. MODY accounts for about 1%–6% of cases of diabetes with onset in youth. Its frequency can differ among diverse racial and ethnic populations and is often likely underestimated. Historically, investigations have largely focused on White Europeans, although more recent research has begun exploring the prevalence in Asian and Australian cohorts. Reported rates of MODY prevalence fluctuate considerably, a disparity frequently attributable to the fact that genetic testing is restricted to individuals meeting specific clinical criteria, even in studies sampling larger populations.

Pathogenic variants in more than 15 genes have been associated with the monogenic diabetes phenotype seen in MODY, and each genetic subtype may require a unique approach to management. Most reported cases among the different MODY variants are primarily caused by mutations in genes like hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. 

HNF1A-MODY is the most frequently encountered form, accounting for roughly 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses. It characteristically demonstrates an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern for diabetes mellitus within affected families. During early adolescence, individuals often remain asymptomatic, with hyperglycemia usually detected incidentally during standard laboratory evaluations, commonly in the absence of clinical features suggestive of insulin resistance. Postprandial hyperglycemia (≥11.1 mmol/L) may be revealed by measuring blood glucose following a meal or through a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Glycosuria can also occur, resulting from a diminished renal threshold for glucose that stems from impaired tubular glucose reabsorption. In advanced disease stages, classic diabetic symptoms such as increased thirst, frequent urination, excessive hunger, and weight loss—similar to those seen in both type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may develop. This considerable clinical overlap between MODY and type 1 or T2D makes distinguishing these disorders particularly challenging in routine practice.

Multiple diagnostic algorithms for MODY have been outlined in various studies. When a clinical suspicion of MODY arises based on physical examination and assessment of family history, and there is an absence of autoantibodies commonly seen in type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—in conjunction with other relevant biochemical investigations, molecular genetic testing becomes pivotal in confirming the diagnosis. An accurate diagnosis is essential for informing appropriate therapy, predicting future complications, evaluating prognosis, and providing effective family counselling. Recent research has documented hundreds of distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene that are linked to HNF1A-MODY.

In the case described within this manuscript, we discovered a previously unreported heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A, in a patient with HNF1A-MODY. This was identified through genetic analysis utilizing a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel, and pathogenicity was assessed in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation. This particular mutation does not appear in the literature, VarSome database, nor ClinVar database. The patient provided written informed consent specifically for the publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment of elevated blood glucose and urinary glucose detected at his routine yearly check-up. His physical examination revealed a body mass index of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse rate of 78 beats per minute. No signs of acanthosis nigricans were found on inspection. The family history revealed several instances of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her thirties, his mother in her late twenties, and his younger brother at the age of 20. No relatives with diabetes had reported cases of premature cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

The patient’s HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting plasma glucose was found to be 12.4 mmol/L. After a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, the 2-hour plasma glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. No evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis was present. The patient described experiencing polydipsia, increased appetite, and unintentional weight loss. Lipid panel revealed an HDL concentration of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L, findings consistent with possible metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). There was no history of significant prior medical or surgical issues.

During glucagon stimulation, serum C-peptide measured 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and increased to 0.83 nmol/L six minutes after glucagon administration, signifying suboptimal insulin production. This test assesses residual β-cell reserve through quantification of C-peptide following glucagon injection. Typically, a post-stimulation C-peptide greater than 0.60 nmol/L denotes maintained endogenous insulin output; lower values are suggestive of impaired β-cell function. However, the glucagon stimulation test serves as an adjunctive diagnostic measure, especially when islet autoantibody findings are negative or the clinical picture is unusual. No microalbuminuria was present. Assessment of sudomotor function using electrochemical skin conductance (e.g., Sudoscan) revealed markedly diminished conductance across the hands, consistent with significant small fibre autonomic dysfunction. Autoantibody analyses for both GAD and islet antigen 2 (IA-2A) returned negative. Genetic screening for MODY, carried out one month after diagnosis, identified a heterozygous, pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), indicative of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased levels of HbA1c and plasma glucose. The first therapeutic approach included basal degludec insulin at eight units daily, combined with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), reflecting an initial assessment suggestive of T2D. This regimen was designed to promptly lower blood glucose and address possible insulin resistance.

Alongside medications, the patient was instructed to follow a structured diet and maintain regular exercise as part of an overall diabetes management plan. Subsequent assessments occurred at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to track glycaemic control and evaluate the effectiveness of therapy.

Nonetheless, due to moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell function demonstrated by the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a notable family history of early-onset diabetes, clinicians strongly suspected MODY.

Treatment initiation occurred before genetic testing results were available, relying on clinical presentation and laboratory findings at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month afterwards, the patient's HbA1c improved to 9.1%. After confirmation of HNF1A-MODY through genetic testing, and in light of the patient's partially retained, though inadequate, β-cell function as well as improved HbA1c, insulin was discontinued and dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) was initiated to help with glycaemic control and conserve pancreatic function. Following 6 months of therapy, HbA1c had dropped to 6.0%, reflecting marked improvement. As glycaemic control stabilized, treatment was simplified to a regimen consisting of metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day) for ongoing maintenance. The patient continues to be monitored routinely, with HbA1c levels remaining under 6.5%. So far, microalbuminuria and diabetic retinopathy have not developed, and the patient is under ongoing surveillance for potential long-term complications.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor situated on chromosome 12q24. It consists of 10 exons across 23,790 base pairs and produces a protein with 631 amino acids, which is present in several tissues such as the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Mutations in HNF1A cause a gradual loss of β-cell function by diminishing the expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes and decreasing pyruvate kinase activity. This ultimately contributes to impaired insulin secretion and the development of glucose intolerance.

For this case, diagnosis followed established MODY criteria, which include clinical, laboratory, and genetic findings such as early onset of diabetes, notable family history, postprandial glucose intolerance, and negative islet autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide greater than 0.6 ng/mL is indicative of a form of diabetes other than type 1. Despite this, ongoing C-peptide testing is important because initial values can be normal in early type 1 diabetes. A major obstacle in MODY diagnosis remains limited recognition and suspicion among clinicians; frequently, patients are mistakenly classified as having type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The Exeter group developed the MODY probability calculator to help clinicians recognize potential cases for genetic screening. Nevertheless, this calculator is based on a White European dataset and may yield higher estimates of MODY probability in non-White populations. Using this tool for our patient’s details yielded a MODY probability of 62.4% (equivalent to approximately 1 in 1.6 odds). Previous case reports have documented instances of autoantibodies among MODY patients. Although most MODY individuals are autoantibody negative, some with HNF1A variants have been autoantibody positive. Research indicates that obesity and metabolic syndrome are less frequent in MODY than in type 2 diabetes. However, with increasing obesity prevalence, the diagnostic usefulness of BMI or features of metabolic syndrome in differentiating MODY from type 2 diabetes has diminished, which may account for the higher BMI in this case. Additionally, while family history is a key factor in identifying MODY, its reliability can be compromised by under-recognition, spontaneous mutations, and the frequent occurrence of type 2 diabetes in relatives.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on the patient’s age and HbA1c values. Sulfonylureas at low doses are initially recommended, as they promote insulin release through modulation of potassium-sensitive ATP channels. As β-cell function deteriorates, individuals with HNF1A-MODY—especially those susceptible to hypoglycaemia—may benefit from GLP-1 receptor agonists, which offer an improved safety profile and greater efficacy compared to sulfonylureas. Evidence from a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study indicated that both liraglutide and glimepiride decreased fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose variability in patients with HNF1A-MODY, with no meaningful difference in effectiveness. Furthermore, GLP-1 receptor agonists were linked to a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycaemia relative to glimepiride. Genetic confirmation permitted discontinuation of insulin following stabilization of glucose levels, allowing the switch to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which continued to provide reliable glycaemic control. This underscores the importance of genetic testing to direct therapeutic approaches. The presence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY stems from diminished SGLT2 expression governed by HNF1A transcriptional activity. One published account described that patients with HNF1A-MODY might encounter severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis because of pronounced inhibition of residual SGLT-2 function. For HNF1A-MODY patients, SGLT-2 inhibitors carry significant risks, including higher probability of dehydration, possible vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, which may surpass their advantages. In the present case, treatment with liraglutide in conjunction with metformin proved both safe and effective, in agreement with previous reports.

Precision medicine plays an essential role in MODY, particularly since individuals with HNF1A-MODY may not satisfy every diagnostic criterion even when guidelines are followed. At present, there are no effective biomarkers to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from both type 1 and T2D. Chen et al demonstrated that MODY occurred in 1.5% of 2,429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D, with 0.74% carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY mutations. Møller et al found in a sizable Danish Caucasian cohort with type 1 diabetes that among the 39 patients lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes, 10% had HNF1A gene mutations. Likewise, a Russian population-based investigation screened 506 patients suspected of having hereditary diabetes without autoimmune markers—evidenced by negative results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, maintained C-peptide levels, reduced insulin requirements within five years, and no severe obesity. This study uncovered pathogenic or LP mutations in 46.4% of participants, with HNF1A being one of the most frequently implicated genes. DNA sequencing remains vital for confirming MODY when clinical suspicion arises. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables simultaneous examination of extensive DNA fragments, thereby streamlining MODY identification. NGS has led to the discovery of numerous new genetic variants, with ACMG guidelines used to determine pathogenic status. Databases like ClinVar list over 1,000 HNF1A variants classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, or uncertain significance. In our patient, we identified a unique heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S), not previously reported in ClinVar or other widely used databases, pointing to its novelty but not detracting from its clinical importance. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid residue adjacent to established pathogenic sites, shows a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and is inherited alongside diabetes within the family—all criteria that support the LP classification according to ACMG. Therefore, although ClinVar serves as a helpful catalogue for variants, this variant's pathogenic potential is reinforced by several independent genetic and clinical findings beyond database listings.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations reside within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain experiences fewer mutations. The novel variant described in this report is also found in the DNA-binding domain. Certain studies have suggested that HNF1A mutations designated as P exert minimal influence on the functional activity of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants that do not independently lead to MODY may nevertheless be linked to a higher susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the necessity of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY for optimizing individual therapy. Ongoing investigations will aim to establish more efficient NGS screening methods in order to enhance MODY detection rates, thereby promoting improved cost efficiency and clinical outcomes.
