Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents an autosomal dominant, monogenic subtype of diabetes. This case report details a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY resulting from a newly identified heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient's family history revealed instances of diabetes occurring at a young age. Genetic analysis revealed a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A not previously documented. Management with a GLP-1 receptor agonist alongside metformin lowered the glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This report underscores the value of genetic testing in early-onset diabetes—which is often mistaken for type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and adds to the catalog of HNF1A mutations significant for individualized medical care.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes marked by an intrinsic defect in pancreatic β-cell formation and function. These disorders usually appear and are diagnosed prior to 25 years of age. MODY accounts for approximately 1% to 6% of cases of diabetes with onset at a young age. The prevalence of MODY may differ across diverse ethnic and racial populations and is likely underrecognized in many groups. Thus far, most research attention has focused on populations of White European descent. However, more recent investigations have aimed to assess the frequency of MODY in Asian and Australian cohorts. Prevalence estimates of MODY demonstrate notable variability, a disparity that largely stems from the restriction of genetic screening to patients who fit specific clinical criteria, even within population studies.

More than 15 distinct gene mutations have been associated with the MODY monogenic diabetes phenotype, and each genetic subtype may warrant a specific treatment approach. Most reported MODY cases are linked to pathogenic variants in genes such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. Among these, HNF1A-MODY is the most frequent, accounting for approximately 30% to 65% of MODY diagnoses, and is notable for its clear autosomal dominant inheritance pattern presenting as symptomatic diabetes within affected families. During early adolescence, affected individuals are often asymptomatic, with hyperglycemia frequently detected incidentally during standard blood work, usually in the absence of clinical signs of insulin resistance. Elevated postprandial blood glucose may be revealed through post-meal glucose testing or via a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), where levels of ≥11.1 mmol/L are observed. Glycosuria may also be present, stemming from a lower renal threshold for glucose reabsorption owing to impaired glucose transport in the renal tubules. As the disease progresses, patients may develop classic diabetic symptoms such as increased thirst, frequent urination, excessive hunger, and unintended weight loss, mirroring features of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). This overlap in clinical presentation makes distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D a persistent diagnostic challenge.

Multiple diagnostic pathways for MODY have been delineated in recent research. When physical examination and an evaluation of family history indicate a clinical pattern consistent with MODY, and type 1 diabetes-associated autoantibodies such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) test negative, molecular genetic testing becomes a critical component of the diagnostic process alongside other confirmatory biochemical assessments. Accurate identification of the specific subtype is essential to inform optimal management, anticipate complications, predict disease course, and provide genetic counseling to families. Recent investigations have revealed that hundreds of mutations within the HNF1A gene contribute to the development of HNF1A-MODY.

In the case described herein, genetic analysis through a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel identified a previously undocumented heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient with HNF1A-MODY. Pathogenicity assessment followed the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria for variant interpretation. This variant was absent from the literature as well as from the VarSome and ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was referred for assessment due to elevated blood glucose and the presence of glucose in the urine detected during a routine annual checkup. Physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. There were no signs of acanthosis nigricans noted. The family history was significant for diabetes with early onset: the maternal grandmother developed diabetes in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and the younger brother at age 20. No cases of early-onset cardiovascular disease were reported among diabetic family members.

Investigations:

His glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and his fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. At two hours following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), glucose concentration reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described polydipsia, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss in the recent period. Lipid studies showed a high-density lipoprotein (HDL) of 1.01 mmol/L and triglyceride level of 4.31 mmol/L, raising suspicion for metabolic syndrome (see tables 1 and 2). The patient had no notable prior medical or surgical issues.

The glucagon stimulation test demonstrated a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline, which increased to 0.83 nmol/L at six minutes, pointing to reduced insulin production. This assessment measures residual beta-cell activity by tracking C-peptide responses after glucagon injection. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide value above 0.60 nmol/L suggests adequate endogenous insulin secretion, whereas levels below that indicate beta-cell impairment. Nonetheless, glucagon stimulation should be viewed as an adjunctive tool, particularly when autoantibody status is unclear or clinical features are atypical. Microalbuminuria screening was negative. Sudomotor evaluation, performed with electrochemical skin conductance (such as Sudoscan), revealed marked reduction in hand sudomotor function, indicating severe small fiber autonomic nerve impairment. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) were negative. Genetic testing for MODY, performed one month after diagnosis, identified a heterozygous, pathogenic HNF1A variant (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), consistent with HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose readings. Initial management included basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, together with metformin at 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin at 5 mg daily, in alignment with an initial working diagnosis of type 2 diabetes (T2D). This approach targeted prompt glycaemic stabilization while also addressing possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, recommendations were made for the patient to follow a structured dietary regimen and to participate in consistent physical activity, constituting an integrated strategy for diabetes care. Subsequent assessments took place in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient setting to track glycaemic parameters and evaluate treatment efficacy.

Nonetheless, due to moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell reserve as evidenced by glucagon-stimulated C-peptide (0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet cell autoantibodies, and a prominent family history of diabetes with early onset, there was a substantial suspicion for maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY).

Initial treatment was started prior to the availability of genetic testing results, based on the clinical presentation and laboratory findings at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. With the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY genetically confirmed, and taking into account the patient’s partially retained but inadequate β-cell function as well as the decrease in HbA1c, insulin therapy was discontinued. The patient was started on dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, at a dose of 1.5 mg once weekly to aid glycemic management and help protect pancreatic function. Six months following the initiation of dulaglutide, the patient achieved an HbA1c of 6.0%, reflecting marked improvement. Upon stabilisation of glycaemic levels, the treatment plan was further streamlined to include metformin (1500 mg per day) together with saxagliptin (5 mg per day), a DPP-4 inhibitor, for ongoing maintenance. Regular monitoring is ongoing, and the patient has maintained HbA1c values consistently below 6.5%. To date, there are no indications of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and continued monitoring for chronic complications is in place.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor situated in the q24 region of chromosome 12. It consists of 10 exons covering 23,790 base pairs, and produces a protein composed of 631 amino acids, which is found in several organs such as the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Alterations in the HNF1A gene cause a gradual loss of β-cell functionality, reflected by reduced expression of the insulin and GLUT2 genes and diminished pyruvate kinase activity. This cascade leads to impaired insulin release and poor glucose tolerance.

Diagnosis in this patient followed established criteria for MODY, which include clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and genetic analysis. Typical features involve an early onset of diabetes, a strong family history, impaired postprandial glycemia, and absence of diabetes autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide level above 0.6 ng/mL is indicative of a non-type 1 diabetes diagnosis, but longitudinal monitoring of C-peptide is crucial, since normal levels can still be seen early in type 1 diabetes. One of the main challenges in identifying MODY is insufficient awareness and suspicion in clinical practice, with many individuals incorrectly classified as either type 1 or type 2 diabetics. The MODY probability calculator, created by the Exeter group, can support healthcare providers in selecting patients for genetic evaluation. Nevertheless, this calculator was based on data from a White European cohort and may inaccurately elevate the probability in non-White populations. For this patient, use of the calculator indicated a 62.4% likelihood (approximately 1 in 1.6 chance) of MODY. There are reports describing positive autoantibodies in some MODY cases. Although most patients with MODY and HNF1A mutations lack autoantibodies, a minority may test positive. Previous research has demonstrated that MODY patients tend to have lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome when compared to those with T2D; however, the increasing prevalence of obesity has diminished the utility of BMI or metabolic syndrome for distinguishing MODY from T2DM, which may account for the higher BMI observed in this case. Furthermore, while family history remains a pivotal element in assessing MODY risk, its reliability is limited by factors such as incomplete reporting, the occurrence of de novo mutations, and the frequent occurrence of T2DM among relatives.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on patient age and glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) measurements. The preferred initial therapy consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin release by interacting with ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As pancreatic β-cell function progressively worsens, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists provide a safer and potentially superior option to sulfonylureas, especially for patients at higher risk of hypoglycemia. Evidence from a small randomized, double-blind, crossover study indicated that both liraglutide and glimepiride significantly lower fasting plasma glucose and mitigate postprandial glucose variation, with comparable efficacy between the two drugs in individuals with HNF1A-MODY. Furthermore, the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists was linked to a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycemia relative to glimepiride. In the current patient, genetic confirmation allowed cessation of insulin therapy following stabilization of glycemic control, enabling successful transition to a GLP-1 receptor agonist while maintaining satisfactory glucose levels. This case underscores the pivotal role of genetic analysis in tailoring diabetes management. The presence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY results from decreased sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) expression, regulated by HNF1A transcriptional activity. Previous case reports describe occurrences of profound dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis in HNF1A-MODY patients stemming from pronounced SGLT-2 inhibition. Due to concerns including dehydration, possible hemodynamic instability, genital infections, and heightened risk of diabetic ketoacidosis, the disadvantages of SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY may surpass their advantages. The combination of liraglutide and metformin employed in this patient proved both efficacious and well-tolerated, aligning with outcomes observed in other studies.

Precision medicine plays an essential role in managing MODY, particularly because individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy every diagnostic benchmark established by current clinical protocols. At present, no reliable biomarkers are available to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Chen et al conducted a study in which 2,429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset type 2 diabetes were analyzed, revealing a MODY prevalence of 1.5% and pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants in 0.74% of participants. Møller et al reported, within a large population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, that among 39 individuals lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes, 10% possessed HNF1A mutations. Similarly, a recent investigation from Russia screened 506 subjects presumed to have hereditary diabetes forms—these individuals were selected due to negative results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, maintained C-peptide secretion, required little insulin within five years, and did not exhibit significant obesity. Pathogenic or LP genetic alterations were detected in 46.4% of these cases, with HNF1A mutations being among the most frequently identified. The diagnosis of MODY remains dependent on confirmatory DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has enhanced the practicality of MODY detection by facilitating comprehensive DNA analysis in a single reaction. The advent of NGS has resulted in the identification of many novel genetic variants. Evaluation of these variants' clinical relevance follows ACMG guidelines, which assist in classifying variants as pathogenic. Databases like ClinVar now list more than 1,000 unique HNF1A mutations encompassing pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In our patient, we identified a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S), absent from both ClinVar and other widely referenced variant repositories, indicating its novelty but not diminishing its clinical importance. Notably, this substitution occurs at an amino acid site adjacent to established pathogenic residues, has a high AlphaMissense prediction score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes within the pedigree—criteria that fulfill ACMG's standards for likely pathogenic designation. Therefore, while ClinVar offers valuable reference data, the pathogenicity of this particular variant is supported by several independent genetic and clinical indicators aside from its absence in such databases.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas mutations in the transactivation domain occur less frequently. The novel variant described in this report is positioned in the DNA-binding domain. Certain studies indicate that some HNF1A mutations, particularly P, exert limited influence on HNF1A protein functionality. Furthermore, specific HNF1A variants that do not independently lead to MODY may nevertheless contribute to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

To summarize, this case underscores the necessity of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its ramifications for personalized management. Future investigations aim to refine NGS screening protocols to enhance MODY diagnostic accuracy, thereby facilitating improved cost efficiency and clinical outcomes.
