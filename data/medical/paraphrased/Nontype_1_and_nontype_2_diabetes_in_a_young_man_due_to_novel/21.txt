Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents an autosomal dominant monogenic diabetes subtype. Here, we detail a patient with HNF1A-MODY resulting from a newly identified heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history revealed diabetes diagnosed at an early age. Molecular analysis identified a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A not previously documented. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist combined with metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This report underscores the role of genetic evaluation in cases of early-onset diabetes, which are often mistakenly diagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and broadens the known range of HNF1A mutations with implications for personalized therapy.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) comprises a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes resulting from a primary impairment in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders generally present and are identified prior to 25 years of age. MODY accounts for an estimated 1%–6% of diabetes cases with onset in youth. The prevalence of MODY differs among diverse ethnic and racial populations, and may be underdiagnosed. Most existing research has focused on White European cohorts. More recently, investigations have been conducted into the prevalence of MODY within Asian and Australian populations. Reported prevalence rates for MODY differ widely, a disparity often attributable to the restriction of genetic testing to patients selected by specific clinical features, including in population-based studies.

More than 15 genetic mutations have been associated with the monogenic MODY phenotype, each mutation corresponding to unique management approaches. Most cases across MODY subtypes are attributed to mutations in specific genes, including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. 

HNF1A-MODY accounts for the largest proportion of MODY, representing approximately 30%–65% of all cases, and demonstrates a distinct autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of diabetes symptoms within affected families. During early adolescence, clinical manifestations may be absent, and hyperglycemia is often detected incidentally through standard blood screenings, generally without evidence of insulin resistance. Postprandial blood glucose measurements or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal marked increases in glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria can also be observed, resulting from a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired glucose transport within the renal tubules. As the disease progresses, symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight loss, resembling those found in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D), may develop. Thus, the clinical similarity between MODY, type 1, and T2D continues to pose challenges for accurate diagnosis.

Multiple diagnostic protocols for MODY have been outlined in published research. When physical examination and assessment of family history indicate a clinical phenotype consistent with MODY, and autoantibodies related to type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, complemented by relevant biochemical testing, molecular genetic analysis plays an essential role in confirming diagnosis. Accurate identification is vital for guiding appropriate therapeutic decisions, predicting possible complications, informing prognosis, and supporting genetic counselling for families. Recent investigations have revealed numerous distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene associated with HNF1A-MODY.

In the case described herein, a previously unreported heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), was detected in exon 4 of HNF1A in a patient with HNF1A-MODY through genetic analysis utilizing a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel. The pathogenicity of this variant was evaluated according to the guidelines from the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) for variant interpretation. This specific mutation has not been documented in the literature, nor is it present in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment of hyperglycemia and glycosuria detected during his routine annual checkup. Physical examination revealed a body mass index of 24.6 kg/m², blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. No evidence of acanthosis nigricans was found on exam. The family history was significant for diabetes presenting at a young age: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at the age of 20. None of these relatives had a documented history of early-onset cardiovascular disease associated with diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and his fasting blood glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. Two hours after a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, the glucose value was 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described increased thirst, heightened appetite, and recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid evaluation showed an HDL of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, which may indicate metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He did not have any notable prior medical or surgical illnesses.

A glucagon stimulation test revealed a serum C-peptide level of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes post-injection, signifying inadequate endogenous insulin production. This assessment determines remaining β-cell capacity by measuring stimulated C-peptide after glucagon administration. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L suggests intact endogenous insulin secretion, while lower values imply impaired β-cell function. Nonetheless, the glucagon stimulation test is considered supportive rather than definitive, particularly when autoantibody tests are indeterminate or clinical features are unusual. No microalbuminuria was detected. Sudomotor function tests revealed markedly diminished sudomotor activity in both hands. This evaluation employed electrochemical skin conductance (such as Sudoscan), a non-invasive procedure to assess small fiber autonomic nerve performance. The results showed severely decreased conductance in the hands, consistent with considerable autonomic neuropathy. Tests for antibodies including GADA and IA-2A were negative. MODY genetic testing, performed one month after diagnosis, identified a heterozygous, pathogenic (P) variant in HNF1A (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), in accordance with HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The first therapeutic approach included basal insulin degludec at a dose of eight units per day, together with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), indicating an initial assessment consistent with type 2 diabetes (T2D). The purpose of this regimen was to achieve swift glycaemic control and target possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient was instructed to follow a structured meal plan and incorporate regular physical exercise as part of an all-encompassing diabetes care strategy. Subsequent assessments took place at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to track blood glucose regulation and evaluate response to therapy.

Nevertheless, considering the patient’s moderately retained but inadequate β-cell function as demonstrated by the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), negative islet autoantibodies, and a notable family history of early-onset diabetes, there was considerable clinical suspicion for MODY.

Treatment commenced prior to the return of genetic test results, relying on the clinical features and laboratory data observed at the initial diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month afterwards, the patient's HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. Once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed through genetic testing, and taking into account the patient’s partially preserved but inadequate β-cell activity and the reduction in HbA1c to 9.1%, insulin was discontinued. Dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg weekly) was initiated to promote glycemic regulation and help sustain pancreatic function. Six months later, HbA1c had further decreased to 6.0%, demonstrating marked improvement. With stabilization of glycemic control, the therapeutic regimen was streamlined to metformin (1500 mg daily) combined with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg daily) as ongoing maintenance. The patient is monitored regularly, and HbA1c has consistently stayed below 6.5%. There have been no findings of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and surveillance for possible long-term complications continues.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene functions as a transcription factor and is situated at the q24 locus of chromosome 12. It consists of 10 exons that together span 23,790 base pairs and codes for a protein composed of 631 amino acids. This protein is present in multiple organs, such as the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Alterations in the HNF1A gene are associated with a gradual reduction in β-cell performance, reflected by disrupted expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes, along with diminished activity of pyruvate kinase. This process ultimately results in lower insulin output and impaired glucose tolerance.

In this case, the diagnostic work-up followed the established criteria for MODY, which requires clinical, laboratory, and genetic substantiation. Criteria include an early onset of diabetes, pronounced familial occurrence, postprandial glucose intolerance, and absence of diabetes-specific autoantibodies. A preserved fasting C-peptide value above 0.6 ng/mL supports a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes. Nevertheless, ongoing C-peptide assessments are vital, as normal readings can occur during the initial phase of type 1 diabetes. One major obstacle in recognizing MODY is insufficient awareness and suspicion among healthcare providers, leading to frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator created by the Exeter team assists in selecting patients for genetic screening, although it is based on information from White European populations and might inflate MODY risk estimations for non-White individuals. For this patient, use of the calculator yielded a MODY likelihood of 62.4% (equivalent to a 1 in 1.6 probability). Some published case reports have found autoantibodies in MODY patients. A subset of individuals with HNF1A mutations do possess autoantibodies, but most cases lack them. Prior research indicates that MODY patients are less frequently affected by obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to those with T2D. However, the rising prevalence of obesity has reduced the effectiveness of BMI and metabolic syndrome for distinguishing MODY from T2DM, which may account for the higher BMI observed in this patient. Furthermore, although family history remains a pivotal component in MODY assessment, its reliability can be hampered by incomplete reporting, de novo mutations, and the widespread occurrence of T2DM in families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by patient age and HbA1c measurements. Low-dose sulfonylureas are the initial therapy of choice, as they stimulate insulin release by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function wanes, GLP-1 receptor agonists may offer a safer and more efficacious alternative to sulfonylureas, especially for those at higher risk for hypoglycaemia. In a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study, both liraglutide and glimepiride were shown to significantly lower fasting and postprandial glucose, with no statistically significant differences observed between the two among patients with HNF1A-MODY. Furthermore, the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists is associated with a decreased incidence of mild hypoglycaemic episodes compared to glimepiride. With the help of genetic testing, we were able to discontinue insulin after glycaemic control was established and switched the patient to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, which maintained optimal glycaemic management. This underlines the importance of genetic screening in shaping therapeutic strategies. The occurrence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY arises from diminished SGLT2 expression regulated by HNF1A at the transcriptional level. It has been reported that individuals with HNF1A-MODY may develop pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to significant inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. The use of SGLT-2 inhibitors in this population poses hazards such as an elevated risk of dehydration, possible vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, which may surpass the potential therapeutic advantages. In this patient, the combination of liraglutide and metformin was both effective and well tolerated, corroborating earlier findings.

Personalized therapeutic approaches are vital in managing MODY, as patients with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy all the established diagnostic criteria despite adherence to existing clinical guidelines. At present, there are no reliable biomarkers that distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes. Chen et al reported that, in a cohort of 2429 young Chinese adults presenting with early-onset type 2 diabetes, MODY accounted for 1.5% of cases, and 0.74% carried pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY-related genetic variants. In a study conducted by Møller et al, within a sizable population-based group of Danish Caucasian type 1 diabetes patients, 10% of the 39 individuals without high-risk HLA haplotypes exhibited mutations in HNF1A. Likewise, a recent Russian population-based analysis evaluated 506 subjects suspected of having inherited forms of diabetes without autoimmune indicators—characterized by negative results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, maintained C-peptide production, minimal insulin requirements over five years, and no severe obesity. This screening uncovered P or LP mutations in 46.4% of these cases, with HNF1A among the genes most often implicated. Confirming clinical suspicion of MODY requires DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) facilitates comprehensive assessment of patient DNA within a single experimental run, thereby enhancing the practicality of MODY detection. Advancements in NGS have led to the identification of numerous novel genetic variants. The ACMG supplies recommendations for interpreting the pathogenicity of such variants. More than 1000 unique HNF1A variants are catalogued in resources like ClinVar, encompassing pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In our patient, a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or other widely used variant repositories, attesting to its novelty without diminishing its clinical significance. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid position adjacent to known pathogenic residues, displays a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and segregates with diabetes in the family—fulfilling ACMG guidelines for a likely pathogenic classification. Accordingly, although ClinVar serves as an important database for variant reporting, the evidence supporting the pathogenicity of this variant is drawn from multiple independent genetic and clinical findings, extending beyond mere database annotation.

Research has shown that most detected mutations occur within the DNA-binding domain, whereas mutations are less frequently found in the transactivation domain. The novel variant described here is also located in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations suggest that HNF1A mutations, including P, only modestly impact the function of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants that do not independently cause MODY may still be linked to a heightened risk of developing T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its influence on tailoring patient-specific therapy. Future investigations should aim to establish more efficient NGS-based screening protocols to enhance the detection rate of MODY, thereby facilitating improved cost-effectiveness and clinical outcomes.
