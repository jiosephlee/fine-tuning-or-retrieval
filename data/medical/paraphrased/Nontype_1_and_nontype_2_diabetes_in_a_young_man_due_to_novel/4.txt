Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) constitutes a monogenic diabetes form inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. This paper presents a case involving hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A)-MODY, attributed to a novel heterozygous mutation in HNF1A. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose levels and glycosuria, but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. A significant family history of early-onset diabetes was documented. Genetic analysis identified a previously uncharacterized pathogenic variant of HNF1A. The patient's glycated haemoglobin decreased from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period following therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin. This case underscores the critical role of genetic testing in individuals with early-onset diabetes—often mistakenly classified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and broadens the catalog of HNF1A mutations with implications for personalized medicine.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes that arise from an intrinsic impairment in pancreatic β-cell maturation and activity. These disorders usually emerge and are identified prior to 25 years of age. MODY is responsible for roughly 1%–6% of diabetes cases presenting in youth. Its occurrence differs among diverse ethnic and racial populations, and its true frequency may often be underreported. To date, the majority of investigations have focused on White European cohorts. However, more recent research efforts have explored the incidence of MODY within Asian and Australian populations. Reported MODY prevalence rates exhibit substantial heterogeneity, which can be largely attributed to the restriction of genetic testing to individuals who fulfill specific clinical criteria, even when studies are conducted at the population level.

More than 15 distinct gene mutations have been associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with each genetic alteration informing specific therapeutic approaches. Most diagnosed instances across the spectrum of MODY subtypes are predominantly attributed to mutations in genes such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. 

HNF1A-MODY is recognized as the most prevalent MODY subtype, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all identified cases, and it demonstrates a marked autosomal dominant inheritance of symptomatic diabetes within affected families. During early adolescence, clinical manifestations are often absent, and hyperglycemia is frequently detected incidentally through standard blood work, typically in the absence of signs suggestive of insulin resistance. Postprandial glucose measurement or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) often uncovers a significant rise in blood glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be present, reflecting a decreased renal threshold for glucose secondary to impaired glucose reabsorption in the renal tubules. In more advanced phases, classic symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss—symptoms common in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may develop. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D remains clinically challenging due to considerable overlap in their phenotypic presentations.

To date, various diagnostic pathways for MODY have been proposed in the literature. When the initial physical examination and review of family history indicate a clinical profile suggestive of MODY, and laboratory analyses reveal negative results for type 1 diabetes-specific autoantibodies—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—supplementary biochemical assessments further strengthen the suspicion. At this stage, molecular genetic testing becomes pivotal in confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification of MODY is essential for guiding appropriate therapeutic choices, anticipating possible complications, providing prognostic insights, and supporting genetic counseling for families. Recent investigations have documented that a multitude of unique mutations within the HNF1A gene contribute to the development of HNF1A-MODY.

In the current case described herein, genetic analysis using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel revealed a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), situated in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY. The potential pathogenicity of this alteration was assessed in line with the criteria set forth by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) for the interpretation of genetic variants. Notably, this mutation was absent from published reports as well as from both the VarSome and ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of these findings.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment after elevated blood glucose and the presence of glucose in urine were detected during his routine annual checkup. Physical assessment revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure reading of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. The examination showed no evidence of acanthosis nigricans. The family history revealed multiple relatives with early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at the age of 20. No family member with diabetes had a recorded history of early-onset cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

The patient's glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), while fasting plasma glucose was recorded at 12.4 mmol/L. Following administration of 75 g oral glucose (OGTT), the 2-hour post-challenge glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence indicative of diabetic ketoacidosis. The individual described increased polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent weight loss. The lipid panel demonstrated a high-density lipoprotein concentration of 1.01 mmol/L and triglyceride levels of 4.31 mmol/L, findings suggestive of a metabolic syndrome phenotype (refer to tables 1 and 2). No significant prior medical or surgical conditions were reported.

A glucagon stimulation test indicated serum C-peptide values of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, reflecting inadequate endogenous insulin secretion. This assessment is utilized to measure the functional reserve of β-cells through stimulated C-peptide following glucagon injection. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide concentration greater than 0.60 nmol/L is indicative of preserved endogenous insulin release, whereas values below this threshold point toward β-cell dysfunction. Nevertheless, interpretation of the glucagon stimulation test should be supportive rather than confirmatory, especially when autoantibody testing is inconclusive or the patient’s presentation is atypical. Screening for microalbuminuria yielded negative results. Assessment of sudomotor function revealed a marked reduction over the hands. Sudomotor activity was evaluated through electrochemical skin conductance testing (for example, using Sudoscan), which non-invasively probes small fibre autonomic nerve integrity. Results showed significantly diminished conductance in the hands, pointing to notable autonomic dysfunction. Testing for antibodies, including both GADA and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A), was negative. One month after diagnosis, genetic analysis for MODY was undertaken and identified a heterozygous, pathogenic (P) variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), consistent with a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased levels of both HbA1c and plasma glucose. The initial therapeutic plan involved administering basal insulin degludec at a dosage of eight units per day, alongside metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), as the clinical picture was initially suggestive of T2D. This combination was intended to rapidly optimize glycaemic control and target underlying insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient received recommendations to follow a regulated diet and participate in consistent physical exercise, integrating lifestyle changes as core components of the diabetes management protocol. Ongoing monitoring of glycaemic parameters and assessment of the patient’s response to therapy took place during scheduled visits at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic.

Despite these measures, several clinical features raised the possibility of MODY: the patient’s glucagon stimulation test revealed moderately maintained but inadequate β-cell activity (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), there were no detectable islet autoantibodies, and there was a pronounced family history of early-onset diabetes.

Given these findings, treatment was initiated based on the clinical scenario and laboratory results available at diagnosis, prior to the receipt of genetic testing outcomes.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c was reduced to 9.1%. With genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and in light of the patient’s partially preserved but still inadequate β-cell function—alongside the observed improvement in HbA1c to 9.1%—insulin therapy was ceased. The patient was instead started on dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist) at 1.5 mg per week to enhance glycaemic control and support pancreatic function preservation. Over a 6-month period of this regimen, the patient’s HbA1c further decreased to 6.0%, reflecting marked clinical improvement. As glycaemic control became stable, the therapeutic approach was streamlined to a maintenance regimen consisting of metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day). The patient continues to attend regular follow-up appointments, with HbA1c levels consistently kept under 6.5%. Thus far, there is no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and surveillance for long-term complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, a transcription factor situated in the q24 locus of chromosome 12, consists of 10 exons covering a span of 23,790 base pairs and codes for a 631-amino-acid protein. This protein is present in several tissues, notably the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Pathogenic variants in HNF1A disrupt β-cell function over time, causing deficits in insulin production and GLUT2 gene expression, along with diminished pyruvate kinase activity. These cellular defects ultimately impair insulin secretion and contribute to the development of glucose intolerance.

The case was evaluated in accordance with established diagnostic guidelines for MODY, incorporating clinical assessment, biochemical markers, and genetic analysis. Key diagnostic elements include an early age at diabetes onset, significant familial aggregation, impaired postprandial glucose tolerance, and the absence of diabetes-associated autoantibodies. A preserved fasting C-peptide level above 0.6 ng/mL generally favors a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes, though repeat monitoring is recommended because C-peptide concentrations may appear normal during the initial phase of type 1 diabetes. Diagnosis of MODY is frequently complicated by low clinical suspicion and limited awareness, with many affected individuals being incorrectly classified as having either type 1 diabetes or T2D. The MODY probability calculator designed by the Exeter group can assist practitioners in selecting candidates for molecular testing, but as it was generated using data from White European cohorts, its predictive accuracy may not translate well to non-White populations, tending toward overestimation in such groups. When our patient’s information was applied to this calculator, a 62.4% likelihood (corresponding to approximately 1 in 1.6 probability) of MODY was produced. While the majority of individuals with MODY lack autoantibodies, occasional cases—particularly those with HNF1A mutations—may present with autoantibody positivity as documented in some reports. Historically, rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome among MODY patients have been lower compared to those with T2D; however, the increasing prevalence of obesity has diminished the discriminatory utility of BMI or metabolic syndrome, possibly accounting for the elevated BMI seen in our patient. Additionally, although a family history of diabetes remains a valuable diagnostic indicator for MODY, its practical value may be compromised by incomplete familial data, occurrence of de novo mutations, and the commonality of T2DM within families.

Therapeutic strategies for HNF1A-MODY are tailored based on the patient’s age and HbA1c measurements. The preferred initial approach is the administration of low-dose sulfonylureas, which potentiate insulin secretion by modulating potassium-sensitive ATP channels. As β-cell function diminishes, GLP-1 receptor agonists have been shown to be a preferable and safer alternative, especially in individuals with a heightened susceptibility to hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small-scale, randomised, double-blind, crossover study indicates that liraglutide and glimepiride both significantly reduce fasting and postprandial plasma glucose concentrations, with no major efficacy differences observed between the two in patients with HNF1A-MODY. Importantly, treatment with a GLP-1 receptor agonist is linked to a lower incidence of mild hypoglycaemia when compared to glimepiride. Genetic confirmation of diagnosis permitted cessation of insulin therapy following adequate glycaemic control, and facilitated a transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment, which ensured continued glucose regulation. This case underlines the pivotal role of genetic testing in shaping management strategies. Glycosuria observed in HNF1A-MODY stems from decreased SGLT2 expression, a transporter whose transcription is regulated by HNF1A. Reports have documented that patients with HNF1A-MODY may be at risk for significant dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to pronounced suppression of residual SGLT-2 activity. The hazards associated with SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy in these patients—including heightened risks of dehydration, potential vascular instability, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may surpass any prospective advantages. In the present instance, a regimen combining liraglutide and metformin proved to be both efficacious and safe, corroborating outcomes from earlier research.

The application of precision medicine is particularly important in the context of MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY may not consistently fulfill all diagnostic benchmarks, even when established clinical recommendations are followed. At present, the absence of robust biomarkers complicates the distinction between HNF1A-MODY and other diabetes types, such as type 1 diabetes and T2D. In a study conducted by Chen et al, involving 2429 young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D, MODY prevalence was found to be 1.5%, with 0.74% possessing pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al’s research, focusing on a population-based cohort of Danish Caucasians with type 1 diabetes, demonstrated that among 39 patients who lacked high-risk HLA haplotypes, 10% had detectable mutations within the HNF1A gene. Comparable findings were obtained in a recent Russian population study, in which 506 participants were screened due to clinical suspicion of hereditary diabetes forms without autoimmune characteristics. Criteria for inclusion were negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, preserved C-peptide production, minimal insulin dependence within five years, and absence of severe obesity. In this group, P or LP genetic variants were detected in 46.4% of cases, with HNF1A ranking among the frequently implicated genes. 

DNA sequencing remains the definitive approach to substantiate clinical suspicion of MODY. The introduction of NGS technology enables the comprehensive analysis of extensive regions of patient DNA simultaneously, thereby enhancing the practicality of routine MODY testing. Through the expanded use of NGS, a considerable number of novel genetic variants have been identified. The ACMG has issued recommendations for determining whether these variants are classified as P. To date, databases such as ClinVar report over 1000 unique HNF1A variants, categorized as P, LP, benign, likely benign, or variants of uncertain significance. In the case presented here, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not found in ClinVar or other widely referenced variant repositories, supporting its uniqueness as opposed to questioning its clinical relevance. Notably, this variant impacts an amino acid in proximity to established pathogenic sites, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense probability score (0.995), and shows segregation with diabetes within the family—all aligning with ACMG criteria for an LP classification. Consequently, although ClinVar is instrumental for variant cataloguing, the pathogenic classification of this variant is corroborated by several independent genetic and clinical data sources extending beyond mere database annotation.

Previous research has demonstrated that most detected mutations predominantly occur within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the frequency of mutations in the transactivation domain is comparatively low. The novel variant identified in this report is also positioned in the DNA-binding domain. Several studies have found that certain HNF1A mutations, particularly while P, exert only minor effects on the functional activity of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, there are HNF1A variants that, though insufficient to cause MODY independently, may nonetheless contribute to heightened susceptibility to T2D.

To summarize, this case underlines the critical role of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY in shaping personalized treatment approaches. Ongoing investigations will concentrate on optimizing NGS screening methodologies in order to enhance the detection rate of MODY, thereby improving clinical outcomes and cost-effectiveness.
