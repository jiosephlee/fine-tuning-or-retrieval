Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic, autosomal dominant subtype of diabetes. In this case, a patient with HNF1A-MODY was identified carrying a newly recognized heterozygous HNF1A mutation. The individual exhibited hyperglycemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. There was a notable family history of diabetes diagnosed at an early age. Molecular analysis detected a novel pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene. Initiation of therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist combined with metformin lowered the patient’s glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This case emphasizes the value of genetic testing for early-onset diabetes, which is commonly mistaken for type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and broadens the range of clinically significant HNF1A variants for precision medicine applications.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetic syndromes marked by fundamental defects in the development and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders generally present and are identified prior to the age of 25 years. MODY accounts for roughly 1%–6% of cases of diabetes with onset at a young age. The prevalence of MODY varies across different racial and ethnic populations, with rates that may be underestimated in some groups. To date, investigations have largely focused on White European subjects. However, more recent research has examined MODY prevalence among Asian and Australian populations. Reported prevalence estimates for MODY show considerable variability, which is frequently due to genetic testing being restricted to individuals with specific clinical profiles, even when the studies involve broader population groups.

Over 15 gene mutations associated with the MODY-monogenic diabetes phenotype have been identified, each linked to distinct management approaches. The most frequently implicated genes among the various forms of MODY are hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. HNF1A-MODY is the most common subtype, making up approximately 30%–65% of all MODY cases, and demonstrates a clear pattern of autosomal dominant inheritance with symptomatic diabetes affecting multiple generations. During early teenage years, symptoms may be absent, and hyperglycemia is often detected incidentally during routine testing, generally in the absence of clinical evidence for insulin resistance. Assessing postprandial blood glucose or utilizing a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal marked increases in glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be present, resulting from a lower renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired tubular glucose handling. As the disorder progresses, features such as excessive thirst, frequent urination, increased appetite, and weight loss may develop, resembling clinical presentations seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D is often challenging due to significant overlap in clinical characteristics.

Numerous diagnostic frameworks for MODY have been described in the literature. When physical examination and family history indicate a clinical phenotype consistent with MODY, and serological markers for type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma-associated antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are negative, supported by additional biochemical findings, molecular genetic testing becomes a key component of diagnosis. Accurate identification of MODY subtype is vital for guiding appropriate management strategies, anticipating complication risk, estimating prognosis, and providing informed counselling to families. Recent research has shown that hundreds of distinct variants in the HNF1A gene are linked to HNF1A-MODY.

In this reported case, we discovered a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY. This variant was detected through next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel analysis and its pathogenicity was evaluated according to the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant classification. This specific mutation has not been documented in the scientific literature, VarSome database, or ClinVar database. Written informed consent was obtained directly from the patient for publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was evaluated after hyperglycemia and glycosuria were detected during a routine annual check-up. His examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m², a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse rate of 78 beats per minute. No signs of acanthosis nigricans were found on physical exam. The patient's family history was significant for diabetes diagnosed at a young age, including the maternal grandmother in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20. No family members with diabetes had documented early-onset cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting glucose was recorded at 12.4 mmol/L. Two hours after a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), his glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described increased thirst, heightened appetite, and noted a recent loss in weight. His lipid panel showed a high-density lipoprotein value of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, which are consistent with possible metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He had no significant prior medical or surgical history.

The glucagon stimulation test resulted in serum C-peptide measurements of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at six minutes, reflecting inadequate insulin secretion. This assay examines remaining β-cell capacity by assessing C-peptide levels in response to glucagon administration. Typically, a stimulated C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L signifies preserved endogenous insulin production, whereas values below this threshold point to β-cell impairment. Nonetheless, the glucagon stimulation test is best used as an adjunctive assessment, especially when autoantibody findings are indeterminate or symptoms do not clearly align with established patterns. Microalbumin was not found in the urine. Sudomotor testing, done through electrochemical skin conductance (such as with Sudoscan), which gauges small-fiber autonomic nerve function, revealed profoundly reduced conductance in the hands—indicating marked autonomic impairment. Antibodies, specifically GADA and IA-2A, tested negative. At one month following the diagnosis, genetic analysis for MODY was performed and identified a heterozygous pathogenic (P) variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose values. The first therapeutic approach included basal insulin degludec (eight units daily), metformin (1500 mg per day), and saxagliptin (5 mg per day), reflecting an initial assessment suggestive of T2D. This regimen was chosen to achieve swift glycaemic regulation while targeting possible insulin resistance.

The patient also received recommendations to follow a systematic dietary plan and maintain consistent physical activity as integral parts of their diabetes management. Periodic reviews were scheduled in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient setting to track glycaemic parameters and evaluate treatment efficacy.

Nonetheless, with evidence of moderately retained but inadequate β-cell activity on the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced family history of early-onset diabetes, MODY became highly suspected clinically.

Therapy was initiated based on clinical symptoms and laboratory data prior to receipt of genetic testing results.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c reduced to 9.1%. With genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY, and given the patient's moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell function together with the observed HbA1c improvement, insulin was discontinued and dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) was initiated to enhance glycaemic management and support pancreatic function. Following 6 months of therapy, the patient’s HbA1c further dropped to 6.0%, demonstrating marked glycaemic improvement. Once glycaemic stability was achieved, therapy was simplified to metformin (1500 mg per day) combined with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg per day) as ongoing maintenance. The patient continues on regular follow-up, maintaining HbA1c levels below 6.5%. To date, there have been no detected cases of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, with continued monitoring for chronic complications underway.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene acts as a transcription factor and is found on chromosome 12 at the q24 locus. It consists of 10 exons over 23,790 base pairs and encodes a 631–amino acid protein present in several organs, notably the pancreas, kidneys, and liver. Mutations in HNF1A result in a gradual deterioration of β-cell function, leading to deficient expression of both insulin and GLUT2, as well as decreased pyruvate kinase activity. These disruptions culminate in impaired insulin release and abnormal glucose tolerance.

For this patient, the diagnostic approach followed established criteria for MODY, which require clinical, biochemical, and genetic components—such as diabetes onset at a young age, a strong familial pattern, postprandial hyperglycemia, and absence of diabetes-related autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide level above 0.6 ng/mL points toward a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes. Nevertheless, repeated assessment of C-peptide is necessary, since values may be normal in early type 1 diabetes. Lack of familiarity and suspicion among clinicians remains a major obstacle to MODY diagnosis, often resulting in misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. To support diagnostic accuracy, the Exeter group developed a MODY probability calculator, which can be a useful tool in determining which individuals may benefit from genetic analysis. It is important to note that this calculator is based on data from a White European cohort and may overstate the likelihood of MODY for other ethnicities. Application of this calculator to our case produced a MODY probability estimate of 62.4% (approximately 1 in 1.6). There have been documented cases of MODY patients presenting with autoantibodies, and though most individuals with HNF1A-MODY lack such markers, a minority do have them. Historically, individuals with MODY demonstrate lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to those with type 2 diabetes; however, with increasing rates of obesity, differentiation between MODY and T2DM based solely on BMI or the presence of metabolic syndrome has become less reliable, potentially contributing to the elevated BMI observed in this patient. Furthermore, while family history remains a pivotal diagnostic clue in MODY, its sensitivity is reduced by factors such as inaccurate reporting, the occurrence of de novo mutations, and the widespread nature of family history in T2DM.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on both the patient’s age and HbA1c results. Initial therapy typically utilizes low-dose sulfonylureas, which increase insulin secretion through their action on ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function declines, GLP-1 receptor agonists may provide a safer and more effective option than sulfonylureas, especially for individuals with heightened risk of hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small, randomized, double-blind, crossover trial showed that liraglutide and glimepiride each significantly lower fasting plasma glucose and mitigate postprandial glucose swings, without significant differences between the two treatments in HNF1A-MODY patients. GLP-1 receptor agonists also carry a reduced likelihood of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic confirmation allowed for cessation of insulin therapy after achieving glycaemic control and replacement with GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment, which sustained adequate glucose regulation. This underscores the indispensable role of genetic testing in optimizing therapy selection. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria arises from decreased SGLT2 expression regulated by HNF1A. There are reports documenting that patients with HNF1A-MODY may be susceptible to pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to marked suppression of remaining SGLT-2 activity. Consequently, potential adverse outcomes—including increased dehydration risk, vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—associated with SGLT-2 inhibitor use in HNF1A-MODY patients may surpass the therapeutic gains. In this patient, the combination treatment with liraglutide and metformin proved both efficacious and safe, corroborating earlier studies.

Precision medicine plays a vital role in managing MODY, since individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all the established diagnostic criteria, even though clinical recommendations are in place. At present, appropriate biomarkers to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes are lacking. According to findings by Chen et al, MODY prevalence was 1.5% among 2429 young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D, and 0.74% had pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al demonstrated that in a population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 individuals without high-risk HLA haplotypes were found to possess HNF1A gene mutations. Likewise, a recent Russian population-based investigation screened 506 subjects with suspected hereditary diabetes lacking autoimmune characteristics—defined by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at presentation, preserved C-peptide levels, low insulin requirements within 5 years, and no severe obesity. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were detected in 46.4% of these cases, with HNF1A being among the genes most frequently involved. DNA sequencing is indispensable for definitive MODY diagnosis. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables comprehensive analysis of patient DNA within a single run, streamlining MODY genetic testing. The use of NGS has facilitated identification of numerous genetic variants, with the ACMG issuing guidelines to determine pathogenicity. Databases such as ClinVar have catalogued over 1000 separate HNF1A variants, encompassing categories of pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and uncertain significance. In the present case, the newly observed heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not listed in ClinVar or other commonly referenced variant repositories, indicating its novelty rather than diminishing its clinical significance. Notably, this variant affects an amino acid site neighboring established pathogenic residues, has an elevated AlphaMissense score (0.995), and is observed in family members with diabetes, all features which fulfill ACMG criteria for a likely pathogenic classification. Therefore, while ClinVar serves as a useful database for documenting variants, the pathogenicity of this variant is corroborated by independent genetic and clinical evidence beyond what is recorded in databases.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a comparatively lower mutation frequency. The newly identified variant in this report resides in the DNA-binding domain. Evidence from several studies suggests that certain HNF1A mutations, such as P, exert limited influence on HNF1A protein function. Moreover, there are HNF1A variants which, although insufficient to independently cause MODY, may contribute to an elevated risk of developing T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the necessity of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its implications for personalized therapy. Ongoing research aims to refine next-generation sequencing (NGS) screening approaches in order to enhance MODY detection rates, thereby optimizing both economic efficiency and clinical outcomes.
