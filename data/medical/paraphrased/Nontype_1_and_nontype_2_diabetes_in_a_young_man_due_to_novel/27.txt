Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Overview: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic diabetes subtype with autosomal dominant inheritance. This case report details an individual diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY attributable to a previously unidentified heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual was found to have elevated blood glucose levels and glycosuria, but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient’s family history revealed cases of early-onset diabetes. Genetic analysis identified a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist alongside metformin lowered the patient's glycated hemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over three months. This report underscores the critical role of genetic testing in the evaluation of early-onset diabetes, commonly misclassified as either type 1 or type 2, and adds to the catalog of HNF1A mutations with implications for personalized therapeutic strategies.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes disorders characterized by an intrinsic defect affecting both development and functionality of pancreatic β-cells. These conditions generally emerge and are identified before 25 years of age. MODY accounts for approximately 1%–6% of cases of diabetes with early onset. The prevalence of MODY differs among diverse ethnic and racial populations, and it may be underrecognized in some groups. Most existing research has focused primarily on White European individuals. More recently, studies have evaluated the occurrence of MODY among Asian and Australian populations. Reported prevalence rates for MODY can be highly variable, often due to genetic screening strategies that are restricted to persons selected for certain clinical features, even in population-based research.

Mutations associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype have been discovered in more than 15 distinct genes, and each gene variant may require a different therapeutic approach. The majority of MODY cases can be attributed to alterations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of MODY diagnoses, and demonstrates an evident autosomal dominant inheritance pattern in affected families. During early adolescence, affected individuals may be asymptomatic, with hyperglycemia often found incidentally during standard laboratory assessments and typically absent signs of insulin resistance. Elevated postprandial glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L) can be detected by measuring blood glucose after meals or following a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Glycosuria may also be present, due to a lowered renal glucose reabsorption threshold stemming from impaired glucose transport in the renal tubules. Nevertheless, in advanced stages of the condition, clinical signs such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight loss—resembling those seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may appear. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 diabetes and T2D can be particularly challenging, given their similar clinical presentations.

Multiple diagnostic pathways for MODY have been delineated in research. When clinical evaluation and a review of family history point toward a MODY phenotype, and autoantibody tests for type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—yield negative results, alongside corroborating biochemical examinations, molecular genetic analysis becomes pivotal in confirming the diagnosis. An accurate diagnosis is essential for selecting proper therapy, predicting complications, evaluating outcomes, and enabling genetic counseling within families. Recent investigations have revealed that numerous distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene are causative for HNF1A-MODY.

In the case presented here, genetic analysis with a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel identified a new heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient with HNF1A-MODY. The pathogenicity of this alteration was assessed following the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) standards for variant interpretation. This specific mutation has not previously been documented in published literature, the VarSome database, or ClinVar database. Written informed consent was directly obtained from the patient for the publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment due to hyperglycemia and glycosuria identified at his routine annual check-up. On physical exam, his body mass index measured 24.6 kg/m2, with a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. The physical evaluation did not reveal any evidence of acanthosis nigricans. His family history was significant for cases of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother developed the condition in her 30s, his mother was diagnosed in her late 20s, and his younger brother at 20 years of age. There were no reports of early-onset cardiovascular disease in those family members with diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting plasma glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. After a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, his 2-hour glucose value reached 19.8 mmol/L. No evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis was present. He described increased thirst, heightened appetite, and unintentional weight loss recently. His lipid panel showed an HDL cholesterol of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings that point toward metabolic syndrome (see tables 1 and 2). He did not have any notable past medical or surgical conditions.

The glucagon stimulation test produced a serum C-peptide concentration of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, suggesting an inadequate endogenous insulin response. This test assesses remaining β-cell activity by analyzing C-peptide secretion following glucagon administration. Clinically, stimulated C-peptide values over 0.60 nmol/L are usually indicative of preserved insulin production, while lower levels reflect β-cell failure. Nevertheless, the glucagon stimulation test serves primarily as an adjunct, especially when islet antibody findings are negative or presentations are not classical. Screening for microalbuminuria was negative. Assessment of sudomotor function, performed using electrochemical skin conductance measurement (e.g., via Sudoscan), revealed profoundly decreased activity in the hands, suggesting marked autonomic small fibre involvement. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibody (GADA) and islet antigen-2 antibody (IA-2A) assays were negative. Genetic testing for MODY, performed one month following diagnosis, identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The initial therapeutic approach included basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, along with metformin at 1500 mg per day and saxagliptin at 5 mg per day, reflecting an initial consideration of T2D. This regimen was selected to facilitate prompt glycaemic control while addressing possible insulin resistance.

Alongside medication, the patient received guidance to follow a structured dietary regimen and maintain regular physical exercise as integral components of diabetes management. Follow-up appointments took place at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to track glycaemic control and evaluate response to treatment.

Despite therapy, the patient demonstrated moderately conserved, yet inadequate, β-cell function on the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), with negative islet autoantibodies and a pronounced family history of early-onset diabetes, raising considerable suspicion for MODY.

Initial management commenced before genetic test results were available, based on clinical presentation and laboratory findings obtained at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient's HbA1c was reduced to 9.1%. With confirmation of HNF1A-MODY on genetic testing, and in light of the patient's intermediate but suboptimal β-cell capacity and the observed improvement in HbA1c, insulin therapy was stopped. Dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist at 1.5 mg weekly, was initiated to assist with blood glucose regulation and to help preserve pancreatic function. Six months later, HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, reflecting marked glycaemic improvement. Given stable glycaemic control, therapy was further streamlined to metformin at 1500 mg/day plus the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin at 5 mg/day for ongoing management. The patient continues to be followed regularly, with HbA1c maintained below 6.5%. No evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy has been detected thus far, and surveillance for long-term sequelae is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, which acts as a transcription factor, is situated at the q24 locus of chromosome 12. It contains 10 exons over 23,790 base pairs and produces a protein consisting of 631 amino acids, found in multiple organs such as the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Pathogenic variants in HNF1A result in a gradual loss of β-cell function, demonstrated by reduced activity of insulin and GLUT2 gene expression, in addition to diminished pyruvate kinase activity. This leads to lower insulin release and the development of glucose intolerance.

Diagnosis in this report followed established MODY diagnostic standards, requiring the presence of clinical, biochemical, and genetic data, such as diabetes with early onset, notable family history, impaired postprandial glucose regulation, and negative islet autoantibodies. Preservation of fasting C-peptide concentrations above 0.6 ng/mL argues against type 1 diabetes. Despite this, repeated evaluation of C-peptide is necessary since initial values can fall within normal limits during the early stages of type 1 diabetes. An important obstacle to diagnosing MODY is insufficient recognition and suspicion in the clinical setting, causing frequent misclassification as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator created by the Exeter group is a supportive tool for clinicians in selecting candidates for genetic screening, but because it was developed using White European cohorts, the probability of MODY may be overestimated when applied to other populations. Using this tool, the likelihood of MODY in our patient was calculated as 62.4% (1 in 1.6 chance). Some reports document the presence of autoantibodies among individuals with MODY; a subset of those carrying HNF1A mutations may be autoantibody positive, but most MODY cases lack autoantibodies. Previous research has indicated that the frequency of obesity and metabolic syndrome in people with MODY is typically lower than in those with type 2 diabetes. However, as obesity becomes more widespread, using BMI or metabolic syndrome features to differentiate MODY from T2DM is less reliable, which could account for the higher BMI observed in this patient. Moreover, while family history remains a key indicator for MODY, its diagnostic value may be restricted because of inadequate reporting, novel mutations, or the high baseline rate of type 2 diabetes among relatives.

Therapeutic strategies for HNF1A-MODY depend upon patient age and HbA1c values. Sulfonylureas at low doses are the primary option, increasing insulin production by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. GLP-1 receptor agonists may be preferable and safer alternatives as β-cell function declines, especially for those at heightened risk of hypoglycaemia. Findings from a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study revealed liraglutide and glimepiride both significantly lowered fasting plasma glucose and managed postprandial glucose variations; no significant difference was observed between these therapies among HNF1A-MODY patients. The GLP-1 receptor agonists also present a reduced likelihood of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic identification enabled us to cease insulin therapy once glycaemic control was attained and shift to GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment, successfully maintaining glycaemic regulation. This underscores the pivotal role of genetic testing in informing therapeutic choices. Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY stems from diminished SGLT2 expression, which is regulated by HNF1A transcription. One publication described HNF1A-MODY patients developing pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis because of strong suppression of residual SGLT2 activity. Use of SGLT2 inhibitors in individuals with HNF1A-MODY is associated with increased risks, including dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infection, and diabetic ketoacidosis, which may overshadow their clinical benefit. In this instance, a regimen of liraglutide plus metformin proved to be safe and efficacious, aligning with previous findings.

Personalized therapeutic approaches are vital in managing MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy all guideline-based diagnostic criteria. To date, appropriate biomarkers distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes and T2D remain unavailable. Chen et al conducted a study showing that MODY was identified in 1.5% of 2429 young Chinese adults diagnosed early with T2D, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al's research demonstrated that, among a population cohort of Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of the 39 cases lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes carried mutations within the HNF1A gene. In parallel, a recent Russian population-based study screened 506 patients suspected of hereditary diabetes without autoimmune characteristics—defined by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibody tests at disease onset, maintained C-peptide secretion, minimal insulin need within 5 years, and the absence of severe obesity. The investigation found P or LP genetic changes in 46.4% of these subjects, with HNF1A being among the most frequently mutated genes. Confirmation of clinical suspicion for MODY relies on DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) facilitates simultaneous examination of substantial patient DNA volumes, enhancing MODY detection efforts. The introduction of NGS technology has yielded the discovery of numerous novel genetic alterations. The ACMG has established standards for determining variant pathogenicity. Databases, such as ClinVar, now record over 1000 unique HNF1A variants encompassing pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In this report, the newly described heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not found in ClinVar or other standard databases, underscoring its novelty rather than diminishing its clinical importance. Of note, this variant changes an amino acid next to recognized pathogenic residues, possesses an elevated AlphaMissense score (0.995), and segregates with diabetes within the patient's family, all features consistent with ACMG criteria for likely pathogenic designation. Therefore, although ClinVar is instrumental for variant documentation, the pathogenic nature of this variant is corroborated by several independent genetic and clinical lines of evidence outside of database listings.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant presented in this report is also positioned in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have revealed that specific HNF1A mutations, including P, exert limited influence on HNF1A protein activity. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants that are not sufficient to cause MODY independently may still contribute to an elevated risk for type 2 diabetes (T2D).

In summary, this case underscores the critical role of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and the resulting implications for individualized therapeutic strategies. Ongoing research will aim to establish more effective next-generation sequencing (NGS) screening methods to enhance the diagnostic yield for MODY, thereby promoting improved cost efficiency and clinical outcomes.
