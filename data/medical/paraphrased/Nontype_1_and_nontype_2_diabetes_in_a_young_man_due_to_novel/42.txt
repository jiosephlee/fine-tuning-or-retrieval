Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic diabetes disorder inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. This report presents a patient with HNF1A-MODY caused by a previously unidentified heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, but did not have diabetic ketoacidosis. Early-onset diabetes was observed among family members. Genetic analysis detected a novel pathogenic HNF1A mutation. Treatment involving a GLP-1 receptor agonist along with metformin lowered glycated haemoglobin levels from 10.2% to 6.0% over 3 months. This case emphasizes the value of genetic testing in patients with early-onset diabetes—commonly mistaken for type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and broadens the catalogue of HNF1A mutations important to individualized medical care.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes that result from primary abnormalities in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders are most often identified before 25 years of age. MODY accounts for an estimated 1%–6% of diabetes cases with onset in youth. The frequency of MODY may differ across ethnic and racial populations and may be underreported. Much of the prior research has focused primarily on White European groups, although more recent work has explored its prevalence within Asian and Australian populations. Reported prevalence rates for MODY demonstrate considerable variability, frequently due to the fact that genetic testing is usually performed only for patients selected by specific clinical criteria, even in studies addressing whole populations.

Pathogenic variants in more than 15 genes have been associated with the monogenic MODY phenotype, each leading to unique management approaches. Most reported MODY cases involve mutations in genes such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. 

HNF1A-MODY accounts for the largest proportion of MODY cases, representing approximately 30%–65% overall, and is characterized by an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of symptomatic diabetes within affected families. In the early adolescent period, patients may lack obvious symptoms, and hyperglycemia is often identified incidentally during routine laboratory evaluations, usually in the absence of clinical signs of insulin resistance. Postprandial glucose testing or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal markedly elevated post-load glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be present, reflecting a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to altered tubular glucose transport. As the condition progresses, patients may develop symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, increased appetite, and weight loss, resembling those seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). The similarity in clinical presentation makes distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D a persistent diagnostic challenge.

Several diagnostic protocols for MODY have been outlined in the literature. When the clinical picture, combined with physical examination and family history, points to MODY, and autoantibody screens for type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are negative, additional biochemical assessments can support the diagnosis. Molecular genetic analysis becomes pivotal at this stage. Accurate identification of the MODY subtype is essential for appropriate therapy selection, predicting complication risks, determining prognosis, and providing family counseling. Recent research has documented numerous mutations in the HNF1A gene as causative for HNF1A-MODY.

In the present case, a previously undescribed heterozygous change, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A, was detected in a patient with HNF1A-MODY using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel. Pathogenicity assessment followed variant interpretation guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG). This mutation has not been found in existing literature, nor indexed in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. Written informed consent for publication of this report was obtained directly from the patient.

Case presentation:

A young adult presented for assessment of elevated blood glucose and the presence of glucose in the urine, both detected during his routine annual checkup. His physical examination showed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m^2, a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse of 78 beats per minute. There were no signs of acanthosis nigricans identified. The patient's family history was significant for diabetes diagnosed at a young age: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. No family member with diabetes had a reported history of premature cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting blood glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), the 2-hour plasma glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. No clinical features of diabetic ketoacidosis were observed. The patient described symptoms of polydipsia, increased appetite, and unintentional weight loss in the preceding period. The lipid panel showed a high-density lipoprotein of 1.01 mmol/L and elevated triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings consistent with possible metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He had no notable past medical or surgical conditions.

Glucagon stimulation testing yielded a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes post-stimulation, reflecting inadequate insulin production. This assessment measures remnant β-cell activity by determining C-peptide levels in response to glucagon. Typically, a peak C-peptide greater than 0.60 nmol/L is indicative of remaining endogenous insulin secretory capacity, whereas values below this point point toward β-cell dysfunction. Nevertheless, interpretation of the glucagon stimulation test is adjunctive, not confirmatory—especially in situations where islet autoantibody data is equivocal or if the clinical features are atypical. There was no evidence of microalbuminuria. Electrochemical skin conductance evaluation (such as with Sudoscan) identified markedly reduced sudomotor activity in the hands, suggestive of significant small fibre autonomic impairment. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibody (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibody (IA-2A) assays were negative. At one month after initial diagnosis, genetic analysis for MODY was undertaken and identified a heterozygous pathogenic (P) HNF1A variant (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), confirming HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly raised HbA1c and plasma glucose levels. The first therapeutic plan involved basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, together with metformin at 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin at 5 mg daily, indicating an initial working diagnosis of T2D. This approach was intended to promptly achieve glycaemic control while managing possible insulin resistance.

Alongside medication, the patient was instructed to follow a structured diet and participate in consistent physical activity, forming part of an integrated diabetes management protocol. Subsequent assessments were carried out at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to track glycaemic status and evaluate the response to therapy.

Nevertheless, considering the patient’s partially maintained but suboptimal β-cell function on glucagon stimulation (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), negative results for islet autoantibodies, and a notable family history of diabetes onset at an early age, MODY was strongly suspected clinically.

The initial management was implemented before genetic test results became available, relying on the patient’s clinical features and laboratory data observed at presentation.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. Following genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and given the patient’s moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell activity, as well as the improvement in HbA1c, insulin therapy was stopped. Instead, dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg weekly) was introduced to maintain glycaemic control and help preserve pancreatic function. Six months into this treatment, the patient’s HbA1c further decreased to 6.0%, demonstrating marked progress. Once glycaemic levels stabilized, the treatment was streamlined to a maintenance regimen consisting of metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day). The patient continues to be closely monitored, with HbA1c persistently below 6.5%. There is currently no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and surveillance for potential long-term complications continues.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene functions as a transcription factor and is situated on chromosome 12 at the q24 locus. It consists of 10 exons, spans 23,790 base pairs, and produces a protein with 631 amino acids that is expressed in multiple tissues such as the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Mutations affecting HNF1A result in a gradual loss of β-cell functionality, evidenced by decreased expression of both insulin and GLUT2 genes as well as diminished activity of pyruvate kinase. This eventually leads to lower levels of insulin secretion and impaired glucose tolerance.

For this case, the diagnostic workup followed established MODY criteria, which require clinical, biochemical, and genetic corroboration—including early diabetes onset, strong familial patterns, postprandial glucose intolerance, and absent autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide above 0.6 ng/mL favors a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes. Nonetheless, continued assessment of C-peptide is warranted, as normal values can appear during early type 1 diabetes. One major obstacle in diagnosing MODY is insufficient clinical awareness, resulting in frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator by the Exeter group is a helpful resource for identifying potential candidates for genetic screening; however, because it was validated in a White European cohort, it may inflate MODY risk estimates for non-White patients. When our patient's information was input into this calculator, the predicted probability of MODY was 62.4% (equivalent to a 1 in 1.6 likelihood). Some published reports have noted the occurrence of autoantibodies in MODY patients, including individuals carrying HNF1A mutations, although most cases of MODY remain negative for autoantibodies. Previous investigations have demonstrated that MODY patients generally have lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome than those with T2D, but with increasing prevalence of obesity, the ability to differentiate MODY from T2DM by BMI or metabolic syndrome status has diminished, possibly accounting for the higher BMI observed in this case. Furthermore, although family history is considered important in identifying MODY, its reliability may be reduced due to factors such as under-reporting, de novo mutations, and the common occurrence of T2DM within families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by patient age and HbA1c concentrations. The initial therapeutic approach typically consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin secretion through activation of potassium-sensitive ATP channels. As β-cell function deteriorates, GLP-1 receptor agonists may offer a preferable and safer substitute for sulfonylureas, especially in those with a propensity for hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study indicated that both liraglutide and glimepiride lowered fasting plasma glucose and minimised postprandial glycaemic excursions to a similar extent in subjects with HNF1A-MODY. Furthermore, use of GLP-1 receptor agonists is associated with a decreased incidence of mild hypoglycaemic episodes when compared to glimepiride. Genetic identification in this patient allowed for cessation of insulin following attainment of glycaemic targets, with a transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy that maintained optimal glucose control. This underscores the important role of genetic testing in shaping clinical management strategies. The presence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY results from diminished SGLT2 expression, regulated transcriptionally by HNF1A. There has been documentation of HNF1A-MODY patients developing marked dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis resulting from substantial inhibition of the remaining SGLT-2 activity. In HNF1A-MODY, the application of SGLT-2 inhibitors is associated with considerable risks, including aggravated dehydration, risk of vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, which may supersede their prospective advantages. In this patient, treatment with liraglutide alongside metformin achieved effective and safe glycaemic control, corroborating prior literature.

Personalized medical approaches are vital for managing MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fully align with established diagnostic guidelines, even though such protocols exist. At present, suitable biomarkers distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes and T2D are lacking. Chen et al conducted a study involving 2429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D, finding a MODY prevalence rate of 1.5%, with 0.74% carrying pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY mutations. Møller et al’s research underscored that within a broad population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 individuals without any high-risk HLA haplotypes were found to possess HNF1A gene mutations. Likewise, a contemporary population-based investigation from Russia screened 506 participants suspected of hereditary diabetes types—characterized by absent IAA, ICA, GADA and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, sustained C-peptide secretion, minimal insulin requirements over five years, and no severe obesity—and found P or LP genetic variants in 46.4% of cases, with HNF1A being among the most frequently impacted genes. Genetic sequencing remains imperative for confirming MODY suspicion in clinical practice. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables extensive genomic analysis per reaction, making MODY screening more practicable. The advent of NGS has led to identification of numerous novel genetic variants. The ACMG outlines criteria for assessing variant pathogenicity. Databases such as ClinVar catalogue more than 1000 unique HNF1A variants, encompassing pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In this report, a new heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not found in ClinVar or other commonly referenced databases, underlining its novel status without casting doubt on its clinical relevance. Significantly, this variant involves an amino acid residue adjacent to established pathogenic residues, has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and demonstrates familial segregation with diabetes, all of which satisfy ACMG guidelines for likely pathogenic classification. Thus, while ClinVar serves as a valuable repository for variants, the pathogenicity of this specific variant is validated by several independent lines of clinical and genetic evidence beyond database inclusion.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, with fewer mutations occurring in the transactivation domain. The novel variant described in this report is also positioned within the DNA-binding domain. Evidence from some studies suggests that certain HNF1A mutations, including P, have only limited effects on the function of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, some HNF1A variants that are insufficient to independently cause MODY might still be linked to a heightened risk of developing T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its influence on personalized therapy. Future investigations will aim to create more efficient NGS-based screening methods to enhance MODY diagnostic yield, ultimately supporting improved cost-effectiveness and clinical outcomes.
