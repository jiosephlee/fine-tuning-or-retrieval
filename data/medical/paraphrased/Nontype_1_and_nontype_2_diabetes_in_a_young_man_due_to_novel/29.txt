Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a monogenic diabetic disorder inherited in an autosomal dominant fashion. This case concerns a patient with HNF1A-MODY resulting from a novel heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) gene. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose and glycosuria, but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient's family history revealed diabetes with early onset. Genetic analysis identified a pathogenic HNF1A variant not previously reported. Initiation of a GLP-1 receptor agonist together with metformin lowered the patient’s glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month interval. This case underscores the value of genetic evaluation in patients with early-onset diabetes—often incorrectly categorized as type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and adds to the known range of clinically significant HNF1A mutations pertinent for precision medicine approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes that arise from a fundamental impairment in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders usually appear and are identified prior to 25 years of age. MODY accounts for roughly 1%–6% of diabetes cases in young individuals. The frequency of MODY can differ among diverse ethnic and racial populations and may frequently be underestimated. Investigations so far have primarily focused on populations of White European descent, though newer studies have examined MODY prevalence in regions such as Asia and Australia. The reported prevalence of MODY is highly variable, which is often attributed to the fact that genetic testing tends to be restricted to patients selected based on clinical criteria, even in studies assessing population groups.

Over 15 gene mutations have been associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, each necessitating distinct approaches to treatment. Most cases across various MODY subtypes stem from alterations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. HNF1A-MODY is the most common MODY subtype, accounting for roughly 30%–65% of diagnosed MODY cases, and is characterized by clear autosomal dominant inheritance of diabetes within affected families. During early adolescence, clinical manifestations are often absent, and hyperglycemia tends to be discovered incidentally through routine laboratory screening, generally without signs indicative of insulin resistance. Measuring postprandial glucose or performing a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal marked elevations in blood glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Moreover, glycosuria may be present, which arises from a diminished renal threshold for glucose, a consequence of impaired renal tubular glucose transport. As the condition progresses, symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss—akin to those in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—can occur. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D poses a diagnostic challenge due to similarities in clinical presentation.

To date, multiple diagnostic protocols for MODY have been outlined in the literature. When physical findings and evaluation of family history raise suspicion for MODY, and autoantibodies commonly linked to type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, alongside other corroborative biochemical assessments, molecular genetic analysis becomes an essential tool for diagnosis. Accurate identification of MODY subtypes is imperative for tailoring treatment, predicting associated complications, evaluating prognosis, and supporting genetic counselling for families. Recent investigations have revealed that several hundred distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene contribute to HNF1A-MODY.

In the patient described in this case report, a previously undescribed heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), was detected within exon 4 of the HNF1A gene using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel, and the variant’s pathogenicity was assessed following the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) variant interpretation criteria. This specific mutation had not been previously documented in published studies, nor was it found in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment after hyperglycaemia and glycosuria were detected at his annual check-up. His physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 bpm. There was no evidence of acanthosis nigricans. The family history indicated several cases of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother developed it in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother was diagnosed at age 20. No family member with diabetes had a documented history of early-onset cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

His HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. Two hours following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient experienced increased thirst, heightened appetite, and had recently lost weight. Lipid panel showed high-density lipoprotein at 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, raising suspicion for metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). There was no notable past medical or surgical history.

A glucagon stimulation test revealed serum C-peptide concentrations of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L after 6 minutes, pointing to inadequate insulin production. This procedure assesses remaining β-cell function by measuring C-peptide levels following glucagon administration. Clinically, stimulated C-peptide values exceeding 0.60 nmol/L suggest intact endogenous insulin secretion, whereas levels below that threshold indicate β-cell impairment. Nonetheless, the glucagon stimulation test serves as a supportive, not definitive, measure—particularly in situations where autoantibody results are ambiguous or clinical features are unusual. There was no evidence of microalbuminuria. Sudomotor assessment showed a markedly diminished sudomotor response in the hands. Electrochemical skin conductance testing (for instance, with Sudoscan) was utilized to evaluate small fiber autonomic nerve function; the patient’s results showed severely reduced conductance over the hands, signifying pronounced autonomic dysfunction. Both GADA and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) tested negative. Genetic testing for MODY, performed one month after diagnosis, identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant (P) in HNF1A (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), confirming HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose levels. The initial therapy included basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, combined with metformin 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin 5 mg daily, reflecting an initial assumption of type 2 diabetes. This approach was selected to rapidly achieve glycaemic control and address possible insulin resistance.

Beyond pharmacological interventions, the patient received guidance to follow a regulated diet and participate in consistent physical exercise, forming part of a holistic strategy for diabetes management. Follow-up assessments took place in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to track glycaemic outcomes and evaluate therapeutic efficacy.

Nonetheless, considering the patient's moderately retained but inadequate β-cell function as demonstrated by a stimulated C-peptide value of 0.83 nmol/L on the glucagon stimulation test, absence of islet autoantibodies, and a notable family history of early-onset diabetes, suspicion for MODY was heightened.

Therapeutic measures were instituted before genetic testing results were available, relying on the presenting clinical features and initial laboratory data.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month later, the patient’s HbA1c had dropped to 9.1%. Following confirmation of HNF1A-MODY via genetic testing and taking into account the patient’s moderately intact but insufficient β-cell activity, in addition to the improved HbA1c level, insulin therapy was discontinued. Dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) was introduced in order to aid glycaemic control and help preserve pancreatic function. After 6 months of this regimen, the patient’s HbA1c reached 6.0%, representing a marked improvement. With stabilisation of glycaemic control, the treatment plan was streamlined to long-term maintenance consisting of metformin (1500 mg daily) and saxagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg daily). The patient continues to be seen regularly, with HbA1c persistently kept below 6.5%. Up to this point, there have been no findings of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and monitoring for chronic complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

HNF1A is a transcription factor situated in the q24 segment of chromosome 12. The gene consists of 10 exons distributed across 23,790 base pairs, coding for a protein with 631 amino acids that is present in multiple organs such as the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Alterations in HNF1A are associated with a gradual deterioration in β-cell activity, leading to diminished insulin and GLUT2 gene expression and lower pyruvate kinase activity. This chain of events eventually causes insufficient insulin release and compromised glucose regulation.

The diagnostic approach for this case followed established MODY criteria, which integrate clinical, biochemical, and genetic aspects such as an early onset of diabetes, a marked family history, post-meal glucose intolerance, and absence of autoantibodies. Maintaining a fasting C-peptide above 0.6 ng/mL favors non-type 1 diabetes as the diagnosis. Nonetheless, serial evaluation of C-peptide is important since normal results may occur in the initial stages of type 1 diabetes. A frequent challenge in recognizing MODY is limited awareness and a low index of suspicion. Many patients are inaccurately diagnosed with type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The Exeter MODY probability calculator can assist in recognizing individuals suited for genetic screening. It should be noted, however, that this calculator is based on White European patient data and might exaggerate MODY likelihood in non-White populations. Using our patient's details in this tool generated a MODY probability of 62.4% (equivalent to a 1 in 1.6 likelihood). Reports exist of MODY cases presenting with autoantibodies; although most MODY patients lack autoantibodies, some with HNF1A mutations do exhibit them. Previous research has demonstrated lower occurrences of obesity and metabolic syndrome in MODY patients compared to those with type 2 diabetes. However, increasing obesity prevalence has made using BMI or metabolic syndrome less reliable for differentiating between MODY and T2DM, which may clarify the elevated BMI observed in our patient. While family history remains significant in assessing MODY risk, its utility may be compromised by factors like under-reporting, the emergence of new mutations, and the widespread presence of T2DM in family backgrounds.

Therapeutic management of HNF1A-MODY depends on the patient’s age and HbA1c values. Low-dose sulfonylureas are typically the initial treatment choice, as they promote insulin release by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. In cases where β-cell function progressively deteriorates, GLP-1 receptor agonists may be preferable to sulfonylureas, particularly in patients with heightened susceptibility to hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small, randomized, double-blind, crossover trial indicates that both liraglutide and glimepiride are similarly effective in lowering fasting and postprandial plasma glucose in HNF1A-MODY patients, with no significant difference observed between them. GLP-1 receptor agonists, however, are associated with a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic confirmation enabled cessation of insulin therapy following glycaemic stabilization, and switching the patient to a GLP-1 receptor agonist maintained optimal glycaemic control. This case emphasizes the pivotal importance of genetic testing in informing clinical management. The presence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY stems from diminished SGLT2 expression, regulated by HNF1A. Previous reports have described that patients with HNF1A-MODY may experience profound dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to extensive suppression of residual SGLT2 activity. The hazards of SGLT-2 inhibitor use in HNF1A-MODY—including increased dehydration, potential circulatory collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may supersede the advantages these drugs offer. For this patient, the combination of liraglutide and metformin proved safe and effective, in accordance with earlier studies.

Precision medicine plays a pivotal role in MODY management, as patients with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not conform to all diagnostic criteria despite established clinical guidelines. Currently, there are no reliable biomarkers to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Chen et al reported that, among 2429 young Chinese adults with early-onset type 2 diabetes, MODY was present in 1.5% of cases, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al found that, in a large, population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian individuals with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 patients who lacked high-risk HLA haplotypes carried HNF1A mutations. Similarly, a recent Russian population-based study evaluated 506 patients suspected of hereditary diabetes without autoimmune markers—criteria included negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at onset, retained C-peptide secretion, low insulin requirements over five years, and absence of significant obesity. The investigation detected P or LP variants in 46.4% of these individuals, with HNF1A among the most frequently altered genes. Confirming clinical suspicion of MODY necessitates DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables the examination of extensive segments of patient DNA in a single procedure, enhancing MODY screening feasibility. The advancement of NGS technology has led to the identification of numerous novel genetic variants. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) has issued recommendations to determine the pathogenic status of these variants. Databases such as ClinVar report more than 1000 unique HNF1A variants, ranging from P, LP, benign, and likely benign, to variants of uncertain significance. In this report, the novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or other widely referenced variant databases, suggesting its novelty rather than raising doubts about its clinical importance. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid residue adjacent to previously recognized P sites, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes within the family, collectively satisfying the ACMG criteria for a LP classification. Therefore, while ClinVar remains an important repository for recorded variants, the pathogenic nature of this variant is substantiated by several independent genetic and clinical findings apart from database entries.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a comparatively lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is positioned within the DNA-binding domain. Certain studies have indicated that some HNF1A mutations, while present, only minimally disrupt HNF1A protein functionality. Moreover, particular HNF1A variants that do not independently cause MODY may still be linked to an elevated risk for T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the significance of precise diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY, as it can directly influence personalized therapeutic approaches. Future investigations will emphasize the refinement of NGS-based screening methods to enhance MODY detection rates, aiming for improved cost efficiency and greater clinical advantage.
