Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a monogenic, autosomal dominant type of diabetes. This case report details an individual with HNF1A-MODY attributable to a novel heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The patient exhibited elevated blood glucose and glucosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. Notably, there was a familial pattern of diabetes developing at a young age. Genetic analysis identified a previously undocumented pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist alongside metformin resulted in a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. The case emphasizes the need for genetic evaluation in patients with early-onset diabetes, often incorrectly classified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and adds to the known array of HNF1A mutations significant for personalized medical approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a set of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes marked by an inherent impairment in the formation and activity of pancreatic β-cells. The onset and recognition of these disorders generally occur before 25 years of age. MODY accounts for about 1%–6% of diabetes cases that arise in youth. Prevalence rates for MODY can differ across distinct ethnic and racial populations and may be subject to underestimation. Existing research has largely focused on White European cohorts. More recently, studies have begun to explore MODY prevalence within Asian and Australian groups. The reported prevalence of MODY varies considerably, largely because genetic testing tends to be reserved for individuals who display certain clinical features, even in population-based research.

Pathogenic variants in more than 15 genes have been associated with the monogenic MODY phenotype, each requiring distinct management approaches. Most cases across the various forms of MODY are predominantly attributable to mutations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. HNF1A-MODY is the most common subtype, representing approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses, and demonstrates a clear autosomal dominant inheritance of overt diabetes within affected families. In early adolescence, patients are often asymptomatic, and hyperglycemia is frequently discovered incidentally during standard blood work, usually without manifestations suggestive of insulin resistance. Postprandial blood glucose measurement or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) often reveals a notable elevation in glucose values (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be identified and is linked to a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, resulting from impaired renal tubular glucose transport. In more advanced stages, classic symptoms such as excessive thirst, frequent urination, increased appetite, and unintended weight loss may develop, which overlap with presentations of type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D presents a significant diagnostic challenge given these shared clinical manifestations.

Numerous diagnostic strategies for MODY have been described in recent research. When clinical assessment, including physical examination and family history, indicates a phenotype consistent with MODY and when autoantibodies commonly seen in type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are found to be negative, supported by further biochemical evaluations, molecular genetic analysis becomes integral to establishing the diagnosis. Accurate identification of MODY is vital for tailoring therapy, anticipating the risk of complications, evaluating the prognosis, and providing appropriate genetic counseling. Current literature has identified hundreds of distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene as causative for HNF1A-MODY.

Within this report, genetic analysis utilizing a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel revealed a previously unrecognized heterozygous nucleotide change, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), in exon 4 of HNF1A in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY. Pathogenicity was assessed according to variant interpretation standards from the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG). This particular variant had not been described in prior publications, nor was it found in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient granted written informed consent for inclusion and publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult presented for assessment following detection of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria during his routine annual health check. His physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. There was no evidence of acanthosis nigricans on inspection. The family history included multiple cases of early-onset diabetes: the maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at the age of 20. No family members with diabetes had reported early-onset cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

His glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, the 2-hour glucose value reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described symptoms of polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent unintended weight loss. Lipid panel showed high-density lipoprotein at 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, pointing towards the possibility of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He did not have notable past medical or surgical history.

Glucagon stimulation test revealed serum C-peptide levels of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, reflecting impaired insulin secretion. This procedure determines remaining β-cell capacity by measuring the increase in C-peptide following administration of glucagon. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide concentration above 0.60 nmol/L is regarded as evidence of adequate endogenous insulin production, whereas values below this threshold indicate β-cell compromise. Despite this, the glucagon stimulation test serves as a supporting tool rather than a conclusive method, especially when autoantibody findings are ambiguous or clinical features are unusual. Microalbuminuria was absent. Sudomotor assessment displayed markedly decreased sudomotor function in the hands. Electrochemical skin conductance testing (such as Sudoscan) was utilized to evaluate small fiber autonomic nerve activity and showed severe reduction in hand conductance, signifying substantial autonomic impairment. Both GADA and islet antigen 2 antibody (IA-2A) tests yielded negative results. Genetic screening for MODY, performed one month after diagnosis, identified a heterozygous pathogenic (P) mutation in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), confirming HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly high levels of HbA1c and plasma glucose. The starting therapeutic protocol included basal insulin degludec at a dose of eight units per day, combined with metformin at 1500 mg per day and saxagliptin at 5 mg per day, aligning with an initial working diagnosis of T2D. This approach was intended to achieve prompt glycemic stabilization and address any underlying insulin resistance.

Alongside medication, the patient was instructed to follow a structured diet and participate in routine exercise as integral components of overall diabetes management. Ongoing follow-up appointments were held at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to evaluate glycemic parameters and monitor the effectiveness of the treatment plan.

Nevertheless, in light of the patient’s moderately retained but inadequate β-cell capacity demonstrated on the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced familial history of diabetes manifesting at an early age, there was a substantial clinical suspicion for MODY.

Therapeutic measures were initiated before the results of genetic testing became available, guided by clinical features and laboratory evidence observed at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c had fallen to 9.1%. Based on confirmation of HNF1A-MODY through genetic analysis, and taking into account the patient’s moderately conserved but inadequate β-cell activity in addition to the decrease in HbA1c to 9.1%, insulin was withdrawn and replaced with dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg administered weekly) to assist in glycaemic management and help protect pancreatic function. Six months later, HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, reflecting marked glycaemic improvement. As glycaemic stability was achieved, therapy was transitioned to a simplified regimen consisting of metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day) for ongoing maintenance. The patient continues with routine follow-up, and HbA1c values have remained below 6.5%. There is no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy thus far, and monitoring for chronic complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, which functions as a transcription factor, is situated on the q24 segment of chromosome 12. It consists of 10 exons that cover 23,790 base pairs and codes for a 631–amino acid protein. This protein is present in several organs, including the liver, pancreas, and kidneys. Alterations in HNF1A result in a gradual loss of β-cell capacity, demonstrated by diminished insulin and GLUT2 gene expression along with decreased pyruvate kinase activity. These defects collectively lead to reduced insulin release and subsequent glucose intolerance.

In this patient, the approach to diagnosis was consistent with current criteria for MODY, integrating clinical signs, laboratory findings, and genetic confirmation—such as an early age of diabetes onset, a notable family background, postprandial hyperglycemia, and negative diabetes autoantibody tests. A fasting C-peptide above 0.6 ng/mL supports the diagnosis of diabetes other than type 1. Nevertheless, ongoing assessment of C-peptide is important, as normal levels might also be detected at the onset of type 1 diabetes. One key obstacle to diagnosing MODY is low awareness and insufficient clinical suspicion; these cases are frequently misclassified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator from the Exeter group is a useful resource for selecting candidates for genetic analysis, though its development from White European data means it might give inflated probabilities in non-White individuals. For our patient, the calculator yielded a 62.4% likelihood (or 1 in 1.6 chance) of MODY. Literature reviews have documented the occasional presence of autoantibodies in MODY cases, including among some with HNF1A mutations; however, the majority lack autoantibodies. Historical research shows lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome in MODY compared to type 2 diabetes, but as overall obesity prevalence has grown, using body mass index or metabolic syndrome to distinguish between MODY and T2D has become less reliable—potentially accounting for the higher BMI observed here. Furthermore, while a positive family history remains a critical criterion for MODY, its diagnostic value may be diminished due to factors such as incomplete family histories, the occurrence of de novo mutations, and the broad prevalence of type 2 diabetes in families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is tailored according to patient age and HbA1c values. Sulfonylureas at low doses are recommended as the initial therapy, as they stimulate insulin release via potassium-sensitive ATP channels. GLP-1 receptor agonists are suitable alternatives in HNF1A-MODY, especially as β-cell function deteriorates or in those susceptible to hypoglycaemia, offering increased safety and efficacy. A small randomized, double-blind, crossover study reported comparable efficacy between liraglutide and glimepiride in lowering fasting plasma glucose and minimizing postprandial glycaemic excursions among individuals with HNF1A-MODY. Moreover, the incidence of mild hypoglycaemia was reduced with GLP-1 receptor agonists compared to glimepiride. Genetic testing enabled the cessation of insulin after stabilization of glycaemic levels and allowed initiation of GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which provided ongoing glycaemic control. This underscores the importance of molecular diagnosis in optimizing treatment strategies. The presence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is linked to diminished SGLT2 expression, regulated transcriptionally by HNF1A. Literature has documented that patients with HNF1A-MODY may develop profound dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to excessive inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. Consequently, the hazards of SGLT-2 inhibitor use in HNF1A-MODY, including elevated risks of dehydration, potential circulatory collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, may surpass their therapeutic benefits. For this patient, therapy with liraglutide and metformin proved both effective and safe, in accordance with established evidence.

Precision medicine plays a vital role in MODY management, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy all existing diagnostic benchmarks despite current clinical recommendations. There are presently no definitive biomarkers capable of distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Chen et al. reported that within a cohort of 2429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D, MODY was identified in 1.5%, with 0.74% carrying pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY gene variants. Møller et al. demonstrated that in a substantial population-based sample of Danish Caucasians with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 patients who lacked high-risk HLA haplotypes were discovered to possess HNF1A gene mutations. Likewise, a recent population study from Russia investigated 506 individuals suspected of hereditary diabetes without autoimmunity—defined by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at onset, retained C-peptide levels, minimal insulin needs within five years, and absence of marked obesity. Pathogenic or LP variants were found in 46.4% of these subjects, with HNF1A among the frequently implicated genes. Confirmation of MODY relies on DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables comprehensive DNA analysis in a single procedure, increasing the practicality of MODY genetic screening. Advances in NGS have led to the identification of numerous novel genetic variants. The ACMG has set forth criteria for determining variant pathogenicity. Databases such as ClinVar contain over 1000 unique HNF1A variants categorized as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, or of uncertain significance. In our patient, the newly observed heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or other commonly referenced databases, underscoring its novelty rather than diminishing its clinical importance. Notably, this variant impacts a residue adjacent to previously established pathogenic sites, has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes in the family, fulfilling ACMG criteria for likely pathogenicity. Accordingly, while ClinVar is an important repository for variant reports, this variant’s pathogenic classification is corroborated by several independent genetic and clinical factors, beyond the absence of prior documentation.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the mutation frequency is lower in the transactivation domain. The novel variant described in this report is positioned in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have suggested that some HNF1A mutations, particularly those denoted as P, exert only limited effects on HNF1A protein function. Moreover, certain HNF1A variants that do not independently cause MODY may still confer a heightened susceptibility to developing T2D.

In summary, this report underscores the necessity for precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and the implications this holds for personalized therapy. Future studies aim to develop more efficient NGS-based screening protocols to enhance the diagnosis rate of MODY, thereby promoting improved cost-effectiveness and clinical outcomes.
