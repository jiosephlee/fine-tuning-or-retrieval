Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is an autosomal dominant single-gene type of diabetes. This case report details an individual with hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A)-MODY resulting from a previously unidentified heterozygous mutation in HNF1A. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. A family history revealed multiple relatives with early-onset diabetes. Genetic analysis identified a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Initiation of therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist in combination with metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This case underscores the necessity of genetic testing in cases of early-onset diabetes, which is often mistakenly classified as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and contributes new information regarding HNF1A mutations in the context of precision medicine.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a set of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes arising from a primary impairment in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders are usually seen in patients younger than 25 years at presentation and diagnosis. MODY accounts for roughly 1%–6% of diabetes diagnosed in the young. Prevalence rates for MODY differ among various racial and ethnic populations and may be underrecognized. Existing research has predominantly targeted White European cohorts. More recently, investigations have explored MODY prevalence in regions of Asia and Australia. Estimates of MODY frequency are variable, largely because genetic testing tends to be restricted to individuals selected on clinical grounds, even in population-based research.

More than 15 genes have been associated with the monogenic MODY phenotype, with each gene alteration linked to distinct treatment approaches. Most instances across MODY subtypes are predominantly due to mutations in genes such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. HNF1A-MODY is the most frequent form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY cases. It displays a clear autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, characterized by symptomatic diabetes passed through affected families. During early adolescence, individuals may be asymptomatic, with hyperglycemia often discovered incidentally during routine screenings, usually without evidence of insulin resistance. Postprandial blood glucose measurement or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal marked postprandial hyperglycemia (≥11.1 mmol/L). Furthermore, glycosuria may be present, resulting from a diminished renal threshold for glucose reabsorption caused by impaired glucose transport within the renal tubules. In more advanced disease, clinical features such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight loss—similar to those seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes—can emerge. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes remains a challenge because of their overlapping clinical manifestations.

Multiple diagnostic pathways for MODY have been delineated in recent research. When physical examination and family history indicate a clinical picture consistent with MODY, and autoantibodies characteristic of type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, accompanied by relevant biochemical tests, molecular genetic analysis becomes pivotal in confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification of the specific MODY subtype is essential for selecting appropriate therapy, anticipating the risk of complications, evaluating prognosis, and supporting family counselling. Current literature highlights that numerous mutations in the HNF1A gene are linked to HNF1A-MODY.

In the present case, genetic testing utilizing a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel uncovered a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient with HNF1A-MODY. The pathogenic potential of this variant was evaluated following the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria for variant interpretation. This mutation had not been documented in prior publications, nor in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for publication of the case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment after hyperglycaemia and glycosuria were found on his routine annual check-up. At presentation, his body mass index measured 24.6 kg/m2, blood pressure was 112/70 mmHg, and his heart rate was 78 bpm. Physical examination did not reveal acanthosis nigricans. His family history included multiple relatives with early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. None of these family members had documented early-onset cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting plasma glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g OGTT, the 2-hour plasma glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. No evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis was present. The patient described experiencing polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent unintended weight loss. Lipid panel assessment showed an HDL cholesterol of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides measured at 4.31 mmol/L, indicating possible metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He denied any notable past medical or surgical history.

The glucagon stimulation test demonstrated C-peptide values of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes post-administration, reflecting limited endogenous insulin production. This test serves to evaluate the secretory function of β-cells by quantifying stimulated C-peptide after glucagon challenge. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide >0.60 nmol/L is considered evidence of preserved insulin secretion, while values below this threshold are consistent with β-cell insufficiency. Nevertheless, the glucagon stimulation test provides supportive information and is not definitive, especially in instances of equivocal autoantibody findings or an atypical clinical picture. Microalbuminuria was absent. Assessment of sudomotor function, performed via non-invasive electrochemical skin conductance testing (such as Sudoscan), showed profoundly reduced conductance in the hands, highlighting significant autonomic impairment. Antibodies against GAD and IA-2 (GADA and IA-2A) were both undetectable. Genetic testing for MODY conducted one month after the initial diagnosis identified a heterozygous, pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), consistent with HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose values. The initial therapeutic plan included basal degludec insulin at eight units per day, combined with metformin at 1500 mg per day and saxagliptin at 5 mg per day, indicating an early assumption of T2D. This approach was intended to achieve swift glycaemic stabilization and target possible insulin resistance.

Alongside medical management, the patient received recommendations to follow a structured nutritional regimen and maintain consistent physical activity, integrating these measures into an overall diabetes care strategy. Subsequent assessments were carried out in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient setting to track glycaemic outcomes and evaluate the effectiveness of therapy.

Nonetheless, with moderate preservation yet inadequate β-cell function as measured by a glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced family history of early-onset diabetes, suspicion for MODY was considerable.

The initial management was initiated prior to receipt of genetic testing results, based upon the clinical characteristics and laboratory data present at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month later, the patient’s HbA1c had declined to 9.1%. With genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and consideration of the patient’s β-cell function—which was moderately preserved but inadequate—insulin was discontinued. Dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist at a dose of 1.5 mg weekly, was initiated in order to enhance glycaemic control and maintain pancreatic function. Six months after starting this therapy, HbA1c had further decreased to 6.0%, demonstrating marked improvement. As glycaemic parameters stabilised, treatment was simplified to metformin (1500 mg/day) combined with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day) for ongoing maintenance. The patient continues to be monitored routinely and HbA1c has consistently stayed below 6.5%. Up to this point, there have been no indications of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and surveillance for chronic complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

HNF1A is a transcription factor encoded within the q24 segment of chromosome 12. The gene consists of 10 exons over a span of 23,790 base pairs and produces a 631-amino-acid protein found in organs such as the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Genetic alterations in HNF1A are associated with a gradual reduction in β-cell performance, demonstrated by diminished insulin and GLUT2 gene expression and lowered pyruvate kinase activity, eventually leading to impaired insulin release and abnormal glucose regulation.

In this case, diagnosis followed established MODY criteria, which require clinical, biochemical, and genetic confirmation, including early diabetes onset, marked family history, postprandial glucose dysregulation, and absence of autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide greater than 0.6 ng/mL supports non-type 1 diabetes. Continued C-peptide assessment remains crucial as early type 1 diabetes may present with normal values. A major obstacle to MODY identification is insufficient awareness and suspicion, with many patients incorrectly labeled as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The Exeter group's MODY probability calculator may assist clinicians in selecting patients for genetic analysis; however, it is based on data from a White European cohort, possibly leading to overestimation of MODY risk in other populations. For our patient, input into this calculator resulted in a MODY likelihood of 62.4% (roughly 1 in 1.6). Multiple reports have noted autoantibody presence in MODY cases; while autoantibodies can occur in HNF1A mutation carriers, most MODY patients do not show them. Research has indicated MODY individuals typically experience lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to type 2 diabetes, but increasing obesity rates have lessened the discriminatory power of BMI or metabolic syndrome in distinguishing MODY from T2DM, potentially accounting for the elevated BMI observed in our patient. Though family history remains a significant consideration in MODY evaluation, its reliability may be compromised by under-reporting, de novo mutations, and widespread type 2 diabetes among relatives.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by patient age and HbA1c levels. Sulfonylureas at low doses are typically initiated as the primary therapy, functioning by stimulating insulin release via potassium-sensitive ATP channels. As β-cell function diminishes and for patients susceptible to hypoglycaemia, GLP-1 receptor agonists may offer a safer and potentially superior alternative to sulfonylureas. Evidence from a small randomized, double-blind, crossover study revealed that both liraglutide and glimepiride similarly lower fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glycemic variability in individuals with HNF1A-MODY, without significant efficacy differences between treatments. Furthermore, GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic testing facilitated withdrawal of insulin once glycemic targets were reached, enabling a successful shift to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy that sustained glycemic control. This underscores the importance of genetic diagnosis in selecting appropriate therapy. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria results from decreased SGLT2 expression, which is governed by HNF1A transcriptional regulation. One report has suggested that HNF1A-MODY patients might develop significant dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis from pronounced inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. Thus, in individuals with HNF1A-MODY, the risks associated with SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy—such as heightened dehydration, possible vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may surpass their therapeutic advantages. In the present case, the combination of liraglutide and metformin proved to be both efficacious and safe, aligning with prior research findings.

Personalized treatment approaches are particularly important for MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all diagnostic requirements, despite established clinical guidelines. At present, there is a lack of specific biomarkers that reliably distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or T2D. In a study conducted by Chen et al involving 2429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D, MODY was found in 1.5% of the participants, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al’s research demonstrated that in a population-based cohort of Danish Caucasians with type 1 diabetes, mutations in the HNF1A gene were detected in 10% of 39 individuals who lacked any high-risk HLA haplotypes. Additionally, a recent study from Russia, which screened 506 participants suspected of having inherited forms of diabetes without autoimmune manifestations—defined by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at onset, sustained C-peptide production, minimal insulin requirements within 5 years, and no severe obesity—revealed P or LP variants in 46.4% of the subjects, with HNF1A among the genes most frequently affected. Confirmatory genetic testing via DNA sequencing is critical when MODY is suspected clinically. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) facilitates the examination of extensive portions of patient DNA in one experiment, enhancing the practicality of MODY diagnosis. The advent of NGS has led to the identification of numerous novel genetic alterations. The ACMG supplies recommendations to determine the pathogenicity of these variants. Databases such as ClinVar now list more than 1000 distinct HNF1A variants, encompassing pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In the present case, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not recorded in ClinVar or other widely referenced variant repositories, signifying its novelty rather than disputing its clinical importance. Notably, this variant affects an amino acid position next to previously documented pathogenic sites, possesses a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes in the family, collectively fulfilling ACMG standards for classification as likely pathogenic. Consequently, although ClinVar is instrumental for variant documentation, the pathogenic nature of this particular variant is supported by diverse independent genetic and clinical findings in addition to database evidence.

Research has shown that most discovered mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas mutations in the transactivation domain occur less frequently. The novel variant described in this report also resides in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have demonstrated that, despite HNF1A mutations, the resultant effect on HNF1A protein function is generally minimal. Moreover, some HNF1A variants that are insufficient to independently cause MODY may nonetheless be linked to a heightened risk of developing T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the significance of precisely diagnosing HNF1A-MODY and understanding its influence on personalized therapy. Ongoing studies will aim to establish more efficient NGS-based screening protocols to enhance the diagnostic yield for MODY, ultimately improving cost efficiency and clinical outcomes.
