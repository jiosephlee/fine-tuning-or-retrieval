Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a monogenic, autosomal dominant variant of diabetes. This case report details an individual with HNF1A-MODY resulting from a previously unidentified heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) gene. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis, alongside a family history of diabetes with early onset. Genetic analysis revealed a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist combined with metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This case underscores the significance of genetic evaluation in patients with early-onset diabetes, a condition often incorrectly classified as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and broadens the known repertoire of HNF1A mutations pertinent to individualized therapy approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes distinguished by an inherent impairment in the formation and functionality of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders generally arise and are identified before the age of 25. MODY accounts for approximately 1%–6% of diabetes cases presenting in young individuals. Prevalence rates of MODY can differ across ethnic and racial populations and may often be underestimated. Historically, most investigations have focused on White European cohorts. More recent research has expanded to examine the occurrence of MODY in Asian and Australian settings. However, reported prevalence rates demonstrate wide variability, largely because genetic testing tends to be restricted to those displaying certain clinical features, even when studying broader population groups.

Over fifteen distinct gene mutations have been implicated in the MODY-monogenic phenotype, each necessitating unique treatment modalities. Most documented cases of various MODY subtypes are attributable to mutations in genes like hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. HNF1A-MODY constitutes the most prevalent form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses. This type demonstrates an evident autosomal dominant pattern of symptomatic diabetes inheritance within families. During early adolescence, clinical symptoms may be absent, and elevated blood glucose is commonly discovered incidentally through standard laboratory screening, usually without clinical signs of insulin resistance. Postprandial blood glucose measurements or the administration of a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) often reveal substantial hyperglycemia (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also occur, resulting from a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption caused by impaired glucose transport in the renal tubules. Nevertheless, as the disease progresses, individuals may develop classic diabetes symptoms, including excessive thirst, increased urination, heightened appetite, and unintended weight loss—symptoms commonly seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). As a result, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D can be diagnostically challenging, given the similarity in clinical presentation.

Multiple studies have developed diagnostic algorithms for MODY. When a patient's physical examination and evaluation of family history raise suspicion for a clinical picture consistent with MODY, and when autoantibodies commonly associated with type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, diagnosis is further supported through additional biochemical tests. At this point, molecular genetic testing becomes central to establishing the diagnosis. Accurate genetic diagnosis enables the selection of an appropriate therapeutic strategy, aids in anticipating possible complications, informs prognosis, and supports genetic counselling for families. Recent research has shown that HNF1A-MODY arises from a wide array of HNF1A gene mutations, numbering in the hundreds.

In the case presented herein, genetic analysis via a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel revealed a novel heterozygous change, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY. The pathogenic potential of this variant was evaluated based on the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria for variant interpretation. Notably, this specific mutation has not been previously documented in the scientific literature, nor is it listed in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided explicit written consent for publication of this case.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment following the detection of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria at his routine annual check-up. On clinical evaluation, his BMI measured 24.6 kg/m2, with a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. Physical examination did not reveal any evidence of acanthosis nigricans. His family history was significant for the presence of early-onset diabetes: the maternal grandmother received a diagnosis in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. No instances of early-onset cardiovascular disease had been reported in those family members with diabetes.

Investigations:

The patient exhibited a glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) of 10.2% (88 mmol/mol) and a fasting blood glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g OGTT, the 2-hour glucose concentration was 19.8 mmol/L. There was no clinical or biochemical evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. Symptoms included polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent unintentional weight reduction. His lipid assessment showed high-density lipoprotein at 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings that are consistent with possible metabolic syndrome (see tables 1 and 2). There was no notable prior medical or surgical history.

A glucagon stimulation test yielded a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L after 6 minutes, reflective of inadequate insulin production. This assay is utilized to assess remaining β-cell function via the measurement of C-peptide release following glucagon administration. In clinical settings, a stimulated C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L is typically taken as evidence of retained endogenous insulin secretion; values below this threshold point toward impaired β-cell function. Nevertheless, this test should be considered an adjunct, especially when autoantibody data are equivocal or the phenotype is not classic. No microalbuminuria was found. Evaluation of sudomotor function through electrochemical skin conductance (such as with the Sudoscan device), which gauges small fibre autonomic nerve integrity, revealed markedly diminished conductance in the hands, consistent with significant autonomic impairment. Autoantibody screening—including GADA and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A)—was negative. Genetic testing for MODY, performed one month after diagnosis, identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), establishing the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly high HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The initial therapeutic approach involved administration of basal insulin degludec (eight units daily), alongside metformin (1500 mg per day) and saxagliptin (5 mg per day), reflecting an initial assessment consistent with T2D. This regimen was designed to facilitate prompt glycaemic stabilization and to address probable insulin resistance.

Beyond medical therapy, recommendations included following a structured nutritional plan and participating in consistent physical activity, in alignment with an integrative strategy for diabetes management. Glycaemic status and treatment effectiveness were subsequently reviewed during visits to the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic.

Nevertheless, due to the patient’s moderately retained yet inadequate β-cell reserve as indicated by the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced family history of early-onset diabetes, MODY emerged as a leading diagnostic consideration.

Therapeutic interventions were initiated prior to receipt of genetic testing outcomes, guided by the presenting clinical profile and laboratory data at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c level dropped to 9.1%. With genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and in light of the patient’s partially preserved but still inadequate β-cell function, together with the observed reduction in HbA1c, insulin was discontinued. Dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist administered at 1.5 mg weekly, was initiated to enhance glycaemic management and support pancreatic β-cell preservation. Following 6 months of this treatment, the patient achieved a further reduction in HbA1c to 6.0%, reflecting marked metabolic improvement. As stable glycaemic control was attained, therapy was transitioned to a maintenance regimen consisting of metformin (1500 mg/day) combined with saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day). The patient continues to be monitored regularly, with HbA1c levels persistently maintained below 6.5%. Thus far, no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy has emerged, and surveillance for chronic diabetes-related complications remains ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor situated in the q24 region of chromosome 12. It consists of 10 exons that together cover 23,790 base pairs, resulting in the production of a 631 amino acid protein. This protein is found in multiple tissues, notably the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Pathogenic variants in HNF1A cause a gradual loss of β-cell activity, evidenced by decreased expression of both insulin and GLUT2 genes and lower pyruvate kinase activity. The net effect is a reduction in insulin release and impaired glucose tolerance.

For the diagnostic assessment in this instance, current MODY diagnostic standards were followed, incorporating clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and genetic data. These criteria include diabetes onset at a young age, notable familial clustering, abnormal postprandial glycemia, and absence of diabetes-associated autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide value above 0.6 ng/mL supports a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes. Despite this, regular evaluation of C-peptide levels is necessary, since early type 1 diabetes can sometimes also show normal C-peptide values. One significant challenge in diagnosing MODY is limited recognition and suspicion among clinicians. Many individuals with MODY are incorrectly identified as type 1 or T2D patients. The Exeter group's MODY probability calculator serves as a supportive tool for identifying potential candidates for genetic screening. Nonetheless, this calculator was validated primarily in a White European cohort, which may lead to overestimation of MODY probability in individuals from other ethnic backgrounds. Application of this calculator to our patient yielded an estimated MODY probability of 62.4% (equivalent to a 1 in 1.6 likelihood). There are reports documenting the presence of autoantibodies in a minority of MODY cases. Specifically, some HNF1A mutation carriers do present with autoantibodies, even though most MODY patients are seronegative. Early research indicates that rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome are generally lower in MODY compared to T2D, but the rising prevalence of obesity complicates discrimination between MODY and T2D based on BMI or features of metabolic syndrome, possibly accounting for the elevated BMI in our patient. Furthermore, while familial history is a key consideration for MODY, its diagnostic value can be constrained by factors such as incomplete family histories, the occurrence of de novo mutations, and the widespread occurrence of T2D in relatives.

Therapeutic strategies for HNF1A-MODY are guided by the patient's age and HbA1c measurements. The preferred initial approach is the use of low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release via modulation of ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function progressively diminishes in HNF1A-MODY, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists emerge as a potentially safer and more efficacious alternative to sulfonylureas, especially in patients susceptible to hypoglycaemic episodes. Evidence from a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study revealed that both liraglutide and glimepiride led to significant reductions in fasting and postprandial plasma glucose levels in individuals with HNF1A-MODY, with no appreciable difference in efficacy; however, GLP-1 receptor agonists were associated with a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic confirmation allowed for withdrawal of insulin following achievement of glycaemic stability and enabled initiation of GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which effectively maintained glucose control. This underscores the importance of genetic diagnostics in informing optimal therapeutic choices. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria is a consequence of diminished expression of SGLT2, whose transcription is regulated by HNF1A. Case reports have described instances where individuals with HNF1A-MODY, due to significant inhibition of residual SGLT-2 function, suffered severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis. Adverse outcomes related to SGLT-2 inhibitor use in this population—such as increased risks of dehydration, vascular instability, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—likely outweigh their therapeutic advantages. In the case presented, the combination of liraglutide and metformin proved both efficacious and well-tolerated, consistent with findings in prior research.

The implementation of precision medicine is particularly important in the context of MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy all existing diagnostic criteria, despite adherence to established clinical protocols. At present, reliable biomarkers that can distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes and T2D are lacking. Evidence from Chen et al demonstrated that, among 2429 young Chinese adults presenting with early-onset T2D, the incidence of MODY was 1.5%, with 0.74% identified as carriers of pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Additionally, work by Møller et al revealed that within a substantial population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian patients diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 individuals who lacked high-risk HLA haplotypes were found to possess mutations in the HNF1A gene. Comparable findings were observed in a recent Russian population study, where 506 participants suspected of hereditary diabetes—those exhibiting negative results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, sustained C-peptide levels, minimal insulin requirements within five years, and absence of severe obesity—were genetically screened. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were detected in 46.4% of these individuals, with the HNF1A gene frequently implicated. Confirmation of clinical suspicion for MODY necessitates DNA sequencing, and the advent of NGS technologies permits comprehensive analysis of large genomic segments in a single assay, thereby enhancing the practicality of MODY diagnostics. NGS has also led to the identification of a plethora of novel genetic alterations. The ACMG has developed standards for determining the pathogenicity of such variants. More than 1000 unique HNF1A variants are catalogued in repositories such as ClinVar, encompassing P, LP, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In the case under discussion, a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was absent from ClinVar and other prevalent variant databases, underlining its novelty as opposed to diminishing its clinical importance. Notably, this mutation impacts an amino acid residue adjacent to those already classified as pathogenic, has an elevated AlphaMissense score (0.995), and demonstrates familial co-segregation with diabetes, collectively fulfilling ACMG criteria for classification as LP. Therefore, while variant databases like ClinVar are indispensable for the annotation of reported variants, multiple lines of genetic and clinical evidence independently substantiate the potential pathogenicity of this novel variant beyond its representation in such databases.

Previous research has demonstrated that most detected mutations occur within the DNA-binding domain, with comparatively fewer mutations identified in the transactivation domain. The novel variant described in this report is also found within the DNA-binding domain. Certain studies have revealed that specific HNF1A mutations, while present, exert limited impact on the functionality of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants, although insufficient to independently trigger MODY, may contribute to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

To summarize, this case underscores the critical role of precise diagnosis for HNF1A-MODY, which can significantly influence personalized treatment approaches. Ongoing investigations aim to refine NGS screening methodologies, thereby enhancing MODY detection rates and facilitating improved clinical outcomes and cost-efficiency.
