Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a monogenic, autosomal dominant form of diabetes. This case report presents an individual with HNF1A-MODY attributable to a novel heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history revealed diabetes with early onset. Genetic analysis identified a pathogenic HNF1A variant not previously described. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist in combination with metformin led to a reduction in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a three-month period. This case underscores the value of genetic testing in early-onset diabetes, which is often erroneously classified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and broadens the known range of HNF1A mutations relevant for targeted therapies.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes conditions characterized by an inherent defect in the formation and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders usually appear and are identified before patients reach 25 years of age. MODY accounts for approximately 1%–6% of diabetes cases with early onset. The prevalence of MODY differs among various racial and ethnic groups, and these differences may be under-recognized. Most existing research has focused on populations of White European descent, although newer investigations have explored MODY rates in Asian and Australian populations. Reported prevalence figures for MODY demonstrate considerable variability, a disparity frequently attributed to the practice of restricting genetic testing to individuals selected for specific clinical features, even in population-based studies.

More than 15 gene mutations have been associated with the MODY monogenic phenotype, each linked to distinct treatment approaches. Most cases among the different MODY subtypes are predominantly caused by alterations in genes like hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. 

HNF1A-MODY is the most common MODY variant, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses, and demonstrates a readily identifiable autosomal dominant pattern of symptomatic diabetes within families. During early adolescence, clinical signs may be absent, with hyperglycemia often discovered incidentally on routine blood work, generally without evidence of insulin resistance. Postprandial blood glucose evaluation or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal a marked elevation in glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be present, resulting from a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired renal tubular glucose transport. As the disease progresses, symptoms such as increased thirst, frequent urination, excessive hunger, and unintended weight loss—paralleling those seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may manifest. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D remains a challenge, given the shared clinical presentations.

Multiple diagnostic pathways for MODY have now been described in the literature. When a physical exam combined with family history points toward a MODY phenotype and diabetes-related autoantibodies such as GADA, IA-2A, and ZnT8 are not detected, in addition to further corroborative biochemical analyses, molecular genetic testing becomes pivotal for making the diagnosis. An accurate identification of MODY is fundamental for selecting the most appropriate therapy, predicting future complications, judging disease outlook, and supporting family counseling. Recent research has identified that a wide range of mutations—numbering in the hundreds—within the HNF1A gene underlie cases of HNF1A-MODY.

In the case described here, a novel heterozygous mutation, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), was detected in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene through next-generation sequencing (NGS) in a patient with HNF1A-MODY. Pathogenicity was evaluated using the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines. This particular variant has not previously been described in the literature, nor is it listed in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent specifically for publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment of elevated blood glucose and urinary glucose detected at his yearly medical check-up. Physical examination showed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. There was no evidence of acanthosis nigricans on inspection. The family history included cases of early-onset diabetes: the maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at age 20. No relatives with diabetes had a documented history of premature cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

His HbA1c measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. The 2-hour glucose value following a 75 g OGTT reached 19.8 mmol/L. Diabetic ketoacidosis was not present. The patient complained of excessive thirst, increased appetite, and recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid testing showed an HDL cholesterol level of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings consistent with possible metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). There was no relevant past medical or surgical history.

Results from the glucagon stimulation test indicated a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L six minutes after glucagon administration, reflecting reduced insulin production. This test is used to assess the function of remaining pancreatic β-cells by measuring C-peptide response following glucagon injection. Typically, a stimulated C-peptide exceeding 0.60 nmol/L is considered evidence of ongoing endogenous insulin secretion, whereas lower values point toward β-cell impairment. Nevertheless, the glucagon stimulation test should be interpreted as an adjunct rather than a definitive diagnostic method, especially when autoantibody tests are negative or clinical findings are unusual. No microalbuminuria was found. Severe impairment of sudomotor function was observed in the hands. Sudomotor activity, gauged by electrochemical skin conductance testing (such as Sudoscan), serves as a non-invasive approach for assessing small fiber autonomic neuropathy. The patient’s low conductance values for the hands pointed to marked autonomic involvement. Both GAD antibodies and IA-2A antibodies were negative. Genetic analysis for MODY, performed a month after diagnosis, identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), confirming HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose measurements. The first line of therapy included basal degludec insulin at a dose of eight units daily, combined with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), reflecting an initial assumption of type 2 diabetes. The purpose of this regimen was to facilitate prompt glycaemic control and address suspected insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient received guidance to follow a structured diet and maintain routine physical activity as integral components of diabetes care. Follow-up appointments were arranged at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient service to observe glycaemic status and evaluate therapeutic efficacy.

Nonetheless, the glucagon stimulation test revealed moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell activity (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), accompanied by negative islet autoantibodies and a pronounced familial history of early-onset diabetes, raising considerable suspicion for MODY.

The initial management commenced prior to receipt of genetic testing outcomes, relying on both the patient's clinical features and laboratory results at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month afterward, the patient’s HbA1c fell to 9.1%. Upon molecular confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and in light of moderately preserved yet inadequate β-cell activity, as well as improved glycaemic parameters (HbA1c 9.1%), insulin was stopped and dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg weekly) was initiated to optimize glycaemic control and help maintain pancreatic function. Following 6 months on this regimen, HbA1c was reduced further to 6.0%, demonstrating notable progress. With sustained glycaemic stability, the therapeutic approach was simplified to maintenance with metformin (1500 mg daily) and saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg daily). The patient continues regular monitoring, with HbA1c persistently below 6.5%. At present, there are no indications of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and surveillance for chronic complications continues.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor found on chromosome 12 at the q24 location. It consists of 10 exons covering 23,790 base pairs and produces a protein made up of 631 amino acids. This protein is present in multiple organs, including the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Genetic alterations in HNF1A contribute to a gradual loss of β-cell activity, characterized by disrupted insulin and GLUT2 gene expression and diminished pyruvate kinase function. These defects ultimately cause a reduction in insulin release and impaired glucose regulation.

In this case, the diagnostic approach followed established MODY criteria, integrating clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and genetic evaluation. Key features included early-onset diabetes, significant family history, abnormal postprandial glucose responses, and the absence of autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide measurement above 0.6 ng/mL favors a non-type 1 diabetes diagnosis, though repeated assessments are warranted since type 1 diabetes may initially show normal C-peptide. One major obstacle in identifying MODY is limited clinician awareness, often resulting in mistaken classification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator developed by the Exeter group serves as a tool to help clinicians select appropriate candidates for genetic analysis, though its predictions—based on data from a White European cohort—could lead to overestimated MODY likelihood in other ethnicities. Applying this calculator to our patient's clinical details generated a MODY probability of 62.4% (1 in 1.6). Published case reports document the occurrence of autoantibodies in some individuals with MODY, including patients harboring HNF1A mutations, despite most MODY cases being autoantibody negative. Previous research indicates lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome among MODY patients compared to those with T2D. With increasing obesity prevalence, differentiating MODY from T2DM using BMI or metabolic syndrome has become more challenging, potentially accounting for the elevated BMI observed in this patient. While family history remains valuable for identifying MODY, its reliability is reduced by factors such as under-reporting, de novo mutations, and the frequent presence of T2DM family history.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is tailored according to the patient’s age and HbA1c values. Sulfonylureas at a low dose are recommended as initial therapy, as they stimulate insulin release via potassium-sensitive ATP channels. GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a preferable and safer alternative, especially as β-cell function deteriorates or for patients with a tendency for hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small, randomized, double-blind, crossover study found that liraglutide and glimepiride both lower fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose excursions in HNF1A-MODY patients, with no significant difference in efficacy. GLP-1 receptor agonists also carry a reduced risk of mild hypoglycaemic episodes compared to glimepiride. After confirming the genetic diagnosis, we were able to discontinue insulin following glycaemic stabilization and transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which successfully maintained glycaemic control. This underscores the importance of genetic testing for therapeutic planning. In HNF1A-MODY, glycosuria stems from decreased SGLT2 expression regulated by HNF1A. According to published reports, HNF1A-MODY patients may develop severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis resulting from pronounced inhibition of remaining SGLT-2 function. Given the elevated risk of dehydration, vascular instability, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, SGLT-2 inhibitors may pose more harm than benefit for this population. In this patient, liraglutide combined with metformin proved both effective and safe, in agreement with prior research.

Precision medicine plays a vital role in managing MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all diagnostic criteria, even though clinical guidelines exist. At present, appropriate biomarkers to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes are lacking. Chen et al conducted a study involving 2429 young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D and found a MODY prevalence rate of 1.5%, with 0.74% exhibiting pathogenic or likely pathogenic MODY variants. Møller et al observed that in a substantial population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian type 1 diabetes patients, 10% of the 39 individuals lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes possessed mutations in the HNF1A gene. Likewise, a recent population-based study from Russia analyzed 506 participants suspected to have hereditary diabetes without autoimmune markers—defined by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, preserved C-peptide secretion, low insulin doses within five years, and no history of marked obesity. Among these cases, pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified in 46.4%, with HNF1A frequently implicated. Confirmatory diagnosis of MODY relies on DNA sequencing. The use of next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables high-throughput analysis of patient DNA samples, facilitating broader screening for MODY. The implementation of NGS has led to the identification of numerous previously uncharacterized genetic variants. The ACMG has established protocols for determining the pathogenicity of these variants. Databases such as ClinVar catalog more than 1000 unique HNF1A variants, including those classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, or of uncertain significance. In the presented case, a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or other routinely utilized variant databases, underscoring its novelty but not detracting from its clinical significance. Notably, this mutation alters an amino acid immediately adjacent to established pathogenic sites, is characterized by a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and is associated with diabetes within the family, collectively satisfying ACMG criteria for a likely pathogenic classification. Therefore, while ClinVar is an important repository for variant information, the pathogenic status of this variant is substantiated by several independent genetic and clinical factors, supplementing database findings.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas mutations occur less frequently in the transactivation domain. The novel variant described in this report is also localized to the DNA-binding domain. Several studies suggest that certain HNF1A mutations, specifically P, exert only a minor impact on the functional activity of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, certain HNF1A variants that are insufficient by themselves to result in MODY may nonetheless contribute to elevated susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the necessity of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its implications for personalized therapeutic strategies. Future investigations will aim to establish more efficient NGS screening protocols to enhance MODY diagnosis rates, thereby optimizing healthcare costs and clinical outcomes.
