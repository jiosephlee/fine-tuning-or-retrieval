Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is an autosomal dominant, monogenic diabetes subtype. This case concerns a patient with HNF1A-MODY attributed to a previously undescribed heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history revealed several members affected by diabetes at a young age. Molecular analysis identified a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Initiation of a GLP-1 receptor agonist in combination with metformin led to a reduction in HbA1c levels from 10.2% to 6.0% over a period of 3 months. This report underscores the role of genetic testing in cases of early-onset diabetes—which is often mistaken for type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and broadens the known spectrum of HNF1A mutations pertinent to individualized therapy.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes disorders resulting from a fundamental impairment in pancreatic β-cell formation and function. These diseases commonly present before age 25 and are usually diagnosed during adolescence or early adulthood. MODY accounts for roughly 1%–6% of cases of diabetes arising at a young age. The frequency of MODY can differ among ethnic and racial populations and may be underestimated in certain groups. Historically, investigations have focused primarily on populations of White European descent. More recent research has assessed the incidence of MODY in both Asian and Australian populations. Reported MODY prevalence rates show considerable discrepancies, largely due to genetic testing being reserved for individuals selected according to clinical features, even when population-level studies are conducted.

Over 15 gene mutations have been associated with the monogenic presentation of MODY, each necessitating unique management approaches. Most identified cases across MODY subtypes are attributed to variants in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta.

HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY occurrences. It is characterized by autosomal dominant inheritance patterns, resulting in the appearance of symptomatic diabetes among family members. During early adolescence, clinical signs often remain absent and hyperglycemia is commonly discovered incidentally on routine laboratory tests, generally in the absence of physical manifestations of insulin resistance. Evaluating postprandial glucose levels or employing a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal a marked postprandial rise in blood sugar (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be observed, reflecting a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired glucose handling in the renal tubules. In more advanced stages, symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight reduction may develop, resembling clinical presentations found in both type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 diabetes and T2D is challenging, as their clinical features frequently overlap.

Numerous diagnostic protocols for MODY have been validated in the literature. Following a clinical evaluation and family history review indicating MODY, and confirmation of negative diabetes-related autoantibodies such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8), supported by additional biochemical evidence, genetic analysis becomes critical for establishing the diagnosis. Accurate identification is vital for guiding therapeutic decisions, predicting risks, determining prognosis, and informing family counseling. Recent investigations have shown that hundreds of distinct HNF1A gene mutations are implicated in HNF1A-MODY.

In the present report, we detected a previously unreported heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A in a patient with HNF1A-MODY through next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel genetic testing. The pathogenicity was evaluated based on guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) for variant interpretation. This variant was absent from the literature, as well as the VarSome and ClinVar databases. Written informed consent was obtained directly from the patient for publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment due to elevated blood glucose and presence of glucose in the urine discovered at his annual check-up. Physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, blood pressure measuring 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. Acanthosis nigricans was absent upon inspection. The patient's family history included several cases of diabetes diagnosed at a young age: his maternal grandmother in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at 20 years old. No cases of early-onset cardiovascular disease were reported in relatives who had diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was elevated at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting blood glucose measured 12.4 mmol/L. After a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, the 2-hour plasma glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. There were no clinical features indicative of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described experiencing polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent unintended weight loss. Lipid studies showed a high-density lipoprotein cholesterol level of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings that suggest the possibility of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He had no notable past medical or surgical problems.

A glucagon stimulation test demonstrated a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline, rising to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes post-glucagon, reflecting impaired insulin production. This assessment is used to estimate residual pancreatic β-cell function by quantifying C-peptide response to glucagon. In clinical settings, a stimulated C-peptide exceeding 0.60 nmol/L typically reflects retained endogenous insulin secretion; values below this threshold are consistent with β-cell deficiency. Still, the glucagon test is best viewed as adjunctive rather than conclusive, especially when autoantibody findings are unclear or the clinical scenario is not classic. No microalbuminuria was found. Evaluation of sudomotor activity via electrochemical skin conductance (for instance, using Sudoscan) demonstrated markedly reduced responses in the hands, confirming profound autonomic impairment. Both anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) returned negative results. A month after the initial diagnosis, genetic testing for maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), establishing a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The starting therapy included basal insulin degludec at eight units daily, metformin at a dose of 1500 mg per day, and saxagliptin at 5 mg per day, reflecting an initial working diagnosis of T2D. This approach was intended to promptly stabilize blood glucose and mitigate suspected insulin resistance.

Alongside medication, the patient received recommendations for following a well-defined dietary regimen and maintaining consistent physical exercise, as components of a holistic diabetes care strategy. Periodic reviews were arranged in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient setting to track blood sugar levels and evaluate therapeutic efficacy.

Despite treatment, further findings—specifically moderate but inadequate β-cell performance on the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced familial pattern of early-onset diabetes—led to substantial suspicion for MODY.

Therapeutic measures were initiated based on the presenting clinical and laboratory evidence prior to receiving genetic test outcomes.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient's HbA1c had declined to 9.1%. In light of the genetic diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY, together with the observation of moderate but inadequate β-cell reserve and an improved HbA1c of 9.1%, insulin was discontinued and substituted with dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) to aid glycaemic management and preserve pancreatic function. Six months into this regimen, HbA1c further dropped to 6.0%, reflecting a marked enhancement in glycaemic control. As blood glucose levels stabilized, therapy was further de-escalated to a maintenance combination of metformin (1500 mg daily) and saxagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg daily). The patient is closely monitored, with HbA1c remaining consistently under 6.5%. No evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy has been detected thus far, and regular assessment for long-term complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, which functions as a transcription factor, is situated on chromosome 12 at the q24 locus. It consists of 10 exons and spans 23,790 base pairs, producing a protein of 631 amino acids. This protein is present in multiple tissues such as the liver, pancreas, and kidneys. Alterations in the HNF1A gene result in a progressive impairment of β-cell activity, causing deficiencies in the expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes, as well as decreased pyruvate kinase function. Consequently, these changes lead to reduced insulin release and diminished glucose tolerance.

In the present case, diagnosis followed established MODY criteria that include clinical, biochemical, and genetic components such as onset of diabetes at an early age, a notable family history, impaired postprandial glucose control, and negative autoantibody findings. A fasting C-peptide concentration above 0.6 ng/mL is indicative of a form of diabetes that is not type 1. Nonetheless, ongoing C-peptide assessment is important, as levels may remain normal early in the course of type 1 diabetes. Lack of clinician awareness and suspicion remains a major obstacle in recognizing MODY, leading to frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator designed by the Exeter group can assist in identifying individuals for genetic evaluation, but since it was constructed from data on a White European cohort, it might overstate MODY likelihood in patients from other backgrounds. Application of this calculator to our patient yielded a 62.4% probability (equivalent to a 1 in 1.6 chance) of MODY. There are documented cases reporting autoantibodies among MODY patients; some people carrying HNF1A mutations do have autoantibodies, but most with MODY are autoantibody negative. Prior research indicates that MODY patients tend to have lower incidences of obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to those with type 2 diabetes. However, increasing rates of obesity have rendered BMI and metabolic syndrome less useful for distinguishing MODY from T2DM, which may account for the elevated BMI observed in our patient. While familial history plays an important role in MODY diagnosis, its reliability can be compromised due to underreporting, sporadic mutations, and the common occurrence of type 2 diabetes in families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by the patient’s age and HbA1c values. The preferred initial approach is low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release by modulating ATP-dependent potassium channels. In individuals with HNF1A-MODY, GLP-1 receptor agonists may represent a safer and potentially more efficacious substitute for sulfonylureas as β-cell function diminishes, particularly in those susceptible to hypoglycaemia. A randomized, double-blind, crossover study of modest size found that both liraglutide and glimepiride lowered fasting plasma glucose and minimized postprandial glycaemic excursions comparably, with no marked distinction in outcomes among participants with HNF1A-MODY. Moreover, therapy with GLP-1 receptor agonists was associated with reduced occurrence of mild hypoglycaemia relative to glimepiride. Using genetic confirmation, we withdrew insulin therapy following stabilization of glucose levels and shifted the patient to GLP-1 receptor agonists, which sustained optimal glycaemic control. This case emphasizes the indispensable role of genetic testing in optimizing therapeutic strategies. Glycosuria observed in HNF1A-MODY stems from diminished SGLT2 expression, controlled by HNF1A transcriptional regulation. One report described severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis in HNF1A-MODY patients resulting from pronounced suppression of residual SGLT-2 activity. The hazards associated with SGLT-2 inhibitor use in HNF1A-MODY patients—including increased risk of dehydration, possible vascular instability, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may outweigh any therapeutic benefit. The combination treatment of liraglutide and metformin in this case proved both effective and safe, in line with established research findings.

Precision medicine plays a vital role in the management of MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy all diagnostic benchmarks even with established clinical guidelines. At present, there are no reliable biomarkers to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or T2D. According to Chen et al, the prevalence of MODY among 2,429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D was 1.5%, and 0.74% were carriers of pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al found in a population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes that 10% of 39 patients lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes harbored HNF1A mutations. Likewise, a contemporary population-based investigation from Russia assessed 506 individuals suspected of monogenic diabetes without autoimmune signs—determined by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at presentation, sustained C-peptide production, minimal insulin requirements over 5 years, and no severe obesity. The study identified P or LP variants in 46.4% of subjects, with HNF1A among the most frequently implicated genes. Confirming a MODY diagnosis necessitates DNA sequencing. NGS enables comprehensive evaluation of large DNA samples from a patient in one assay, thereby facilitating MODY detection. The advent of NGS has led to the recognition of numerous new genetic variants. The ACMG has established criteria for determining the pathogenicity (P) of these variants. Databases like ClinVar have documented over 1,000 distinct HNF1A variants, including those classified as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In this report, a previously undescribed heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not found in ClinVar or any standard variant repository, underscoring its originality without diminishing its clinical significance. This variant is located adjacent to residues already known to be pathogenic, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and shows familial co-segregation with diabetes, which together align with ACMG standards for an LP classification. Therefore, even though ClinVar is an important tool for variant annotation, the pathogenicity of this variant is substantiated by several independent lines of genetic and clinical evidence apart from database entries.

Research has demonstrated that most of the detected mutations are concentrated in the DNA-binding domain, with relatively fewer mutations found in the transactivation domain. The novel variant described in this report is also located within the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have revealed that mutations in HNF1A, such as P, exert limited influence on HNF1A protein functionality. Moreover, some HNF1A variants, which do not independently result in MODY, may still be linked to a heightened susceptibility to type 2 diabetes (T2D).

In summary, this case underscores the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its implications for personalized treatment. Future studies will aim to refine NGS-based screening approaches to enhance MODY detection rates, leading to improved cost control and clinical outcomes.
