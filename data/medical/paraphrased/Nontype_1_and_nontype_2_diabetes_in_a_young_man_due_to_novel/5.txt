Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a single-gene, autosomal dominant type of diabetes. In this report, we present a patient with hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A)-MODY resulting from a previously unrecognized heterozygous mutation in HNF1A. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. There was a significant family history of diabetes manifesting at a young age. Genetic analysis identified a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant not documented before. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist together with metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a three-month period. This case underscores the critical role of genetic evaluation in patients with early-onset diabetes—often incorrectly diagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and contributes to the expanding catalogue of HNF1A mutations with implications for individualized medical management.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a spectrum of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes, primarily arising from intrinsic abnormalities in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders usually present before 25 years of age and are identified as such. MODY accounts for an estimated 1%–6% of diabetes cases with onset in youth. The occurrence of MODY varies among different racial and ethnic populations, with the true frequency likely being underreported. Historically, most investigations have centered on White European cohorts; however, more recent research efforts have extended to populations in Asia and Australia. Reported prevalence rates for MODY show considerable discrepancies, a phenomenon that often stems from the fact that genetic testing is generally offered to individuals selected by clinical features, even within broadly sampled population studies.

Mutations across more than 15 genes have been implicated in the development of the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with each mutation often guiding specific treatment protocols. Most MODY cases stem from alterations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. Among these, HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent subtype, accounting for approximately 30\%–65\% of all diagnosed MODY instances. This subtype commonly demonstrates an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, with affected family members manifesting diabetes symptoms across generations. During early adolescence, clinical manifestations may be minimal or absent, and hyperglycemia is frequently discovered incidentally during standard screening, often in the absence of overt insulin resistance. Elevated postprandial glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L) can be confirmed through post-meal glucose testing or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Furthermore, the presence of glycosuria is common, resulting from a decreased renal threshold for glucose due to impaired tubular glucose reabsorption. Nonetheless, as the disease advances, affected individuals may present with classic diabetic symptoms such as excessive thirst, frequent urination, increased hunger, and unintentional weight loss—features that parallel those seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). As such, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D remains challenging because of considerable clinical overlap.

At present, various diagnostic strategies for MODY have been delineated in the literature. When clinical evaluation and family history point toward a diagnosis of MODY—especially in the context of absent autoantibodies commonly linked to type 1 diabetes, such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—and are supplemented by other relevant biochemical assessments, molecular genetic analysis becomes a pivotal diagnostic tool. Achieving an accurate diagnosis is fundamental for tailoring therapy, predicting disease course, evaluating the risk of complications, and enabling effective family guidance. Recent investigations have shown that a multitude of mutations within the HNF1A gene can cause HNF1A-MODY, with several hundred unique mutations being identified to date.

In this particular case, we detected a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient with HNF1A-MODY, through genetic testing utilizing a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel. The variant was evaluated for pathogenic significance based on the criteria outlined by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG). This specific alteration had not been described in prior publications, nor was it catalogued in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was assessed after being identified with hyperglycaemia and glycosuria during a routine annual check-up. Physical assessment revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure reading of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. There were no signs of acanthosis nigricans upon clinical examination. The individual’s family history was remarkable for the presence of diabetes at an early age: the maternal grandmother had been diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at 20 years old. None of the family members with diabetes had a documented history of premature cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

His glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and his fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), his 2-hour plasma glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no clinical evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. Symptomatically, the patient experienced polydipsia, polyphagia, and unintentional recent weight loss. Analysis of the lipid panel showed a high-density lipoprotein concentration of 1.01 mmol/L and elevated triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings suggestive of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). The patient's history was otherwise negative for notable medical or surgical conditions.

A glucagon stimulation test revealed a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L six minutes post-injection, which is indicative of reduced endogenous insulin production. This assessment, used to determine residual β-cell activity, relies on quantifying C-peptide response to glucagon administration. In routine clinical use, a post-stimulation C-peptide value above 0.60 nmol/L typically reflects adequate intrinsic insulin secretion, while lower concentrations are consistent with impaired β-cell function. Nonetheless, the glucagon stimulation test should be interpreted as supplementary, particularly in scenarios where diabetes-associated autoantibodies are absent or where clinical characteristics deviate from expected patterns. No evidence of microalbuminuria was observed. Assessment of sudomotor function by electrochemical skin conductance (such as Sudoscan) showed profoundly decreased conductance in the hands, pointing to severe autonomic nerve involvement affecting small fibres. Immunological evaluation for diabetes-specific autoantibodies, including GADA and IA-2A, yielded negative results. One month following diagnosis, genetic testing for MODY was pursued, identifying a heterozygous, pathogenic (P) variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), thus confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon initial diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased levels of HbA1c and plasma glucose. The starting therapeutic protocol comprised basal insulin degludec at a dose of eight units per day, coupled with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), in alignment with the working diagnosis of T2D. This approach was intended to establish prompt glycaemic regulation and to mitigate potential insulin resistance.

Alongside medical management, the patient was instructed to maintain a carefully structured diet and to participate in consistent physical exercise, as part of an integrative diabetes care plan. Subsequent follow-up was arranged in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient department to oversee glycaemic status and evaluate responsiveness to therapy.

Nevertheless, owing to the combination of modestly preserved but inadequate β-cell functionality upon glucagon stimulation (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), the absence of islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced familial incidence of early-onset diabetes, there emerged a considerable suspicion for MODY.

The initial regimen was instituted in advance of genetic testing results, guided by clinical assessment and laboratory parameters at the time of presentation.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient's HbA1c declined to 9.1%. With genetic testing confirming HNF1A-MODY and assessment revealing the patient retained moderate but suboptimal β-cell function—as well as noting the HbA1c improvement to 9.1%—insulin therapy was ceased. Dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg administered weekly) was initiated instead, aiming to enhance glycaemic management and preserve pancreatic function. Six months into this regimen, HbA1c further decreased to 6.0%, demonstrating marked glycaemic improvement. As stability was achieved, the treatment was streamlined to maintenance with metformin (1500 mg/day) combined with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day). The patient continues regular monitoring, with HbA1c persistently under 6.5%. There are, to date, no indications of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and ongoing evaluation for potential long-term complications is maintained.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene codes for a transcription factor situated on the q24 locus of chromosome 12. It consists of 10 exons across a span of 23,790 base pairs, and produces a protein with 631 amino acids that is present in multiple tissues, such as the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Alterations in the HNF1A gene result in a gradual deterioration of β-cell activity, reflected in decreased expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes, as well as diminished activity of pyruvate kinase. These changes ultimately cause lower insulin secretion and contribute to glucose intolerance.

Diagnosis in the reported case followed the established MODY criteria, which integrate clinical, biochemical, and genetic indicators: early onset of diabetes, a pronounced familial occurrence, postprandial hyperglycemia, and the absence of diabetes-associated autoantibodies. A preserved fasting C-peptide concentration above 0.6 ng/mL is indicative of non-type 1 diabetes; nonetheless, continued measurement of C-peptide is crucial, as normal levels may still be observed in initial phases of type 1 diabetes. One significant diagnostic obstacle is the general lack of clinician awareness and suspicion for MODY, frequently resulting in mistaken identification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator, created by the Exeter group, serves as a clinical decision aid for selecting patients for genetic assessment. It should be noted, however, that this tool is calibrated from White European population data, which may lead to an overestimation of MODY likelihood in non-White populations. Application of our patient’s clinical profile into this calculator yielded a calculated MODY probability of 62.4% (corresponding to a 1 in 1.6 chance). Some case reports have identified the presence of autoantibodies in MODY cases, and although a minority of those with HNF1A mutations demonstrate autoantibody positivity, the majority are autoantibody negative. Prior investigations suggest that both obesity and metabolic syndrome are less prevalent among individuals with MODY than with type 2 diabetes. However, rising obesity rates have eroded the utility of BMI and metabolic syndrome status for distinguishing MODY from T2DM, which may clarify the elevated BMI observed in our patient. Furthermore, although familial history remains a valuable consideration in diagnosing MODY, its reliability is compromised by factors such as under-reporting, de novo mutations, and the widespread occurrence of T2DM in family backgrounds.

The approach to managing HNF1A-MODY is influenced by the patient's age and HbA1c values. The standard initial treatment is low-dose sulfonylurea therapy, which stimulates insulin secretion by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell capacity deteriorates in individuals with HNF1A-MODY—especially in those with a heightened risk of hypoglycaemia—GLP-1 receptor agonists can represent a more favorable and safer therapeutic option compared to sulfonylureas. Evidence from a small, randomized, double-blind, crossover study indicates that both liraglutide and glimepiride are comparably efficacious in decreasing both fasting and postprandial plasma glucose levels, with no notable distinction between the two treatments in this population. Moreover, the incidence of mild hypoglycaemic events is lower with GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment relative to glimepiride. In the present case, genetic confirmation of the diagnosis enabled the withdrawal of insulin upon stabilization of glycaemic levels and allowed for a successful switch to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, maintaining glycaemic control. This underscores the pivotal importance of genetic testing in informing optimal therapeutic strategies. Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is a consequence of downregulated SGLT2 expression, regulated by HNF1A-dependent transcription. There have been reports that individuals with HNF1A-MODY may develop pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to substantial suppression of the remaining SGLT-2 function. Potential adverse effects linked to SGLT-2 inhibitors in this context, including heightened risks of dehydration, vascular instability, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, may outweigh the therapeutic gains these agents offer. In this case, treatment with liraglutide alongside metformin was both safe and efficacious, in line with established literature.

The implementation of precision medicine is particularly important in the context of MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all established diagnostic criteria, even with current clinical protocols in place. To date, there are no robust biomarkers that can effectively distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or T2D. In research conducted by Chen et al, it was shown that within a cohort of 2429 young Chinese adults exhibiting early-onset T2D, the incidence of MODY was 1.5%, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Another study by Møller et al demonstrated that, among a broadly sampled Danish Caucasian cohort diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, mutations in HNF1A were found in 10% of 39 individuals lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes. Similarly, a recent Russian population-based investigation screened 506 individuals suspected of hereditary diabetes but without autoimmune markers—these participants had negative results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, maintained C-peptide secretion, had low insulin requirements within five years, and did not present with severe obesity. This study identified P or LP genetic variants in 46.4% of subjects, with HNF1A ranking among the most frequently implicated genes. Confirmation of MODY diagnosis depends on DNA sequencing, where NGS technology has increased both the efficiency and scale of genetic analysis, facilitating broad MODY screening efforts. The rise of NGS has led to the identification of numerous novel genetic alterations. The ACMG supplies criteria for assessing whether these mutations are pathogenic. Databases such as ClinVar now list over 1000 unique HNF1A variants, spanning pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In the present study, we identified a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S), absent from ClinVar and major variant repositories, underscoring its novelty but not diminishing its clinical import. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid adjacent to established pathogenic sites, possesses a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and cosegregates with diabetes within the pedigree—fulfilling ACMG standards for classification as ‘likely pathogenic.’ Consequently, although resources like ClinVar are invaluable for variant cataloguing, the case for this variant's pathogenicity is substantiated by convergent genetic and clinical findings that extend beyond mere database annotation.

Research has demonstrated that most mutations detected are concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a comparatively reduced mutation frequency. The novel variant identified in the present case is also positioned within the DNA-binding domain. Certain studies suggest that some HNF1A mutations, such as P, exert limited influence on the functional activity of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, specific HNF1A variants that are not independently pathogenic for MODY may nonetheless contribute to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

To summarize, this case underscores the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its relevance to individualized therapeutic strategies. Ongoing investigations aim to advance the development of enhanced NGS screening protocols, which could increase the detection rates of MODY and optimize both cost-effectiveness and patient outcomes in clinical practice.
