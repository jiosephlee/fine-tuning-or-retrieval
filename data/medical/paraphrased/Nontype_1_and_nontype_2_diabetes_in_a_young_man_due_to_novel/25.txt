Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) constitutes an autosomal dominant single-gene diabetes subtype. This case report features an individual with HNF1A-MODY associated with a newly identified heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose and presence of glucose in urine without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history revealed diabetes diagnosed at a young age. Molecular analysis detected a novel disease-causing HNF1A variant. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist in combination with metformin resulted in a reduction in HbA1c from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month interval. This report emphasizes the significance of genetic evaluation in young-onset diabetes—which is commonly mistaken for type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and broadens the range of HNF1A mutations with implications for precision therapeutics.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes disorders, marked by an inherent abnormality in the formation and function of pancreatic β-cells. These conditions usually arise and are identified in individuals before reaching 25 years of age. MODY accounts for roughly 1%–6% of cases of diabetes presenting at a young age. Prevalence of MODY may differ across distinct ethnic and racial populations and may be underrecognized. Historically, investigation has been primarily focused on White European cohorts. Recently, prevalence studies have also targeted Asian and Australian populations. Estimates for MODY prevalence show notable discrepancies, largely attributable to the fact that genetic testing is often restricted to people selected on the basis of their clinical presentation, including studies examining population samples.

More than 15 genetic mutations have been associated with the monogenic MODY phenotype, each aligning with distinct treatment approaches. The bulk of MODY cases across its various subtypes stem from mutations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. Among these, HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of MODY diagnoses, and demonstrates a clear autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of symptomatic diabetes within families. During early adolescence, individuals are often asymptomatic, with hyperglycemia most commonly identified incidentally on routine laboratory evaluations, and there are typically no clinical signs of insulin resistance. Postprandial glucose measurement or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) frequently reveals marked elevations in glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Furthermore, glycosuria may occur due to a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption secondary to impaired glucose transport in the renal tubules. As the condition advances, symptoms including polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintended weight loss—mirroring those seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may develop. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D can be challenging owing to their shared clinical presentations.

Various diagnostic approaches for MODY have been delineated in recent research. When physical evaluation and family history imply a MODY phenotype and autoimmune markers associated with type 1 diabetes, such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) antibodies, return negative—along with corroborative biochemical assessments—molecular genetic testing becomes an essential diagnostic tool. Accurate identification is imperative for selection of appropriate therapy, prediction of complication risks, estimation of prognosis, and provision of genetic counselling for families. Current investigations have demonstrated that several hundred mutations within the HNF1A gene have been linked to HNF1A-MODY.

In this paper, we describe the discovery of a previously unreported heterozygous nucleotide change, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY, detected through next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel testing and evaluated for its pathogenic potential following the guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG). This variant has not been documented in published sources, nor listed in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment following the detection of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria at his routine annual health check. Physical examination showed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m², blood pressure measured at 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse rate of 78 beats per minute. No acanthosis nigricans was identified on examination. The family history was significant for early-onset diabetes mellitus, with cases in the maternal grandmother diagnosed in her 30s, the patient’s mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at 20 years old. No family members with diabetes had a recorded history of premature cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

The patient's HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, the 2-hour glucose value was 19.8 mmol/L. There was no clinical or laboratory evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. Reported symptoms included polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid panel showed a high-density lipoprotein of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L, suggestive of a possible diagnosis of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). The patient’s past medical and surgical histories were unremarkable.

A glucagon stimulation test yielded serum C-peptide levels of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, pointing to inadequate endogenous insulin production. This procedure is employed to assess remaining β-cell function by determining the increase in C-peptide following glucagon administration. In practical terms, a stimulated C-peptide value exceeding 0.60 nmol/L generally signifies retained insulin secretion, while reduced levels are indicative of β-cell impairment. Nonetheless, this assessment should be interpreted as supplementary rather than conclusive, particularly when autoantibody testing is negative or the presentation is not characteristic. Microalbuminuria screening was negative. Assessment of sudomotor function revealed severely impaired results in the hands, measured via electrochemical skin conductance (such as with Sudoscan), a non-invasive approach to evaluating small fiber autonomic nerves. These results indicated marked autonomic impairment. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) returned negative. At one month following the initial diagnosis, a MODY genetic panel identified a heterozygous, pathogenic (P) mutation in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), consistent with HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At the point of diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly high HbA1c and plasma glucose values. The first therapeutic approach included a basal insulin degludec (eight units per day), alongside metformin (1500 mg daily) and saxagliptin (5 mg daily), indicating an initial assumption of T2D. This regimen was selected to rapidly stabilize blood glucose and manage possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medications, the patient received counseling to follow a structured dietary program and participate in regular physical exercise, as part of a holistic diabetes care plan. Ongoing assessments were performed at the endocrinology and metabolism clinic to observe glycaemic levels and evaluate response to therapy.

Nonetheless, considering the patient's modestly maintained but inadequate β-cell activity on glucagon stimulation (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), negative findings for islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced familial history of early-onset diabetes, the likelihood of MODY was considered high.

Therapy was initiated prior to obtaining genetic testing results, relying on the clinical profile and laboratory data present at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c had fallen to 9.1%. With molecular confirmation of HNF1A-MODY, and given the patient’s moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell function as well as a reduction in HbA1c to 9.1%, insulin therapy was stopped. The regimen was switched to dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) to aid glycemic control and help maintain pancreatic function. Following six months on this therapy, the patient’s HbA1c further improved to 6.0%. As glycemic stability was achieved, treatment was streamlined to a maintenance regimen of metformin (1500 mg per day) combined with saxagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg per day). The patient continues to be monitored regularly, with HbA1c levels consistently remaining below 6.5%. There have been no findings of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy thus far, and regular monitoring for chronic complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene functions as a transcription factor situated on the q24 segment of chromosome 12. It consists of 10 exons, spans 23,790 base pairs, and codes for a protein with 631 amino acids. This protein is found in several organs such as the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Mutations in HNF1A result in a gradual deterioration of β-cell activity, reflected by disrupted insulin and GLUT2 gene expression as well as diminished pyruvate kinase activity. These molecular changes culminate in reduced insulin production and impaired glucose tolerance.

For this case, diagnosis was consistent with the established MODY criteria, encompassing clinical, biochemical, and genetic components such as early onset of diabetes, robust family history, postprandial glycemic intolerance, and negative autoantibody results. A fasting C-peptide concentration exceeding 0.6 ng/mL supports the diagnosis of diabetes types other than type 1. Nevertheless, ongoing monitoring of C-peptide levels remains vital, as normal values can be observed in initial stages of type 1 diabetes. One major challenge in recognizing MODY lies in the insufficient clinical awareness, which often leads to patients being incorrectly categorized as having type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator, designed by the Exeter group, serves as an aid for clinicians in identifying potential candidates for genetic screening. However, it was formulated using data from a White European cohort and may produce inflated MODY probability estimates for non-White populations. Inputting this patient's clinical data into the calculator yielded a predicted MODY probability of 62.4%, or roughly 1 in 1.6 likelihood. Several case reports note the occurrence of autoantibodies in MODY patients; some with HNF1A variants may exhibit autoantibodies, although most MODY cases do not. Previous literature indicates that individuals with MODY typically display lower prevalence of obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to T2D patients. Still, the increasing rates of obesity have reduced the effectiveness of using BMI or metabolic syndrome to distinguish MODY from T2DM, which may account for our patient's higher BMI. Moreover, family history remains a relevant criterion for MODY but is limited by factors such as under-reporting, spontaneous mutations, and the common presence of T2DM family history.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on patient age and HbA1c values. The primary treatment utilizes low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. For individuals with HNF1A-MODY experiencing declining β-cell function and an increased susceptibility to hypoglycaemia, GLP-1 receptor agonists provide a safer and potentially superior therapeutic option compared to sulfonylureas. Evidence from a small randomized, double-blind, crossover study indicates that both liraglutide and glimepiride significantly decrease fasting plasma glucose and reduce postprandial glucose variability, showing no substantial difference in efficacy among HNF1A-MODY patients. Furthermore, GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a reduced likelihood of mild hypoglycaemia relative to glimepiride. Genetic confirmation enabled cessation of insulin therapy after attaining glycaemic control and facilitated a switch to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which continued to effectively manage blood glucose levels. This underscores the essential role of genetic testing in selecting treatment strategies. Glycosuria observed in HNF1A-MODY results from diminished SGLT2 expression, regulated by HNF1A transcription factors. One report noted that patients with HNF1A-MODY can develop severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to pronounced inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. The potential adverse effects linked to SGLT-2 inhibitors in these patients—including increased dehydration, risk of vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may surpass the expected benefits. In this particular case, liraglutide combined with metformin demonstrated efficacy and safety, aligning with findings from prior investigations.

Personalized treatment approaches are vital in the context of MODY, particularly since individuals with HNF1A-MODY may not consistently fulfill established diagnostic benchmarks even when clinical guidelines are applied. At present, no robust biomarkers exist to reliably distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Findings from Chen et al demonstrated that, among 2,429 young Chinese adults presenting with early-onset type 2 diabetes, MODY had a prevalence of 1.5%, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al further showed that within a broad population-based sample of Danish Caucasians with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 individuals lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes were found to possess HNF1A gene mutations. Correspondingly, a recent Russian population-based investigation assessed 506 subjects suspected of hereditary diabetes based on the absence of autoimmune markers—negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 at onset, maintained C-peptide secretion, low insulin needs within five years, and no marked obesity. This study revealed that 46.4% of the cohort carried P or LP variants, with HNF1A identified as one of the predominant genes involved. Genetic analysis via DNA sequencing is crucial for the definitive diagnosis of MODY. The implementation of next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables simultaneous evaluation of substantial segments of the genome, making MODY detection more attainable. The expanding use of NGS has led to the identification of numerous novel variants. The ACMG has established recommendations for determining the pathogenic status of these variants. Over 1,000 unique HNF1A sequence alterations are currently catalogued in resources such as ClinVar, ranging from P, LP, benign, likely benign, to variants of uncertain significance. In this instance, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not listed in ClinVar or other widely referenced variant repositories, indicating its novelty but not disputing its potential pathogenicity. Notably, this substitution impacts an amino acid position near previously identified pathogenic sites, possesses a high AlphaMissense probability score of 0.995, and segregates with diabetes within the patient’s family—fulfilling ACMG criteria for an LP classification. Consequently, while ClinVar serves as a comprehensive variant database, classification of the present variant as likely pathogenic is corroborated by convergent genetic and phenotypic evidence, beyond the confines of these databases.

Research has shown that most detected mutations are concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain demonstrates a lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant identified in this report is also positioned within the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations reveal that HNF1A mutations, particularly those designated as P, have limited influence on the functionality of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, some HNF1A variants that do not cause MODY on their own might be linked to a heightened susceptibility to T2D.

To summarize, this case underscores the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its relevance to personalized therapeutic approaches. Ongoing studies aim to establish improved NGS screening protocols to enhance the diagnostic rate for MODY, which may facilitate more efficient resource allocation and optimize clinical outcomes.
