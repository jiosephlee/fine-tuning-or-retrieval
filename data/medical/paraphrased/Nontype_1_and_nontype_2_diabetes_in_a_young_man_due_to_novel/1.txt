Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic form of diabetes inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. This case report details an individual diagnosed with hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A)-MODY resulting from a previously unidentified heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. There was a significant family history of diabetes diagnosed at an early age. Genetic analysis identified a novel, pathogenic HNF1A variant. The patient's therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist in combination with metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This report underscores the significance of genetic testing in cases of early-onset diabetes, which are commonly misclassified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and further broadens the known range of HNF1A mutations of relevance to precision medicine strategies.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes marked by a fundamental impairment in pancreatic β-cell formation and function. The onset and diagnosis of these disorders generally occur before individuals reach 25 years of age. MODY is responsible for roughly 1%–6% of diabetes cases presenting at a young age. The frequency of MODY differs among various ethnicities and racial backgrounds, and these figures may be underreported. Until recently, most investigations have focused on populations of White European descent. However, recent research has begun to address MODY prevalence in Asian and Australian cohorts. The reported prevalence of MODY shows considerable disparity across studies. Much of this discrepancy is attributable to the fact that genetic testing is predominantly carried out in subjects selected for specific clinical features, even within population-based research.

Mutations associated with MODY have been identified in more than 15 distinct genes, each implicating unique treatment modalities. The most frequently encountered cases among the different MODY subtypes are predominantly attributed to alterations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. 

Among these, HNF1A-MODY accounts for the largest proportion, comprising approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses. This form demonstrates a well-defined pattern of autosomal dominant inheritance within affected families. During early adolescence, affected individuals often exhibit no symptoms, with hyperglycemia frequently identified incidentally during standard laboratory screenings, and typically in the absence of clinical evidence for insulin resistance. Measurement of postprandial glucose or application of a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal marked elevations in post-meal glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria is another possible finding, arising from a lower renal threshold for glucose reabsorption that stems from impaired tubular glucose transport. Nevertheless, as the disorder progresses, manifestations such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintended weight loss may develop, mirroring symptoms commonly observed in type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D remains challenging due to considerable clinical overlap.

At present, multiple diagnostic strategies for MODY have been outlined in various research studies. Once physical assessment and family history indicate a phenotype consistent with MODY, and diabetes-associated autoantibodies such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) are found to be negative—supported by other relevant biochemical analyses—molecular genetic testing becomes a pivotal tool in the diagnostic process. An accurate diagnosis is essential for selecting an appropriate therapeutic regimen, predicting possible complications, evaluating prognosis, and providing informed genetic counselling for families. Recent research has documented hundreds of distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene as causative for HNF1A-MODY.

In the present report, we describe the identification of a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene, in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY. This variant was discovered using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel and evaluated for its pathogenicity in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria for variant interpretation. Notably, this mutation has not been previously described in the literature, nor is it present in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. Written informed consent for publication of this case report was obtained directly from the patient.

Case presentation:

A young adult presented for assessment following the incidental discovery of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria during a routine annual check-up. Clinical evaluation revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m\(^2\), a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. The physical examination did not reveal acanthosis nigricans. The patient's family history was significant for diabetes manifesting at an early age: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at the age of 20. There was no reported occurrence of early-onset cardiovascular disease in relatives with diabetes.

Investigations:

His glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting plasma glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. Two hours after administering a 75 g OGTT, his plasma glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no clinical or biochemical evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described experiencing polydipsia, polyphagia, and had recently lost weight. Lipid analysis showed high-density lipoprotein at 1.01 mmol/L and triglyceride concentration of 4.31 mmol/L, findings consistent with possible metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). The patient’s past medical and surgical histories were unremarkable.

A glucagon stimulation test demonstrated serum C-peptide levels of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L six minutes following glucagon administration, suggestive of impaired insulin secretion. This test is utilized to assess residual β-cell function by quantifying C-peptide levels after pharmacological stimulation with glucagon. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L is usually indicative of preserved endogenous insulin release, while lower concentrations reflect compromised β-cell performance. Nonetheless, this assay serves as an adjunct rather than a standalone diagnostic tool, particularly when autoantibody findings are equivocal or clinical features are atypical. Urinalysis did not reveal microalbuminuria. Sudomotor testing revealed marked reduction in sudomotor activity in the hands, as measured by electrochemical skin conductance methods (such as Sudoscan), which assess autonomic small fibre integrity non-invasively. These findings confirm substantial autonomic dysfunction affecting the hands. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) were negative. Genetic analysis for MODY was conducted one month after diagnosis and identified a heterozygous, pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), consistent with a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly high levels of HbA1c and plasma glucose. The initial therapeutic approach involved administering basal insulin degludec (eight units daily) alongside metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), reflecting an initial presumption of T2D. This regimen was selected to promote prompt glycaemic stabilization and counteract possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient received recommendations for a regimented dietary program and consistent physical activity, forming an integral component of a holistic diabetes care plan. Subsequent monitoring took place during follow-up visits at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic, focusing on ongoing glycaemic metrics and therapeutic efficacy.

Nevertheless, the patient's intermediate but inadequate β-cell reserve as measured by glucagon-stimulated C-peptide (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and notable family history of early-onset diabetes collectively heightened clinical suspicion for MODY.

Therapeutic interventions commenced before genetic testing results were obtained, based on the patient’s clinical features and laboratory data at presentation.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c was reduced to 9.1%. With HNF1A-MODY confirmed through genetic analysis, and taking into account the patient’s partially retained but inadequate β-cell function, as well as the decrease in HbA1c to 9.1%, insulin therapy was withdrawn. Instead, dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg administered weekly) was initiated to enhance glycaemic regulation and help preserve pancreatic function. Six months following this adjustment, HbA1c had further dropped to 6.0%, reflecting a marked improvement. Upon achieving stable glycaemic control, treatment was transitioned to a maintenance regimen of metformin (1500 mg/day) combined with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day). The patient continues to receive regular follow-up care, with HbA1c levels consistently remaining below 6.5%. At present, there is no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and the patient remains under surveillance for the development of long-term complications.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, found in the q24 region of chromosome 12, encodes a transcription factor comprising 10 exons and extending across 23,790 base pairs. The gene produces a protein consisting of 631 amino acids, which is distributed in several organs, notably the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Pathogenic variants in HNF1A disrupt β-cell performance by diminishing the expression of insulin and GLUT2, as well as by lowering pyruvate kinase activity. This sequence of dysfunctions results in reduced insulin release and impaired glucose tolerance.

For diagnosis in this scenario, current MODY diagnostic guidelines were observed, integrating clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and genetic confirmation. These criteria include an early age at onset of diabetes, marked familial clustering, abnormal postprandial glycemic regulation, and absence of diabetes-associated autoantibodies. Fasting C-peptide concentrations above 0.6 ng/mL are consistent with non-type 1 diabetes, yet ongoing surveillance of C-peptide is important as initial values may remain within the normal range in early-stage type 1 diabetes. One substantial obstacle in identifying MODY is insufficient recognition or clinical consideration, leading to frequent misclassification as type 1 or T2D. The MODY probability calculator, formulated by the Exeter research group, is a useful resource to flag patients for further genetic evaluation. Nonetheless, because its development relied on data from White European cohorts, the model may overpredict MODY prevalence in other populations. In our patient's case, data input into the calculator suggested a MODY probability of 62.4% (equivalent to approximately 1 in 1.6). Multiple case series have observed the existence of autoantibodies in certain MODY individuals. While most MODY patients are autoantibody negative, there have been instances where autoantibodies coexist with HNF1A mutations. Early reports indicate that both obesity and metabolic syndrome occur less frequently among those with MODY than with T2D, but escalating obesity trends have made the distinction between MODY and T2DM based on BMI or metabolic syndrome less clear, which could account for the elevated BMI in this instance. Moreover, while a family history of diabetes is a key consideration for MODY diagnosis, its reliability can be compromised by reporting inaccuracies, the presence of de novo mutations, and the widespread incidence of T2DM within families.

The therapeutic approach for HNF1A-MODY depends on both patient age and HbA1c measurements. Typically, initial management consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release by targeting potassium-sensitive ATP channels. However, as β-cell function diminishes, individuals with HNF1A-MODY, especially those at elevated risk for hypoglycaemia, may benefit more from GLP-1 receptor agonists, given their superior safety and efficacy profiles. Evidence from a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study showed that both liraglutide and glimepiride substantially decreased fasting plasma glucose as well as postprandial glycaemic excursions, and no statistically significant differences were observed between these agents in HNF1A-MODY patients. Furthermore, the incidence of mild hypoglycaemia was lower with GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment compared to glimepiride. Utilizing genetic testing enabled discontinuation of insulin after stabilisation of glycaemic control, permitting a successful switch to GLP-1 receptor agonist monotherapy, which continued to provide effective glucose regulation. This underscores the pivotal role of genetic diagnostics in informing clinical management. Glycosuria observed in HNF1A-MODY results from diminished SGLT2 expression, as this transporter is regulated by HNF1A at the transcriptional level. There have been accounts of severe dehydration and episodes of diabetic ketoacidosis among HNF1A-MODY individuals, potentially resulting from pronounced inhibition of any remaining SGLT-2 activity. Adverse outcomes related to SGLT-2 inhibitor use in these patients—such as increased dehydration, potential for vascular collapse, greater susceptibility to genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may outweigh any prospective benefits from these agents. In the presented scenario, a combination therapy of liraglutide and metformin proved both effective and safe, aligning with findings reported in earlier literature.

Personalized therapeutic approaches are particularly important in the context of MODY, especially since individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all diagnostic benchmarks outlined in established clinical guidelines. To date, effective biomarkers capable of distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or T2D remain unavailable. Chen et al conducted an investigation involving 2429 Chinese young adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D, revealing a MODY prevalence of 1.5%, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. In a separate analysis, Møller et al found that, within a sizeable Danish Caucasian cohort of type 1 diabetes patients, 10% of 39 subjects without high-risk HLA haplotypes possessed mutations in the HNF1A gene. Analogously, a recent Russian population-based investigation assessed 506 subjects suspected of hereditary diabetes—characterized by the absence of autoimmune markers (negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis), maintained C-peptide levels, low insulin demand within five years, and lack of significant obesity. This study identified P or LP variants in 46.4% of cases, with HNF1A being among the most frequently implicated genes. To validate MODY diagnoses, DNA sequencing is indispensable. The use of NGS enables comprehensive genomic assessment in a single assay, making MODY screening more practical. As NGS technology advances, the catalogue of recognized genetic variants expands considerably. The ACMG has issued guidance on classifying these variants’ pathogenicity. More than 1000 distinct HNF1A variants are documented in repositories like ClinVar, spanning P, LP, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In our reported case, a previously undescribed heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not identified in ClinVar or similar variant repositories, underscoring its novel character without undermining its possible clinical impact. Notably, the affected amino acid lies directly adjacent to established pathogenic residues, has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and segregates with diabetes within the family, all supporting ACMG criteria for an LP classification. Therefore, while ClinVar is instrumental for documenting known variants, the pathogenic assessment of this novel variant draws on diverse strands of genetic and clinical data, extending well beyond database listings.

Research has shown that most detected mutations are concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits comparatively fewer mutations. The novel variant identified in this report is also found in the DNA-binding domain. Several investigations suggest that certain HNF1A mutations, though present, have limited influence on the overall function of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, there are HNF1A variants that do not independently result in MODY but may nonetheless contribute to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the critical need for precise diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY and its consequences for individualized therapeutic approaches. Ongoing and future studies aim to refine NGS screening protocols, with the objective of increasing MODY detection rates and optimizing both healthcare costs and patient outcomes.
