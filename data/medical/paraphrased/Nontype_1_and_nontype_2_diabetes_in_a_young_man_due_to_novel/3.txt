Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic form of diabetes inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. In this paper, we present a case involving HNF1A-MODY attributed to a newly identified heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. Notably, the family history revealed multiple instances of diabetes with early onset. Genetic analysis detected a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A. The combination therapy of a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin led to a reduction in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over 3 months. This report underscores the critical role of genetic testing in cases of early-onset diabetes—conditions often misclassified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and further broadens the known range of HNF1A mutations that are significant in precision medicine.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes that are primarily attributable to inherent abnormalities in the formation and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders usually present clinically and are identified before individuals reach 25 years of age. MODY accounts for approximately 1\%–6\% of diabetes cases with onset at a young age. Prevalence rates for MODY can differ among various ethnic and racial populations and are likely underreported. Up until now, investigations have predominantly focused on cohorts of White European descent. More recent research has begun to address MODY occurrence in Asian and Australian populations. Reported prevalence figures for MODY differ markedly, largely as a result of genetic testing being restricted to individuals selected according to particular clinical features, which applies even within population-based studies.

Over fifteen distinct genes with relevant mutations have been implicated in the MODY-monogenic phenotype, each associated with specific treatment approaches. Most cases across different MODY subtypes are largely attributed to mutations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β.

HNF1A-MODY is the most common variant, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all identified MODY cases and is characterized by an evident autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, whereby symptomatic diabetes manifests across successive generations within families. During early adolescence, affected individuals often lack overt symptoms, and elevated glucose levels are typically detected incidentally during standard laboratory tests, generally without evidence of insulin resistance. Abnormal postprandial glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L) can be revealed through either post-meal glucose assessments or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Furthermore, patients may present with glycosuria due to a lower renal threshold for glucose reabsorption, reflecting defective glucose handling within renal tubules. As the condition advances, classic symptoms such as excessive thirst, increased urination, heightened appetite, and unintended weight loss—paralleling those seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may develop. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D can be challenging, given the overlap in clinical presentation.

To date, a number of diagnostic protocols for MODY have been outlined in the literature. When clinical evaluation and review of family medical history indicate a likely MODY phenotype, and if autoantibodies typically present in type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, supported by further relevant biochemical assessments, molecular genetic analysis becomes an essential step in confirming the diagnosis. Achieving an accurate diagnosis is fundamental for selecting appropriate therapeutic interventions, predicting disease progression, estimating future risks, and guiding family-based counseling. Recent research has revealed that a wide range of mutations, numbering in the hundreds, within the HNF1A gene underlie cases of HNF1A-MODY.

In the patient case described herein, we discovered a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A, through next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel-based genetic testing. The variant's pathogenicity was evaluated in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) standards for variant interpretation. To our knowledge, this mutation has not been documented in the literature, VarSome database, or ClinVar database. Informed written consent for publication was obtained directly from the individual involved in this report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment following the discovery of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria during a routine annual check-up. Physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure reading of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. There were no clinical signs of acanthosis nigricans. The family history revealed several cases of early-onset diabetes, including the maternal grandmother who was diagnosed in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother who developed the condition at age 20. None of these family members with diabetes had a reported history of early-onset cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

The patient’s glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting glucose level of 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), the 2-hour glucose level reached 19.8 mmol/L. No clinical or biochemical evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis was present. The patient described symptoms including polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid panel results showed a high-density lipoprotein concentration of 1.01 mmol/L and elevated triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, raising suspicion for metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). There was no notable prior medical or surgical history.

The glucagon stimulation test resulted in serum C-peptide levels of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes post-injection, consistent with reduced insulin secretion. This test assesses residual β-cell capacity by determining the stimulated C-peptide response following glucagon administration. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide value exceeding 0.60 nmol/L is indicative of preserved endogenous insulin production; levels below this threshold imply β-cell impairment. Nevertheless, the glucagon stimulation test should be considered adjunctive, especially if antibody status is ambiguous or the clinical context is unusual. No evidence of microalbuminuria was found. Assessment of sudomotor function using electrochemical skin conductance (such as Sudoscan) showed severely impaired function in the hands, demonstrating significant autonomic nervous system involvement. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) were negative. A month after the initial diagnosis, molecular testing for MODY identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), supporting a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon initial diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly high levels of HbA1c and plasma glucose. The first-line therapy comprised basal insulin degludec at a dose of eight units per day, combined with metformin at 1500 mg per day and saxagliptin at 5 mg per day, reflecting an early working diagnosis of T2D. The therapeutic goal was to quickly normalize blood glucose levels and mitigate suspected insulin resistance.

Alongside pharmacologic interventions, the patient received recommendations to follow a carefully planned diet and participate in regular exercise, in alignment with an integrated diabetes care plan. Subsequent follow-ups at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient department were scheduled to track glycaemic status and evaluate the effectiveness of treatment.

Despite these measures, moderate but suboptimal β-cell function was detected during a glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), and in the absence of islet autoantibodies together with a notable family history of early-onset diabetes, clinicians strongly suspected MODY.

The initial therapeutic approach was instituted before the genetic testing results were available, guided by the clinical features and laboratory data observed at presentation.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient's HbA1c was reduced to 9.1%. With the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY genetically confirmed, and given the patient's partially retained but still inadequate β-cell function, as well as the observed decline in HbA1c to 9.1%, insulin therapy was discontinued. Dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist (1.5 mg administered weekly), was initiated to further support glycaemic regulation and aid in the preservation of pancreatic function. Six months into this therapeutic approach, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 6.0%, reflecting marked clinical improvement. With stabilization of glycaemic metrics, the treatment regimen was transitioned to a maintenance combination of metformin (1500 mg/day) and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day). The patient continues to be closely monitored, with HbA1c persistently staying below 6.5%. To date, no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy has emerged, and vigilant monitoring for long-term complications continues.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor positioned at the q24 locus of chromosome 12. This gene contains 10 exons and spans 23,790 base pairs, ultimately producing a protein composed of 631 amino acids that is present in multiple organs, such as the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Mutations in the HNF1A gene result in a gradual deterioration of β-cell function, which is characterized by diminished insulin and GLUT2 gene expression and lowered pyruvate kinase activity. These defects cumulatively contribute to reduced insulin output and impaired glucose tolerance.

Diagnosis in this instance followed established MODY diagnostic protocols, incorporating clinical, biochemical, and genetic criteria—these include an early age of diabetes onset, a strong familial pattern, abnormal postprandial glycemia, and absence of diabetes-related autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide level exceeding 0.6 ng/mL is indicative of a non-type 1 diabetes etiology; however, ongoing C-peptide surveillance remains important because normal results can be observed during initial stages of type 1 diabetes. One major obstacle in the identification of MODY stems from limited recognition and a low index of clinical suspicion, leading to frequent misclassification as type 1 diabetes or T2D. The MODY probability calculator devised by the Exeter group provides clinicians with a tool to select individuals suitable for genetic testing. Nonetheless, since this calculator was validated primarily in White European cohorts, it may yield inflated MODY probabilities in non-White populations. For our patient, entering relevant clinical variables into the calculator returned a MODY likelihood of 62.4% (approximately 1 in 1.6). Notably, multiple case reports document the presence of autoantibodies in a subset of MODY cases, and while some carriers of HNF1A mutations do exhibit autoantibodies, the majority of MODY patients are seronegative. Previous research suggests that rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome are generally lower among those with MODY compared to individuals with T2D. However, increasing obesity rates have eroded the discriminative power of BMI or the metabolic syndrome for differentiating MODY from T2DM, which may partly account for the elevated BMI seen in our patient. Finally, although a family history of diabetes is a key MODY indicator, its diagnostic utility may be undermined by factors such as incomplete family histories, de novo mutations, and the commonality of T2DM among relatives.

Therapeutic strategies for HNF1A-MODY are based on both patient age and HbA1c measurements. Sulfonylureas at low doses are typically used as the initial pharmacological intervention, leveraging their mechanism of augmenting insulin secretion via potassium-sensitive ATP channels. For those with HNF1A-MODY, particularly individuals susceptible to hypoglycaemia or with diminishing β-cell functionality, GLP-1 receptor agonists represent a safer and, potentially, more efficacious alternative to sulfonylureas. Evidence from a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study indicated that liraglutide and glimepiride similarly decreased both fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glycaemic excursions, without meaningful efficacy differences observed between them in this patient group. Nevertheless, therapy with GLP-1 receptor agonists was associated with a reduced frequency of mild hypoglycaemic events compared to glimepiride. Genetic testing facilitated cessation of insulin administration after glycaemic targets were achieved, enabling a successful transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy that sustained appropriate glycaemic regulation. This case underscores the pivotal role of genetic diagnostics in tailoring management strategies. The occurrence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is due to attenuated SGLT2 expression, regulated transcriptionally by HNF1A. Published reports suggest that patients with HNF1A-MODY may be at risk for severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis when residual SGLT-2 function is highly suppressed. The adverse event profile for SGLT-2 inhibitors in this context, including elevated likelihood of dehydration, vascular instability, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, may exceed their anticipated therapeutic advantages. In the present case, a combination of liraglutide and metformin provided effective and well-tolerated glycaemic control, consistent with previous research findings.

The implementation of precision medicine is particularly significant in the context of MODY, given that individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fully satisfy existing diagnostic standards, even when current clinical protocols are followed. At present, no reliable biomarkers exist to clearly distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or type 2 diabetes. In research conducted by Chen et al, it was observed that among 2429 Chinese young adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D, the occurrence of MODY was 1.5%, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Findings by Møller et al demonstrated that within a large, population-derived sample of Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 individuals lacking any high-risk HLA haplotypes exhibited HNF1A gene mutations. Correspondingly, a recent Russian population-based investigation screened 506 subjects with a clinical suspicion of hereditary diabetes—criteria included negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at disease onset, maintained C-peptide levels, reduced insulin needs for 5 years, and no marked obesity. P or LP genetic variants were identified in 46.4% of this cohort, with HNF1A emerging as a frequently implicated gene. To substantiate MODY diagnoses, DNA sequencing plays a crucial role. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology has enhanced the efficiency of MODY screening by enabling large-scale DNA analyses in a single experiment, and its proliferation has facilitated the discovery of numerous previously unidentified variants. The ACMG supplies protocols to adjudicate the pathogenic status of such variants. Over 1000 individual HNF1A variants—classified as P, LP, benign, likely benign, or variants of uncertain significance—are catalogued in repositories like ClinVar. In our analysis, the unique heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not found in ClinVar or other widely referenced variant repositories, emphasizing its novelty rather than undermining its clinical relevance. Notably, this alteration occurs adjacent to previously established pathogenic residues, is characterized by a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and is observed to segregate with diabetes in affected family members—evidence that aligns with the ACMG's criteria for an LP designation. Therefore, while ClinVar provides an essential platform for variant annotation, the evidence for the pathogenicity of this specific variant is reinforced by multiple genetic and clinical data sources beyond what is contained within database records.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations occur within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the mutation frequency in the transactivation domain is comparatively lower. The novel variant identified in this case study also resides in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have found that, despite the presence of HNF1A mutations, particularly those denoted as P, there is minimal disruption to HNF1A protein activity. Moreover, specific HNF1A variants that are insufficient to cause MODY independently may nonetheless be linked to a heightened susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the critical role of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY for guiding personalized therapeutic strategies. Ongoing research aims to devise improved NGS screening methodologies to enhance the detection rates of MODY, thereby facilitating more effective cost management and superior clinical outcomes.
