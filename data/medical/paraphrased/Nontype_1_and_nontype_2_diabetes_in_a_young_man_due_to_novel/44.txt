Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a monogenic, autosomal dominant form of diabetes mellitus. Here, we describe an individual diagnosed with hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A)-MODY attributed to a novel heterozygous mutation in HNF1A. The clinical presentation included elevated blood glucose and the presence of glucose in the urine without diabetic ketoacidosis. There was a documented family history of diabetes occurring at a young age. Molecular analysis identified a new pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist along with metformin led to a reduction in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over three months. This case underlines the critical role of genetic evaluation in diabetes presenting at an early age, which is often incorrectly classified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and broadens the catalog of HNF1A mutations that inform precision therapeutic strategies.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes marked by an intrinsic abnormality in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders generally present and are identified prior to the age of 25. MODY is responsible for approximately 1%–6% of cases of early-onset diabetes. Prevalence rates of MODY differ across various ethnic and racial populations and may be subject to underreporting. Historically, investigations have predominantly focused on individuals of White European descent. More recent research has explored MODY rates in Asian and Australian cohorts. Reported prevalence figures fluctuate considerably, a discrepancy often attributed to the restriction of genetic testing to select patients meeting clinical criteria, even in population-based studies.

More than 15 genes with relevant mutations have been associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, each linked to distinct management approaches. Most instances among different MODY forms arise from mutations in genes such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. 

HNF1A-MODY is the most frequently occurring subtype, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY cases, and is characterized by a clearly defined autosomal dominant inheritance of symptomatic diabetes within families. During early adolescence, individuals may lack clinical symptoms, with elevated blood glucose often discovered incidentally during routine laboratory tests, generally without evidence of insulin resistance. Assessment of postprandial blood glucose or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal marked increases in glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Furthermore, glycosuria may be found, resulting from a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired glucose handling in the renal tubules. In more advanced stages, clinical manifestations such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss—features that overlap with both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may develop. As a result, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D remains challenging because of similar clinical presentations.

Various studies have developed diagnostic protocols for MODY. When physical examination and family history suggest clinical features indicative of MODY, and diabetes-associated autoantibodies—including glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are negative, complemented by supportive biochemical findings, molecular genetic analysis becomes central to establishing the diagnosis. An accurate diagnosis is essential for choosing an appropriate therapeutic strategy, predicting the risk of complications, estimating prognosis, and guiding genetic counseling for the patient and family. Recent investigations have revealed hundreds of distinct HNF1A gene mutations linked to HNF1A-MODY.

In this case, genetic testing utilizing a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel revealed a previously unrecognized heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient with HNF1A-MODY. Assessment of the variant’s pathogenicity followed the criteria set by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) for variant classification. This particular mutation had not been recorded in published literature, nor in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. Informed written consent for the publication of this case was obtained directly from the patient.

Case presentation:

A young adult was referred for assessment due to findings of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria detected during his routine annual examination. Physical assessment revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. There were no signs of acanthosis nigricans on inspection. His family history was significant for early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at 20 years of age. There were no reported cases of early-onset cardiovascular disease among diabetic relatives.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and his fasting plasma glucose level was 12.4 mmol/L. The 2-hour glucose concentration following a 75 g OGTT reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no clinical or biochemical evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described polydipsia, polyphagia, and unintentional recent weight reduction. Lipid studies showed a high-density lipoprotein value of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, consistent with a possible diagnosis of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). No notable past medical or surgical history was reported.

On glucagon stimulation testing, serum C-peptide measured 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and rose to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, pointing to impaired insulin production. This test determines residual beta cell function by quantifying C-peptide after glucagon administration. In clinical practice, a stimulated C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L is indicative of retained endogenous insulin secretion, whereas values below this threshold denote beta cell impairment. Nevertheless, interpretation of glucagon stimulation test results should be as supportive rather than conclusive, especially where autoantibody testing is negative or the clinical presentation is unusual. Microalbuminuria screening was negative. Sudomotor assessment revealed severe impairment of sudomotor activity in the hands. This was evaluated with electrochemical skin conductance methods such as Sudoscan, which are non-invasive and assess autonomic small nerve fiber function. The findings reflected markedly reduced hand conductance, supporting significant autonomic involvement. Testing for autoantibodies, including GADA and IA-2A, returned negative. One month after the initial diagnosis, genetic testing for MODY identified a heterozygous, pathogenic (P) mutation in HNF1A (p.N270S, HNF1A:c.809A>G), compatible with HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose values. The starting therapeutic approach included basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, along with metformin at a dosage of 1500 mg per day and saxagliptin at 5 mg per day, indicative of an initial working diagnosis of T2D. This protocol was selected to achieve prompt glycaemic stabilization and to address possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient was instructed to follow a regimented dietary program and participate in routine exercise, as elements of a holistic diabetes management strategy. Subsequent follow-up was carried out in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient setting to track glycaemic status and evaluate treatment efficacy.

Nonetheless, given that the patient demonstrated moderately retained but inadequate β-cell capacity on glucagon stimulation testing (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced familial history of early-onset diabetes, MODY was strongly suspected clinically.

Therapy was initiated ahead of the genetic testing results, grounded in both the patient's clinical features and the laboratory data available at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient's HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. With genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and taking into account the patient's intermediate, but inadequate, β-cell capacity alongside the HbA1c improvement, insulin was discontinued in favor of dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist administered at 1.5 mg weekly) to aid in glycaemic management and protect pancreatic function. Six months into this therapy, the HbA1c further decreased to 6.0%, demonstrating marked progress. Once glycaemic stability was achieved, treatment was streamlined to a maintenance regimen consisting of metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day). The patient continues to be monitored routinely and maintains HbA1c values under 6.5%. There have been no detectable signs of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and surveillance for potential chronic complications remains ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, situated in the q24 segment of chromosome 12, functions as a transcription factor. It consists of 10 exons covering 23,790 base pairs and produces a protein made up of 631 amino acids, which is found in multiple tissues such as the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Alterations in HNF1A trigger a gradual loss of β-cell activity, presenting as diminished expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes, along with lowered pyruvate kinase activity. This process leads to reduced insulin release and impaired glucose tolerance.

In this case, the diagnostic evaluation followed established criteria for MODY, including clinical, biochemical, and genetic findings. These criteria incorporate early onset of diabetes, strong family history, impaired postprandial glycemic response, and negative autoantibody results. A fasting C-peptide concentration above 0.6 ng/mL is indicative of a form of diabetes other than type 1. Nevertheless, ongoing assessment of C-peptide is critical since normal levels can occur in early type 1 diabetes. One substantial challenge to MODY identification is the absence of clinical suspicion and awareness, often resulting in misclassification as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator by the Exeter group assists clinicians in selecting patients for genetic analysis but was created using data from a White European cohort, which may lead to overestimation of MODY risk among non-White populations. Using this calculator with our patient’s details, the likelihood of MODY was calculated at 62.4% (roughly 1 in 1.6). Multiple case reports have identified autoantibody positivity in MODY patients; although some people with HNF1A mutations possess autoantibodies, most MODY individuals lack them. Historical data suggests that MODY patients have lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to those with T2DM. Nevertheless, increasing obesity prevalence has made differentiating MODY from T2DM using BMI or metabolic syndrome less reliable, which may account for the higher BMI observed in this case. Moreover, although family history is a significant diagnostic aspect of MODY, it may be less dependable due to factors such as under-reporting, de novo mutations, and the widespread occurrence of T2DM within families.

Therapeutic strategies for HNF1A-MODY are guided by the patient's age and HbA1c levels. Initial management typically employs low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin release through modulation of ATP-sensitive potassium channels. For individuals with HNF1A-MODY, especially those at higher risk for hypoglycaemia or with diminishing β-cell function, GLP-1 receptor agonists represent a safer and potentially more efficacious option compared to sulfonylureas. Evidence from a small, randomized, double-blind, crossover study indicated that liraglutide and glimepiride both significantly lowered fasting and postprandial plasma glucose in HNF1A-MODY subjects, without notable differences in efficacy; however, GLP-1 receptor agonists were associated with fewer mild hypoglycaemic episodes relative to glimepiride. In the presented case, genetic testing allowed for discontinuation of insulin therapy once glycaemic targets were met, facilitating a transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy while maintaining adequate glycaemic control. This case underscores the importance of genetic testing to inform therapeutic decision-making. The presence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY results from decreased expression of SGLT2, which is regulated by HNF1A. One report described severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis in HNF1A-MODY patients following pronounced inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. Potential adverse effects linked to SGLT-2 inhibitor use in this population include an elevated risk for dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, which may supersede the anticipated advantages of these agents. In this patient, combination therapy with liraglutide and metformin proved both effective and well-tolerated, aligning with prior literature.

The importance of precision medicine in MODY is underscored by the observation that individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy all standard diagnostic criteria, despite established clinical guidelines. Currently, there are no reliable biomarkers available to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or type 2 diabetes (T2D). Chen et al conducted a study involving 2429 Chinese young adults with early-onset T2D and found a MODY prevalence of 1.5%, with 0.74% carrying pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY gene variants. Møller et al demonstrated that within a sizable, population-based group of Danish Caucasian type 1 diabetes patients, 10% of 39 individuals lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes were identified with mutations in the HNF1A gene. Along similar lines, a recent Russian population-based investigation examined 506 people suspected of having hereditary diabetes absent of autoimmune markers—based on negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibody tests at diagnosis, sustained C-peptide levels, minimal insulin needs over five years, and no marked obesity. This study detected P or LP variants in 46.4% of subjects, most frequently in the HNF1A gene. Genetic sequencing remains a critical step for validating clinical suspicion of MODY. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) facilitates simultaneous assessment of extensive segments of patient DNA, enhancing MODY screening capacity. Advances in NGS have revealed numerous novel genetic variants. The ACMG offers guidance for classifying variants as pathogenic. More than 1000 distinct HNF1A variants have been catalogued in repositories like ClinVar, including variants deemed pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, or of uncertain significance. In this particular case, a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not found in ClinVar or other major variant databases, signifying its uniqueness rather than raising doubts about its clinical impact. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid position next to established pathogenic sites, possesses a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes within the family, which aligns with ACMG criteria for an LP classification. Therefore, although ClinVar serves as a valuable repository for documented variants, multiple independent genetic and clinical findings—aside from database listing—support the pathogenic nature of this variant.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the frequency of mutations in the transactivation domain is lower. The novel variant identified in this patient is also located within the DNA-binding domain. Certain studies indicate that, when present as P, HNF1A mutations exert limited influence on the functional activity of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, several HNF1A variants that do not independently lead to MODY may still contribute to an elevated risk for T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the critical role of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and the subsequent implications for personalized therapeutic approaches. Future investigations will prioritize the development of more robust NGS-based screening methods to enhance the detection rate of MODY, with the goal of optimizing costs and maximizing clinical outcomes.
