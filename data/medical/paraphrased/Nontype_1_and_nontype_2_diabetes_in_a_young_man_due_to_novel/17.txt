Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is an autosomal dominant, monogenic type of diabetes. This case report details a patient with HNF1A-MODY caused by a previously unidentified heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose and glycosuria but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. There was a significant family history of diabetes with early onset. Genetic analysis identified a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Initiation of therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist in combination with metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This report underscores the value of genetic evaluation in cases of diabetes with early onset, as these are often incorrectly categorized as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and further broadens the array of HNF1A mutations significant for precision treatment strategies.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes marked by an intrinsic abnormality in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders generally present clinically and are diagnosed prior to the age of 25 years. MODY accounts for an estimated 1%–6% of diabetes cases with early onset. The frequency of MODY differs among various ethnic and racial groups, and the actual rates may be underreported. Historically, most investigations have focused on White European cohorts. More recently, studies have begun to assess MODY prevalence in Asian and Australian populations. Reported rates of MODY differ widely, in part because genetic testing for MODY is frequently restricted to patients selected by certain clinical criteria, even in research based on population cohorts.

More than 15 gene mutations have been implicated in causing the monogenic form of MODY, with each genetic subtype requiring distinct approaches to treatment. The bulk of reported MODY cases stem from alterations in genes like hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. HNF1A-MODY is the most common subtype, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of MODY diagnoses, and demonstrates a distinct autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of symptomatic diabetes within families. During early adolescence, individuals may be asymptomatic, and hyperglycemia is often incidentally detected during standard blood screening, usually in the absence of clinical signs of insulin resistance. Postprandial blood glucose testing or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal a significant elevation in glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also occur, which results from a lowered renal threshold for glucose due to impaired glucose reabsorption in the renal tubules. In advanced disease stages, manifestations such as excessive thirst, frequent urination, increased hunger, and unintentional weight loss—symptoms paralleling those of type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may arise. As a result, distinguishing MODY from type 1 or T2D can be challenging because of these shared clinical features.

To date, a number of diagnostic pathways for MODY have been delineated in research. Following physical examination and evaluation of family history suggestive of MODY, along with the absence of type 1 diabetes-specific autoantibodies such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8), together with additional supportive biochemical assessments, molecular genetic analysis plays a pivotal role in reaching a definitive diagnosis. Accurate identification is essential to guide appropriate management strategies, predict the risk of complications, estimate prognosis, and enable genetic counselling for affected families. Recent investigations have shown that HNF1A-MODY can be attributed to hundreds of distinct mutations within the HNF1A gene.

In the present case, we detected a previously unreported heterozygous missense mutation, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A, in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY through a next-generation sequencing (NGS) gene panel. Pathogenicity assessment of the variant was conducted following American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) criteria for interpreting sequence variants. This particular mutation was not found in existing literature, the VarSome database, or the ClinVar database. The patient provided written informed consent for publication of this report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment after elevated blood glucose and glycosuria were found during his routine annual check-up. Physical evaluation revealed a body mass index of 24.6 kg/m^2, blood pressure measuring 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse of 78 beats per minute. The examination did not reveal acanthosis nigricans. The family history was significant for diabetes diagnosed at a young age, with the maternal grandmother developing the condition in her 30s, the mother in her late 20s, and a younger brother at 20 years old. There was no reported family history of early-onset cardiovascular disease in relatives with diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, his 2-hour plasma glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. He exhibited no clinical signs of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient complained of polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid studies showed high-density lipoprotein at 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, which may be indicative of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). His prior medical and surgical history was unremarkable.

Glucagon stimulation testing demonstrated serum C-peptide levels of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, pointing toward reduced insulin production. This test assesses residual pancreatic β-cell function via measurement of C-peptide following glucagon administration. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide value above 0.60 nmol/L typically reflects retained endogenous insulin production, while values below this suggest impaired β-cell function. Still, the glucagon stimulation test should be viewed as a supplementary diagnostic tool, especially when autoantibody panels yield negative or equivocal results, or when the patient’s clinical features are unusual. No microalbuminuria was found. Sudomotor evaluation, carried out through electrochemical skin conductance testing (for example, with Sudoscan), confirmed markedly impaired sudomotor function in the hands, signifying severe autonomic disturbance. Laboratory analysis for autoantibodies, including GADA and IA-2A, returned negative. One month after the initial diagnosis, genetic analysis for MODY identified a pathogenic heterozygous variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), supporting the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The initial management plan included basal insulin degludec at eight units daily, alongside metformin at a daily dose of 1500 mg and saxagliptin 5 mg daily, based on an initial assessment consistent with T2D. This regimen was selected to facilitate prompt glycaemic stabilization and to address possible insulin resistance.

Beyond pharmacological intervention, recommendations were provided for the patient to follow a regimented dietary protocol and maintain routine physical exercise as integral elements of a multifaceted diabetes care plan. Serial follow-up visits were arranged at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient department to track glycaemic indices and evaluate response to the prescribed therapy.

Nevertheless, the detection of moderately retained but inadequate β-cell function on glucagon stimulation testing (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet cell autoantibodies, and a notable familial predisposition to early-onset diabetes raised significant suspicion for MODY.

Initial treatment was started prior to the availability of genetic testing results, based on the clinical presentation and laboratory findings at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. With confirmation of HNF1A-MODY through genetic testing and recognizing that the patient retained moderate, though insufficient, β-cell function, insulin was discontinued in light of the improvement in HbA1c to 9.1%. Dulaglutide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist, was initiated at 1.5 mg weekly to enhance glycaemic control and help maintain pancreatic function. Six months following this change, HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, reflecting marked glycaemic improvement. As the patient achieved stable glycaemic levels, the treatment regimen was streamlined to metformin (1500 mg/day) combined with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day) for ongoing maintenance. Regular follow-up continues, and the patient’s HbA1c has consistently remained below 6.5%. There have been no indications of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy thus far, with surveillance for chronic complications ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, situated on the q24 locus of chromosome 12, acts as a transcription factor. It contains 10 exons and extends over 23,790 base pairs, coding for a 631-amino-acid protein that is present in multiple organs, such as the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Genetic alterations in HNF1A result in a gradual loss of β-cell function, demonstrated by decreased expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes and diminished activity of pyruvate kinase. These changes ultimately cause a reduction in insulin output and impaired glucose tolerance.

In this case, diagnostic evaluation was based on established MODY criteria, integrating clinical presentation, laboratory data, and genetic evidence. The criteria include an early age at diabetes onset, notable family history, intolerance to postprandial glucose loads, and the absence of diabetes-specific autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide level above 0.6 ng/mL supports a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes. Nevertheless, regular monitoring of C-peptide remains important since individuals with new-onset type 1 diabetes may initially have C-peptide within the normal range. One of the main obstacles in identifying MODY is a low index of suspicion and limited clinician awareness; affected individuals are frequently misdiagnosed with type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator developed by the Exeter team can assist clinicians in selecting patients for molecular testing. It should be noted that this calculator was generated from data on a White European cohort and could yield inflated MODY probabilities in people of other ethnicities. Applying this tool to the presented patient, the calculated likelihood of MODY was 62.4% (or 1 in 1.6). There are reports describing the presence of autoantibodies in certain MODY cases; although autoantibody positivity occurs in some individuals harboring HNF1A mutations, most MODY cases lack autoantibodies. Previous literature also indicates that the prevalence of obesity and metabolic syndrome in MODY is lower than in type 2 diabetes; however, increasing obesity rates have reduced the discriminative value of BMI and metabolic syndrome for differentiating MODY from type 2 diabetes, potentially accounting for the elevated BMI in this patient. While a family history is often key in MODY, its diagnostic utility is diminished by factors such as incomplete reporting, occurrence of de novo mutations, and the widespread nature of type 2 diabetes among relatives.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on the patient’s age and HbA1c levels. Initial therapy typically consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release via their action on ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function diminishes, GLP-1 receptor agonists may represent a safer and more effective option compared to sulfonylureas, especially in patients with a tendency toward hypoglycaemia. In a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study, both liraglutide and glimepiride were found to significantly reduce fasting and postprandial plasma glucose in HNF1A-MODY patients, with no statistically significant difference between the therapies. Furthermore, the risk of mild hypoglycaemia was lower with GLP-1 receptor agonist use than with glimepiride. By employing genetic testing, insulin therapy was discontinued after glycaemic control was attained and the patient transitioned successfully to GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment, resulting in continued glucose control. This underscores the importance of genetic analysis for appropriate therapeutic decision-making. Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY occurs due to diminished SGLT2 expression, which is regulated by HNF1A transcriptional activity. According to one report, HNF1A-MODY patients may be at risk for severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis, possibly due to extensive inhibition of remaining SGLT-2 function. The potential adverse effects associated with SGLT-2 inhibitor use in HNF1A-MODY, including a heightened risk for dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, may outweigh any therapeutic gain. In this patient, a combination of liraglutide and metformin proved to be a safe and effective regimen, in agreement with previously reported data.

The application of precision medicine is particularly important in the context of MODY, given that individuals with HNF1A-MODY may not consistently fulfill all existing diagnostic standards, even when clinical guidelines are applied. At present, there are no adequate biomarkers capable of reliably distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes (T2D). Chen et al reported that within a cohort of 2,429 young Chinese adults presenting with early-onset T2D, MODY accounted for 1.5% of cases, with 0.74% carrying pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Similarly, Møller et al demonstrated that among a large, population-based sample of Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 individuals lacking any high-risk HLA haplotypes had mutations in the HNF1A gene. In a comparable fashion, a recent Russian population-based study screened 506 participants suspected of having hereditary, non-autoimmune diabetes—characterized by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, persistent C-peptide secretion, low insulin requirements during the first 5 years, and no evidence of severe obesity. Among these individuals, 46.4% harbored P or LP variants, with HNF1A being one of the most frequently involved genes. The use of DNA sequencing plays a pivotal role in substantiating clinical suspicion of MODY. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) technology enables the examination of extensive regions of patient DNA in a single test, making MODY screening more practical. Advancements in NGS have led to the identification of numerous novel genetic variants. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) has established criteria for determining whether these variants are P. Databases such as ClinVar have documented over 1,000 unique HNF1A variants, including P, LP, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In this report, the previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not present in ClinVar or other widely used databases, underscoring its novelty rather than casting doubt on its potential clinical impact. Significantly, this variant involves an amino acid residue directly adjacent to other known P residues, has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and segregates with diabetes within the family, which satisfies ACMG standards for classification as LP. Therefore, while ClinVar provides a valuable archive of known variants, the pathogenicity of this variant is reinforced by multiple independent genetic and clinical criteria beyond database documentation.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas mutations in the transactivation domain are less common. The novel variant described in this case is positioned in the DNA-binding domain. Certain reports indicate that HNF1A mutations, while P, have limited influence on the functionality of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, some HNF1A variants that are insufficient to independently cause MODY may nonetheless elevate the risk of developing T2D.

To summarize, this case underscores the significance of correctly diagnosing HNF1A-MODY and its implications for personalized therapy. Upcoming studies will aim to establish more robust NGS-based screening approaches in order to enhance the detection rate of MODY, resulting in improved resource utilization and clinical outcomes.
