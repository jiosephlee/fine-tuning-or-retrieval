Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Overview: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents an autosomal dominant, single-gene form of diabetes. Here, we present a patient diagnosed with hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A)-MODY, attributed to a previously undescribed heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose and glycosuria but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. The family’s medical history revealed diabetes manifesting at an early age. Subsequent genetic analysis identified a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist combined with metformin resulted in a reduction of glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This report underscores the critical role of genetic testing in the diagnosis of early-onset diabetes—commonly mistaken for type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and contributes to the growing body of knowledge regarding HNF1A mutations that inform precision treatment approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited diabetes syndromes that are non-autoimmune in nature, with the underlying cause being an intrinsic abnormality in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These syndromes are generally identified before individuals reach 25 years of age. MODY accounts for approximately 1%–6% of diabetes cases with onset at a young age. Prevalence rates for MODY differ among distinct ethnic and racial populations and are likely to be underestimated in some groups. Research efforts have predominantly centered on White European cohorts, though there has been a recent shift toward examining MODY prevalence in Asian and Australian populations. Reported prevalence rates for MODY exhibit considerable variability, largely because genetic testing is frequently confined to those displaying specific clinical features, even in population-based studies.

More than 15 distinct genes have been implicated in the pathogenesis of the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with each genetic defect necessitating tailored treatment approaches. Among the various subtypes of MODY, mutations in hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β account for most cases identified. HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent form, representing approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses, and exhibits a well-defined autosomal dominant inheritance pattern for symptomatic diabetes within affected families. During early adolescence, individuals are often asymptomatic, with hyperglycemia frequently detected incidentally through routine blood analyses and typically in the absence of clinical signs suggestive of insulin resistance. Elevated postprandial blood glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L) may be uncovered via post-meal glucose checks or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Moreover, glycosuria may be present, a consequence of a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption owing to impaired tubular glucose transport. In more advanced stages, individuals may display classical diabetic symptoms—such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss—that parallel those of type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). This symptomatic overlap renders the distinction between MODY, type 1, and T2D a persistent diagnostic challenge.

Several studies have established a variety of diagnostic algorithms for MODY. Once a clinical suspicion of MODY arises from a detailed physical examination and assessment of familial diabetes history, and autoimmune markers such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) autoantibodies are found to be negative, additional biochemical tests are used to support the diagnosis. In this context, molecular genetic testing is instrumental in confirming MODY. Achieving an accurate diagnosis is vital, as it guides therapeutic decision-making, allows for prediction of disease progression and complications, informs prognosis, and provides a basis for genetic counseling of affected families. Recent investigations have identified hundreds of distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene as causative factors for HNF1A-MODY.

In the current report, we describe the identification of a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY. This genetic alteration was detected by applying a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel, and its pathogenicity was evaluated in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) standards for variant classification. This specific variant has not been documented in the literature, nor is it present in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. Written informed consent was directly obtained from the patient prior to the publication of this case study.

Case presentation:

A young adult was assessed following the discovery of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria at his routine annual health check. His physical assessment revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, blood pressure measured at 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. Physical findings did not include acanthosis nigricans. The patient's family history indicated multiple relatives with diabetes of early onset: the maternal grandmother developed the condition in her 30s, the mother was diagnosed in her late 20s, and the younger brother received a diagnosis at age 20. There was no reported history of premature cardiovascular disease in those family members with diabetes.

Investigations:

His glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting glucose was elevated at 12.4 mmol/L. Two hours after a 75 g OGTT, his plasma glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. Clinically, the patient reported polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid analysis showed high-density lipoprotein at 1.01 mmol/L and triglyceride levels of 4.31 mmol/L, pointing toward the likelihood of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). The patient’s medical and surgical histories were unremarkable.

A glucagon stimulation test revealed a baseline serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L, increasing to 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, signifying suboptimal insulin secretion. This procedure gauges the function of residual pancreatic β-cells by assessing C-peptide response following glucagon administration. In routine practice, a stimulated C-peptide level above 0.60 nmol/L is interpreted as sufficient endogenous insulin production; values beneath this cutoff reflect impaired β-cell activity. Nevertheless, the glucagon stimulation test should primarily provide supportive information, especially when autoantibody studies yield ambiguous results or the phenotype does not fit typical patterns. Urinalysis showed no microalbuminuria. Electrochemical skin conductance testing (for instance, via Sudoscan) indicated markedly diminished sudomotor activity in the hands, consistent with pronounced autonomic neuropathy. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) returned negative. Genetic testing for MODY performed a month after diagnosis uncovered a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose values. The treatment initiated consisted of basal insulin degludec (eight units per day), combined with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), in line with an initial working diagnosis of T2D. The therapeutic goal was to achieve prompt glycaemic stabilization while managing presumed insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient received recommendations to follow a regimented diet and maintain consistent physical exercise, forming a key part of an integrated diabetes care plan. Ongoing follow-up appointments were held at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to track glycaemic parameters and evaluate treatment efficacy.

Nevertheless, the combination of modest but inadequate β-cell functional reserve demonstrated on the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a notable family history of early-onset diabetes raised considerable suspicion for MODY.

Therapy was initiated based on clinical judgement and presenting laboratory data, as genetic testing results were not yet available at the outset of treatment.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c was reduced to 9.1%. With the genetic diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY confirmed and taking into account the patient’s partially retained, though still inadequate, β-cell function as well as the improvement in HbA1c, insulin therapy was discontinued. In its place, dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg administered weekly) was initiated to aid in glycaemic management and safeguard residual pancreatic function. Following 6 months on this regimen, HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, reflecting a marked enhancement in glycaemic control. As stability was achieved, treatment was streamlined to include metformin (1500 mg/day) and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day) for ongoing maintenance. The patient continues to receive routine monitoring, maintaining HbA1c consistently below 6.5%. To this point, there is no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and systematic screening for long-term diabetes-related complications is being maintained.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor mapped to the q24 locus on chromosome 12. It consists of 10 exons covering 23,790 base pairs and produces a 631–amino acid protein, which is found in multiple organs, such as the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Pathogenic variants in HNF1A are associated with a gradual loss of β-cell functionality, primarily through diminished expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes and lowered pyruvate kinase activity. This series of molecular disruptions leads to reduced insulin output and the onset of glucose intolerance.

The evaluation in this instance followed contemporary diagnostic protocols for MODY, integrating clinical, laboratory, and molecular evidence. Key criteria included an early age at diabetes diagnosis, a pronounced familial clustering, postprandial glucose intolerance, and negative diabetes-specific autoantibodies. Retention of fasting C-peptide levels above 0.6 ng/mL supports a diagnosis of diabetes types other than type 1. Nevertheless, continued C-peptide surveillance is warranted, as type 1 diabetes can present with normal levels in its early phase. One major diagnostic hurdle for MODY remains limited recognition and suspicion among clinicians, often leading to its confusion with type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The Exeter group’s MODY probability calculator offers a helpful strategy for selecting patients for molecular testing, although it is constructed from data on White European cohorts and may produce an inflated risk estimate for non-White populations. For our patient, inputting the clinical details into this calculator yielded a MODY probability of 62.4% (equivalent to a 1 in 1.6 chance). While several published reports have noted the presence of autoantibodies in certain individuals with MODY, the majority—particularly those with HNF1A mutations—are negative for autoantibodies. Historically, rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome have been reported to be lower in MODY patients compared to those with type 2 diabetes. However, as global obesity trends rise, the diagnostic utility of BMI and metabolic syndrome in distinguishing MODY from T2DM has diminished, potentially accounting for the increased BMI observed in our patient. Furthermore, although familial history remains a cornerstone of MODY assessment, its sensitivity is limited by incomplete reporting, the emergence of de novo mutations, and the common occurrence of type 2 diabetes within families.

Therapeutic strategies for HNF1A-MODY are guided by patient age and HbA1c measurements. The primary approach typically consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin release through modulation of ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As the function of β-cells wanes—especially in patients susceptible to hypoglycaemia—GLP-1 receptor agonists can provide a safer and more efficacious option than sulfonylureas. Evidence from a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study indicates that liraglutide and glimepiride are comparably effective in lowering fasting plasma glucose and mitigating postprandial glycaemic variability among individuals with HNF1A-MODY. Moreover, GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a decreased incidence of mild hypoglycaemic episodes relative to glimepiride. Following a genetic diagnosis, insulin therapy was withdrawn after satisfactory glycaemic control was achieved, and the patient was successfully managed with a GLP-1 receptor agonist, ensuring ongoing glycaemic stability. This case underscores the importance of genetic testing for optimizing therapeutic choices. The hallmark glycosuria observed in HNF1A-MODY arises from diminished SGLT2 expression regulated by HNF1A at the transcriptional level. Literature also suggests that individuals with HNF1A-MODY are at risk for pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis when residual SGLT2 activity is intensely inhibited. Consequently, the disadvantages associated with SGLT2 inhibitor use in this population—including heightened risks of dehydration, possible vascular instability, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—are likely to surpass any therapeutic advantages. The combined use of liraglutide and metformin in this patient proved both safe and effective, consistent with outcomes previously reported in the literature.

Precision medicine is of paramount importance in the context of MODY, given that individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all established diagnostic criteria despite the availability of detailed clinical recommendations. Presently, there are no reliable biomarkers capable of distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from both type 1 and T2D. Chen et al reported that, within a cohort of 2429 young Chinese adults presenting with early-onset T2D, the incidence of MODY was 1.5%, with 0.74% of participants harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY mutations. In another investigation, Møller et al demonstrated that among Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of the 39 individuals lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes possessed mutations in HNF1A. Likewise, a recent Russian population study involved screening 506 patients suspected of having hereditary diabetes forms—criteria included negativity for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, retained C-peptide levels, reduced insulin needs within five years, and no evidence of severe obesity. This screening identified P or LP variants in 46.4% of cases, with HNF1A as one of the predominant genes implicated. Genetic confirmation of MODY requires DNA sequencing, and next-generation sequencing (NGS) has enhanced the efficiency of such diagnostic efforts by enabling broad-scale analysis of patient DNA in a single assay. Advances in NGS technology have facilitated the discovery of numerous previously unknown variants. The ACMG has published criteria for designating the pathogenicity status of these variants. To date, more than 1000 unique HNF1A variants—encompassing P, LP, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance—are catalogued in resources such as ClinVar. In the present study, we identified a novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) that is absent from ClinVar and other common variant repositories, indicating its uniqueness rather than diminishing its potential clinical importance. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid next to established P residues, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense probability (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes in affected family members, thus satisfying the ACMG’s standards for classification as LP. Therefore, while ClinVar serves as a comprehensive compendium of existing variants, multiple independent genetic and clinical data support the pathogenicity of this novel variant, extending beyond reliance on database annotation alone.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain experiences comparatively fewer mutations. The novel variant described in this report is also localized to the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have found that some HNF1A mutations, although present, exert limited influence on HNF1A protein functionality. Moreover, specific HNF1A variants, which by themselves do not lead to MODY, may nonetheless confer an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

To summarize, this case emphasizes the critical role of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY in guiding personalized therapeutic decisions. Ongoing studies aim to enhance next-generation sequencing (NGS) methodologies for MODY detection, thereby optimizing both cost-effectiveness and clinical outcomes.
