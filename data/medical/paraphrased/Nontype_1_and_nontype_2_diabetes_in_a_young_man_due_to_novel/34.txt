Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is an autosomal dominant, single-gene diabetes subtype. This case report details an individual with HNF1A-MODY associated with a previously undescribed heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose and glucose in the urine, without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history included early diabetes onset. Genetic analysis identified a novel pathogenic HNF1A mutation. Initiation of therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist together with metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over 3 months. This report underscores the significance of genetic evaluation in patients with young-onset diabetes, commonly mistaken for type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and adds a new HNF1A mutation to those applicable to personalized therapy approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes marked by an underlying defect in pancreatic β-cell maturation and function. These disorders are usually apparent and diagnosed before 25 years of age. MODY accounts for approximately 1%–6% of cases of early-onset diabetes. The prevalence of MODY differs across distinct racial and ethnic populations, and rates may be underreported. Most investigations to date have concentrated on White European groups, although recent research has begun to explore MODY frequency in Asian and Australian populations. Estimates regarding MODY prevalence display considerable variability, which often results from the restriction of genetic testing to patients identified by clinical criteria, even in population-based studies.

Over fifteen gene mutations have been associated with the monogenic MODY phenotype, with each variant requiring distinct approaches to management. Most MODY cases are attributed to mutations in genes such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. HNF1A-MODY is the most common form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of MODY cases, and demonstrates a recognizable autosomal dominant inheritance pattern with the occurrence of symptomatic diabetes across affected families. During early adolescence, clinical manifestations may be absent, and hyperglycemia is often incidentally discovered during routine laboratory assessments, typically without evidence of insulin resistance. Postprandial glucose measurement or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal significant postprandial hyperglycemia (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be observed and is explained by a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired glucose transport in the renal tubules. As the disease progresses, classic symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintended weight loss—similar to those seen in type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may develop. As a result, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D poses a diagnostic challenge because of the considerable overlap in clinical presentation.

Various diagnostic frameworks for MODY have been outlined in published studies. If physical examination and family history point toward MODY, and type 1 diabetes-associated autoantibodies such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) are absent, alongside additional supporting biochemical analyses, molecular genetic testing is pivotal in confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification is essential for selecting appropriate therapies, predicting complications, evaluating prognosis, and guiding family counseling. Recent investigations have demonstrated that several hundred distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene lead to HNF1A-MODY.

In the present case, a previously unreported heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), was detected in exon 4 of HNF1A in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY, using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel. The pathogenicity of this variant was assessed following the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) for variant interpretation. This particular mutation is absent from the literature, VarSome, and ClinVar databases. Written informed consent for publication of the case report was obtained from the patient.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment due to findings of elevated blood glucose and glucose in the urine detected at his routine checkup. Physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m^2, a blood pressure reading of 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse of 78 beats per minute. No signs of acanthosis nigricans were evident. The family history included cases of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. No family members with diabetes had a documented history of premature cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

His HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting blood glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, the 2-hour glucose value reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described polydipsia, polyphagia, and a recent decrease in weight. Lipid testing identified a high-density lipoprotein concentration of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, raising suspicion for metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He denied any notable prior medical or surgical problems.

A glucagon stimulation test yielded a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, indicating inadequate insulin secretion. This procedure measures stimulated C-peptide after glucagon administration to assess remaining β-cell activity. In practice, stimulated C-peptide >0.60 nmol/L implies retained endogenous insulin production; values below this threshold reflect β-cell impairment. Nonetheless, this test serves as an adjunctive assessment, not definitive, particularly when autoantibody assays are inconclusive or the patient’s presentation is atypical. Microalbuminuria screening was negative. Sudomotor assessment with electrochemical skin conductance testing (such as Sudoscan) disclosed severely impaired sudomotor function in the hands, reflecting marked autonomic involvement. Autoantibody evaluation for GADA and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) returned negative results. One month following diagnosis, MODY genetic screening detected a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), confirming HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased levels of HbA1c and plasma glucose. The initial therapeutic approach included basal insulin degludec at a dosage of eight units per day, together with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), consistent with an early consideration of T2D. This combination was selected to facilitate prompt glycaemic stabilization and to address possible insulin resistance.

Alongside medication, the patient received recommendations to follow a regimented dietary protocol and maintain routine physical exercise, as components of an integrated strategy for diabetes management. Subsequent assessments took place at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient service, aiming to track glycaemic outcomes and evaluate the effectiveness of the chosen therapy.

Nevertheless, the patient’s glucagon stimulation test indicated moderate preservation of β-cell function, though insufficient (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), with absence of islet autoantibodies and a notable familial incidence of early-onset diabetes, heightening the likelihood of MODY.

The initial therapeutic regimen was implemented before the availability of genetic testing, relying on clinical findings and laboratory parameters present at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month afterwards, the patient’s HbA1c had fallen to 9.1%. After genetic testing confirmed HNF1A-MODY and considering the patient's moderately preserved, though inadequate, β-cell activity along with the HbA1c improvement, insulin treatment was stopped and dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg weekly) was initiated to enhance glycaemic management and maintain pancreatic function. Six months later, HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, demonstrating notable progress. As glycaemic stability was achieved, therapy was streamlined to metformin (1500 mg/day) combined with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day) as a long-term maintenance approach. The patient continues with regular monitoring, sustaining HbA1c levels consistently below 6.5%. So far, there is no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, with ongoing monitoring for potential chronic complications.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, a transcription factor situated at chromosome 12q24, contains 10 exons within a region spanning 23,790 base pairs, coding for a 631-amino acid protein that is expressed in organs such as the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Mutations affecting HNF1A cause a gradual reduction in β-cell activity, characterized by diminished expression of genes such as insulin and GLUT2, along with lowered pyruvate kinase function. The consequence is reduced insulin release and impaired glucose tolerance.

The approach to diagnosis in this instance followed established MODY criteria, combining clinical, biochemical, and genetic data, including features such as early onset diabetes, prominent family history, postprandial glucose intolerance, and absence of autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide greater than 0.6 ng/mL supports the classification of non-type 1 diabetes, though ongoing monitoring of C-peptide remains necessary, as normal readings can occur early in type 1 diabetes. An important obstacle in identifying MODY is inadequate awareness and clinical suspicion, as affected individuals are frequently incorrectly diagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetic. The MODY probability calculator created by the Exeter group offers assistance for determining which patients should undergo genetic screening; however, this tool was validated using a White European cohort and may overpredict MODY likelihood in people of other ancestries. Applying our patient’s data to this calculator yielded a 62.4% probability (1 in 1.6 likelihood) of MODY. There are documented instances of MODY patients possessing autoantibodies; although most MODY cases, especially with HNF1A variants, do not. Previous research indicates MODY individuals exhibit lower prevalence of obesity and metabolic syndrome than those with T2D. With increasing obesity rates, the capacity to differentiate MODY from T2DM via BMI or metabolic syndrome has diminished, possibly contributing to the elevated BMI in our patient. Furthermore, despite its significance for MODY assessment, family history can be a less reliable indicator due to factors such as under-reporting, de novo mutations, and frequent T2DM among relatives.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by patient age and HbA1c measurements. Initial therapy typically involves low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin release via activation of ATP-sensitive potassium channels. In individuals with HNF1A-MODY, as β-cell function diminishes, GLP-1 receptor agonists can provide a safer and potentially more effective alternative to sulfonylureas, especially in patients susceptible to hypoglycemia. A small randomized, double-blind, crossover study showed that both liraglutide and glimepiride significantly lowered fasting plasma glucose and minimized postprandial glycemic variability, with no notable differences in efficacy between the two treatments for HNF1A-MODY. Moreover, GLP-1 receptor agonists were associated with fewer episodes of mild hypoglycemia than glimepiride. Genetic confirmation enabled the discontinuation of insulin therapy upon attaining glucose control, allowing the patient to transition to GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment, which continued to maintain appropriate glycemic regulation. This underscores the importance of genetic testing in therapeutic planning. Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY results from decreased SGLT2 expression, as this transporter is regulated by HNF1A. One report noted that patients with HNF1A-MODY may develop significant dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to marked inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. Thus, the adverse effects of SGLT-2 inhibitors in this population, including heightened risk of dehydration, potential vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, may exceed their possible therapeutic advantages. In the present case, therapy with liraglutide in combination with metformin proved to be both effective and safe, which is consistent with findings from previous research.

Precision medicine is fundamental in the management of MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all established diagnostic criteria, despite the presence of current clinical protocols. At this time, no reliable biomarkers exist to clearly distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or T2D. Chen et al conducted a study involving 2,429 young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D, finding MODY in 1.5% of cases and identifying 0.74% with pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al further demonstrated that in a broad population sample of Danish Caucasian patients diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 patients lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes possessed mutations within the HNF1A gene. Likewise, a recent Russian population-based investigation screened 506 people suspected to have hereditary diabetes without autoimmune markers—characterized by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at onset, preserved C-peptide, minimal insulin use within 5 years, and no severe obesity. This research detected P or LP variants in 46.4% of participants, with HNF1A being among the most commonly mutated genes. DNA sequencing remains the definitive method for confirming suspicions of MODY. NGS technology facilitates extensive sequencing of patient DNA in a single assay, thereby enhancing the practicality of MODY detection. NGS has led to the identification of numerous new genetic variants, and the ACMG has published recommendations to assess the pathogenicity of these findings. There are now more than 1,000 distinct HNF1A variants recorded in repositories such as ClinVar, spanning pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In the present case, the novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not found in ClinVar nor other widely referenced variant databases, underscoring its originality rather than undermining its clinical impact. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid near known pathogenic residues, has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and is found in multiple family members affected by diabetes—all criteria consistent with an LP classification according to ACMG standards. Therefore, although ClinVar provides a robust repository for previously described variants, multiple genetic and clinical factors independently affirm the pathogenicity of this variant beyond database documentation.

Research has shown that most of the mutations identified are concentrated in the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is also found within the DNA-binding domain. Certain studies indicate that HNF1A mutations, including some classified as P, have limited impact on the functional activity of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, particular HNF1A variants that are insufficient on their own to induce MODY may still confer an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

To summarize, this case underscores the necessity of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its consequences for individualized therapeutic management. Future research will aim to establish more robust NGS-based screening protocols in order to enhance the diagnostic yield for MODY, which may allow for optimized healthcare expenditures and improved patient outcomes.
