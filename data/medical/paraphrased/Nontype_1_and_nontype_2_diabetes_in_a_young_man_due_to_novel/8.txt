Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents a monogenic, autosomal dominant form of diabetes. This case report details a patient diagnosed with hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A)-MODY, attributed to a newly identified heterozygous mutation in HNF1A. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose levels and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. A significant family history of diabetes with early onset was noted. Genetic analysis revealed a novel, pathogenic variant in HNF1A not previously documented. Initiation of therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist in combination with metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a three-month period. This case underlines the necessity of genetic evaluation in patients with early-onset diabetes—conditions often mistakenly classified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and contributes to the growing knowledge of HNF1A mutations relevant to individualized therapeutic strategies.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes, which arise from intrinsic defects in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders commonly appear and are identified prior to age 25. MODY is responsible for about 1%–6% of diabetes cases in youth. The prevalence of MODY differs across diverse racial and ethnic populations, and it is possible that these differences have been underestimated. Historically, investigations have primarily focused on White European cohorts. More recently, research attention has shifted towards understanding the prevalence of MODY in Asian and Australian populations. Reported prevalence rates for MODY exhibit wide variability. This disparity is frequently attributable to the fact that genetic testing tends to be confined to those individuals who meet specific clinical criteria, even in population-based research.

More than 15 genes have been implicated in the development of the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with each gene alteration necessitating a tailored therapeutic approach. The majority of documented MODY cases are predominantly associated with mutations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. Among these, HNF1A-MODY is the most common form, representing approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses and displaying a distinct pattern of autosomal dominant inheritance characterized by symptomatic diabetes occurring across generations. In many adolescents, the condition can be asymptomatic, and hyperglycemia is often discovered incidentally during standard blood screening, usually in the absence of signs indicating insulin resistance. Diagnostic methods such as postprandial blood glucose assessment or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) often reveal significant postprandial hyperglycemia (≥11.1 mmol/L). Moreover, glycosuria may also be present, which is due to a diminished renal threshold for glucose as a consequence of impaired tubular glucose reabsorption. As the disease progresses, clinical manifestations such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintended weight loss—symptoms also typical of type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may arise. Consequently, accurately distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D can be challenging, given their shared clinical presentations.

Multiple diagnostic strategies for MODY have been outlined in recent literature. Once physical assessment and an evaluation of family medical history indicate a MODY phenotype—coupled with the absence of autoantibodies commonly linked to type 1 diabetes, such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—and with corroborative results from additional biochemical tests, molecular genetic analysis becomes integral to establishing a diagnosis. Accurate identification of MODY subtypes is essential for selecting an appropriate therapeutic strategy, anticipating possible disease outcomes, prognostication, and providing family genetic counselling. Recent research has shown that HNF1A-MODY is attributable to several hundred unique mutations in the HNF1A gene.

Within this report, we describe the discovery of a previously undocumented heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), in exon 4 of HNF1A in an individual diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY, utilizing next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel analysis. Assessment of the mutation's pathogenicity followed the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) variant interpretation criteria. Notably, this variant has not been referenced in prior literature, nor is it listed in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case.

Case presentation:

A young adult presented following the detection of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria at a routine health check-up. Physical assessment revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure of 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse of 78 beats per minute. No acanthosis nigricans was detected on examination. The family history indicated multiple cases of early-onset diabetes, including the maternal grandmother (diagnosed in her 30s), the mother (late 20s), and a younger brother (at age 20). There was no reported incidence of early-onset cardiovascular disease among relatives affected by diabetes.

Investigations:

The patient's glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and his fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, the 2-hour post-load glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. He reported polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent involuntary weight loss. Lipid assessment showed a high-density lipoprotein concentration of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings which are compatible with the presence of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). His past medical and surgical histories were unremarkable.

The glucagon stimulation test revealed serum C-peptide levels of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes post-stimulation, suggesting impaired insulin production. This test, which assesses the residual capacity of β-cells by quantifying C-peptide after glucagon administration, is used in clinical settings to infer endogenous insulin reserve; a value exceeding 0.60 nmol/L following stimulation usually points to sufficient β-cell activity, while lower levels indicate compromised function. However, the diagnostic utility of the glucagon stimulation test is considered supportive, especially in situations where autoimmune markers are negative or clinical features do not conform to classic disease categories. No microalbuminuria was observed. Evaluation of autonomic function via electrochemical skin conductance (for instance, with Sudoscan) demonstrated markedly decreased sudomotor function in the hands, reflecting significant small fibre autonomic neuropathy. Both glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) tested negative. One month following the initial diagnosis, genetic analysis for MODY was conducted and identified a heterozygous, pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), which is diagnostic for HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The therapeutic approach initiated included a daily dose of basal insulin degludec (eight units), along with metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), reflecting an initial assessment suggestive of T2D. The objective of this regimen was to promptly control hyperglycaemia and address possible insulin resistance.

Besides medication, recommendations included strict adherence to a dietary regimen and consistent physical exercise as integral components of holistic diabetes care. Subsequent reviews took place at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to track glycaemic parameters and evaluate the efficacy of therapy.

Nevertheless, the combination of moderately retained, though inadequate, β-cell reserve on glucagon stimulation testing (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), the absence of islet autoantibodies, and a significant familial occurrence of early-onset diabetes, raised considerable clinical suspicion for MODY.

Therapeutic intervention was commenced prior to receipt of genetic analysis results, informed by the patient’s initial clinical and laboratory data.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c had reduced to 9.1%. In light of the genetic diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY, the patient’s partially retained but inadequate β-cell function, and the improved HbA1c, insulin therapy was discontinued. The patient was instead initiated on dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg weekly) to assist with glycaemic regulation and preservation of pancreatic function. Six months into this treatment, HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, reflecting marked glycaemic improvement. With stable glycaemic control achieved, the therapeutic approach was then transitioned to a simplified maintenance regimen comprising metformin (1500 mg/day) in conjunction with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day). The patient continues to attend regular follow-up appointments, maintaining an HbA1c consistently below 6.5%. Thus far, there has been no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and continued monitoring for potential long-term complications remains in place.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene codes for a transcription factor found in the q24 region of chromosome 12. This gene consists of 10 exons distributed across 23,790 base pairs and produces a 631-amino-acid protein, which is present in a variety of tissues such as the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Mutations in the HNF1A gene are associated with progressive deterioration in β-cell function, which is evident through deficient expression of the insulin and GLUT2 genes and diminished pyruvate kinase enzyme activity. These molecular defects eventually contribute to reduced insulin secretion and subsequent glucose intolerance.

The diagnosis in this instance followed established MODY diagnostic guidelines, which incorporate a combination of clinical, biochemical, and genetic evidence, such as diabetes onset at an early age, a notable familial clustering, postprandial hyperglycemia, and absence of diabetes-related autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide concentration above 0.6 ng/mL favors a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes. Nevertheless, it is important to track C-peptide levels over time since initially normal levels can be found in early type 1 diabetes. Lack of clinical suspicion and awareness remains a significant obstacle to accurate MODY diagnosis, as patients are frequently mistaken for having type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The Exeter group’s MODY probability calculator can assist clinicians in determining candidates for genetic analysis, though it was originally validated in White European cohorts and may produce inflated MODY probability estimates for non-White individuals. Application of this tool to our patient's clinical data yielded a 62.4% probability (or a 1 in 1.6 chance) of MODY. There are also documented cases in which individuals with MODY, including those carrying HNF1A mutations, have tested positive for autoantibodies; nevertheless, most people with MODY do not exhibit autoantibodies. Previous research suggests that MODY patients generally display lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to those with type 2 diabetes. However, with increasing prevalence of obesity, relying on BMI or metabolic syndrome for differentiation between MODY and T2DM has become less reliable, which may account for the elevated BMI seen in our patient. Moreover, while family history remains a key diagnostic element for MODY, its diagnostic utility can be compromised due to factors such as incomplete family records, the occurrence of de novo mutations, or the widespread nature of type 2 diabetes within families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by patient age and HbA1c measurements. The initial therapeutic approach typically utilizes low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function deteriorates, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a preferable and safer substitute, especially for patients susceptible to hypoglycaemic episodes. Evidence from a small, randomized, double-blind, crossover study has shown that both liraglutide and glimepiride substantially lower fasting plasma glucose and attenuate postprandial glucose variations in HNF1A-MODY patients, with no significant efficacy differences observed between the two agents. Notably, GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycaemia when compared to glimepiride. Through molecular genetic diagnosis, insulin therapy was discontinued once glycaemic control was attained, and the patient was transitioned to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, which successfully maintained glycaemic stability. This underscores the pivotal importance of genetic testing in tailoring individualized therapeutic strategies. Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY arises from decreased SGLT2 expression, regulated at the transcriptional level by HNF1A. There are reports indicating that HNF1A-MODY patients may face serious dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to significant inhibition of any remaining SGLT-2 activity. The application of SGLT-2 inhibitors in individuals with HNF1A-MODY is associated with heightened risks—including dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—which may outweigh their intended therapeutic advantages. In the present case, the combination of liraglutide and metformin has proven to be both efficacious and safe, corroborating prior research findings.

Personalized therapeutic approaches are particularly important in managing MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all standard diagnostic criteria, despite the presence of clinical guidelines. Currently, the absence of reliable biomarkers complicates the distinction of HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes (T2D). Chen et al reported that among 2,429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D, the occurrence of MODY was 1.5%, and 0.74% possessed P or likely pathogenic (LP) variants related to MODY. In work by Møller et al, it was observed that 10% of 39 Danish Caucasian individuals with type 1 diabetes lacking any high-risk HLA haplotypes carried mutations in the HNF1A gene. Correspondingly, a recent population-based investigation in Russia examined 506 people suspected of hereditary diabetes, characterized by negative results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at onset, preserved C-peptide production, low insulin needs within five years of diagnosis, and absence of severe obesity. This study found that 46.4% harbored P or LP variants, with the HNF1A gene frequently implicated. Genetic confirmation of MODY suspicion relies on DNA sequencing, with next-generation sequencing (NGS) enabling high-throughput analysis of patient DNA and thereby enhancing the practicality of MODY genetic screening. The adoption of NGS has facilitated the discovery of numerous novel genetic alterations. The ACMG has issued recommendations for assessing the pathogenicity of these variants. Databases such as ClinVar have now catalogued over 1000 unique HNF1A variants, comprising pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In our report, we identified a previously unlisted heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S), which was not present in ClinVar or other widely utilized variant repositories, emphasizing its novelty rather than challenging its possible clinical significance. Notably, this alteration impacts an amino acid immediately adjacent to residues already established as pathogenic, demonstrates a substantial AlphaMissense score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes within the studied family. These features collectively satisfy the ACMG criteria for designation as likely pathogenic (LP). Hence, although ClinVar remains a valuable compendium of reported variants, assessment of pathogenicity for this variant is underpinned by converging genetic and clinical data beyond database documentation.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the mutation frequency is lower in the transactivation domain. The novel variant described in this report is also located in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have revealed that HNF1A mutations, including those classified as P, may exert only limited influence on the functional capacity of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants that are insufficient to cause MODY independently may nonetheless be linked to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the critical role of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its implications for personalized therapy. Ongoing studies will aim to enhance the efficacy of NGS-based screening methods to raise MODY detection rates, enabling improved cost-effectiveness and greater clinical advantage.
