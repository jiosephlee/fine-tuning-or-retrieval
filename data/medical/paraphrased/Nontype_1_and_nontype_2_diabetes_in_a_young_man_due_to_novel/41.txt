Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic, autosomal dominant form of diabetes. This report presents a case of HNF1A-MODY associated with a novel heterozygous mutation in the hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A) gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient's family history included early-onset diabetes. Genetic analysis identified a pathogenic HNF1A variant not previously documented. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist along with metformin lowered the patient's glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3 month period. The case underscores the critical role of genetic testing in early-onset diabetes, which is often misclassified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and broadens the range of known HNF1A mutations with relevance to personalized medicine.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetes syndromes marked by an intrinsic defect in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders are most often detected and diagnosed before the age of 25 years. MODY is responsible for roughly 1%–6% of diabetes diagnosed in younger individuals. Prevalence rates can differ among diverse ethnic and racial groups, with the possibility of significant underdiagnosis. Most investigations thus far have predominantly focused on White European cohorts. More recent research has evaluated MODY frequency in Asian and Australian populations. Prevalence figures reported in the literature show considerable variability, largely attributable to the practice of restricting genetic testing to people who exhibit certain clinical features, even in population-based studies.

Over fifteen genetic mutations have been associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with each gene implicating distinct management approaches. Most instances across the MODY subtypes are linked to pathogenic variants in hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β genes. 

HNF1A-MODY accounts for the largest proportion of MODY, comprising approximately 30%–65% of all cases, and demonstrates a discernible autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of symptomatic diabetes within affected families. During early adolescence, clinical symptoms may be absent, and increased blood glucose concentrations are often incidentally uncovered through standard laboratory screening, usually without physical signs suggestive of insulin resistance. Postprandial glucose measurement or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may uncover marked elevations in glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria can also occur, attributed to a lower renal threshold for glucose reabsorption secondary to impaired glucose transport in the renal tubules. As the disease progresses, classical features such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight loss—characteristics shared with both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may manifest. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 diabetes or T2D remains challenging because of their overlapping clinical presentations.

Various diagnostic protocols for MODY have been outlined in existing research. When clinical evaluation and family history review point to a MODY phenotype, and autoantibodies common in type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, together with additional biochemical assessments, molecular genetic testing becomes highly relevant for diagnostic confirmation. Accurate identification is essential for tailoring therapy, anticipating complications, predicting disease outcome, and supporting family guidance. Recent investigations have identified numerous distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene responsible for HNF1A-MODY.

In the case detailed herein, we discovered a previously unreported heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), within exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY, using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel and assessed its pathogenicity per American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) variant interpretation criteria. This variant has not been described in published reports, the VarSome database, or the ClinVar database. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment due to findings of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria identified at his yearly health screening. On physical examination, his body mass index measured 24.6 kg/m2, his blood pressure was recorded at 112/70 mmHg, and his heart rate was 78 beats per minute. No signs of acanthosis nigricans were detected. The family history revealed several instances of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother developed diabetes in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother was diagnosed at age 20. There was no reported history of early-onset cardiovascular disease among those family members with diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting plasma glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, the 2-hour glucose value reached 19.8 mmol/L. There were no clinical indications of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described experiencing polydipsia, increased appetite, and unintentional recent weight loss. Lipid panel findings included a high-density lipoprotein concentration of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L, raising suspicion for a possible metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He did not report any notable medical or surgical conditions in his history.

A glucagon stimulation test produced a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L six minutes post-administration, reflecting inadequate insulin output. This procedure measures β-cell reserve by determining C-peptide after glucagon injection. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide exceeding 0.60 nmol/L points toward maintained endogenous insulin production, whereas lesser values suggest β-cell insufficiency. Nonetheless, the glucagon stimulation test serves as an adjunctive, rather than conclusive, diagnostic tool, particularly in scenarios with atypical presentation or inconclusive autoantibody findings. Screening for microalbuminuria was negative. Evaluation of autonomic nerve function with electrochemical skin conductance (such as Sudoscan) showed severely diminished sudomotor activity in both hands, signifying notable autonomic impairment. Autoantibody assessment, including both GADA and IA-2A, was negative. At one month post-diagnosis, genetic analysis for MODY was performed, identifying a heterozygous, pathogenic (P) HNF1A mutation (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), confirming HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly raised HbA1c and plasma glucose values. The initial management included basal insulin degludec at a dose of eight units per day, combined with metformin 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin 5 mg daily, consistent with an early consideration of T2D. This approach was intended to rapidly establish glycaemic control and address possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, recommendations included following a structured meal plan and maintaining regular exercise, as components of a holistic diabetes management strategy. Subsequent assessments took place at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to oversee glycaemic control and evaluate therapeutic efficacy.

Nevertheless, with the patient's moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell response on glucagon stimulation (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and notable family history of early-onset diabetes, suspicion for MODY was heightened.

Therapy was initiated prior to receipt of genetic testing results, relying on the patient’s clinical presentation and laboratory data observed at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month later, the patient's HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. With genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and assessment revealing moderately retained, though inadequate, β-cell function, as well as the HbA1c improvement to 9.1%, insulin was discontinued. Glycaemic management was transitioned to dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg weekly) to assist in glucose regulation and maintain pancreatic function. Following 6 months of dulaglutide therapy, HbA1c further decreased to 6.0%, demonstrating notable clinical improvement. As glycaemic levels became stable, the treatment protocol was simplified to include metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day) for ongoing maintenance. The patient continues to be monitored regularly, with HbA1c values persistently below 6.5%. Up to this point, there has been no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and ongoing assessment for long-term complications remains in place.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene functions as a transcription factor situated at the q24 locus of chromosome 12. It contains 10 exons distributed over 23,790 base pairs and produces a protein consisting of 631 amino acids, which is found in multiple organs such as the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Mutations in HNF1A result in gradual deterioration of β-cell activity, evidenced by reduced expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes as well as diminished pyruvate kinase activity. This ultimately leads to lower insulin production and impaired glucose tolerance.

For this case, the diagnostic workup followed established criteria for MODY, including clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and genetic confirmation—such as an early onset of diabetes, prominent familial occurrence, intolerance to postprandial glucose, and absence of autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide above 0.6 ng/mL supports the diagnosis of a non-type 1 diabetes. However, ongoing assessment of C-peptide is crucial because normal values might be seen during the initial phase of type 1 diabetes. An important obstacle in diagnosing MODY is insufficient awareness and clinical suspicion, which often results in patients being erroneously labeled as having type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator developed by the Exeter group offers a helpful means for clinicians to identify patients suitable for genetic evaluation. Nonetheless, as this tool was created using data from a White European population, it may overstate MODY likelihood in individuals from other ethnic backgrounds. Applying our patient's data to this calculator yielded a MODY probability of 62.4% (approximately 1 in 1.6 chance). There are case reports documenting the presence of autoantibodies among MODY patients, and some carriers of HNF1A mutations do have autoantibodies, although most MODY cases lack them. Previous research indicates that obesity and metabolic syndrome are less common among MODY individuals compared to those with type 2 diabetes; however, increasing prevalence of obesity makes differentiating between MODY and T2DM based on BMI or metabolic syndrome less reliable, potentially accounting for the elevated BMI seen in our patient. Family history remains a significant consideration in MODY diagnosis, but its utility may be compromised due to factors such as incomplete reporting, new genetic mutations, and the widespread occurrence of type 2 diabetes in family histories.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by both patient age and HbA1c values. The initial preferred therapy is low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release via the modulation of potassium-sensitive ATP channels. In individuals diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer and more efficacious substitute for sulfonylureas, especially as β-cell function deteriorates and for patients susceptible to hypoglycaemia. Findings from a small randomised, double-blind crossover trial revealed that liraglutide and glimepiride similarly lowered fasting plasma glucose and mitigated postprandial glycaemic variability, with no significant distinction between the effects of these agents in HNF1A-MODY patients. Moreover, therapy with GLP-1 receptor agonists results in a lower incidence of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Following genetic confirmation of the diagnosis, insulin was discontinued once glycaemic targets were met, and the patient was transitioned to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which successfully sustained glycaemic control. This underscores the importance of genetic analysis for informing therapeutic choices. Glycosuria seen in HNF1A-MODY stems from decreased SGLT2 expression regulated by HNF1A. An earlier report described that patients with HNF1A-MODY might encounter profound dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis owing to pronounced suppression of residual SGLT-2 activity. The hazards associated with SGLT-2 inhibitor use in HNF1A-MODY—including heightened risk of dehydration, vascular instability, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—could surpass their clinical advantages. In the present instance, a combination regimen of liraglutide and metformin proved both effective and safe, aligning with prior published findings.

The application of precision medicine is vital for MODY, since individuals with HNF1A-MODY may not always fulfill all established diagnostic parameters despite adherence to current clinical guidelines. At present, there are no reliable biomarkers to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from either type 1 diabetes or T2D. According to findings by Chen et al, among 2429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D, MODY accounted for a prevalence of 1.5%, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY gene variants. Additionally, Møller et al demonstrated that within a broad population-based cohort of Danish Caucasians with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 patients lacking any high-risk HLA haplotypes possessed mutations in the HNF1A gene. In line with this, a contemporary population-based investigation from Russia screened 506 subjects with suspected hereditary diabetes absent autoimmune markers—including negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, preserved C-peptide levels, reduced insulin needs within five years, and no evidence of severe obesity. This study found P or LP variants in 46.4% of cases, with HNF1A frequently implicated. Definitive MODY diagnosis relies upon DNA sequencing to corroborate clinical suspicion. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables comprehensive analysis of large volumes of patient genetic material in a single assay, thereby enhancing the practicality of MODY testing. The advent of NGS has led to identification of numerous additional genetic variants. The ACMG supplies recommendations for determining pathogenicity of these variants. To date, databases like ClinVar have catalogued over 1000 HNF1A variants classified as P, LP, benign, likely benign, or of uncertain significance. In our patient, the newly detected heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not recorded in ClinVar or any major variant repositories, underscoring its novelty but not necessarily its clinical importance. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid adjacent to previously documented P residues, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and shows familial segregation with diabetes—satisfying ACMG guidelines for LP classification. Therefore, while ClinVar is instrumental for cataloging variants, multiple lines of independent genetic and clinical data support the pathogenicity of this variant, irrespective of its absence in database entries.

Research has demonstrated that most discovered mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits fewer mutations. The novel variant described in this report is also located within the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations suggest that some HNF1A mutations, when present as P, exert limited influence on the function of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, there are HNF1A variants that, while not sufficient on their own to induce MODY, may contribute to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case underlines the significance of precise diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY and how it influences individualized therapeutic strategies. Future research should aim to devise enhanced NGS-based screening methods to increase the detection rate of MODY, leading to improved cost-effectiveness and better clinical outcomes.
