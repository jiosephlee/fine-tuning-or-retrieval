Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic diabetes subtype inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. This case report details an instance of hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A)-MODY resulting from a newly identified heterozygous HNF1A mutation. The patient exhibited elevated blood glucose and the presence of glucose in the urine, but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. There was a significant family history involving early-onset diabetes. Genetic analysis revealed a pathogenic HNF1A variant that had not been previously described. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist alongside metformin brought the patient’s HbA1c down from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This case underscores the value of genetic testing in cases of early-onset diabetes, which are often inaccurately categorized as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and adds to the catalog of HNF1A mutations relevant to personalized medical care.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited diabetes syndromes, which are non-autoimmune and marked by a fundamental defect in the formation and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders commonly present before the age of 25 and are identified during this period. MODY accounts for approximately 1%–6% of diabetes diagnosed in young individuals. Prevalence rates of MODY differ among ethnic and racial populations and may be underestimated in certain groups. Most investigations so far have focused on White Europeans. Recently, studies have begun to examine MODY prevalence in Asian and Australian populations. Reported prevalence estimates for MODY are highly variable, largely because genetic screening is restricted to individuals selected based on specific clinical features, even in population-based studies.

Pathogenic mutations in more than 15 different genes have been associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, and each specific genetic alteration carries its own therapeutic implications. Most frequently, mutations found in hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β account for the majority of MODY cases seen in clinical practice. 

HNF1A-MODY is the most common MODY subtype, responsible for roughly 30%–65% of all cases. It typically follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, manifesting as symptomatic diabetes across multiple family members. During early teenage years, affected individuals might not display overt symptoms, and increased blood glucose is often incidentally discovered through routine laboratory assessments, in the absence of signs indicating insulin resistance. Elevations in postprandial blood glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L) can be unmasked by random blood glucose testing after meals or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Glycosuria may also be present, which results from a diminished renal threshold for glucose because of impaired glucose transport mechanisms in the renal tubules. In advanced stages, clinical features such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss may arise, mirroring symptoms typically seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). As a consequence, the diagnostic distinction between MODY and other forms of diabetes is often complicated by overlapping clinical presentations.

Multiple studies have outlined diagnostic pathways for MODY. When the clinical evaluation and family history indicate MODY, and autoantibodies commonly seen in type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, accompanied by relevant biochemical findings, genetic analysis becomes a key element for diagnosis. An accurate diagnosis enables appropriate treatment selection, anticipates possible complications, informs prognosis, and aids in genetic counselling for families. Recent research has revealed that hundreds of distinct mutations within the HNF1A gene contribute to HNF1A-MODY.

In the patient presented in this report, genetic testing performed with a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel detected a novel heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A. Pathogenicity assessment was carried out in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation. This mutation had not been described previously in published studies, nor documented in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment of elevated blood glucose and glucose detected in the urine, findings that emerged during his routine annual health check. Physical examination showed a body mass index of 24.6 kg/m2, blood pressure measuring 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse of 78 beats per minute. No acanthosis nigricans was identified on inspection. The patient’s family history revealed several instances of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her thirties, his mother in her late twenties, and his younger brother at age 20. There was no reported occurrence of premature cardiovascular disease among relatives with diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and his fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. Two hours after administering a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), his plasma glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. There were no clinical features of diabetic ketoacidosis observed. The patient described increased thirst and appetite, as well as recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid studies found an HDL cholesterol of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, which are suggestive of possible metabolic syndrome (see tables 1 and 2). The patient denied any notable prior medical or surgical illnesses.

A glucagon stimulation test was performed, showing a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline, rising to 0.83 nmol/L after 6 minutes, consistent with deficient insulin production. This test assesses the remaining functional capacity of pancreatic β-cells by determining C-peptide concentrations following glucagon administration. Typically, a post-stimulus C-peptide value above 0.60 nmol/L is regarded as a sign of ongoing endogenous insulin output, while lower concentrations indicate β-cell impairment. Nonetheless, the glucagon stimulation test is best considered as an adjunctive, rather than a definitive, tool—particularly in cases with indeterminate autoantibody findings or non-standard presentations. No microalbuminuria was present. Assessment of sudomotor function revealed marked reduction over both hands. Sudomotor activity was evaluated by electrochemical skin conductance testing (such as Sudoscan), which is a non-invasive method for examining small fibre autonomic nervous system function. The test results for this patient showed significantly decreased hand conductance, indicative of substantial autonomic involvement. Autoantibody testing for GADA and IA-2A returned negative. One month following diagnosis, molecular analysis for MODY identified a heterozygous, pathogenic (P) variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), which is diagnostic of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The first therapeutic approach comprised basal insulin degludec (eight units daily), together with metformin (1500 mg daily) and saxagliptin (5 mg daily), reflecting a tentative classification of T2D. This regimen was selected to facilitate prompt glycaemic stabilization and address possible insulin resistance.

Alongside medication, the patient received guidance to follow a structured nutritional program and maintain consistent physical activity, forming part of an integrative strategy for diabetes care. Glycaemic status and treatment effectiveness were periodically reviewed at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient setting.

Nonetheless, the presence of moderately retained but insufficient β-cell capacity, as indicated by the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), the absence of islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced family history of early-onset diabetes raised substantial suspicion for MODY.

Therapy was initiated prior to obtaining genetic test results, based on the clinical scenario and diagnostic laboratory data present at initial evaluation.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient's HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. With molecular confirmation of HNF1A-MODY, and in light of the patient's partially retained, but inadequate, β-cell reserve—as well as the observed decrease in HbA1c to 9.1%—insulin was discontinued in favor of dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg administered weekly) to enhance glycaemic regulation and maintain pancreatic function. Following 6 months of dulaglutide, the HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, demonstrating marked glycaemic improvement. Upon stabilisation of blood glucose levels, the therapeutic approach was adjusted to long-term management with metformin (1500 mg per day) combined with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg per day). The patient continues to be closely monitored, and HbA1c levels have consistently remained under 6.5%. No microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy has developed, and vigilant monitoring for chronic complications persists.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor situated at chromosome 12q24. It consists of 10 exons and extends across 23,790 base pairs, producing a 631-amino acid protein that is present in multiple tissues, such as the pancreas, kidneys, and liver. Mutations in HNF1A result in the gradual loss of β-cell activity, characterized by diminished insulin and GLUT2 gene expression and decreased pyruvate kinase function. This cascade ultimately causes reduced insulin release and impaired glucose tolerance.

The diagnostic workup for this patient followed established criteria for MODY, incorporating clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and genetic analysis—including early-onset diabetes, significant family history, postprandial hyperglycemia, and negative pancreatic autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide concentration above 0.6 ng/mL supports a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes. Nevertheless, continued assessment of C-peptide is important since normal levels may appear during early type 1 diabetes. Limited recognition and clinical suspicion of MODY frequently hinder proper diagnosis, leading to frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator from the Exeter group serves as a helpful tool to select patients for genetic testing, though it was developed using data from a White European cohort and may produce inflated probability estimates for individuals from other backgrounds. Based on our patient’s data, the calculator estimated a MODY probability of 62.4% (or 1 in 1.6). There are documented cases of autoantibody positivity among MODY patients, including some with HNF1A mutations, even though most are seronegative for autoantibodies. Earlier reports demonstrated that rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome were lower among MODY patients compared to those with type 2 diabetes. However, with increasing prevalence of obesity, BMI and metabolic syndrome have become less reliable in differentiating between MODY and T2DM, possibly contributing to our patient’s higher BMI. Furthermore, while family history can support a MODY diagnosis, its reliability can be compromised due to factors such as insufficient reporting, occurrence of de novo mutations, or widespread T2DM in families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on the patient's age and HbA1c values. Initial therapy commonly consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin release by targeting potassium-sensitive ATP channels. As β-cell function diminishes, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer and potentially more efficacious option, especially for those at increased risk of hypoglycaemia. A small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study found liraglutide and glimepiride to be equally effective in lowering fasting plasma glucose and minimizing postprandial glucose variability in HNF1A-MODY patients, with no notable differences observed. Furthermore, treatment with GLP-1 receptor agonists was associated with fewer instances of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic confirmation enabled us to withdraw insulin therapy after achieving glycaemic stability and subsequently switch to GLP-1 receptor agonists, which continued to provide robust glucose control. This underscores the pivotal role of genetic testing in directing therapeutic choices. Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY results from diminished SGLT2 expression regulated by HNF1A transcriptional activity. One report described that patients with HNF1A-MODY can develop profound dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis owing to strong suppression of remaining SGLT-2 activity. For HNF1A-MODY patients, the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors carries considerable risks, including higher probability of dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, which may outweigh any benefits these drugs offer. In the present case, the combination of liraglutide and metformin proved both safe and effective, in alignment with findings from earlier research.

Precision medicine is especially important in managing MODY, since patients with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all established diagnostic criteria despite available clinical recommendations. At present, there are no definitive biomarkers that reliably distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or T2D. Chen et al conducted a study in which, among 2429 young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D, MODY was identified in 1.5% of cases, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al found that in a sizable cohort of Danish Caucasian individuals diagnosed with type 1 diabetes, 10% of the 39 patients lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes possessed mutations in the HNF1A gene. Likewise, a recent Russian population-based investigation screened 506 subjects with suspected hereditary diabetes forms lacking autoimmune characteristics, as evidenced by negative results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, retention of C-peptide secretion, modest insulin requirements over five years, and absence of marked obesity. Among these, 46.4% were found to have P or LP variants, with HNF1A among the most frequently implicated genes. DNA sequencing remains necessary to substantiate MODY diagnosis. The introduction of next-generation sequencing (NGS) facilitates the simultaneous evaluation of extensive patient DNA, greatly enhancing MODY detection capability. NGS advances have led to the identification of numerous genetic variants. The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) offers criteria to ascertain pathogenicity. Databases like ClinVar have registered over 1000 different HNF1A variants, encompassing P, LP, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In the present case, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not listed in ClinVar or other widely referenced variant databases, underscoring its novelty rather than implying it lacks clinical significance. Of note, this variant affects an amino acid immediately adjacent to previously documented P residues, displays a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and shows familial co-segregation with diabetes, fulfilling ACMG guidelines for LP classification. Therefore, while ClinVar serves as an important repository of documented variants, the pathogenicity of this specific variant is supported by several independent genetic and clinical factors, apart from its absence in database records.

Research has demonstrated that most mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a comparatively lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is also located in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations suggest that specific HNF1A mutations, including P, exert only a modest influence on the functionality of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants, although not sufficient to cause MODY independently, may be linked to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the necessity of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY for guiding patient-specific management. Future studies will aim to establish improved NGS screening methods to enhance MODY detection, thereby advancing cost efficiency and optimizing clinical outcomes.
