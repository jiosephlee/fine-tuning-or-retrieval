Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic, autosomal dominant diabetes subtype. This case details an individual with HNF1A-MODY caused by a novel heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The patient exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The family medical history included diabetes with an early age at onset. Molecular analysis identified a pathogenic HNF1A variant that had not been previously reported. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist alongside metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a three-month period. This case underscores the significance of genetic testing in diagnosing early-onset diabetes—which is often confused with either type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and adds to the catalogue of HNF1A mutations pertinent to individualized treatment strategies.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) comprises a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes that result from a fundamental abnormality in pancreatic β-cell development and performance. These syndromes commonly present and are identified before patients reach 25 years of age. MODY accounts for about 1% to 6% of early-onset diabetes diagnoses. Prevalence rates differ among racial and ethnic groups, with some evidence suggesting the true rates may be underestimated. Up until now, most investigations have focused on populations of White European descent. More recently, assessments of MODY frequency have included regions in Asia and Australia. Reported prevalence figures for MODY fluctuate widely, often due to genetic testing being restricted to selected individuals based on their clinical profiles, which remains the case even in larger population studies.

Over 15 gene mutations have been implicated in the development of the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with each gene variant necessitating specific management approaches. The most prevalent forms of MODY are predominantly associated with mutations in genes like hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β. HNF1A-MODY is the most common subtype, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of MODY cases, and demonstrates a clear autosomal dominant inheritance pattern for symptomatic diabetes within affected families. During early adolescence, individuals may not exhibit symptoms, and hyperglycemia is often detected incidentally through routine laboratory tests, usually in the absence of clinical signs of insulin resistance. Measurement of postprandial blood glucose or administration of a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal marked postprandial hyperglycemia (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be present, attributed to a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption caused by impaired tubular glucose transport. In more advanced stages, features such as increased thirst, excessive urination, increased appetite, and weight loss—paralleling symptoms seen in type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—may develop. Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes poses a significant diagnostic challenge due to substantial overlap in clinical presentation.

Multiple diagnostic pathways for MODY have been described in various studies. When clinical assessment and family history indicate MODY, and testing reveals absence of type 1 diabetes-related autoantibodies—including glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—as well as supplementary biochemical findings, molecular genetic analysis becomes an essential component for confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification of the specific MODY subtype is vital to selecting optimal management strategies, estimating complication risks, determining prognosis, and guiding family counseling. Recent research has shown that a wide range of mutations in the HNF1A gene are implicated in HNF1A-MODY.

In the patient presented in this paper, genetic analysis via a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel revealed a novel heterozygous missense change, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), in exon 4 of HNF1A. The pathogenicity of this variant was evaluated following the criteria set by the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG). This specific mutation had not been previously documented in the literature, the VarSome database, or the ClinVar database. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment due to findings of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria detected during his routine annual health screening. Physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure reading of 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse of 78 beats per minute. No evidence of acanthosis nigricans was found. His family history was remarkable for diabetes presenting at a young age: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. None of the affected relatives had documented early-onset cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

The patient's glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting glucose concentration of 12.4 mmol/L. After a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), the 2-hour glucose value reached 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. Symptomatically, the patient described increased thirst, augmented appetite, and recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid analysis demonstrated a high-density lipoprotein cholesterol of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L, findings that raise suspicion for metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He had no notable medical or surgical background.

A glucagon stimulation test revealed serum C-peptide levels of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, consistent with suboptimal insulin secretion. This test measures residual β-cell function by assessing stimulated C-peptide concentrations after glucagon administration. In usual clinical settings, a stimulated C-peptide value greater than 0.60 nmol/L suggests retained endogenous insulin production, while lower results imply β-cell impairment. It is important to note, however, that the glucagon stimulation test should be considered an adjunct rather than a conclusive test, especially in cases with unclear autoantibody status or atypical disease manifestations. No microalbuminuria was detected. Sudomotor evaluation indicated profound impairment in sudomotor activity in the hands, as determined by electrochemical skin conductance assessment (e.g., Sudoscan), a noninvasive approach for evaluating small fiber autonomic nervous system involvement. This demonstrated severely diminished hand conductance, reflecting marked autonomic compromise. Testing for autoantibodies—including GADA and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A)—yielded negative results. A genetic test for maturity onset diabetes of the young (MODY), conducted one month after diagnosis, identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), consistent with HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly raised HbA1c and plasma glucose readings. The first therapeutic approach included basal insulin degludec at a dose of eight units per day, combined with metformin at 1500 mg per day and saxagliptin at 5 mg per day, which reflected an early presumption of type 2 diabetes (T2D). This regimen was designed to achieve prompt glycaemic stabilization and address possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, recommendations were made for the patient to follow a structured diet and maintain regular physical activity as components of an integrated diabetes care plan. Ongoing assessments were carried out at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient service to track glycaemic status and evaluate therapeutic effectiveness.

Nonetheless, the patient demonstrated a moderately maintained yet inadequate β-cell activity as shown by the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a notable familial pattern of early-onset diabetes, all of which raised strong suspicion for MODY.

The initial management was instituted before genetic testing outcomes were available, guided by the clinical features and laboratory data observed at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c had dropped to 9.1%. Once HNF1A-MODY was confirmed genetically, and taking into account the patient’s partially maintained but inadequate β-cell activity in addition to improved HbA1c, insulin was stopped and replaced with dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) to aid glycaemic management and help maintain pancreatic function. Six months into this therapy, the patient’s HbA1c reached 6.0%, reflecting substantial improvement. With glycaemic control now stable, the regimen was streamlined to metformin (1500 mg daily) plus the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg daily) as a long-term strategy. The patient continues regular follow-up, and HbA1c has consistently stayed below 6.5%. To date, there have been no findings of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and monitoring for chronic complications continues.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor situated on the q24 region of chromosome 12. It consists of 10 exons over a total length of 23,790 base pairs and codes for a 631–amino acid protein present in multiple organs, such as the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Alterations in HNF1A cause a progressive reduction in β-cell activity, resulting in defective insulin and GLUT2 gene expression as well as diminished pyruvate kinase function. This sequence of changes ultimately impairs insulin release and leads to glucose intolerance.

The diagnostic approach in this report followed established MODY criteria, which include clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and genetic confirmation—specifically, early-onset diabetes, significant family history, postprandial hyperglycemia, and absence of autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide concentration above 0.6 ng/mL supports a diagnosis distinct from type 1 diabetes. Nevertheless, ongoing assessment of C-peptide is vital, since normal results can be present at the onset of type 1 diabetes. One main challenge in diagnosing MODY lies in the limited recognition and low index of suspicion, resulting in frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator from the Exeter group serves as a clinical aid for selecting patients for genetic testing. It is important to note that this calculator was created using data from a White European population and may produce inflated MODY probability estimates among non-White individuals. Using this calculator with our patient's information yielded an estimated MODY likelihood of 62.4% (equivalent to a 1 in 1.6 chance). There are published reports documenting autoantibodies in some MODY patients; while most individuals with HNF1A variants do not have autoantibodies, they can occasionally be present. Early research found lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome among people with MODY compared to those with type 2 diabetes. However, with increasing obesity prevalence, using BMI or metabolic syndrome criteria to distinguish between MODY and type 2 diabetes has become less reliable, which may account for the elevated BMI in this patient. Furthermore, although family history remains a key consideration in diagnosing MODY, its diagnostic value can be diminished by factors such as incomplete reporting, de novo mutations, and the widespread occurrence of type 2 diabetes within families.

The management approach for HNF1A-MODY depends on the patient’s age and HbA1c values. Initial therapy typically involves low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function diminishes, GLP-1 receptor agonists may be preferable and safer than sulfonylureas, particularly in patients susceptible to hypoglycaemia. Results from a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover study indicated that liraglutide and glimepiride both effectively lower fasting plasma glucose and minimize postprandial glycaemic variability, with no significant difference between the two treatments in HNF1A-MODY individuals. Furthermore, GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a reduced risk of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic identification allowed for discontinuation of insulin therapy after achieving glycaemic control, and the patient was subsequently switched to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which successfully maintained glucose regulation. This emphasizes the importance of genetic analysis in informing therapeutic choices. Glycosuria observed in HNF1A-MODY results from decreased expression of SGLT2, regulated by the transcription factor HNF1A. One report suggested that individuals with HNF1A-MODY may encounter significant dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to heightened inhibition of residual SGLT-2 function. The adverse effects related to SGLT-2 inhibitor use in this population—including elevated dehydration risk, possibility of vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may surpass the potential benefits. For the patient described, liraglutide and metformin therapy has proven both effective and safe, aligning with findings from earlier studies.

Personalized approaches are vital in the management of MODY, since patients with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy every diagnostic requirement laid out by current clinical protocols. There are currently no robust biomarkers available to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from both type 1 and T2D. Chen et al's study revealed that among 2,429 young Chinese adults presenting with early-onset T2D, the frequency of MODY was 1.5%, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al's research further demonstrated that in a large sample of Danish Caucasian patients with type 1 diabetes, 10% of 39 individuals without any high-risk HLA haplotypes were found to possess mutations in HNF1A. In another example, a recent Russian population-based investigation assessed 506 individuals suspected of inherited diabetes lacking autoimmune characteristics—characterized by negative antibody results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 at diagnosis, retention of C-peptide, minimal insulin needs over a five-year period, and absence of marked obesity. Within this group, pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were detected in 46.4%, and HNF1A was among the most frequently mutated genes. DNA sequencing is a critical tool for validating clinical suspicion of MODY. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables comprehensive evaluation of patient DNA in a single assay, increasing the practicality of MODY detection. The advent of NGS has facilitated the identification of numerous new genetic variants. The ACMG offers guidance to assess the pathogenicity of these discoveries. Over 1,000 unique HNF1A variants are cataloged in ClinVar, spanning categories of pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In this particular instance, the novel heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not listed in ClinVar nor other popular variant repositories, underscoring its novelty instead of casting doubt on its clinical impact. Of note, this variant changes an amino acid next to established pathogenic residues, demonstrates a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes in the patient's pedigree, which collectively satisfy ACMG criteria for classification as likely pathogenic. Accordingly, while ClinVar serves as an important resource for documenting previously identified variants, the evidence supporting the pathogenic nature of this variant is based on multiple, independent genetic and clinical factors beyond simple database inclusion.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain experiences a lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is localized to the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations indicate that some HNF1A mutations, including P, exert minimal influence on the functionality of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, particular HNF1A variants, which alone are insufficient to cause MODY, may nonetheless contribute to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and how it can influence individualized therapeutic approaches. Future work will prioritize the development of more robust NGS-based screening methods to enhance the diagnostic yield for MODY, with the aim of optimizing both healthcare expenditure and patient outcomes.
