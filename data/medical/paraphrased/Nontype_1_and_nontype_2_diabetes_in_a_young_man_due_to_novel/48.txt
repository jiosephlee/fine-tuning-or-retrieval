Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) constitutes a monogenic form of diabetes inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. This case report details a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY arising from a novel heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. Clinical findings included hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient's family history revealed instances of diabetes manifesting at a young age. Genetic analysis identified a previously undescribed pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Administration of a GLP-1 receptor agonist in combination with metformin led to a reduction in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over 3 months. This case underscores the value of genetic testing in individuals with early-onset diabetes—often incorrectly diagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and broadens the known spectrum of HNF1A mutations relevant for individualized medical management.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) refers to a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetic disorders resulting from a primary abnormality in pancreatic β-cell development and function. These disorders usually arise and are identified prior to 25 years of age. MODY accounts for approximately 1%–6% of cases of early-onset diabetes. The frequency of MODY differs across various ethnic and racial groups, and its true prevalence may be underestimated. Historically, investigations have largely focused on populations of White European descent. More recent research has explored MODY prevalence in Asian and Australian populations. Estimates of MODY frequency differ considerably, frequently due to the fact that genetic testing is reserved for individuals selected on the basis of clinical criteria, even in studies involving broader cohorts.

More than 15 gene mutations have been associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with each mutation necessitating tailored treatment approaches. Most diagnosed MODY subtypes are attributed to alterations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent form, making up approximately 30% to 65% of all MODY diagnoses, and is characterized by an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern, frequently resulting in symptomatic diabetes across generations. In the initial adolescent years, affected individuals may remain asymptomatic, and raised blood glucose values are often detected incidentally during standard screening, usually without evidence of insulin resistance. Assessment of blood glucose after meals or via a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) commonly uncovers a marked rise in postprandial glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Moreover, glycosuria may occur due to a lowered renal threshold for glucose reabsorption secondary to impaired tubular glucose transport. As the disease advances, patients might develop clinical manifestations such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight loss—symptoms similar to those seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and type 2 diabetes can be challenging because of the shared clinical presentations.

Several diagnostic strategies for MODY have been delineated in the literature. Once clinical evaluation and family history suggest MODY, and autoimmune markers for type 1 diabetes such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8) are negative, supplemented by relevant biochemical investigations, molecular genetic analysis becomes pivotal for confirmation. An accurate identification of the subtype is essential for tailoring treatment decisions, anticipating complications, evaluating prognosis, and enabling genetic counselling for the family. Recent research has revealed that numerous distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene lead to HNF1A-MODY.

In the presented case, genetic testing via a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel identified a previously unreported heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient diagnosed with HNF1A-MODY. The pathogenicity of this variant was assessed in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation. This particular mutation had not been documented in the scientific literature, VarSome database, or ClinVar database. Informed written consent for the publication of the case report was provided directly by the patient.

Case presentation:

A young adult was referred for assessment of elevated blood glucose and the presence of glucose in the urine detected during his routine yearly health check. Upon physical examination, his body mass index measured 24.6 kg/m2, his blood pressure was 112/70 mmHg, and his heart rate was recorded at 78 beats per minute. No evidence of acanthosis nigricans was found. His family history was significant for diabetes diagnosed at an early age: his maternal grandmother was affected in her 30s, his mother developed the disease in her late 20s, and his younger brother was diagnosed at 20 years old. There was no reported family history of premature cardiovascular disease among relatives with diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting blood glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. Two hours after a 75 g OGTT, his glucose level reached 19.8 mmol/L. He exhibited no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described experiencing polydipsia, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss. Laboratory findings showed an HDL cholesterol of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, indicative of probable metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). There was no notable past medical or surgical history.

Assessment with a glucagon stimulation test found serum C-peptide levels of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, signifying reduced insulin secretion. The glucagon stimulation test determines remaining β-cell activity by quantifying C-peptide in response to glucagon. Typically, in clinical settings, a stimulated C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L points to retained endogenous insulin release, while values below this threshold denote β-cell impairment. Nonetheless, the glucagon test serves as a supplementary assessment, especially when antibody findings are unclear or the clinical features are not characteristic. Microalbuminuria was absent. Sudomotor testing through electrochemical skin conductance (for example, Sudoscan) showed marked impairment of sudomotor function in the hands, indicating severe small fiber autonomic dysfunction. Both GADA and IA-2A antibody assays were negative. Genetic testing for MODY was conducted one month following diagnosis and identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S; HNF1A:c.809A>G), confirming a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose readings. The initial therapeutic approach involved administering basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, together with metformin at a dose of 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin 5 mg daily, indicative of an early consideration for type 2 diabetes. This regimen was selected to promptly control blood glucose and address possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient received guidance to follow a structured diet and participate in consistent physical activity as components of an integrated diabetes management plan. Glycaemic status and treatment efficacy were monitored through regular follow-ups at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic.

Nonetheless, moderate yet inadequate β-cell function observed via glucagon stimulation testing (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a notable family history of early-onset diabetes contributed to a pronounced clinical suspicion for MODY.

Therapeutic measures were implemented prior to obtaining genetic test outcomes, relying on the patient’s clinical features and diagnostic laboratory data at presentation.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c was reduced to 9.1%. With the confirmation of HNF1A-MODY by genetic testing, and taking into account the patient’s moderately preserved but inadequate β-cell function as well as the improved HbA1c, insulin was withdrawn. Dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) was initiated to aid glycaemic control and to support pancreatic function preservation. At six months post-initiation, HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, demonstrating marked glycaemic improvement. Given the stabilization of blood glucose levels, therapy was streamlined to metformin (1500 mg/day) in combination with saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day) as ongoing maintenance treatment. The patient is regularly monitored, and HbA1c values have been persistently below 6.5%. To this point, there is no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and surveillance for chronic complications continues.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, located at chromosome 12q24, acts as a transcription factor with 10 exons spanning 23,790 base pairs. It encodes a protein consisting of 631 amino acids, which is found in multiple organs, including the liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Alterations in the HNF1A gene cause a gradual loss of β-cell functionality, evidenced by diminished expression of insulin and GLUT2 genes, alongside decreased pyruvate kinase activity. These changes lead to impaired insulin release and glucose intolerance.

Diagnostic evaluation in this instance followed established MODY criteria, which require clinical, biochemical, and genetic indicators, such as early onset of diabetes, a pronounced familial tendency, intolerance to postprandial blood glucose, and negative results for autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide concentration above 0.6 ng/mL indicates non-type 1 diabetes, though repeated assessment of C-peptide is needed because normal levels can occur in initial stages of type 1 diabetes. Diagnosis of MODY is frequently hindered by insufficient recognition and suspicion in clinical settings, resulting in these cases being erroneously categorized as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator introduced by the Exeter group can assist clinicians in deciding which individuals may benefit from genetic screening; however, its predictive value is primarily based on White European populations and could yield inflated MODY probabilities for those from non-White backgrounds. Using our patient’s data in this calculator produced a 62.4% estimated likelihood of MODY (equating to a 1 in 1.6 probability). Multiple case reports have documented autoantibody positivity in some MODY patients. While autoantibodies may be present in individuals with HNF1A mutations, most MODY cases do not exhibit autoantibodies. Prior research demonstrates lower incidences of obesity and metabolic syndrome among MODY patients compared to those with T2D, but as obesity prevalence rises, the use of BMI or metabolic syndrome as distinguishing criteria has diminished, which may account for the elevated BMI observed in this case. Additionally, although a strong family history supports MODY diagnosis, it may be limited by factors such as insufficient reporting, de novo mutations, and common occurrence of T2D in families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on patient age and HbA1c values. Initial therapy consists of administering low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release by targeting potassium-sensitive ATP channels. In individuals with HNF1A-MODY, as β-cell functionality decreases and susceptibility to hypoglycaemia increases, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer and potentially superior option to sulfonylureas. A randomized, double-blind, crossover study found that liraglutide and glimepiride both significantly lowered fasting plasma glucose and minimized postprandial glucose variability in HNF1A-MODY patients, without notable differences between the two drugs. Moreover, GLP-1 receptor agonists carry a reduced risk of mild hypoglycaemia when compared with glimepiride. Genetic testing allowed for withdrawal of insulin therapy following glycaemic stabilization, and replacement with GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment sustained satisfactory glucose control in our patient, underscoring the importance of genetic diagnostics for informing clinical interventions. Glycosuria in individuals with HNF1A-MODY arises from diminished SGLT2 expression, regulated transcriptionally by HNF1A. According to one report, patients with HNF1A-MODY could face severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis owing to pronounced inhibition of residual SGLT-2 activity. The potential hazards of SGLT-2 inhibitor use in HNF1A-MODY—such as increased dehydration risk, possibility of vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may surpass the expected advantages of these drugs. In this instance, liraglutide in combination with metformin produced effective and safe glycaemic management, corroborating previous research findings.

Precision medicine plays a vital role in MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy all diagnostic criteria even when clinical guidelines are followed. At present, no reliable biomarkers are available to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. Chen et al. reported that among 2,429 Chinese young adults diagnosed early with T2D, MODY was present in 1.5%, with 0.74% possessing pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al. demonstrated that within a large, population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian type 1 diabetes patients, 10% of 39 individuals lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes had mutations in HNF1A. Likewise, a recent population-based investigation in Russia included 506 persons with suspected hereditary diabetes and no signs of autoimmunity—based on negative results for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at onset, preserved C-peptide, low insulin requirements during the first 5 years, and absence of significant obesity. This study revealed pathogenic or LP variants in 46.4% of participants, with HNF1A mutations among the most prevalent. Confirming MODY requires DNA sequencing, which is key to establishing a diagnosis. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) enables examination of large quantities of patient DNA in a single procedure, enhancing the practicality of MODY detection. Advances in NGS technology have resulted in the identification of numerous novel genetic changes. The ACMG has issued standards to determine whether these alterations are pathogenic. In databases like ClinVar, more than 1,000 distinct HNF1A variants have been documented, encompassing pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In the present case, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A mutation (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not listed in ClinVar or other major variant databases, underscoring its novelty rather than questioning its potential clinical impact. Notably, the affected residue is adjacent to recognized pathogenic sites, has an elevated AlphaMissense score (0.995), and demonstrates familial segregation with diabetes, all of which satisfy ACMG criteria for an LP classification. Therefore, while ClinVar serves as a valuable repository for variant reporting, the pathogenic potential of this specific variant is substantiated by a range of genetic and clinical data apart from database presence.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations occur within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain experiences a lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is also found in the DNA-binding domain. According to several studies, certain HNF1A mutations, particularly P, exert only minor influences on the function of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants that do not independently cause MODY may nonetheless contribute to an elevated risk of developing T2D.

In summary, this report underscores the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its relevance to personalized management. Upcoming investigations will emphasize advancing NGS-based screening approaches to enhance the detection rate of MODY, with the goal of optimizing healthcare costs and improving patient outcomes.
