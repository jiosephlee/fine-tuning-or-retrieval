Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) represents an autosomal dominant single-gene form of diabetes. This report details a case of HNF1A-MODY attributed to a novel heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria, without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history revealed the occurrence of early-onset diabetes. Genetic analysis identified a pathogenic HNF1A variant not previously documented. Initiation of therapy with a GLP-1 receptor agonist and metformin led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over 3 months. This case underscores the significance of genetic testing in cases of early-onset diabetes, which are often erroneously classified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and contributes to the expanding list of HNF1A mutations applicable to personalized medicine.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetic disorders, distinguished by an inherent impairment in the formation and activity of pancreatic β-cells. These syndromes generally present and are identified in individuals under 25 years of age. MODY accounts for roughly 1%–6% of diabetes cases with early onset. The occurrence of MODY differs across diverse racial and ethnic populations, with its true prevalence possibly being underestimated. Most investigations so far have focused on White European cohorts. More recently, MODY prevalence has also been explored in Asian and Australian populations. Reported prevalence rates of MODY show considerable variability, frequently attributed to the restriction of genetic testing to subjects selected through clinical criteria, even in population-based research studies.

More than 15 genes have been implicated in the development of the MODY monogenic phenotype, each associated with distinct management approaches. The majority of MODY cases are linked to mutations in genes including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta.

HNF1A-MODY is the most common subtype, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of MODY cases, and demonstrates a clear autosomal dominant inheritance pattern with symptomatic diabetes appearing in affected families. During early adolescence, clinical manifestations are often absent, and hyperglycemia is frequently discovered incidentally through routine screening, typically without evidence of insulin resistance. Assessment of postprandial blood glucose or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal significant elevations in glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be present, resulting from a diminished renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired transport in the renal tubules. In advanced stages, patients may exhibit symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and weight loss, similar to those seen in type 1 diabetes and type 2 diabetes (T2D). As a result, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D is challenging because of shared clinical presentations.

Multiple diagnostic pathways for MODY have been outlined in the literature. When physical examination and an evaluation of family history point toward MODY, and autoantibodies typically found in type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, alongside corroborating biochemical tests, molecular genetic analysis becomes pivotal in establishing the diagnosis. Accurately identifying MODY is essential to guide treatment choices, anticipate complications, predict outcomes, and provide counseling for family members. Recent investigations have shown that a wide range of mutations in the HNF1A gene are implicated in HNF1A-MODY.

In this case, we detected a previously unreported heterozygous substitution, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A, in a patient with HNF1A-MODY using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel. The pathogenicity of the variant was evaluated following the guidelines from the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) for interpreting variants. This mutation has not been described in the literature, the VarSome database, or ClinVar database. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was assessed following a referral for hyperglycaemia and glycosuria identified during a routine annual examination. Physical evaluation revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m^2, a blood pressure measurement of 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse rate of 78 beats per minute. There was no evidence of acanthosis nigricans on examination. His family history was significant for diabetes manifesting at a young age: his maternal grandmother developed diabetes in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at age 20. Family members diagnosed with diabetes did not have a history of premature cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

The patient's HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), and fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. At 2 hours following administration of a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), his glucose was 19.8 mmol/L. There was no clinical evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. He described experiencing polydipsia, increased hunger, and unintentional recent weight loss. Lipid assessment revealed an HDL cholesterol of 1.01 mmol/L and triglyceride concentration of 4.31 mmol/L, findings consistent with a probable diagnosis of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). The patient's prior medical and surgical histories were unremarkable.

Upon glucagon stimulation, serum C-peptide measured 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, suggesting impaired insulin secretory capacity. This test gauges residual beta cell activity through C-peptide quantification following glucagon administration. In clinical use, a stimulated C-peptide value above 0.60 nmol/L suggests retained endogenous insulin release; values below this cutoff point to beta cell compromise. Nevertheless, the glucagon stimulation test is considered an adjunctive diagnostic tool rather than conclusive, especially when diabetes-related autoantibody status is negative or the phenotype is atypical. No microalbuminuria was identified. Electrochemical skin conductance testing (e.g., Sudoscan) revealed marked reduction in sudomotor activity of the hands, indicative of notable small-fiber autonomic neuropathy. GAD antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) were both negative. One month after diagnosis, a MODY panel identified a heterozygous, pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), in line with HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose measurements. The initial management involved basal insulin degludec at eight units per day, alongside metformin at 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin at 5 mg daily, reflecting the tentative assumption of type 2 diabetes (T2D). This regimen was selected to rapidly control blood glucose levels and address possible insulin resistance.

Beyond pharmacologic interventions, the patient received recommendations to follow a structured diet and participate in regular exercise, as components of a holistic approach to diabetes care. Glycaemic control and response to therapy were monitored during subsequent visits to the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic.

Nevertheless, with moderately maintained, yet inadequate, β-cell activity demonstrated by a glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a significant family history of early-onset diabetes, suspicion for maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) was heightened.

The initial treatment was initiated before genetic testing results became available, guided by the patient’s clinical presentation and laboratory assessments at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c had fallen to 9.1%. With the molecular diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY established and in view of the patient’s moderately preserved but still inadequate β-cell activity, along with the HbA1c improvement, insulin was withdrawn and dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) was initiated to optimize glycaemic control and protect remaining pancreatic function. Six months after starting this regimen, HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, reflecting notable glycaemic improvement. As stable glycaemic control was achieved, treatment was streamlined to metformin (1500 mg/day) combined with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day) as maintenance therapy. The patient continues with routine monitoring, maintaining HbA1c values below 6.5%. Thus far, neither microalbuminuria nor diabetic retinopathy has developed, and monitoring for chronic complications remains ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene encodes a transcription factor situated in the q24 segment of chromosome 12. It contains 10 exons over a span of 23,790 base pairs and produces a 631-amino-acid protein present in tissues such as the liver, pancreas, and kidneys. Alterations in HNF1A contribute to the gradual loss of β-cell function, characterized by diminished insulin and GLUT2 gene expression and lower pyruvate kinase activity. This progression leads to reduced insulin output and compromised glucose tolerance.

In this case, the approach to diagnosis followed established MODY criteria, integrating clinical presentation, biochemical testing, and genetic evaluation—features include early diabetes onset, notable family history, postprandial hyperglycemia, and negative diabetes-associated autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide value above 0.6 ng/mL supports the exclusion of type 1 diabetes; nonetheless, ongoing measurement of C-peptide is important as initial readings in early type 1 diabetes may also be within the normal range. A notable impediment in diagnosing MODY lies in insufficient recognition and suspicion among clinicians. As a consequence, patients are frequently incorrectly identified as having type 1 diabetes or T2D. The MODY probability calculator, created by the Exeter group, is a tool intended to facilitate the identification of patients eligible for genetic analysis, yet it is based on data predominantly from White Europeans, potentially overestimating MODY risk in patients from other ethnic backgrounds. Application of this calculator to our patient’s data yielded an estimated 62.4% likelihood (approximately 1 in 1.6) for MODY. Case reports have noted instances of autoantibody positivity in MODY, including among those carrying HNF1A mutations, though most MODY patients lack autoantibodies. Past studies indicate that the prevalence of obesity and metabolic syndrome is lower among MODY patients compared to those with T2D. However, given increasing obesity rates, parameters such as BMI and presence of metabolic syndrome have become less reliable for differentiating MODY from T2DM, which may clarify the elevated BMI observed in our patient. Moreover, while family history serves as an important diagnostic clue for MODY, its reliability can be affected by incomplete reporting, newly arisen mutations, or the commonality of T2DM among relatives.

Therapy for HNF1A-MODY is based on patient age and HbA1c values. Low-dose sulfonylureas are typically used initially, as they promote insulin release by targeting potassium-sensitive ATP channels. GLP-1 receptor agonists may be preferable to sulfonylureas, especially as β-cell function diminishes and in those susceptible to hypoglycaemia. A small randomized, double-blind, crossover study found liraglutide and glimepiride were both successful in lowering fasting plasma glucose and minimizing postprandial glucose variability, with no notable difference between the two drugs in individuals with HNF1A-MODY. Furthermore, therapy with GLP-1 receptor agonists is linked to a decreased risk of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic identification allowed for cessation of insulin following glycaemic stabilization, and the patient was transitioned to GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment, which preserved optimal glucose control. This underscores the importance of genetic analysis in determining therapeutic approaches. Glycosuria seen in HNF1A-MODY is due to diminished SGLT2 expression, which HNF1A regulates transcriptionally. One report described that people with HNF1A-MODY can develop pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to significant suppression of remaining SGLT-2 activity. Potential adverse effects of SGLT-2 inhibitors in HNF1A-MODY, including higher dehydration risk, possible circulatory collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, might surpass their benefits. In this particular case, using liraglutide together with metformin proved to be both effective and safe, which aligns with previous published findings.

Precision medicine is particularly important in the context of MODY, since individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all standard diagnostic parameters, even though clinical guidelines exist. At present, suitable biomarkers capable of distinguishing HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or type 2 diabetes (T2D) are lacking. According to Chen et al, among 2429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D, MODY occurred in 1.5% of cases, with 0.74% demonstrating pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY genetic variants. Møller et al found in a large Danish Caucasian type 1 diabetes cohort that 10% of 39 patients without high-risk HLA haplotypes possessed mutations within the HNF1A gene. Similarly, a recent Russian population-based investigation evaluated 506 subjects suspected of inherited diabetes forms who were negative for autoimmune markers—including IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at presentation—had retained C-peptide secretion, had low insulin demands in the first 5 years, and were not severely obese. This research revealed P or LP variants in 46.4% of the cohort, with HNF1A being among the most frequently affected genes. Confirmation of MODY clinically requires DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) facilitates extensive analysis of patient DNA in one procedure, enhancing the practicality of MODY genetic screening. With the advent of NGS, numerous novel genetic alterations have emerged. The ACMG offers guidelines for the determination of pathogenicity. Databases like ClinVar list over 1000 HNF1A variants, which include P, LP, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In the present case, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not listed in ClinVar or other commonly referenced variant databases, signifying its novel nature rather than challenging its clinical implication. Notably, this variant affects an amino acid adjacent to established P sites, has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and segregates with diabetes within the patient’s family; these features fulfill the ACMG criteria for LP classification. Thus, although ClinVar remains useful for collecting known variants, multiple independent genetic and clinical factors substantiate the pathogenic nature of this variant, apart from its absence in database records.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a relatively low mutation frequency. The novel variant discussed in this report is positioned in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have suggested that some HNF1A mutations, particularly P, exert limited influence on the functionality of the HNF1A protein. Moreover, specific HNF1A variants that do not independently cause MODY may nonetheless contribute to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this report underscores the necessity for precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its significance for individualized therapeutic strategies. Upcoming studies will aim to establish more robust NGS screening methodologies to enhance MODY detection rates, thereby promoting improved cost efficiency and clinical outcomes.
