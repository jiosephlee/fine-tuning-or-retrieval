Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic form of diabetes inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. This case report presents a patient with HNF1A-MODY associated with a newly identified heterozygous HNF1A gene mutation. The individual exhibited elevated blood glucose and urinary glucose without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history revealed diabetes with early onset. Genetic analysis identified a novel pathogenic HNF1A variant not previously described. Therapy using a GLP-1 receptor agonist in combination with metformin led to a decline in glycated haemoglobin levels from 10.2% to 6.0% over three months. The case emphasizes the significance of genetic evaluation in early-onset diabetes—which is often inaccurately classified as either type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and broadens the catalog of HNF1A mutations significant for precision medical approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetic disorders marked by an intrinsic abnormality in the formation and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders usually present before 25 years of age. MODY accounts for an estimated 1%–6% of diabetes occurring in young individuals. The frequency of MODY can differ among distinct ethnic and racial populations and may be underrecognized. The bulk of prior investigations has focused on populations of White European descent. More recent research has assessed MODY prevalence in Asian and Australian cohorts. Reported rates of MODY differ considerably, in large part because genetic evaluation tends to be directed toward subjects selected for specific clinical criteria, even in population-based studies.

Pathogenic variants in more than 15 genes have been associated with the MODY-monogenic phenotype, with each gene mutation corresponding to distinct management approaches. Most commonly, cases of various MODY subtypes are attributed to alterations in genes such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β.

HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses, and is marked by clear autosomal dominant inheritance of symptomatic diabetes within affected families. During early adolescence, manifestations are frequently absent, and hyperglycemia is often discovered incidentally on routine laboratory tests, usually without clinical evidence of insulin resistance. Assessment of postprandial glucose or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal pronounced postprandial hyperglycemia (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also occur due to a decreased renal glucose reabsorption threshold stemming from impaired tubular glucose transport. Nevertheless, in advanced disease stages, symptoms such as polydipsia, polyuria, increased appetite, and weight loss, resembling features of both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D), may develop. Thus, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D continues to be clinically challenging due to shared phenotypic characteristics.

Multiple diagnostic strategies for MODY have been described in the literature. When clinical evaluation and assessment of family history indicate features consistent with MODY, and testing for autoimmune markers linked to type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—is negative, additional biochemical investigations may be conducted. Molecular genetic analysis plays a pivotal role in confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification of MODY subtype is essential for guiding therapeutic decisions, predicting clinical outcomes, evaluating prognosis, and enabling effective family counseling. Recent research has documented that hundreds of distinct mutations within the HNF1A gene are implicated in HNF1A-MODY.

In this case, we detected a previously undescribed heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of HNF1A in a patient with HNF1A-MODY by employing a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel. Pathogenicity was assessed in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) standards for variant classification. This mutation had not been noted in published studies, nor was it found in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent for publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment due to elevated blood glucose and the presence of glucose in urine identified at his routine annual check-up. On physical evaluation, his body mass index was 24.6 kg/m², blood pressure measured 112/70 mmHg, and his pulse rate was 78 beats per minute. The physical exam did not reveal acanthosis nigricans. His family history was remarkable for diabetes manifesting at a young age: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at 20 years old. There were no reported instances of early-onset cardiovascular disease among family members with diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting glucose value of 12.4 mmol/L. Two hours after administration of a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, his plasma glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. No clinical or laboratory evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis was found. He described experiencing polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent unintentional weight loss. Lipid testing revealed a high-density lipoprotein concentration of 1.01 mmol/L and triglyceride level of 4.31 mmol/L, suggestive of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). The patient denied any notable past medical or surgical conditions.

A glucagon stimulation test revealed a serum C-peptide of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, reflecting impaired insulin production. This procedure determines remaining pancreatic β-cell function by measuring C-peptide levels following glucagon administration. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide value greater than 0.60 nmol/L is indicative of preserved endogenous insulin secretion, while values below this threshold imply β-cell impairment. Nevertheless, this test should be interpreted as supplementary, particularly in patients with equivocal autoantibody findings or unusual clinical courses. Testing did not detect microalbuminuria. Sudomotor evaluation showed severe reduction in sudomotor activity in the hands. Sudomotor assessment was conducted with electrochemical skin conductance methodology (such as Sudoscan), which non-invasively assesses small fiber autonomic nerve function. Findings demonstrated markedly decreased conductance over the hands, pointing to profound autonomic involvement. Autoantibody testing for both GADA and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A) returned negative results. A genetic screen for maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY), performed one month after diagnosis, identified a heterozygous pathogenic mutation (P) in HNF1A (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), confirming HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

Upon diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The starting therapy involved basal insulin degludec at a dose of eight units per day, alongside metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), indicative of an initial working diagnosis of T2D. This regimen was designed to achieve prompt glycaemic stabilization and target possible insulin resistance.

Beyond medication, the patient was instructed to follow a structured nutrition plan and maintain consistent physical activity, forming part of an integrated diabetes management strategy. Subsequent assessments were performed at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient service to track glycaemic parameters and evaluate therapeutic effectiveness.

Nonetheless, with the patient's glucagon stimulation test revealing a moderately retained yet inadequate β-cell function (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced familial history of early-onset diabetes, there was considerable clinical suspicion for MODY.

Treatment initiation took place before genetic testing results were available, guided by the clinical features and laboratory evidence present at the time of diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month afterward, the patient’s HbA1c dropped to 9.1%. With genetic testing confirming HNF1A-MODY and acknowledging the patient’s intermediate but inadequate β-cell reserve as well as the improved HbA1c, insulin was withdrawn and dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg weekly) was initiated to optimize glucose management and help conserve pancreatic function. Six months later, HbA1c had further improved to 6.0%. As glycaemic control became steady, the treatment plan was streamlined to include metformin (1500 mg/day) in combination with saxagliptin (a DPP-4 inhibitor, 5 mg/day) for ongoing maintenance. The patient is seen routinely for follow-up and has maintained HbA1c levels below 6.5%. No evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy has been observed, and continuous monitoring for chronic complications remains in place.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, a transcription factor situated at chromosome 12q24, consists of 10 exons spanning 23,790 base pairs and codes for a 631-amino acid protein. This protein is present in multiple tissues such as the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Genetic alterations in HNF1A result in a gradual deterioration of β-cell function, demonstrated by lowered expression of both insulin and GLUT2 genes, along with decreased pyruvate kinase activity. This cascade ultimately diminishes insulin secretion and impairs glucose regulation.

Diagnosis in this scenario followed established MODY criteria, which integrate clinical, laboratory, and molecular data, such as an early onset of diabetes, pronounced familial aggregation, evidence of postprandial hyperglycemia, and absence of autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide above 0.6 ng/mL supports the exclusion of type 1 diabetes; nonetheless, ongoing C-peptide surveillance is important since normal levels may still be seen at initial type 1 diabetes presentation. Diagnostic accuracy for MODY is often compromised by limited provider awareness and low index of suspicion, resulting in frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator designed by the Exeter group is a helpful tool for identifying candidates for genetic confirmation. Yet, this tool is derived from data in White European cohorts and could inflate MODY probability estimates in individuals from non-White backgrounds. Using this calculator with our patient's details, the likelihood of MODY was 62.4% (1 in 1.6 chance). Autoantibodies have occasionally been identified in MODY case reports; while a subset of patients with HNF1A mutations display autoantibodies, the vast majority of MODY cases lack them. Previous investigations have found that rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome in MODY are generally lower compared to type 2 diabetes; however, as overall obesity prevalence rises, traditional markers such as BMI or features of metabolic syndrome are less useful in differentiating MODY from T2D, which may have contributed to the higher BMI in this patient. Furthermore, while family history remains a relevant diagnostic clue for MODY, it may have decreased utility owing to factors like incomplete reporting, spontaneous mutations, and the frequent occurrence of type 2 diabetes within families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by the patient's age and their HbA1c values. The preferred initial approach is administration of low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin release by targeting ATP-sensitive potassium channels. As β-cell function deteriorates, GLP-1 receptor agonists may offer a safer and potentially superior alternative, especially for patients at higher risk of hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small randomised, double-blind, crossover study revealed that both liraglutide and glimepiride led to comparable reductions in fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glycaemic excursions in individuals with HNF1A-MODY. Notably, GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a decreased incidence of mild hypoglycaemia relative to glimepiride. Genetic testing enabled us to withdraw insulin after achieving glycaemic stability, and initiate GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which successfully sustained glycaemic control. This case underscores the pivotal importance of genetic diagnostics in guiding therapeutic choices. Glycosuria observed in HNF1A-MODY results from diminished SGLT2 expression regulated by HNF1A transcriptional activity. Literature has documented that patients with HNF1A-MODY may be susceptible to pronounced dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis as a consequence of heightened inhibition of residual SGLT-2 function. Thus, the adverse events linked to SGLT-2 inhibitors—including increased dehydration, risk of vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—could exceed their intended therapeutic advantages in HNF1A-MODY. Here, a regimen comprising liraglutide and metformin proved both effective and safe, in line with findings from previous research.

Precision medicine is of key importance in the management of MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all diagnostic criteria outlined in current clinical guidelines. At present, there are no optimal biomarkers that can reliably distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or T2D. Chen et al conducted a study involving 2429 Chinese young adults with early-onset T2D, finding that MODY accounted for 1.5% of cases, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY mutations. Møller et al, in a large population-based cohort of Danish Caucasians with type 1 diabetes, observed that among 39 patients lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes, 10% exhibited HNF1A mutations. In addition, a recent Russian study screened 506 subjects suspected to have hereditary diabetes without autoimmunity—criteria included negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, retained C-peptide, low insulin demand within 5 years, and no significant obesity. P or LP variants were identified in 46.4% of these participants, with HNF1A being among the most frequently involved genes. Confirmatory diagnosis of MODY depends on DNA sequencing, and next-generation sequencing (NGS) now facilitates high-throughput analysis of patient DNA in a single procedure, enhancing the feasibility of comprehensive MODY testing. Advances in NGS have led to the discovery of many novel genetic variants. The ACMG provides recommendations for determining the pathogenicity of these variants. More than 1000 distinct HNF1A variants—including P, LP, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance—are documented in databases such as ClinVar. In this case, the unique heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not listed in ClinVar or other widely referenced variant repositories, demonstrating its novel nature rather than questioning its significance. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid residue adjacent to previously described P residues, is associated with a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes within the family—criteria consistent with an ACMG LP classification. Therefore, although ClinVar offers a valuable compendium of variants, the pathogenicity assessment of this mutation is substantiated by several independent genetic and clinical findings, extending beyond what is available in existing databases.

Research has demonstrated that most detected mutations reside within the DNA-binding domain, whereas mutations in the transactivation domain occur less frequently. The novel variant described in this report is located in the DNA-binding domain. Certain publications suggest that mutations in HNF1A, specifically the P mutation, exert only modest influence on HNF1A protein function. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants, although insufficient to independently induce MODY, may be linked to an elevated risk of developing T2D.

In summary, this report emphasizes the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its relevance for tailoring therapy. Upcoming investigations will aim to establish more effective NGS-based screening approaches to enhance MODY detection rates, with the goal of optimizing clinical outcomes and controlling associated costs.
