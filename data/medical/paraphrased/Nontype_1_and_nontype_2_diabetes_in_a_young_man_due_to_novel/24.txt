Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic diabetes subtype with autosomal dominant inheritance. This case concerns a patient with HNF1A-MODY caused by a newly identified heterozygous HNF1A mutation. The individual exhibited hyperglycemia and glycosuria, but did not develop diabetic ketoacidosis. The family history was significant for diabetes diagnosed at a young age. A previously undescribed pathogenic variant in HNF1A was detected by genetic analysis. Management with metformin and a GLP-1 receptor agonist achieved a decrease in glycated hemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month interval. This case underscores the value of genetic testing in cases of early-onset diabetes, which is often mistakenly classified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and contributes to the expanding catalog of HNF1A mutations of relevance to individualized treatment strategies.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a collection of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune diabetic disorders, marked by an intrinsic abnormality in the formation and function of pancreatic β-cells. These disorders generally present and are identified prior to the age of 25 years. MODY accounts for approximately 1%–6% of cases of diabetes with early onset. The frequency of MODY differs across various racial and ethnic populations, and its true incidence may be underestimated. Historically, investigations have primarily targeted White European cohorts. More recently, studies have begun to evaluate MODY prevalence in Asian and Australian populations. Reported rates of MODY demonstrate considerable variability, which is often attributed to the restriction of genetic screening to subjects selected according to clinical features, even in population-based analyses.

More than 15 gene mutations have been associated with the MODY monogenic phenotype, and each mutation may warrant a different treatment approach. The majority of documented MODY cases involve mutations in specific genes, including hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. HNF1A-MODY is the most common form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses and exhibiting a distinct autosomal dominant inheritance pattern with symptomatic diabetes appearing in affected families. During early adolescence, clinical symptoms often remain absent, with hyperglycemia commonly being incidentally discovered during standard laboratory testing. Insulin resistance is generally not observed at this stage. Diagnostic tools such as postprandial glucose measurements or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal marked post-meal hyperglycemia (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also occur, which results from a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired glucose transport within the renal tubules. As the condition advances, patients may develop classic symptoms including polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintended weight loss, similar to manifestations in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D can be challenging because of these overlapping clinical characteristics.

Multiple diagnostic frameworks for MODY have been outlined in the literature. When clinical features and family history evaluation point towards MODY, and autoantibodies characteristic of type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—are absent, alongside further relevant biochemical assessments, molecular genetic analysis assumes an essential role in confirming the diagnosis. Accurate identification of the MODY subtype is vital for guiding treatment decisions, anticipating potential complications, predicting outcomes, and enabling informed family counselling. Recent studies have documented numerous distinct mutations in the HNF1A gene associated with HNF1A-MODY.

In the present case, a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), located in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene, was found in a patient with HNF1A-MODY by means of a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel. The pathogenicity of the variant was assessed based on the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant interpretation. This mutation has not been described in the literature, nor in the VarSome or ClinVar databases. The patient provided written informed consent specifically for publication of this case report.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment due to findings of hyperglycaemia and glycosuria discovered during his routine annual health screening. His physical evaluation revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure measurement of 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute. No evidence of acanthosis nigricans was seen on physical inspection. The patient’s family history included instances of early-onset diabetes: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at 20 years old. There was no reported familial history of premature cardiovascular disease in relatives with diabetes.

Investigations:

His HbA1c was measured at 10.2% (88 mmol/mol) and his fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. Following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test, his 2-hour plasma glucose reached 19.8 mmol/L. No evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis was present. The patient described experiencing polydipsia, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss in the recent period. Lipid studies revealed an HDL cholesterol of 1.01 mmol/L and triglyceride concentration of 4.31 mmol/L, suggesting the possibility of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). There was no notable medical or surgical history.

The glucagon stimulation test yielded serum C-peptide values of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, consistent with impaired insulin secretion. This assay assesses endogenous β-cell reserve by recording the increment in C-peptide after glucagon administration. Typically, a peak C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L is regarded as a sign of adequate β-cell activity, while diminished responses imply underlying β-cell dysfunction. Nevertheless, interpretation should consider that this test serves a supplementary rather than decisive role, especially in cases with equivocal antibody findings or non-classical presentations. There was no microalbuminuria. Electrochemical skin conductance measurement (such as using Sudoscan) revealed markedly decreased sudomotor function in the hands, signifying pronounced autonomic involvement; this technique provides a noninvasive assessment of small fiber autonomic nerve integrity. Autoantibody assays for both GADA and IA-2A were negative. MODY genetic testing, performed one month after diagnosis, identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G)), in keeping with a diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose. The first therapeutic approach involved administering basal insulin degludec (eight units daily), alongside metformin (1500 mg per day) and saxagliptin (5 mg per day), indicative of an early assessment for T2D. This regimen was chosen to achieve prompt glycaemic regulation while targeting probable insulin resistance.

Beyond medical management, the patient received guidance to follow a structured meal plan and participate in consistent physical activity as part of an integrated diabetes care strategy. Periodic assessments occurred in the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient department to evaluate glycaemic status and treatment effectiveness.

Despite these measures, the patient demonstrated moderate, but inadequate, β-cell reserve on the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced familial history of early-onset diabetes, which heightened clinical suspicion for MODY.

Therapy was initiated before genetic analysis results were available, relying on the presenting clinical features and laboratory data observed at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c was reduced to 9.1%. With genetic confirmation of HNF1A-MODY and recognition of the patient’s moderately retained but inadequate β-cell function, as well as the improved HbA1c, insulin was withdrawn and dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg once weekly) was initiated in order to enhance glycaemic control and preserve pancreatic function. Six months after starting therapy, the patient's HbA1c had dropped to 6.0%, reflecting marked improvement. Once stable glycaemic control was achieved, the regimen was simplified to include metformin (1500 mg per day) combined with the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg daily) for ongoing maintenance. The patient continues to be monitored regularly, with HbA1c consistently kept below 6.5%. Currently, there is no evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and surveillance for long-term complications remains in place.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene functions as a transcription factor and is situated on chromosome 12q24. It consists of 10 exons covering 23,790 base pairs and encodes a protein with 631 amino acids, which is found in several organs such as the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Mutations in HNF1A cause a gradual deterioration of β-cell activity, leading to impaired expression of the insulin and GLUT2 genes and diminished pyruvate kinase function. These changes contribute to reduced insulin secretion and the development of glucose intolerance.

The approach to diagnosis in this case conformed to established MODY diagnostic criteria, which integrate clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and genetic confirmation. These criteria include features such as an early onset of diabetes, a pronounced familial tendency, intolerance to postprandial glucose, and absence of autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide concentration above 0.6 ng/mL favors a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes. Nonetheless, repeated C-peptide testing is important since values may still be within the normal range during early type 1 diabetes. Difficulty recognizing MODY stems from low clinical awareness and suspicion, resulting in frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The MODY probability calculator devised by the Exeter group provides assistance to clinicians seeking candidates for genetic screening. Yet, because this tool was validated on a White European cohort, it may overestimate MODY likelihood in non-White populations. Upon entering our patient’s clinical details into this calculator, the predicted probability for MODY was 62.4% (or 1 in 1.6). There are reports documenting autoantibodies in MODY cases; although most with HNF1A mutations do not have autoantibodies, some do. Previous research indicates MODY patients exhibit lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome in comparison to those with T2D. With rising obesity, BMI and metabolic syndrome are becoming less reliable for differentiating MODY from T2DM, which could account for the elevated BMI in this patient. Furthermore, while family history is typically a key consideration for MODY, its diagnostic usefulness can be affected by factors such as incomplete reporting, new genetic mutations, and the frequent occurrence of T2D in families.

Management of HNF1A-MODY is guided by the patient’s age and their HbA1c values. Sulfonylureas at low doses are recommended as initial therapy, as they stimulate insulin release via ATP-dependent potassium channels. For individuals with HNF1A-MODY, especially those susceptible to hypoglycaemia or with diminishing β-cell function, GLP-1 receptor agonists offer a safer and potentially more efficacious alternative to sulfonylureas. Findings from a small randomized, double-blind, crossover trial revealed that liraglutide and glimepiride similarly lower fasting plasma glucose and moderate postprandial glucose variation, with no substantial difference between the two agents in HNF1A-MODY cases. Furthermore, GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. Genetic testing enabled us to discontinue insulin upon attainment of glycaemic stability and to initiate GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which successfully sustained blood glucose control. This underscores the important role of genetic diagnostics in determining optimal treatment. Glycosuria observed in HNF1A-MODY results from diminished SGLT2 expression, controlled at the transcriptional level by HNF1A. One publication described significant dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis in HNF1A-MODY patients, related to marked suppression of residual SGLT-2 activity. The disadvantages of SGLT-2 inhibitor use in HNF1A-MODY, including heightened risk for dehydration, vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, may exceed their benefits. In this instance, the combination therapy of liraglutide and metformin proved both effective and safe, corroborating prior research.

Tailored therapeutic approaches are essential for MODY management, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not fulfill all diagnostic parameters outlined in current clinical recommendations. At present, no reliable biomarkers exist to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 diabetes or T2D. Chen et al demonstrated that, among 2429 young Chinese adults with early-onset T2D, MODY had a prevalence of 1.5%, with 0.74% possessing pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY mutations. Research by Møller et al showed that, in a comprehensive population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian type 1 diabetes patients, 10% of 39 subjects lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes were identified to carry HNF1A mutations. Likewise, a recent Russian population-based investigation examined 506 individuals suspected of having inherited forms of diabetes that did not display autoimmune characteristics—characterized by negative antibody testing for IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 at disease onset, sustained C-peptide levels, minimal insulin requirements within five years, and the absence of marked obesity. The study detected P or LP mutations in 46.4% of participants, with HNF1A being among the most frequently impacted genes. Confirmation of MODY diagnosis requires DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) permits examination of extensive genetic material from patients in a single assay, which enhances the practicality of MODY genetic screening. NGS has led to the identification of numerous novel genetic alterations. The ACMG has established criteria to determine the pathogenic status of these variants. Databases such as ClinVar contain more than 1000 unique HNF1A variants, including pathogenic, likely pathogenic, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In this report, a previously unreported heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not found in ClinVar or other standard variant repositories, emphasizing its uniqueness and not detracting from its potential clinical impact. Notably, this variant alters an amino acid residue adjacent to established pathogenic residues, shows a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and segregates with diabetes in the affected family, satisfying ACMG criteria for an LP classification. Therefore, although ClinVar serves as a comprehensive database for genetic variants, multiple independent genetic and clinical findings affirm the pathogenicity of this variant beyond mere inclusion in a database.

Research has shown that most detected mutations are found within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this case occurs in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations suggest that some HNF1A mutations, while present, exert only a limited influence on HNF1A protein function. Moreover, particular HNF1A variants that are insufficient on their own to cause MODY may nonetheless contribute to an elevated susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the significance of precise identification of HNF1A-MODY and its implications for personalized therapy. Future studies aim to establish more efficient NGS-based screening protocols to enhance MODY detection rates, optimize cost-effectiveness, and improve patient outcomes.
