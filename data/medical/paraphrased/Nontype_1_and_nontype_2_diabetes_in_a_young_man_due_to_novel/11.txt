Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic, autosomal dominant type of diabetes. In this case, a patient with HNF1A-MODY resulting from a novel heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene is discussed. The individual exhibited hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient’s family history included relatives with diabetes developing at a young age. Genetic analysis identified a previously unreported pathogenic variant in HNF1A. Initiation of a GLP-1 receptor agonist alongside metformin therapy led to a decrease in glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a three-month period. This report underscores the significance of genetic evaluation in cases of early-onset diabetes, which are often erroneously categorized as type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and contributes to the growing catalog of HNF1A mutations with implications for individualized treatment strategies.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) encompasses a spectrum of dominantly inherited, non-autoimmune forms of diabetes resulting from an intrinsic abnormality in pancreatic β-cell development and activity. These disorders generally appear and are diagnosed prior to 25 years of age. MODY accounts for roughly 1%–6% of all early-onset diabetes cases. The frequency of MODY varies among different racial and ethnic populations and may frequently be under-recognized. Most existing research has centered on individuals of White European ancestry, but recent investigations have assessed MODY prevalence within Asian and Australian populations. Reported rates of MODY prevalence demonstrate wide variability, which is often attributable to the restriction of genetic testing to subjects identified through specific clinical criteria, even in studies evaluating broader populations.

Pathogenic variants in more than 15 different genes have been associated with the monogenic MODY phenotype, with each gene linked to specific management approaches. Most cases across MODY subtypes arise from mutations in hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, or hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-β genes. HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent subtype, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY cases, and is characterized by a clear autosomal dominant inheritance pattern of symptomatic diabetes within affected families. In early adolescence, affected individuals often lack clinical symptoms, and hyperglycemia is typically discovered incidentally during routine laboratory assessments, commonly without evidence of insulin resistance. Postprandial blood glucose measurement or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) can reveal marked elevations in post-meal glucose (≥11.1 mmol/L). Glycosuria may also be present, explained by a lowered renal threshold for glucose due to impaired tubular glucose reabsorption. In more advanced stages, affected patients can exhibit manifestations such as polydipsia, polyuria, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss, akin to symptoms found in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D). Consequently, the overlap in clinical features makes distinguishing MODY from type 1 and T2D particularly challenging in clinical practice.

Multiple diagnostic pathways for MODY have been outlined in the literature. When physical examination and a detailed family history indicate the likelihood of MODY, and autoantibody tests commonly associated with type 1 diabetes—such as glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8)—yield negative results, further laboratory assessments are performed to corroborate the diagnosis. Genetic analysis is then an essential step in confirming the diagnosis. An accurate diagnosis is vital for selecting optimal therapy, predicting risks of complications, evaluating long-term outlook, and enabling proper genetic counseling for affected families. Recent research has shown that HNF1A-MODY can be caused by a wide spectrum of HNF1A gene mutations.

In this report, we describe the detection of a previously unreported heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), in exon 4 of the HNF1A gene in a patient with HNF1A-MODY, identified using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel. The pathogenic potential of this variant was evaluated following the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant classification. This specific mutation was absent from both published reports and from the VarSome and ClinVar databases. Informed written consent for publication of this case was obtained directly from the patient.

Case presentation:

A young adult was sent for assessment after hyperglycaemia and glycosuria were detected during his routine yearly checkup. On physical examination, he had a BMI of 24.6 kg/m2, a blood pressure reading of 112/70 mmHg, and a pulse of 78 beats per minute. No acanthosis nigricans was present on inspection. The family history revealed several cases of diabetes developing at a young age: his maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her 30s, his mother in her late 20s, and his younger brother at 20 years old. There was no reported occurrence of premature cardiovascular disease in relatives with diabetes.

Investigations:

The patient's HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol), with a fasting plasma glucose of 12.4 mmol/L. After administration of 75 g glucose for an OGTT, the 2-hour plasma glucose was found to be 19.8 mmol/L. There were no indications of diabetic ketoacidosis. He described experiencing polydipsia, polyphagia, and recent unintended weight loss. Lipid studies demonstrated an HDL cholesterol of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides at 4.31 mmol/L, findings compatible with a possible diagnosis of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). His past medical and surgical histories were unremarkable.

A glucagon stimulation test resulted in serum C-peptide levels of 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L six minutes post-stimulation, indicating compromised insulin production. This procedure serves to assess residual pancreatic β-cell function by quantifying the increase in C-peptide following glucagon administration. Typically, a stimulated C-peptide concentration greater than 0.60 nmol/L suggests preserved endogenous insulin output; values below this threshold imply β-cell impairment. Nonetheless, this test is considered supportive and not conclusive, especially in situations where diabetes autoantibody results do not clarify the diagnosis or when the presentation deviates from typical patterns. Microalbuminuria was absent. Evaluation of sudomotor activity, performed using electrochemical skin conductance methods such as Sudoscan, revealed marked reduction in sudomotor function in the hands, pointing to significant small fibre autonomic neuropathy. Autoantibody screening—including tests for GAD antibodies (GADA) and islet antigen 2 antibodies (IA-2A)—was negative. Approximately one month after the initial diagnosis, genetic testing for MODY identified a heterozygous, pathogenic variant in the HNF1A gene (p.N270S, HNF1A:c.809A>G), confirming HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly increased HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The initial therapy included basal insulin degludec at a dose of eight units per day, alongside metformin (1500 mg/day) and saxagliptin (5 mg/day), reflecting an initial working diagnosis of T2D. This regimen was selected to achieve prompt glycaemic control while simultaneously addressing possible insulin resistance.

Beyond pharmacologic measures, the patient received recommendations to follow a structured diet and maintain consistent physical activity as part of an overall diabetes care strategy. Ongoing monitoring was conducted at the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient clinic to evaluate glycaemic status and therapy effectiveness.

Nonetheless, considering the patient’s moderately retained but suboptimal β-cell function indicated by the glucagon stimulation test (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), the absence of islet autoantibodies, and a pronounced family history of early-onset diabetes, there was a strong clinical suspicion for MODY.

Initial treatment was started prior to the availability of genetic testing results, based on the clinical presentation and laboratory findings at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

One month after initial management, the patient’s HbA1c had dropped to 9.1%. Once genetic testing confirmed HNF1A-MODY and considering the patient’s partial but inadequate β-cell function as well as the noted reduction in HbA1c, insulin was discontinued in favor of dulaglutide (GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg administered weekly) to improve glycaemic control and help preserve pancreatic function. After six months of this therapy, HbA1c had fallen to 6.0%, demonstrating marked improvement. With sustained glycaemic stability, therapy was then streamlined to a regimen of metformin (1500 mg per day) combined with saxagliptin, a DPP-4 inhibitor (5 mg per day), for long-term control. The patient continues to attend regular follow-up visits, maintaining HbA1c consistently below 6.5%. Up to the present, there have been no findings of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy, and regular monitoring for chronic complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, situated at chromosomal location 12q24, functions as a transcription factor. It contains 10 exons spread over 23,790 base pairs and codes for a protein consisting of 631 amino acids. This protein is found in multiple tissues, such as the liver, kidney, and pancreas. Mutations affecting HNF1A result in a gradual deterioration of β-cell performance, which presents as diminished expression of the insulin and GLUT2 genes and lower activity of pyruvate kinase. These abnormalities ultimately contribute to decreased insulin production and impaired glucose tolerance.

In this case, the approach to diagnosis followed recognized criteria for MODY, which require integration of clinical presentation, laboratory findings, and molecular testing. Criteria include diabetes appearing early in life, a notable familial pattern, difficulty controlling postprandial blood glucose, and the absence of diabetes-related autoantibodies. A fasting C-peptide value exceeding 0.6 ng/mL is indicative of a diabetes type other than type 1. Despite this, regular re-evaluation of C-peptide levels is advised, since initial normal readings can also occur in early type 1 diabetes. One major obstacle to recognizing MODY is insufficient knowledge and clinical suspicion, leading to frequent misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. The Exeter group’s MODY probability calculator offers support for identifying patients suitable for genetic evaluation; however, its development in a White European cohort means it might yield inflated probability estimates for those from non-White backgrounds. With our patient’s clinical details inputted, the tool estimated a 62.4% (or approximately 1 in 1.6) likelihood of MODY. There are reported instances where autoantibodies are found in MODY patients, including some harboring HNF1A mutations, even though most are negative for autoantibodies. Past research demonstrates that MODY is generally associated with lower frequencies of obesity and metabolic syndrome compared to type 2 diabetes. Nevertheless, rising obesity rates now reduce the discriminatory power of BMI and metabolic syndrome for distinguishing MODY from type 2 diabetes, a trend reflected in the higher BMI observed in our patient. While family history continues to be significant in diagnosing MODY, its relevance may be limited because of incomplete reporting, the occurrence of new mutations, and the widespread prevalence of type 2 diabetes in families.

The management strategy for HNF1A-MODY is influenced by the patient’s age and HbA1c values. Initial therapy typically consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which promote insulin release via action on ATP-sensitive potassium channels. In individuals with HNF1A-MODY, GLP-1 receptor agonists are considered a safer and potentially more effective substitute for sulfonylureas as β-cell functionality diminishes, particularly among those susceptible to hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small, randomized, double-blind, crossover study revealed that both liraglutide and glimepiride substantially reduced fasting and postprandial glucose variability, without significant differences in efficacy among patients with HNF1A-MODY. Furthermore, treatment with GLP-1 receptor agonists carries a diminished risk of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. A precise genetic diagnosis enabled us to stop insulin therapy following glycaemic stabilization and commence GLP-1 receptor agonist treatment, successfully sustaining glycaemic control. This case underscores the essential role of genetic testing in selecting the appropriate therapeutic approach. Glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY arises from decreased SGLT2 expression, as this transporter is regulated by HNF1A. Previous reports have noted that individuals with HNF1A-MODY can develop severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis due to marked suppression of remaining SGLT-2 activity. Consequently, the use of SGLT-2 inhibitors in patients with HNF1A-MODY presents increased risks, such as dehydration, possible vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis, which may surpass the advantages these agents provide. In the present scenario, the combination of liraglutide and metformin proved both efficacious and safe, aligning with findings from earlier reports.

Precision medicine plays a pivotal role in MODY, as individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy all established diagnostic parameters, even when current clinical guidelines are applied. At present, reliable biomarkers that can distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes are lacking. Chen et al reported that, among 2,429 young Chinese adults diagnosed with early-onset type 2 diabetes, MODY accounted for 1.5%, with 0.74% harboring pathogenic or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. Møller et al, studying a large, population-based cohort of Danish Caucasian type 1 diabetes patients, found that 10% of the 39 individuals lacking high-risk HLA haplotypes carried mutations in HNF1A. Likewise, a recent Russian population-based investigation screened 506 patients for hereditary diabetes devoid of autoimmune markers—characterized by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at diagnosis, sustained C-peptide secretion, low insulin needs during the first five years, and the absence of significant obesity. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were identified in 46.4% of this group, with HNF1A among the genes most frequently implicated. Confirming MODY clinical suspicions necessitates DNA sequencing. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) permits analysis of extensive regions of patient DNA simultaneously, enhancing the practicality of screening for MODY. The emergence of NGS has facilitated the discovery of numerous novel genetic variants. The ACMG offers a framework for interpreting the pathogenicity of these variants. Databases such as ClinVar have catalogued more than 1,000 unique HNF1A variants, ranging from pathogenic and likely pathogenic to benign, likely benign, or of uncertain significance. In our patient, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A variant (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not documented in ClinVar or other standard variant repositories, underlining its novelty but not undermining its clinical impact. Notably, this variant is located adjacent to established pathogenic residues, has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and demonstrates co-segregation with diabetes in family members, fulfilling ACMG criteria for classification as LP. Therefore, while ClinVar is instrumental for cataloging published variants, the pathogenicity of this newly detected variant is substantiated by multiple, independent lines of genetic and clinical evidence extending beyond database listing.

Research has shown that most mutations detected are concentrated within the DNA-binding domain, whereas the transactivation domain exhibits a lower frequency of mutations. The novel variant described in this report is also located in the DNA-binding domain. Several publications suggest that certain HNF1A mutations, specifically P, produce only minimal alterations in the function of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants that do not cause MODY independently might nonetheless be linked to an elevated risk of developing T2D.

To summarize, this case underscores the significance of correctly diagnosing HNF1A-MODY, given its implications for personalized therapy. Ongoing investigations will be directed towards enhancing NGS-based screening approaches in order to raise the detection rate of MODY, thereby improving cost-effectiveness and clinical outcomes.
