Title: Non-type 1 and non-type 2 diabetes in a young man due to novel mutation in HNF1A

Yi-Chen Liu, Sheng-Chiang Su, Feng-Chih Kuo, Yi-Jen Hung, Yen-Lin Chen

Abstract:

Summary: Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) is a monogenic, autosomal dominant form of diabetes. This case involves an individual with HNF1A-MODY attributable to a newly identified heterozygous mutation in the HNF1A gene. The clinical presentation included hyperglycaemia and glycosuria without evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient’s family history revealed diabetes diagnosed at a young age. Genetic analysis detected a novel pathogenic variant in HNF1A not previously described. Therapy using a GLP-1 receptor agonist combined with metformin resulted in a decline of glycated haemoglobin from 10.2% to 6.0% over a 3-month period. This report underscores the significance of genetic evaluation in cases of diabetes presenting at a young age—which are often incorrectly classified as type 1 or type 2 diabetes—and adds to the known array of HNF1A variants relevant for individualized treatment approaches.

Background:

Maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) comprises a collection of autosomal dominant, non-autoimmune diabetic conditions marked by an inherent defect in the formation and functionality of pancreatic β-cells. The onset and diagnosis of these disorders generally occurs before 25 years of age. MODY accounts for approximately 1% to 6% of diabetes cases presenting in younger individuals. Prevalence rates of MODY may differ among various ethnic and racial populations, and actual frequencies may be underreported. The majority of current data has primarily been derived from studies involving White European cohorts. More recent investigations have begun to assess MODY prevalence in both Asian and Australian settings. Reported prevalence figures for MODY demonstrate considerable variability, often attributable to the practice of limiting genetic testing to selected patients identified by clinical features, even in population-based studies.

Over 15 distinct gene mutations have been associated with the monogenic phenotype of MODY, and each is linked to unique treatment approaches. Most MODY cases result from alterations in genes such as hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-alpha (HNF1A), glucokinase, hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha, and hepatocyte nuclear factor 1-beta. Among these, HNF1A-MODY is the most prevalent form, accounting for approximately 30%–65% of all MODY diagnoses, and typically demonstrates a clear autosomal dominant inheritance pattern with symptomatic diabetes manifesting in affected families. During early adolescence, affected individuals may lack overt symptoms, with hyperglycemia often being incidentally discovered through routine laboratory screening, generally without any evident signs of insulin resistance. Postprandial glucose measurements or a 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) may reveal a marked rise in glucose levels (≥11.1 mmol/L). Furthermore, glycosuria may occur, which is the result of a decreased renal threshold for glucose reabsorption due to impaired tubular glucose transport. In advanced disease, clinical manifestations such as increased thirst (polydipsia), frequent urination (polyuria), excessive hunger (polyphagia), and weight loss—symptoms similar to those seen in both type 1 and type 2 diabetes (T2D)—can develop. As a result, distinguishing MODY from type 1 or T2D presents a clinical challenge due to the considerable overlap in their presentations.

Various studies have developed diagnostic protocols for MODY. When clinical assessment and family history indicate possible MODY and autoantibody tests for type 1 diabetes, including glutamic acid decarboxylase antibodies (GADA), insulinoma antigen-2 autoantibody (IA-2A), and zinc transporter 8 (ZnT8), return negative results, further biochemical evaluation can be supplemented with molecular genetic analysis, which plays an essential role in confirming the diagnosis. An accurate diagnosis is important for guiding appropriate therapy, predicting likely complications, evaluating prognosis, and informing family counseling. Recent research has shown that HNF1A-MODY can be caused by numerous distinct mutations within the HNF1A gene.

In the current report, a previously undescribed heterozygous variant, p.N270S (HNF1A:c.809A>G), was detected in exon 4 of HNF1A in a patient with HNF1A-MODY through next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel testing. The pathogenicity of this variant was evaluated using the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG) guidelines for variant classification. This genetic change had not been documented in existing literature, the VarSome database, or the ClinVar database. Written informed consent for publication of the case report was obtained directly from the patient.

Case presentation:

A young adult presented for assessment after hyperglycaemia and glycosuria were detected during his routine annual health check. Physical examination revealed a BMI of 24.6 kg/m^2, blood pressure measuring 112/70 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 bpm. No evidence of acanthosis nigricans was found on inspection. The family history was significant for early-onset diabetes mellitus: the patient’s maternal grandmother was diagnosed in her third decade, his mother in her late twenties, and his younger brother at 20 years old. None of these relatives had a documented history of premature cardiovascular disease.

Investigations:

The patient's HbA1c measured 10.2% (88 mmol/mol) and his fasting plasma glucose was 12.4 mmol/L. At two hours following a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), his glucose level was 19.8 mmol/L. There was no evidence of diabetic ketoacidosis. The patient described polydipsia, polyphagia, and unintentional weight loss prior to presentation. Lipid studies identified a high-density lipoprotein of 1.01 mmol/L and triglycerides of 4.31 mmol/L, findings suggestive of metabolic syndrome (refer to tables 1 and 2). He denied any relevant previous medical or surgical conditions.

A glucagon stimulation test found the serum C-peptide to be 0.39 nmol/L at baseline and 0.83 nmol/L at 6 minutes, reflecting compromised insulin release. This assessment quantifies residual β-cell function by measuring C-peptide secretion after glucagon administration. Clinically, a stimulated C-peptide above 0.60 nmol/L is indicative of intact endogenous insulin production, while reduced values point toward β-cell impairment. Nevertheless, the glucagon stimulation test should be interpreted as supplementary rather than confirmatory, especially when antibody assessments are ambiguous or clinical features do not align with typical presentations. Screening for microalbuminuria returned negative. Sudomotor evaluation, performed by electrochemical skin conductance testing (e.g., Sudoscan), a non-invasive modality that assesses small fiber autonomic nerve function, demonstrated markedly diminished conductance in the hands, indicative of substantial autonomic neuropathy. Autoantibody assays for both GADA and islet antigen 2 (IA-2A) were negative. A month following diagnosis, genetic testing for MODY detected a heterozygous pathogenic variant in HNF1A (p.N270S [HNF1A:c.809A>G]), confirming the diagnosis of HNF1A-MODY.

Treatment:

At diagnosis, the patient exhibited markedly raised HbA1c and plasma glucose concentrations. The initial therapy included basal insulin degludec at eight units daily, along with metformin 1500 mg daily and saxagliptin 5 mg daily, reflecting an initial diagnosis of type 2 diabetes. This regimen was chosen to rapidly control hyperglycemia and to manage potential insulin resistance.

The therapeutic plan also included structured dietary guidance and encouragement to maintain regular physical exercise, constituting an integrated diabetes management approach. Glycemic status and treatment effectiveness were monitored during scheduled visits to the endocrinology and metabolism outpatient service.

Nonetheless, due to moderately maintained but inadequate β-cell secretory capacity on glucagon stimulation testing (stimulated C-peptide: 0.83 nmol/L), absence of islet autoantibodies, and a significant familial prevalence of early-onset diabetes, clinical suspicion for MODY remained high.

Initial treatment was started prior to the availability of genetic testing results, based on the clinical presentation and laboratory findings at diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After one month, the patient’s HbA1c fell to 9.1%. With genetic testing confirming HNF1A-MODY and noting the patient’s partially preserved, though inadequate, β-cell function alongside the HbA1c reduction to 9.1%, insulin was stopped. Dulaglutide (a GLP-1 receptor agonist, 1.5 mg weekly) was initiated to enhance glycemic management and aid in the preservation of pancreatic function. Following 6 months on this regimen, the patient’s HbA1c further declined to 6.0%, demonstrating substantial improvement. Once glycemic control stabilized, therapy was transitioned to maintenance with metformin (1500 mg/day) and the DPP-4 inhibitor saxagliptin (5 mg/day). The patient continues with regular monitoring, with HbA1c consistently remaining under 6.5%. No evidence of microalbuminuria or diabetic retinopathy has emerged, and routine screening for long-term complications is ongoing.

Discussion:

The HNF1A gene, situated at chromosome 12q24, codes for a transcription factor composed of 10 exons spanning 23,790 base pairs and produces a 631-amino acid protein that is present in tissues such as the pancreas, liver, and kidneys. Mutations in this gene result in a gradual loss of β-cell functionality, demonstrated by diminished transcription of insulin and GLUT2, as well as decreased pyruvate kinase enzymatic activity. This cascade ultimately impairs insulin release and contributes to abnormal glucose tolerance.

For this case, diagnosis followed established criteria for MODY, incorporating clinical, laboratory, and genetic findings, such as early diabetes onset, strong familial aggregation, abnormal postprandial glucose handling, and absence of diabetes-associated autoantibodies. A preserved fasting C-peptide above 0.6 ng/mL favors a diagnosis other than type 1 diabetes, though ongoing surveillance of C-peptide is crucial, as normal levels can occur early in type 1 diabetes progression. Limited awareness and a low index of suspicion often impede MODY recognition, and misclassification as type 1 or type 2 diabetes is common. The MODY probability calculator, developed by the Exeter group, can help flag candidates for genetic confirmation, but its validity is mainly restricted to White European populations, potentially leading to overestimation in non-White patients. Applying this tool to our patient’s data yielded a MODY probability of 62.4% (or a 1 in 1.6 likelihood). There are case reports of MODY patients with detectable autoantibodies, and some with HNF1A mutations display these, although most individuals with MODY lack autoantibodies. Prior literature indicates that MODY is associated with lower rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome than T2D, but increasing obesity prevalence has blurred BMI-based distinctions between MODY and type 2 diabetes, possibly accounting for the elevated BMI seen here. Moreover, although a positive family history often supports MODY diagnosis, its practical value may be reduced due to under-recognition, novel mutations, or frequent family histories of type 2 diabetes.

Management of HNF1A-MODY depends on the patient's age and HbA1c. Initial treatment usually consists of low-dose sulfonylureas, which stimulate insulin release via potassium-sensitive ATP channels. As β-cell function diminishes, GLP-1 receptor agonists may represent a safer and more efficacious option for individuals with HNF1A-MODY, especially those at higher risk for hypoglycaemia. Evidence from a small, randomised, double-blind, crossover trial has shown that liraglutide and glimepiride are comparably effective in lowering fasting plasma glucose and postprandial glucose excursions in this population, without significant differences in efficacy. However, GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a reduced incidence of mild hypoglycaemia compared to glimepiride. In this patient, confirmation of the genetic diagnosis allowed insulin therapy to be discontinued once glycaemic targets were met, and the patient was switched to a GLP-1 receptor agonist, which continued to provide stable glycaemic control. This case underscores the essential role of genetic testing in therapeutic planning. The presence of glycosuria in HNF1A-MODY is linked to decreased SGLT2 expression regulated by HNF1A. One report described that individuals with HNF1A-MODY can develop severe dehydration and diabetic ketoacidosis as a consequence of marked inhibition of remaining SGLT2 activity. In patients with HNF1A-MODY, potential hazards related to SGLT-2 inhibitor therapy—such as increased likelihood of dehydration, risk of vascular collapse, genital infections, and diabetic ketoacidosis—may surpass any clinical benefit. In this context, the combination of liraglutide and metformin proved both effective and safe, consistent with previously published literature.

Precision medicine plays a key role in managing MODY, since individuals with HNF1A-MODY frequently do not satisfy all clinical diagnostic standards, even when guidelines are followed. At present, no reliable biomarkers are available to distinguish HNF1A-MODY from type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Chen et al reported that among 2,429 Chinese young adults diagnosed with early-onset T2D, MODY was identified in 1.5%, with 0.74% possessing pathogenic (P) or likely pathogenic (LP) MODY variants. In research conducted by Møller et al, 10% of 39 Danish Caucasian type 1 diabetic patients—who lacked any high-risk HLA haplotypes—were found to carry HNF1A mutations. Additionally, a recent Russian population-based investigation screened 506 people suspected of hereditary diabetes without evidence of autoimmunity, defined by negative IAA, ICA, GADA, and ZnT8 antibodies at presentation, retained C-peptide production, reduced insulin needs over five years, and the absence of pronounced obesity. Of these, 46.4% harbored P or LP variants, with HNF1A representing one of the most frequently implicated genes. DNA sequencing remains the definitive method to validate clinical suspicion of MODY. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) facilitates comprehensive analysis of patient DNA in a single assay, enhancing the practicality of MODY detection. Advancements in NGS have uncovered many novel genetic alterations. The ACMG has established frameworks for determining if such variants are pathogenic. Currently, more than 1,000 unique HNF1A variants are catalogued in resources such as ClinVar, including those categorized as P, LP, benign, likely benign, and variants of uncertain significance. In our patient, the newly identified heterozygous HNF1A change (c.809A>G, p.N270S) was not found in ClinVar or other standard variant repositories, demonstrating its novelty without undermining clinical importance. Notably, this mutation affects an amino acid neighboring established P residues, has a high AlphaMissense score (0.995), and co-segregates with diabetes within the family, collectively fulfilling ACMG’s criteria for an LP classification. Therefore, while ClinVar serves as a useful database for documented variants, the pathogenic potential of this newly detected mutation is substantiated through several independent genetic and clinical findings, beyond its representation in variant archives.

Research has shown that most mutations detected are found within the DNA-binding domain, with fewer mutations occurring in the transactivation domain. The novel variant described in this report is located in the DNA-binding domain. Certain investigations have revealed that HNF1A mutations, while present, exert limited influence on the functionality of the HNF1A protein. Furthermore, some HNF1A variants that are insufficient to cause MODY independently may still contribute to a higher susceptibility to T2D.

In summary, this case underscores the necessity for precise identification of HNF1A-MODY, which is essential for optimizing patient-specific therapy. Ongoing studies will prioritize the development of advanced NGS-based screening approaches to enhance the detection rate of MODY, thereby facilitating improved cost efficiency and clinical outcomes.
