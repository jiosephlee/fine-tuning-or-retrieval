Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V. Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the various technical complexities inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve interventions, management must be individualized for each patient. We describe a man in his late 70s with a complicated medical background that included two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced severe degeneration of his aortic prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. His initial therapy consisted of a Lotus valve implanted in 2014, followed by a TAV-in-TAV procedure using an Evolut prosthesis in 2022; this was complicated by endocarditis-related prosthetic degeneration and significant restenosis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 valve was deployed as a third prosthetic using the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV technique. As indications for TAVI broaden and patient survival increases, the occurrence of such complex, multi-prosthesis procedures is likely to rise, underscoring the importance of enhanced procedural planning, advanced imaging, and rigorous long-term monitoring. At a 1-year follow-up, the patient continues to be asymptomatic and valve function remains intact. This report demonstrates the practical application of a third TAVI following prosthetic valve endocarditis, employing CT-guided planning and providing 1-year follow-up data—a scenario infrequently documented in the literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely accepted as a therapeutic choice for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, regardless of surgical risk category. With a rising volume of TAVI procedures and improved patient longevity, clinicians are more frequently facing cases of structural valve degeneration and subsequent requirements for repeat procedures.

Within this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures have become an effective option for addressing the failure of transcatheter aortic valve prostheses. Nonetheless, these interventions pose unique anatomical and procedural difficulties, such as a heightened risk of coronary artery occlusion and a decreased effective orifice area, which may lead to significant prosthesis–patient mismatch. These concerns become even more pronounced with repeat procedures, like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where the complexity introduced by multiple overlapping stent frames must be taken into account.

There is a paucity of data concerning repeated transcatheter valve interventions, especially among individuals with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these complicated cases, thorough imaging before the procedure, tailored risk evaluation, and collaborative decisions involving multiple specialties are crucial to assess procedural suitability and improve outcomes.

This case demonstrates the importance of CT-based procedural planning and targeted transcatheter intervention strategies in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI interventions and previously managed prosthetic valve endocarditis, underscoring crucial aspects relevant to the long-term management of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center due to complaints of shortness of breath, a spasmodic feeling localized to the left side of the chest brought on by exertion such as walking 200–300 meters, as well as episodes of dizziness and generalized fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension and three-vessel coronary artery disease, with several percutaneous coronary interventions: in 2009 for the right coronary artery, in 2010 for the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, in 2012 for a repeat procedure on the LAD, and in 2014 for the circumflex artery. Other conditions consisted of third-degree atrioventricular block managed with pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

In 2014, as a result of advancing aortic valve degeneration and critical stenosis, the patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) via transcatheter implantation. By December 2021, the patient experienced escalating exertional dyspnoea, and echocardiography demonstrated significant stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. In early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had decreased to 42%, down from 54% in December.

In May 2022, the patient received a transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure with placement of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). The intervention was complicated by the onset of a pulsatile hematoma and pseudoaneurysm at the femoral site, necessitating open vascular surgical intervention.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient's left ventricular ejection fraction increased from 42% to 54%, demonstrating an early positive haemodynamic effect. He was treated with standard guideline-recommended heart failure medications, which included beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted once more due to ongoing fever and aggravated symptoms of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed marked restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete regimen of targeted antimicrobial treatment for E. faecalis PVE, consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy, followed by oral antibiotics as per guidance from infectious disease specialists. By the time of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedure, antibiotic therapy had concluded, and there was no ongoing suppressive antibiotic treatment. Comprehensive clinical evaluation, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) confirmed the infection had resolved, with no signs of current endocarditis.

After finishing the course of antimicrobial treatment and experiencing some improvement in symptoms, a follow-up visit in January 2024 showed deterioration in the performance of the prosthetic valve, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate to severe aortic regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient measured 49 mm Hg, and the left ventricular ejection fraction had improved to 66%.

It was decided to undertake a repeat intervention. Upon hospital admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed ongoing grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly diminished aortic valve area (AVA) measuring 0.5 cm², findings indicative of severe dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA was determined via the continuity equation, utilizing measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract alongside Doppler-obtained velocity-time integrals. Visualization confirmed the presence of two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT identified a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut valve, indicating marked haemodynamic impairment and informing the approach for a planned third transcatheter valve placement (figure 1). The preprocedural MSCT assessment was utilized to evaluate both the anatomical suitability and the likelihood of coronary artery obstruction. This assessment involved detailed measurement of the coronary ostial height, the dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, valve-to-coronary distances, and interactions among the stent frames of the prior transcatheter valves. Specific focus was directed toward analyzing the spatial relationship between the predicted neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, and determining the appropriateness of a high implantation technique to maintain coronary perfusion and ensure future coronary access. These considerations influenced the choice of valve, implantation position, and overall procedural approach, permitting the intervention to proceed without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed prior to the reintervention and confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Considering the patient’s intricate anatomy with three prior stents, history of infective endocarditis, and high surgical risk, a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was scheduled. The procedure was carefully planned using CT imaging, focusing on strategies to safeguard the coronary arteries, achieve commissural alignment, and maximize the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through left femoral artery access. The aortic valve was pre-dilated using a 22/40 mm balloon catheter while rapid ventricular pacing was maintained at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortographic imaging verified that coronary perfusion was not compromised (figure 3).

A self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (Acurate neo2, size S) was placed in the aortic position. Alignment of the device with the Evolut valve leaflets was performed using preprocedural CT modeling to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the chance of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was utilized for post-dilation under rapid pacing to enhance valve expansion. Subsequent aortography demonstrated proper placement, maintained coronary flow, and no evidence of either perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (see figure 4).

Valve deployment incorporated consideration of commissural alignment in order to maintain coronary accessibility and minimize complexity in potential subsequent procedures, utilizing both preprocedure CT scans and fluoroscopic reference points for guidance.

Outcome and follow-up:

The evaluation of procedural outcomes and prosthetic valve function was carried out using the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 standards, which encompass assessment of haemodynamic performance, detection of any remaining regurgitation, and identification of complications related to the procedure.

A transthoracic echocardiogram obtained immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 verified appropriate positioning of the third transcatheter valve. The valve leaflets demonstrated full mobility with complete excursion and there was no indication of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Nonetheless, the haemodynamic assessment revealed markedly elevated transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The measured aortic valve area was 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured 0.24, findings consistent with severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

At one month post-implantation, transthoracic echocardiography revealed significant improvement: the mean gradient measured 27 mm Hg, and the aortic valve area had expanded to 1.1 cm². Remodeling of the left ventricle was evident, characterized by decreased chamber volumes. There was no remaining regurgitation, and the valve continued to function stably, consistent with moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months post-procedure, the patient continued to exhibit no symptoms and demonstrated satisfactory exercise capacity. There were no indications of heart failure or relapse of infection. The prosthetic device continued to function well, with persistently favorable haemodynamic measurements.

One year after the device implantation (June 2025), the patient continued to do well clinically, maintaining an active lifestyle without shortness of breath or evidence of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mean transvalvular gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 1.1 cm², and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%. There were no observed valve-associated adverse events, regurgitation, or signs suggestive of recurrent endocarditis.

These findings confirm the durability of the result and suggest a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case highlights the decision-making process and technical challenges of performing a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV procedures and a history of PVE.

Although TAVI is now a standard therapy for aortic stenosis, there is ongoing debate regarding its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advocate for surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach in cases of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially when associated with heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines highlight the importance of tailoring management to each patient by considering the degree of infection control, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

In 2014, TAVI was selected as the initial management approach due to the patient’s elevated operative risk and several coexisting conditions, which rendered standard surgical valve replacement unfavorable at that stage. When the patient later experienced structural degeneration of the valve, a transcatheter repeat procedure was carried out in 2022 to re-establish valve function, thereby circumventing the hazards associated with open-heart surgery. After effective PVE treatment and with no evidence of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter intervention was chosen in 2024 owing to persistently prohibitive surgical risk, challenging anatomical issues arising from the existing valve structures, and CT demonstration that this approach would maintain coronary patency and deliver satisfactory haemodynamic results.

In this case, failure of the prosthetic valve appeared to result from several contributing factors. Although infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage and consequent mechanical impairment, ongoing deterioration of the transcatheter valve structure was also a significant factor. Notably, after resolution of the infection and in the absence of a need for long-term suppressive antibiotics, the approach to managing prosthetic valve dysfunction was consistent with strategies used for patients without current or persistent endocarditis. As such, procedural planning was chiefly determined by anatomical suitability, the degree of haemodynamic compromise, and the level of surgical risk, rather than by the antecedent infectious episode alone.

In this instance, the patient had finished their course of antimicrobial treatment and exhibited neither clinical nor radiographic indications of ongoing infection. Comprehensive preprocedural assessment—including both TEE and MSCT—confirmed there were no vegetations or paravalvular complications present. Additionally, the patient’s EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, and their medical history was notable for multiple comorbid conditions (COPD, diabetes, and prior gastrointestinal hemorrhage), along with two previously placed transcatheter valves showing substantial stent incorporation into the aortic wall. These factors contributed to an elevated surgical risk, making traditional open surgery particularly dangerous and complex. Owing to the deep-seated nature of the existing prostheses, an operative approach would likely require complete replacement of the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple reports describing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions have been documented in the literature,37 illustrating a range of anatomical and technical difficulties encountered. This case further contributes to this scarce evidence base, distinguished by the occurrence of post-endocarditis degeneration and maintained prosthetic valve function after one year.

This case highlights the increasing significance of comprehensive, lifelong approaches to managing structural valve disease. Successive transcatheter reinterventions introduce additional challenges, including stacked device frames, alignment of commissures, the ability to access coronary arteries, and maintenance of adequate effective orifice area. Proactive planning and early conversations regarding procedural options and future interventions are essential, starting with the initial procedure—especially for patients who are younger or considered high-risk.

In this patient, structural valve degeneration was characterized by leaflet fibrosis due to previous degeneration and likely mechanical limitation after infection. The medical team elected to implant a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—after preprocedural CT imaging and three-dimensional modeling confirmed procedural feasibility and sufficient coronary clearance (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The third transcatheter valve was intentionally implanted at a high position. Placement at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets secured the prior leaflets against the stent frame, which helped avoid further decrease in the effective orifice area that could result from leaflet infolding. Moreover, this technique reduced the risk of forming a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, which supported preservation of coronary perfusion and maintained access for future coronary interventions. The procedure was closely informed by preprocedural CT analysis and was vital to obtaining a satisfactory hemodynamic outcome in the context of three overlapping stent frames. Although placing three transcatheter valves presents considerable complexity, this technique proved both technically practical and reproducible with thorough preprocedural imaging and a well-defined procedural plan.

The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet placement, comparatively short frame, and advantageous haemodynamic properties in restricted anatomies. The prosthesis is engineered to facilitate commissural alignment, an important factor in this scenario to maintain the potential for future coronary interventions.

A significant technical issue in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions is the potential for restricted coronary access resulting from several overlapping stent frames. Even with efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the aggregate mesh structure can complicate subsequent coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural planning for patients with established coronary artery disease or where future coronary access is likely to be necessary. Preprocedural CT was utilized to assess the landing site, examine the interaction between frames, and approximate the valve-to-coronary distances; however, comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis was not conducted with specialized software.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third implantation likely resulted from several factors: improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilatation, and partial mechanical widening of the pre-existing Evolut stent, which together facilitated better leaflet movement and central hemodynamics. Although a deliberate frame fracture was not attempted, post-dilatation with a high-pressure balloon probably played a role in expanding the Evolut frame radially, leading to enhanced valve performance.

The intervention took place with institutional authorization for the off-label application of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. The patient and his family participated in shared decision-making, and the case was assessed during a multidisciplinary heart team conference that included interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the existing prosthesis were to fail earlier than expected, the available therapeutic avenues would be severely constrained. Surgical removal would still pose significant technical challenges and carry considerable risk. Novel transcatheter techniques, including bioprosthetic valve fracture or intentional leaflet laceration—such as bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—could be contemplated, but their practicality and safety in cases involving triple-stented anatomy are still not well established.
