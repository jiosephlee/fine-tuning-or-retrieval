Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the varied technical difficulties inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, therapeutic approaches should be customized for each patient. We report the case of a male patient in his late seventies with a complicated medical background, including two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced advanced deterioration of his aortic prosthesis that necessitated a third transcatheter intervention. The patient initially received a Lotus valve in 2014, followed by a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis in 2022, and subsequently developed valve degeneration with severe restenosis associated with prior endocarditis. In 2024, a third prosthetic device (Acurate neo2) was deployed via the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV method. As TAVI is increasingly indicated and patient survival improves, complex procedures involving multiple prosthetic valves may arise more frequently, emphasizing the importance of advanced planning, optimized imaging protocols, and continuous long-term follow-up. At a 1-year evaluation, the patient demonstrates no symptoms and maintains satisfactory valve performance. This report exemplifies the practicality of performing a third TAVI following successfully managed prosthetic valve endocarditis, aided by CT-based procedural preparation and supported by a year-long follow-up—a scenario rarely documented in the literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely accepted for patients suffering from severe aortic stenosis, encompassing a diverse spectrum of surgical risk categories. With the increasing numbers of TAVI procedures and improved patient longevity, issues such as structural deterioration of the implanted valve and subsequent repeat interventions are becoming more prevalent in clinical settings.

To address failure of transcatheter prosthetic valves, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) techniques have been introduced as effective management options. Nonetheless, these interventions bring specific anatomical and procedural complications, including potential coronary artery blockage and diminished effective orifice area, which may result in significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These difficulties are magnified in repeat procedures, such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where multiple superimposed valve frameworks require careful attention.

Currently, data concerning sequential transcatheter valve reintervention is sparse, especially among patients with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). For such challenging cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, personalized risk evaluation, and collaborative multidisciplinary planning are crucial to assess procedural feasibility and maximize the chances of successful outcomes.

This report demonstrates how CT-based planning and a tailored interventional approach were used for a patient with several previous TAVI procedures and managed PVE, underscoring important factors for long-term structural valve disease care.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after presenting with shortness of breath, a spasmodic sensation on the left side of his chest during physical activity (walking 200–300 m), episodes of dizziness, and generalized fatigue.

His prior medical history included arterial hypertension, triple-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: in 2009 (right coronary artery), 2010 (left anterior descending (LAD) artery), 2012 (repeat LAD intervention), and 2014 (circumflex artery). Other notable conditions were third-degree atrioventricular block with pacemaker placement (2014), type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022.

In 2014, due to worsening aortic valve degeneration and critical stenosis, he underwent transcatheter implantation of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, his exertional dyspnoea had intensified, and echocardiography demonstrated severe stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. By early 2022, the mean gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg and the ejection fraction (EF) had dropped to 42%, compared to 54% the previous December.

In May 2022, he received a TAV-in-TAV procedure with implantation of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). This intervention was complicated by a pulsatile femoral haematoma and pseudoaneurysm, necessitating open vascular surgery.

Post-procedure, his left ventricular EF increased from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive early haemodynamic outcome. He was treated with guideline-recommended heart failure medications, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted due to ongoing fever and worsening symptoms of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) identified severe restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures isolated Enterococcus faecalis, establishing a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete targeted antibiotic regimen for E. faecalis-associated PVE, which comprised 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy, followed by oral antibiotics as per infectious disease specialist guidance. By the time the third TAVI procedure was considered, antimicrobial therapy was finalized, and long-term suppressive antibiotics were not administered. Subsequent clinical examination, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) verified resolution of infection with no detectable signs of ongoing endocarditis.

Following antimicrobial completion and some symptomatic improvement, a January 2024 follow-up assessment indicated deterioration in prosthetic valve performance, demonstrating severe aortic stenosis accompanied by moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean pressure gradient had increased to 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction had recovered to 66%.

A reintervention was therefore planned. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE revealed persistent moderate-to-severe (grade 2–3) intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a sustained mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², consistent with advanced prosthetic valve dysfunction. The AVA was determined using the continuity equation, with inputs from left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler velocity-time integrals. Visualization showed two overlapping bioprosthetic valves in place.

MSCT identified the minimum lumen diameter within the Evolut prosthesis as 13.7 mm, confirming marked haemodynamic impairment and informing procedural planning for a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT enabled detailed evaluation of anatomical suitability and potential coronary artery obstruction. The assessment included measurement of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva size, the distance from prosthetic valve to coronaries, and the spatial relationship between the frames of the previously placed transcatheter valves. Special attention was devoted to analyzing the interface between the anticipated neo-skirt of the new valve and coronary ostia, as well as the potential for a high implant position to optimize coronary patency and future access. These results influenced selection of valve type, implantation depth, and overall procedural technique, facilitating a strategy that did not require coronary protection measures.

Prior to the reintervention, transoesophageal echocardiography confirmed there was no evidence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Due to the patient's challenging anatomy involving three stents, history of infective endocarditis, and significant surgical risk, the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was undertaken with CT-guidance, focusing on strategies such as coronary protection, precise commissural alignment, and ensuring optimal residual effective orifice area.

The intervention was carried out under local anesthesia utilizing left radial and left femoral artery access. Severe aortic regurgitation was confirmed via aortography employing a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was inserted into the left ventricle through the left femoral artery. The aortic valve was pre-dilated with a 22/40 mm balloon catheter during rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography verified unobstructed coronary flow (figure 3).

A self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (Acurate neo2, size S) was positioned at the aortic location. The deployment was guided by preprocedural CT modeling and aligned with the existing Evolut valve leaflets, aiming to maximize the effective orifice area and minimize the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was used for post-dilation under rapid pacing to further enhance valve expansion. Final aortography demonstrated accurate placement, maintained coronary perfusion, and no evidence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Commissural alignment was carefully managed during valve placement to retain coronary access and decrease the likelihood of procedural difficulties in the future, using preprocedural CT scans and fluoroscopic markers as guides.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural success and the function of the prosthetic valve were determined based on Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 standards, encompassing assessments of haemodynamic performance, detection of any residual regurgitation, and occurrence of procedure-related adverse events.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) conducted immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 verified that the third transcatheter valve was correctly positioned. The valve leaflets demonstrated normal mobility and complete opening, with no aortic regurgitation detected (grade 0). Nevertheless, haemodynamic measurements revealed high transvalvular gradients, as evidenced by a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The calculated aortic valve area (AVA) was 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured 0.24, which indicated severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

One month following implantation, TTE demonstrated substantial improvement: the mean gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg, and AVA expanded to 1.1 cm². The left ventricle exhibited signs of remodelling, including decreased chamber size. No persistent regurgitation was observed, and valve function stabilized within the moderate stenosis range.

By 3.5 months, the patient reported no symptoms and showed good tolerance for physical activity. There was no evidence of heart failure or recurring infection. The prosthetic valve continued to perform well, with ongoing improvement in haemodynamic profiles.

At the one-year mark post implantation (June 2025), the patient remained clinically stable and active, exhibiting neither dyspnoea nor clinical signs of decompensation. TTE findings included a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. There were no valve-related complications, regurgitation, or signs of recurrent endocarditis.

Collectively, these observations support the longevity and sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach in this patient with elevated procedural risk.

Discussion:

This case illustrates the complexities involved in clinical decision-making and procedural technique when undertaking a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient who previously underwent TAV-in-TAV procedures and has a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI is now a well-established therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in patients with antecedent endocarditis remains subject to debate. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for managing valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advise surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach in PVE, especially when complicated by heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines underscore the need for an individualized management plan, incorporating considerations for infection control, surgical risk, and anatomical viability.

TAVI was initially selected as the treatment modality in 2014 due to the patient’s elevated surgical risk and extensive comorbidities, which rendered standard surgical valve replacement an unsuitable option at that time. Following the development of structural valve degeneration, a repeat transcatheter approach was implemented in 2022 to reestablish valve function while avoiding the hazards associated with open-heart surgery. After successful management of PVE and confirmation of absence of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter intervention was performed in 2024. This decision was informed by the presence of unacceptable surgical risk, intricate anatomical challenges associated with previously implanted valve frames, and CT-based assessment supporting the feasibility of a procedure aimed at maintaining coronary flow and delivering satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

The failure of the prosthetic valve in this patient appeared to result from multiple contributing factors. The episode of infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage, causing mechanical restriction, while ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves substantially contributed to dysfunction. Crucially, after confirmation that the infection had resolved and no extended antibiotic suppression was indicated, the therapeutic approach to the prosthetic valve disorder followed standard practices for patients not affected by persistent or active endocarditis. Thus, procedural choices were determined largely by anatomical suitability, degree of haemodynamic compromise, and operative risk, rather than solely by the earlier infectious episode.

For this patient, completion of antimicrobial treatment was documented, with both clinical assessment and imaging—specifically TEE and MSCT—demonstrating the absence of active infection, vegetations, or paravalvular abnormalities following comprehensive preprocedural work-up. The patient’s EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, and multiple significant comorbidities were present (COPD, diabetes, previous gastrointestinal hemorrhage), in addition to two prior transcatheter valve implants showing considerable integration of their stents within the aortic wall. These factors made open-heart surgery exceptionally risky and challenging. Due to the deep fixation of the earlier devices, surgical management would likely require replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Several published case reports have described TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 each illustrating distinct anatomical and procedural difficulties. Our report contributes to this small collection, presenting a rare scenario of degeneration following endocarditis and maintaining valve function one year post-procedure.

This case highlights the increasing significance of comprehensive lifetime management in structural valve disease. As patients undergo repeated transcatheter procedures, factors including layered stent frames, commissural orientation, access to coronary arteries, and the remaining effective orifice area become progressively complicated. Early and careful consideration of future procedural pathways is imperative—particularly at the initial intervention—for younger individuals and those with elevated risk.

The patient’s structural valve deterioration involved fibrotic thickening of the leaflets resulting from earlier degeneration and suspected mechanical constraint induced by infection. The team opted to implant a third prosthetic valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 device—after preprocedural CT imaging and 3D modeling indicated the procedure was viable with sufficient clearance of the coronary arteries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The third valve was intentionally positioned high. Deploying the prosthesis at the plane of the Evolut valve’s leaflets enabled the immobilization of the previous leaflets against the stent frame, which helped avert additional compromise of the effective orifice area from leaflet inward folding. Moreover, this technique reduced formation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia, thereby maintaining coronary perfusion and facilitating potential future coronary interventions. The choice of this approach was informed by preprocedural CT and proved fundamental for achieving satisfactory haemodynamics within a triple-stent structure. Though the procedure involved a complex three-layer transcatheter valve setup, the method was feasible and could be replicated, provided that detailed imaging and a preset strategy guided the intervention.

The Acurate neo2 prosthesis was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet orientation, relatively low stent frame height, and advantageous haemodynamic properties in restricted anatomies. The device’s design facilitates commissural alignment, which was prioritized in this scenario to ensure the possibility of future coronary intervention.

An important technical issue in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures is the potential for reduced coronary accessibility resulting from multiple, overlapping stent frames. Despite efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the accumulated stent mesh could still hinder future coronary procedures. This underscores the necessity for thorough procedural planning, particularly in patients with existing coronary disease or those who might require subsequent coronary access. Preprocedural CT scans were utilized to measure the landing zone, assess possible interactions between valve frames, and estimate the distances between the valve and coronary arteries; however, dedicated software for detailed three-dimensional expansion analysis was not used.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implantation likely stemmed from the optimized commissural positioning, successful post-dilatation, and partial mechanical expansion of the previously implanted Evolut frame, all contributing to enhanced leaflet mobility and improved central flow. Although no deliberate frame fracture was undertaken, high-pressure balloon post-dilation probably resulted in additional radial expansion of the Evolut frame, further optimizing valve function.

Institutional approval was obtained for the off-label application of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis in this procedure. Shared decision-making included the patient and his family, and the case was evaluated by a multidisciplinary heart team comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anaesthesiologists.

In the event of premature prosthesis failure, available treatment options would be highly restricted. Surgical removal would pose significant technical challenges and carry considerable risk. Alternative catheter-based techniques, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration procedures (for example, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction [BASILICA]), could potentially be explored, although their practicality and safety in cases involving triple-stented valves remain unresolved.
