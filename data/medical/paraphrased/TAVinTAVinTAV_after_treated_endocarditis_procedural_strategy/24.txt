Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the various technical complexities involved in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures, individualized treatment plans are essential for each patient. We describe the case of a male patient in his late 70s with a complicated medical background, including two prior transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently developed significant structural failure of the aortic prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The patient initially received a Lotus valve in 2014, underwent TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut device in 2022, and later experienced endocarditis-associated deterioration and severe restenosis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 prosthesis was placed using a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As TAVI indications broaden and the life expectancy of treated patients increases, complex cases involving multiple prostheses are likely to become more prevalent, underscoring the importance of optimized procedural planning, advanced imaging, and ongoing surveillance. At 1 year post-procedure, the patient remains symptom-free with well-preserved prosthetic function. This case demonstrates the practicality of a third TAVI following managed prosthetic valve endocarditis, utilizing CT-based planning and long-term follow-up, a scenario that remains rarely documented.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely accepted for the treatment of severe aortic stenosis in patients with varying levels of surgical risk. With the increasing numbers of TAVI procedures performed and improved long-term survival rates, clinicians are encountering structural valve deterioration and the need for repeat procedures more frequently.

To address failed transcatheter prosthetic valves, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) techniques have been developed as an effective management option. Nonetheless, these procedures present specific anatomical and technical challenges, such as heightened risk for coronary artery occlusion and diminished effective orifice area, which can lead to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These complexities are further compounded in repeated interventions—such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV)—where the presence of several overlapping valve stents necessitates meticulous planning.

Data pertaining to successive transcatheter valve reinterventions are sparse, especially in individuals with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these intricate clinical circumstances, thorough preprocedural imaging, tailored risk evaluation, and collaborative decision-making among a multidisciplinary team are critical for assessing procedural feasibility and achieving optimal results.

The presented case demonstrates the application of CT-based procedural planning and strategic transcatheter therapy in a patient with multiple previous TAVI procedures and managed PVE, underscoring important aspects for the long-term approach to structural valve disease.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late 70s was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after experiencing shortness of breath, a spastic-type sensation localized to the left chest during exertion (noted after walking 200–300 m), episodes of dizziness, and general fatigue.

His past medical background included arterial hypertension, triple-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: the right coronary artery in 2009, the left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, a repeat procedure on the LAD in 2012, and the circumflex artery in 2014. Other chronic conditions included third-degree atrioventricular block that necessitated pacemaker placement in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and repeated gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022.

Progression of aortic valve disease with severe stenosis in 2014 prompted transcatheter implantation of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, he reported aggravated exertional dyspnoea, and echocardiography revealed significant bioprosthetic valve stenosis. At the beginning of 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, while the left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had dropped further to 42%, compared to 54% recorded in December.

The patient underwent a transcatheter valve-in-valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure in May 2022 with placement of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). Postprocedure, he developed a pulsatile hematoma and pseudoaneurysm at the femoral access site, necessitating surgical intervention.

Following the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient’s left ventricular EF rose from 42% to 54%, reflecting an early positive hemodynamic effect. His management included guideline-based heart failure therapy, comprising beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted with ongoing fever and progressive heart failure manifestations. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures yielded Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient completed an appropriate regimen of targeted antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis PVE, consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral antibiotics as recommended by the infectious diseases team. By the time the third TAVI procedure was planned, the antimicrobial course had concluded, with no ongoing long-term suppressive antibiotics. Clinical evaluations, repeat blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) all confirmed eradication of the infection, with no signs of active endocarditis.

After antimicrobial therapy was finished and there was partial improvement in symptoms, follow-up in January 2024 demonstrated deterioration of the prosthetic valve, with findings of severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient had increased to 49 mm Hg, and the left ventricular ejection fraction had recovered to 66%.

A plan was formulated for another intervention. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE revealed ongoing grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a markedly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², indicating significant prosthetic valve dysfunction. The AVA calculation used the continuity equation, derived from left ventricular outflow tract measurements and Doppler velocity-time integrals. Two overlapping bioprosthetic valves were visualized.

MSCT demonstrated a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, confirming severe hemodynamic impairment and aiding in the planning for a third transcatheter valve placement (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT assessment addressed anatomical suitability and the potential for coronary artery obstruction, including evaluations of coronary ostium height, sinus of Valsalva size, valve-to-coronary distance, and interactions between the frames of prior transcatheter valves. Special emphasis was placed on the spatial relationship between the expected neo-skirt and coronary ostia, as well as the practicality of high implantation to maintain coronary perfusion and allow future coronary access. These anatomical considerations informed the selection of valve type, implantation depth, and procedural approach, ultimately enabling the procedure to be carried out without coronary protection.

Pre-intervention transoesophageal echocardiography confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Considering the patient’s intricate triple-stented anatomy, history of infective endocarditis, and excessive surgical risk, the third TAVI was undertaken as a strategy-based, CT-guided intervention, focusing on coronary protection, precise commissural alignment, and preservation of optimal residual effective orifice area.

The intervention proceeded under local anesthesia, utilizing left radial and left femoral arterial approaches. Severe aortic regurgitation was verified by aortography with a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle via the left femoral access. Pre-dilatation of the aortic valve employed a 22/40 mm balloon catheter during rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography indicated unimpeded coronary blood flow (figure 3).

Implantation of a self-expanding bioprosthesis (Acurate neo2, size S) was conducted at the aortic valve. Device orientation was matched with Evolut valve leaflets based on preprocedural CT simulation to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was subsequently used for post-dilation under rapid pacing to ensure optimal prosthesis expansion. The concluding aortogram demonstrated satisfactory prosthesis position, ongoing coronary flow, and no evidence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Commissural alignment during deployment was deliberately planned, utilizing preprocedural CT and fluoroscopic reference points, to safeguard future coronary access and minimize subsequent procedural complexity.

Outcome and follow-up:

The success of the procedure and the performance of the prosthetic valve were evaluated according to Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, which included assessing haemodynamic parameters, detecting any residual regurgitation, and monitoring for procedure-related adverse events.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) performed immediately following the intervention on 7 June 2024 demonstrated correct placement of the third transcatheter valve. The valve leaflets appeared fully mobile and unobstructed, with no signs of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Despite this, haemodynamic assessment revealed elevated transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient measuring 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated as 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24, findings indicative of severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

On repeat TTE at 1 month post-procedure, substantial improvement was noted: the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg, and AVA increased to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular reverse remodelling was present, with decreased chamber sizes. No residual regurgitation was seen, and the valve continued to function reliably, falling within the range for moderate stenosis.

By 3.5 months post-implantation, the patient exhibited no symptoms and maintained excellent exercise capacity. There was no clinical or echocardiographic evidence of heart failure or infection recurrence. The prosthetic valve preserved stable function with persistently favourable haemodynamic indices.

At the 1-year follow-up in June 2025, the patient remained active and clinically stable without dyspnoea or signs of heart failure. TTE indicated a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. No valve-related complications, regurgitation, or recurrence of endocarditis were observed.

These serial findings demonstrate the sustained durability and functional advantage of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach in this high-risk individual.

Discussion:

This case underscores the complex decision-making and technical difficulties encountered during a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient with previous TAV-in-TAV interventions and a background of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI is now a recognized modality for treating aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis remains debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines addressing valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advocate for surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach in PVE cases, especially when heart failure or refractory infection are present. Nevertheless, the guidelines highlight the need to tailor therapy, weighing factors such as infection eradication, the risks associated with surgery, and anatomical suitability.

The initial selection of TAVI in 2014 was prompted by the patient’s elevated surgical risk and substantial comorbidities, which rendered standard surgical valve replacement less favorable. Following the onset of structural valve deterioration, a transcatheter re-intervention was chosen in 2022 to re-establish valve functionality while mitigating the hazards of an open surgical approach. After effective PVE management and confirmation of infection resolution, a third transcatheter procedure was elected in 2024. This choice reflected unacceptably high surgical risk, intricate anatomical issues due to prior valve prostheses, and preprocedural CT imaging confirming that the procedure could maintain coronary perfusion and deliver satisfactory haemodynamic results.

The reasons for prosthetic valve failure in this patient appeared to be complex, involving multiple factors. Although the occurrence of infective endocarditis may have resulted in leaflet damage and subsequent mechanical dysfunction, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves was also a major contributing factor. Notably, after infection was considered resolved and there was no indication for continuing suppressive antibiotic treatment, the management of prosthetic valve dysfunction was consistent with the approach used in patients without active or residual endocarditis. Consequently, the selection of procedural interventions was determined chiefly by anatomical suitability, severity of haemodynamic compromise, and perioperative risk, rather than by the history of infection.

In this scenario, the patient had finished a course of antimicrobial therapy, did not exhibit any clinical or imaging evidence of persisting infection, and underwent extensive preprocedure assessment—including TEE and MSCT—that verified no vegetations or paravalvular abnormalities. Additionally, the patient had a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, multiple comorbid conditions (COPD, diabetes, previous gastrointestinal bleeding), and two earlier transcatheter prostheses with substantial stent incorporation into the aortic wall. These factors made open cardiac surgery extremely high-risk and posed significant technical challenges. With the previous valves anchored deeply, surgical management would likely have required replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple published case reports have documented TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures,37 illustrating a range of anatomical and procedural difficulties. Our report contributes additional evidence, distinguished by the occurrence of valve deterioration following endocarditis and maintained valve performance at the one-year mark.

This case highlights the increasing relevance of lifelong management approaches in structural valve disorders. With each subsequent transcatheter intervention, factors such as multiple stent frames, alignment of commissures, accessibility of the coronary arteries, and the remaining effective orifice area present greater complexity. Early consideration and planning regarding procedural options are essential from the initial intervention, especially for younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

The patient’s structural valve deterioration featured leaflet fibrosis attributed to prior degeneration, with likely mechanical impairment subsequent to infection. The clinical team selected implantation of a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 device—following assessment with preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modelling, which indicated technical viability and sufficient clearance of the coronary arteries (figures 1, 5 and 7). Intentional high placement of the third transcatheter valve was chosen as a procedural tactic. Positioning the prosthesis at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets secured the existing leaflets against the stent framework, thereby avoiding further reduction of the effective orifice area by preventing leaflet folding inward. Moreover, this technique limited the development of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, helping to safeguard coronary flow and facilitate future coronary access. This methodology was informed by preprocedural CT findings and was pivotal to obtaining favorable haemodynamics in the setting of three overlapping stent frames. Although triple transcatheter valve stacking poses significant technical challenges, this strategy was achievable and repeatable with comprehensive imaging and a predetermined implantation plan.

The Acurate neo2 device was chosen because its supra-annular leaflet position, relatively short frame, and advantageous haemodynamic characteristics suit constricted anatomies. Its structural design facilitates commissural alignment, which was particularly important in this case to maintain the possibility of future coronary intervention.

In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures, a major technical challenge is maintaining coronary access when multiple stent frames overlap. Even with efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the accumulated mesh may still complicate subsequent coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of careful procedural planning, especially for patients with established coronary disease or those in whom future access could be critical. Preprocedural computed tomography (CT) was used to determine the landing area, assess the interactions between stent frames, and estimate the space between the valve and coronary arteries, though comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis with specialized software was not performed.

Elimination of the transvalvular gradient after the third valve placement is likely due to the combination of improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the previous Evolut frame, resulting in enhanced leaflet movement and improved central flow. Although frame fracture was not intentionally attempted, post-dilation using a high-pressure balloon appears to have further expanded the Evolut frame radially, thereby supporting improved prosthesis function.

Institutional approval was obtained for the off-label implantation of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. Shared decision-making included discussions with the patient and his family, and the case received review by a multidisciplinary heart team, which comprised interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the current prosthesis fails unexpectedly, treatment choices would be severely restricted. Surgical removal would be highly technical and carry significant risk. Alternative transcatheter options, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration (for example, BASILICA), may be explored, though their practicality and safety in patients with three overlapping stents are not yet established.
