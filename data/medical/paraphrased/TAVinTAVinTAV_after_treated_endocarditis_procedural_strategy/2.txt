Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the varied and intricate technical issues inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve interventions, management decisions must be personalized for each patient. Here, we detail the clinical course of a male patient in his late 70s, characterized by significant comorbidities including two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures and a history of infective endocarditis. Subsequently, the patient exhibited advanced deterioration of the aortic prosthetic valve, necessitating a third transcatheter intervention. The initial intervention in 2014 involved implantation of a Lotus valve, which was succeeded by a TAV-in-TAV using an Evolut prosthesis in 2022. Following this, the patient developed prosthetic valve degeneration associated with infective endocarditis and significant restenosis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 valve was successfully placed utilizing a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As TAVI indications continue to broaden and patients experience increased survival, the frequency of such technically demanding, multiple-prosthesis cases is likely to rise, underscoring the importance of advancements in procedural planning, imaging methodologies, and extended follow-up protocols. One year after the third procedure, the patient exhibits no symptoms and demonstrates maintained prosthetic valve function. This report demonstrates the practicality of performing a third TAVI procedure post-prosthetic valve endocarditis, guided by CT-based procedural planning and documented 1-year follow-up—a scenario seldom addressed in the current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a well-recognized therapy for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, regardless of their surgical risk category. With the expanding use of TAVI and the corresponding improvement in long-term patient outcomes, clinicians are encountering an increasing incidence of structural valve degeneration and subsequent necessity for repeated procedures.

Within this clinical landscape, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) interventions have proven to be a practical solution for addressing malfunctioning transcatheter valves. Nevertheless, these procedures present distinct anatomic and technical complexities, notably the potential for obstructing the coronary arteries and the likelihood of diminishing the effective orifice area, potentially leading to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. The complexity is compounded in instances of successive interventions—such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV)—where the presence of several superimposed prosthetic frames necessitates thorough procedural planning.

There remains a scarcity of robust data on the outcomes of repeated transcatheter valve procedures, especially in cases involving previous prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Managing such challenging situations requires comprehensive preprocedural imaging, personalised assessment of risks, and collaborative multidisciplinary planning to determine procedural suitability and enhance success rates.

The present case report demonstrates the importance of CT-guided procedural preparation and tailored transcatheter strategies in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI interventions and resolved PVE, underscoring significant factors involved in the lifelong management of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was admitted to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after presenting with shortness of breath, a sensation of spasm on the left side of the chest induced by physical activity (notably when walking 200–300 m), dizziness, and general fatigue.

The patient’s medical history revealed arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions, specifically in 2009 (right coronary artery), 2010 (left anterior descending (LAD) artery), 2012 (repeat LAD intervention), and 2014 (circumflex artery). Additional significant comorbidities were noted: a third-degree atrioventricular block treated with pacemaker placement in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes starting in 2022.

Due to ongoing degeneration and severe stenosis of the aortic valve, a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) was placed via transcatheter approach in 2014. However, by December 2021, his symptoms of dyspnoea with exertion had worsened, and echocardiography revealed significant bioprosthetic valve stenosis. By early 2022, the transvalvular mean gradient had escalated to 83 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had dropped further to 42%, down from 54% in December.

In May 2022, the patient underwent a TAV-in-TAV procedure involving the placement of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). The postoperative period was complicated by the formation of a pulsatile femoral haematoma and pseudoaneurysm, necessitating surgical vascular repair.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV intervention, a notable improvement in left ventricular EF from 42% to 54% was observed, signifying an early positive haemodynamic effect. He subsequently received guideline-based pharmacotherapy for heart failure, incorporating beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and, where necessary, diuretics.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient required readmission due to unrelenting fever and aggravated heart failure manifestations. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, indicated by a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete regimen of antimicrobial treatment tailored to E. faecalis PVE, including 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy, followed by oral antibiotics as per infectious disease specialist guidance. At the time of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), the antimicrobial regimen had been finished, and there was no ongoing chronic suppressive antibiotic therapy. Clinical evaluation, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) all indicated that the infection had resolved, with no signs of persistent endocarditis.

After antimicrobial therapy completion and partial improvement in symptoms, a follow-up assessment in January 2024 demonstrated deterioration of prosthetic valve performance, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean pressure gradient increased to 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction improved to 66%.

A decision was reached to undertake further intervention. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE continued to demonstrate grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a sustained mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly diminished aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², which confirmed severe dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA was determined using the continuity equation based on measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler velocity-time integrals. Imaging showed two superimposed bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT found the minimum lumen diameter in the Evolut prosthesis to be 13.7 mm, confirming substantial haemodynamic compromise and assisting in the preparation for a third transcatheter valve procedure (see figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT enabled assessment of anatomical suitability and estimation of the risk of coronary obstruction. Evaluation encompassed coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva metrics, valve-to-coronary distance, and interactions between the structures of the previously implanted transcatheter valves. Special consideration was given to the spatial relationship between the predicted neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, and to the potential for high valve positioning to sustain coronary perfusion and permit future coronary interventions. These preprocedural findings guided selection of valve type, implantation depth, and overall procedural plan, thereby allowing the intervention to proceed without the need for coronary protection.

A transoesophageal echocardiogram conducted before reintervention verified the absence of active endocarditis and ruled out paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Given the patient's intricate anatomy characterized by three prior stents, a history of infective endocarditis, and an extremely high surgical risk profile, a third TAVI was undertaken using a strategy guided by CT imaging. This approach prioritized protection of the coronary arteries, proper commissural orientation, and maximization of the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention was carried out under local anesthesia with arterial access via both the left radial and left femoral arteries. Severe aortic regurgitation was corroborated by aortography using a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

Through the left femoral access, a Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle. Balloon pre-dilatation of the aortic valve was achieved using a 22/40 mm catheter while inducing rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography confirmed unobstructed coronary flow (figure 3).

A self-expanding Acurate neo2 (size S) bioprosthesis was then deployed at the aortic valve position. The valve was oriented in alignment with the leaflets of the previously placed Evolut valve, a step guided by CT modeling before the procedure, to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the risk of patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

To further enhance valve expansion, post-dilatation was performed using a 20/40 mm balloon under conditions of rapid pacing. Final aortographic assessment confirmed accurate valve deployment, sustained coronary circulation, and the absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Commissural orientation was deliberately considered during device placement in order to maintain future access to the coronaries and decrease procedural complexity, using insights from preprocedural CT scans and fluoroscopic markers.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural outcome and prosthetic valve function were evaluated per the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, which include assessment of haemodynamic status, residual regurgitation, and procedure-specific complications.

Immediately following the intervention on 7 June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) verified the correct placement of the third transcatheter valve. The valve leaflets demonstrated full mobility with complete excursion and showed no detectable aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Nonetheless, haemodynamic analysis revealed elevated transvalvular gradients, with a mean value of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated at 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured 0.24, indicating severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

After one month, TTE documented substantial improvement: the mean gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg, and the AVA expanded to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodelling was apparent, with decreased chamber sizes. The prosthetic valve continued to demonstrate no regurgitation, and its function stabilized, corresponding to moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months post-procedure, the patient remained free of symptoms and displayed preserved exercise tolerance, without indications of heart failure or recurrent infection. The prosthetic valve continued to perform well, with stable and improved haemodynamic metrics.

By the 1-year follow-up in June 2025, the patient continued to be clinically stable and maintained an active lifestyle, reporting no dyspnoea or signs of cardiac decompensation. TTE findings demonstrated a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. No complications related to the valve, regurgitation, or recurrence of endocarditis were detected.

Collectively, these results provide evidence for the sustained efficacy and functional durability of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy in a patient considered to be at elevated risk.

Discussion:

This report illustrates the complexities involved in clinical decision-making and the technical considerations when undertaking a third TAVI procedure in a patient with a previous history of TAV-in-TAV interventions and PVE.

Although TAVI is now a standard approach for treating aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis continues to be debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis still advocate for surgical valve replacement as the preferred option in cases of PVE, especially when patients experience heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, these guidelines underscore the need for a tailored approach, factoring in infection management, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

The initial selection of TAVI in 2014 was primarily influenced by the patient’s elevated surgical risk and extensive comorbid conditions, rendering traditional valve surgery less viable. When the patient later developed structural valve degeneration, a repeat transcatheter approach was selected in 2022 to restore valve function and circumvent the hazards associated with open-heart surgery. After PVE was successfully managed and with no evidence of ongoing infection, the subsequent decision in 2024 to proceed with a third transcatheter intervention was justified by a high surgical risk profile, intricate anatomical challenges due to existing prosthetic frames, and CT confirmation of the feasibility of a strategy prioritising coronary flow preservation and adequate haemodynamic outcomes.

The underlying causes of prosthetic valve dysfunction in this patient were likely complex and involved several contributing factors. Although the previous occurrence of infective endocarditis may have led to damage of the valve leaflets and resulted in impaired mobility, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter prosthetic valves was also a major contributor. Crucially, after confirmation that the infection had resolved and in the absence of a need for extended suppressive antibiotics, the approach to managing prosthetic valve dysfunction was comparable to protocols used in patients without current or residual endocarditis. As such, procedural choices were primarily influenced by anatomical considerations, the severity of haemodynamic compromise, and the overall surgical risk profile, rather than by the patient’s past infective episode alone.

For this patient, antimicrobial treatment had been completed, and comprehensive clinical and imaging assessments revealed no indicators of persistent infection; detailed preprocedural investigations, including TEE and MSCT, verified there were no vegetations or paravalvular complications present. Additionally, the patient's EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, signifying elevated operative risk, further compounded by multiple comorbidities—namely COPD, diabetes mellitus, and a prior history of gastrointestinal bleeding—as well as by the presence of two previously deployed transcatheter valves with significant stent adherence to the aortic wall. Collectively, these factors made open surgical intervention exceptionally risky and technically formidable. Moreover, due to the extensive fixation of the existing prosthetic valves, surgical management would almost certainly have required replacement of both the entire aortic root and the ascending aorta.

Multiple reports have detailed TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 illustrating the diverse anatomical and technical difficulties encountered. Our report contributes further evidence, particularly given the rare context of degeneration following endocarditis and maintained valve function at one-year follow-up.

This scenario emphasizes the increasing relevance of comprehensive, lifelong strategies for managing structural valve disease. Each subsequent transcatheter intervention introduces additional complexity, including issues such as overlapping stent frames, achieving commissural alignment, ensuring future coronary access, and maintaining sufficient residual effective orifice area. Consequently, meticulous long-term planning and proactive discussion about future procedural options are crucial from the outset—especially for younger patients or those at elevated risk.

In this patient, structural deterioration included leaflet fibrosis as a consequence of prior degeneration and likely mechanical restriction after infection. The heart team elected to deploy a third prosthesis—a self-expanding Acurate neo2—after preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modelling confirmed the procedure's feasibility and safe coronary clearance (refer to figures 1, 5, and 7). The decision to implant the third transcatheter valve at a high position was intentional. Positioning the prosthesis at the level of the existing Evolut valve’s leaflets facilitated apposition of the degenerated leaflets to the stent frame, effectively preventing further decreases in effective orifice area due to leaflet infolding. Furthermore, this method limited the development of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, thereby supporting continued coronary perfusion and access for future interventions. Preprocedural CT assessment informed this approach, which proved essential for obtaining favorable haemodynamic outcomes in a patient with triple-stented anatomy. Even with the added complexity of three layered transcatheter valves, the procedure remained both technically viable and reproducible when conducted with extensive imaging and a clear procedural plan.

The Acurate neo2 device was chosen based on its supra-annular leaflet orientation, modest frame height, and superior haemodynamic characteristics in constricted anatomical settings. Its structural design facilitates commissural alignment—an especially important factor in this scenario to maintain future access to the coronary arteries.

One of the principal technical challenges encountered in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions involves the potential for compromised coronary access arising from successive, overlapping stent frames. Despite deliberate efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the accumulation of overlapping metallic meshes may complicate any subsequent coronary interventions. This underscores the necessity for comprehensive procedural planning, especially in patients with pre-existing coronary artery disease or those in whom the need for future access is anticipated. Preprocedural CT imaging was instrumental in delineating the landing zone, assessing possible stent frame interactions, and approximating valve-to-coronary distances; however, a dedicated three-dimensional expansion analysis was not conducted.

The elimination of the transvalvular pressure gradient following the third valve deployment is likely attributable to the synergistic effects of optimised commissural alignment, efficient post-dilatation, and partial mechanical dilation of the previously implanted Evolut frame, all of which enhanced leaflet kinematics and promoted central haemodynamic flow. Although no intentional frame fracture was carried out, it is probable that high-pressure balloon post-dilation contributed to additional radial expansion of the Evolut frame, thus improving the overall function of the prosthetic valve.

Institutional approval was secured for the off-label application of the Acurate neo2 device during the procedure. The patient and his family were engaged in a shared decision-making process, and the clinical strategy was discussed at a multidisciplinary heart team conference, which brought together interventional cardiologists, cardiothoracic surgeons, imaging specialists, and anaesthesiologists.

In the event of early failure of the current prosthesis, therapeutic alternatives would be markedly constrained. Surgical explantation would remain technically formidable and entail substantial risk. Innovative catheter-based techniques—such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or intentional leaflet laceration approaches (for example, BASILICA)—could be contemplated; however, their application, efficacy, and safety in scenarios involving three overlapping stent frames have yet to be established.
