Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: The array of technical complexities inherent to transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures necessitates that therapeutic approaches be individualized for each patient. We describe a man in his late 70s with a multifaceted medical background, including two previous TAVI operations and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced advanced deterioration of the aortic prosthesis requiring a third transcatheter intervention. The patient initially received a Lotus valve in 2014, followed by a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut device in 2022. This was complicated by prosthetic degeneration secondary to endocarditis and resultant severe restenosis. In 2024, a third prosthetic valve (Acurate neo2) was deployed via a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As the utilization of TAVI widens and survival increases among recipients, such intricate multi-prosthesis procedures may become increasingly prevalent, emphasizing the importance of optimizing procedural planning, imaging modalities, and extended monitoring. At the 1-year follow-up mark, the patient remains without symptoms and maintains intact valve function. This report demonstrates the practicality of undertaking a third TAVI procedure subsequent to prosthetic valve endocarditis managed by prior interventions, utilizing CT-based procedural preparation and reporting one-year follow-up, an approach infrequently documented in existing literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become a well-established therapeutic option for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, encompassing a wide range of surgical risk categories. With increasing numbers of patients receiving TAVI and improved long-term outcomes, clinicians are facing a growing incidence of structural valve deterioration and subsequent need for additional procedures.

In this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) techniques have developed as a feasible method for treating malfunctioning transcatheter prosthetic valves. Nonetheless, these procedures present unique anatomical and technical difficulties, such as the potential for coronary artery occlusion and a decrease in effective orifice area leading to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. Such complications are heightened with repeated interventions, like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), which require the consideration of multiple superimposed valve structures.

There is a paucity of data regarding serial transcatheter valve replacements, especially among patients with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these intricate cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, personalized risk evaluation, and a multidisciplinary approach are vital to assess procedural suitability and improve clinical outcomes.

The present case demonstrates the importance of computed tomography (CT)-based planning and strategy-oriented transcatheter therapy in a patient with several previous TAVI interventions and managed PVE, emphasizing critical factors relevant to long-term treatment of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A man in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after experiencing shortness of breath, a spasmodic sensation in the left chest during exertion (such as walking 200–300 meters), dizziness, and fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, a repeat LAD procedure in 2012, and circumflex artery in 2014. He also had third-degree atrioventricular block that necessitated pacemaker placement in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurring gastrointestinal bleeding beginning in 2022.

Progressive degeneration of the aortic valve resulting in severe stenosis led to the implantation of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) via transcatheter approach in 2014. By December 2021, the patient’s exertional dyspnoea had become more pronounced and echocardiography revealed severe bioprosthetic valve stenosis. Early in 2022, the mean gradient across the valve had increased to 83 mm Hg, with a drop in left ventricular ejection fraction from 54% in December to 42%.

In May 2022, the patient underwent a transcatheter valve-in-valve procedure (TAV-in-TAV) using a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). This intervention was complicated by the occurrence of a pulsatile haematoma and femoral pseudoaneurysm, which necessitated open vascular repair.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the left ventricular ejection fraction improved from 42% to 54%, demonstrating an early positive haemodynamic effect. The patient was treated according to guideline-based heart failure therapy, which included beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted again due to ongoing fever and deteriorating heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis, confirming a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete regimen of targeted antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis PVE, comprising 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment followed by oral therapy as advised by infectious disease specialists. By the time of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), the antimicrobial therapy had been finished, and no prolonged suppressive antibiotic regimen was in use. Assessment—including clinical evaluation, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT)—demonstrated clearance of infection and absence of ongoing endocarditis.

Following completion of antimicrobial treatment and partial alleviation of symptoms, subsequent review in January 2024 indicated progressive deterioration of the prosthetic valve, with severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient had increased to 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction had improved to 66%.

A reintervention was scheduled. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE identified continued grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², indicative of pronounced prosthetic valve dysfunction. The AVA was determined using the continuity equation from left ventricular outflow tract measurements and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. Imaging visualised two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT showed the minimum lumen diameter of the Evolut prosthesis was 13.7 mm, confirming substantial haemodynamic impairment and informing the planning for a third transcatheter valve implantation (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT evaluated anatomical suitability and assessed risk for coronary obstruction, including measurement of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and interaction with prior valve frames. The analysis focused on the relationship between the projected neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, as well as on the feasibility of high implantation to maintain coronary flow and allow for future coronary access. This information guided choices regarding valve type, implantation depth, and procedural strategy, enabling the procedure to proceed without coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography performed before the reintervention verified there was no active endocarditis or paravalvular issues.

Treatment:

Owing to the patient’s intricate history involving three stents, previous infective endocarditis, and a high-risk surgical profile, a third TAVI was scheduled as a procedure guided by CT imaging, incorporating a strategy that prioritised coronary protection, commissural alignment, and enhancement of the residual effective orifice area.

The intervention was conducted under local anaesthetic, employing arterial access via the left radial and left femoral sites. Severe aortic regurgitation was corroborated by aortography with a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the left femoral approach. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilation with a 22/40 mm catheter during rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortographic imaging established that coronary flow was not impeded (figure 3).

A self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (Acurate neo2, size S) was positioned within the aortic location. The implant was orientated to match the leaflets of the Evolut valve—as determined by CT modelling performed before the procedure—to increase the effective orifice area and minimise the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Valve post-dilation was achieved using a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing to ensure optimal expansion of the prosthesis. Subsequent aortography verified proper device placement, ongoing coronary flow, and the absence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

The procedure incorporated considerations for commissural alignment at the time of valve deployment to maintain future coronary access and decrease potential procedural challenges, utilising guidance from preprocedural CT imaging and fluoroscopy landmarks.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural outcome and prosthetic valve function were evaluated using the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) standards, which encompass assessment of haemodynamic performance, detection of residual regurgitation, and identification of procedure-associated complications.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) conducted directly after the intervention on 7 June 2024 verified proper implantation of the third transcatheter valve. The valve leaflets exhibited full mobility with complete opening, and there was an absence of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Nonetheless, haemodynamic data revealed significant transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient measuring 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) calculated was 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured 0.24, indicative of severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

One month following implantation, TTE demonstrated considerable improvement: the mean gradient was reduced to 27 mm Hg, and AVA increased to 1.1 cm². Left ventricular remodelling was evident with decreased chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was present, and valve function remained consistent within a moderate stenosis range.

By 3.5 months post-procedure, the patient continued to be symptom-free and maintained good exercise capacity. There were no indications of heart failure or recurring infection. The prosthetic valve sustained effective performance, with maintained improvements in haemodynamic measurements.

At the 1-year follow-up in June 2025, the patient remained clinically well and physically active, with no shortness of breath or signs of cardiac decompensation. TTE revealed a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and ejection fraction (EF) at 65%. No complications related to the valve, regurgitation, or recurrent endocarditis were detected.

Collectively, these results demonstrate the lasting efficacy of the intervention and support the sustained functional advantages of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach in this patient considered to be at high risk.

Discussion:

This case illustrates the complexities involved in clinical decision-making and procedural execution when performing a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient with previous TAV-in-TAV interventions and a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI is a well-established option for aortic stenosis, its application in patients with previous endocarditis continues to be debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for managing valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advocate for surgical valve replacement in cases of PVE, especially when heart failure or persistent infection complicate the clinical picture. Nevertheless, the guidelines underscore that therapy should be tailored to each patient, taking into account factors such as infection resolution, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

The initial selection of TAVI in 2014 was influenced by the patient’s elevated operative risk and extensive comorbidities, rendering traditional surgical replacement inappropriate at that juncture. Upon the emergence of structural valve deterioration, a repeat transcatheter approach was undertaken in 2022 to re-establish valve functionality while mitigating the dangers associated with surgical intervention. After effective management of PVE and with no evidence of ongoing infection, the rationale for a third transcatheter procedure in 2024 centered on unacceptable surgical risk, intricate anatomical features due to existing valve prostheses, and CT-confirmed feasibility with goals of maintaining coronary perfusion and attaining satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

The failure of the prosthetic valve in this patient appears to stem from multiple contributing factors. Although the episode of infective endocarditis may have resulted in damage to the leaflets and imposed mechanical restriction, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves substantially influenced the outcome. Crucially, after infection was deemed cured and lifelong suppressive antibiotics were not indicated, management of the prosthetic valve dysfunction mirrored that used for patients who did not have persistent endocarditis. As a result, procedural planning focused primarily on anatomical suitability, the severity of haemodynamic compromise, and overall surgical risk, rather than solely on the previous infectious episode.

In this instance, the patient had finished antibiotic therapy, lacked any signs—either clinically or radiographically—of ongoing infection, and underwent comprehensive preprocedural assessment. Both TEE and MSCT verified the absence of vegetations and paravalvular abnormalities. The patient’s EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, alongside notable comorbidities such as COPD, diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding. Additionally, two transcatheter valves were already present, with extensive stent incorporation into the aortic wall, making open surgery especially precarious from both technical and risk perspectives. Owing to the firm anchoring of prior prostheses, surgical management would likely have required replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple published case reports have detailed TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures,37 illustrating a range of anatomical and procedural difficulties. The present case contributes to this scarce literature by presenting a scenario of post-endocarditis degeneration with sustained valve function at the 1-year follow-up.

This case highlights the increasing significance of lifelong management approaches in structural valve disease. Each additional transcatheter intervention introduces more intricate considerations, including frame stacking, alignment of commissures, coronary access, and maintaining an adequate effective orifice area. Comprehensive long-term planning and early discussion regarding procedural strategies are imperative, especially during the initial intervention—more so for younger or high-risk individuals.

The structural deterioration in this patient consisted of leaflet fibrosis attributable to prior degeneration and likely mechanical impairment as a result of infection. The clinical team elected to implant a third device—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—after preprocedural CT and 3D modelling confirmed its feasibility and sufficient coronary clearance (see figures 1, 5, and 7). Purposefully positioning the third valve at a high level was a tactical choice; placement at the Evolut valve leaflet plane facilitated fixation of the existing leaflets against the stent frame, which prevented further decrease in the effective orifice area due to inward leaflet folding. Furthermore, this method reduced neo-skirt formation beneath the coronary ostia, helping to preserve coronary flow and future access. This technique was based on preprocedural CT evaluation and was crucial for attaining a satisfactory haemodynamic outcome in the context of a triple-stented valve structure. Although a triple transcatheter valve anatomy presents considerable procedural complexity, this method was technically achievable and reproducible when supported by meticulous preprocedural imaging and a clearly defined implantation plan.

The Acurate neo2 device was chosen based on its supra-annular leaflet orientation, comparatively modest frame height, and favourable haemodynamic characteristics in constrained anatomies. Its architecture facilitates commissural alignment, an important factor in this scenario to maintain potential for subsequent coronary access.

An important procedural challenge in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions is the possibility of compromised coronary access resulting from several overlapping stent frames. Even with efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the accumulation of mesh could complicate later coronary procedures. This emphasizes the necessity for detailed procedural planning for patients with established coronary disease or those for whom future access is anticipated. Preprocedural CT provided measurements of the landing zone, assessment of frame overlap, and approximation of distances between the valve and coronary arteries, though a full three-dimensional expansion study using specialized software was not conducted.

Elimination of the transvalvular gradient following the third device placement was likely due to optimized commissural alignment, thorough post-dilatation, and partial mechanical expansion of the previous Evolut frame, resulting in improved leaflet movement and central blood flow. Although a deliberate frame fracture was not performed, post-dilation with high-pressure balloons probably promoted additional radial expansion of the Evolut device, thereby enhancing valve performance.

The intervention was carried out after obtaining institutional approval for off-label application of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. Shared decision-making included the patient and his family, and comprehensive evaluation by a multidisciplinary heart team involving interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anaesthesiologists.

If the current prosthesis were to fail earlier than expected, therapeutic options would be markedly restricted. Surgical removal would remain complex and associated with elevated risk. Novel catheter-based strategies such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration techniques—for example, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—could be contemplated, though their applicability and safety in a triple-stented context are yet to be clarified.
