Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Owing to the various technical difficulties inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve interventions, therapeutic approaches must be customized for each individual case. Here, we describe an elderly male in his late seventies with a complicated medical history, including two prior TAVI procedures and infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced advanced aortic prosthesis degeneration that necessitated a third transcatheter treatment. His initial intervention was a Lotus valve implantation in 2014, succeeded by a TAV-in-TAV procedure utilizing an Evolut prosthesis in 2022, after which he suffered from endocarditis-associated prosthetic degeneration leading to severe restenosis. In 2024, a third valve (Acurate neo2) was delivered through a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV technique. As the eligibility for TAVI expands and patients’ survival increases, the occurrence of multi-prosthesis cases like this may become more prevalent, underlining the importance of refined procedural planning, advanced imaging protocols, and rigorous long-term monitoring. At one-year post-procedure, the patient remains symptom-free with maintained prosthetic valve function. This case demonstrates the practicality of performing a third TAVI following treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, guided by CT-based planning and evidenced by one-year follow-up—an approach that remains rarely documented in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a widely accepted therapeutic option for individuals with severe aortic stenosis spanning diverse levels of surgical risk. With the expanding use of TAVI and improvements in long-term survival rates, clinicians are more frequently encountering structural valve degeneration and the necessity for subsequent interventions.

Within this context, performing a transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) has surfaced as an effective approach to address dysfunction of previously implanted transcatheter valves. Nevertheless, these procedures pose unique anatomical and technical complexities, such as an elevated risk for coronary obstruction and a potentially diminished effective orifice area leading to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These issues are exacerbated when multiple reinterventions occur, as in transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) procedures, where the presence of several stacked valve frames requires meticulous planning.

Available data on performing repeated transcatheter valve procedures are scarce, especially in those with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these intricate cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, individualized assessment of risks, and input from a multidisciplinary team are vital for assessing procedure feasibility and achieving optimal results.

This case demonstrates the value of computed tomography-based procedural planning and a strategy-focused transcatheter approach in a patient who previously underwent multiple TAVI procedures and received treatment for PVE, emphasizing important aspects of lifelong management in structural valve disease.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after developing shortness of breath, a tight or spastic feeling in the left chest during exertion (such as walking 200–300 meters), dizziness, and general fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions: the right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, repeat intervention on the LAD in 2012, and the circumflex artery in 2014. Other significant comorbidities consisted of third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating pacemaker placement in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes since 2022.

As a result of advancing aortic valve disease with significant stenosis, he underwent transcatheter implantation of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) in 2014. By December 2021, his exertional dyspnoea had intensified, and echocardiography demonstrated severe bioprosthetic valve stenosis. By the beginning of 2022, the mean pressure gradient had escalated to 83 mm Hg, with a decrease in ejection fraction (EF) to 42% from 54% previously observed in December.

The patient received a TAV-in-TAV procedure in May 2022, with placement of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). The intervention was complicated by the formation of a pulsatile hematoma and femoral pseudoaneurysm, which required surgical intervention via open vascular repair.

Post-Evolut TAV-in-TAV, the patient's left ventricular EF rose from 42% to 54%, reflecting an early improvement in haemodynamics. He was treated according to heart failure guidelines, including therapy with beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as clinically appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted due to persistent fever and escalating heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed severe restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, and prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) was diagnosed. The patient underwent a complete regimen of targeted antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis-associated PVE, consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral therapy in accordance with infectious disease specialist recommendations. By the time of the third TAVI procedure, antimicrobial therapy had concluded, with no ongoing long-term suppressive antibiotic regimen. Clinical evaluation, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) demonstrated resolution of the infection, with no signs of persistent endocarditis.

Following the completion of antimicrobial treatment and partial improvement in symptoms, a follow-up in January 2024 demonstrated progressive deterioration of prosthetic valve function, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient had increased to 49 mm Hg, while left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had recovered to 66%.

A decision was made to perform another intervention. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE indicated ongoing grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly decreased aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², consistent with severe prosthetic valve dysfunction. The AVA was determined using the continuity equation, incorporating measurements of the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler velocity-time integrals. Imaging demonstrated two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT depicted a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, confirming pronounced haemodynamic impairment and informing planning for the third transcatheter valve implantation (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT was used to evaluate anatomical suitability and coronary artery obstruction risk, including analysis of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and interactions among the frames of the previously placed transcatheter valves. Particular attention was directed to the relationship between the expected neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, as well as to the feasibility of high valve placement to maintain coronary perfusion and facilitate future coronary access. These assessments informed valve choice, implantation depth, and procedure strategy, enabling the intervention to be performed without coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography conducted prior to reintervention verified the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Considering the patient's intricate anatomy with three prior stent placements, history of infective endocarditis, and high surgical risk, a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was scheduled utilizing computed tomography (CT) guidance and a strategic approach focusing on coronary protection, achieving commissural alignment, and maximizing the residual effective orifice area.

The intervention was carried out under local anesthesia using both left radial and left femoral arterial entry points. Aortography conducted with a pigtail catheter revealed pronounced aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was inserted into the left ventricle via the left femoral approach. Pre-dilation of the aortic valve was accomplished with a 22/40 mm balloon catheter during rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography confirmed ongoing coronary patency (figure 3).

Implantation of a self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (Acurate neo2, size S) was performed in the aortic location. The valve was oriented to coincide with the Evolut valve leaflets, according to preprocedural CT-based modeling, to enhance the effective orifice area and reduce the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Post-dilation utilized a 20/40 mm balloon under rapid pacing conditions in order to ensure optimal valve expansion. Subsequent aortography verified the proper placement of the valve, preserved coronary flow, and the absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Commissural alignment was addressed throughout valve deployment to maintain coronary accessibility and diminish potential future procedural difficulty, with guidance provided by preprocedural CT and fluoroscopic markers.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural success and performance of the prosthetic valve were evaluated based on the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, including assessment of haemodynamic status, detection of any residual regurgitation, and identification of procedure-related adverse events.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) obtained immediately after the procedure on 7 June 2024 verified appropriate positioning of the third transcatheter valve. The valve leaflets demonstrated mobility and fully opened, with no aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Despite this, haemodynamic measurements revealed elevated transvalvular gradients, with a mean value of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) measured 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), while the Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24, indicating severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

One month following valve implantation, repeat TTE demonstrated significant improvement, showing a mean gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg and an AVA increased to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodeling was seen with smaller chamber volumes. There was no residual regurgitation, and valve function remained steady within the moderate stenosis range.

At 3.5 months post-procedure, the patient reported no symptoms and maintained good exercise capacity. There were no clinical manifestations of heart failure or recurrence of infection. The valve prosthesis continued to function effectively, with maintained improvements in haemodynamic indices.

After 1 year (June 2025), the patient continued to be clinically stable and physically active, without shortness of breath or evidence of cardiac decompensation. TTE findings included a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. There were no valve-related complications, absence of regurgitation, and no signs of recurrent endocarditis.

Overall, these results demonstrate the sustained durability and long-term functional benefits associated with the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure for this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This report illustrates the complexities in clinical judgment and procedural execution when undertaking a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient who previously underwent two TAV-in-TAV interventions and had a background of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI is now a recognized therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for managing valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis continue to endorse surgical valve replacement as the preferred treatment for PVE, especially when complicated by heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, these guidelines advocate for tailoring management strategies to the individual, factoring in the extent of infection control, surgical risk profile, and anatomical suitability.

The decision to opt for TAVI as the primary therapy in 2014 was influenced by the patient’s elevated operative risk and numerous comorbidities, which rendered standard surgical valve replacement unfavorable. With later onset of structural valve degeneration, a transcatheter re-intervention was selected in 2022 to restore valvular function while minimizing the hazards associated with repeat surgery. After successful resolution of the PVE and no evidence of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter procedure in 2024 was chosen due to extreme surgical risk, intricate anatomy arising from the existing valve structures, and pre-procedural CT imaging that confirmed the feasibility of a strategy designed to maintain coronary perfusion and provide satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

The cause of the prosthetic valve failure in this patient appeared to be due to multiple factors. The episode of infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage and subsequent restriction in valve mobility; however, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves was also a major contributor. Notably, after infection was deemed eradicated and prolonged antibiotic suppression was no longer indicated, the approach to prosthetic valve dysfunction paralleled that for patients without persistent or current endocarditis. As such, procedural planning focused primarily on anatomical suitability, hemodynamic significance, and operative risk, rather than the patient’s prior infectious episode.

For this patient, antimicrobial treatment had been completed, there were no signs of persistent infection by clinical assessment or imaging, and comprehensive pre-procedural evaluations—including transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT)—affirmed the absence of vegetations or paravalvular abnormalities. Additionally, the patient’s European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE II) was 27.36%, and he had substantial comorbid conditions (including COPD, diabetes, previous gastrointestinal hemorrhage) and two existing transcatheter valves with extensive stent incorporation into the aortic wall. These factors made conventional surgery exceedingly high-risk and technically challenging. Due to the significant integration of prior stents, a surgical approach would likely require complete replacement of the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple reports detailing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions have been described,37 illustrating a range of anatomical and technical hurdles encountered during these procedures. Our report expands upon this sparse literature, presenting a case characterized by degeneration after endocarditis and maintained prosthetic function at the 1-year mark.

This scenario highlights the increasing necessity of considering lifelong management pathways for structural valve pathology. With each subsequent transcatheter valve placement, issues including multiple frame layering, precise commissural orientation, coronary access, and preservation of the effective orifice area become progressively more complicated. It is essential to incorporate long-term procedural planning and discuss possible future interventions from the initial procedure—especially for younger patients or those at elevated risk.

Structural deterioration in this particular patient manifested as leaflet fibrosis attributable to prior degeneration and likely mechanical impairment post-infection. The clinical team elected to insert a third device—the self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—based on findings from preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modeling that confirmed procedural viability and sufficient coronary ostia clearance (figures 1, 5 and 7). Purposeful high deployment of the third transcatheter valve was chosen to anchor it at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets. This maneuver immobilized the preexisting leaflets against the stent structure, effectively preventing inward folding and further diminishing the effective orifice area. Furthermore, this technique minimized development of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, thus aiding in preservation of both coronary flow and prospective coronary access. Detailed preprocedural CT analysis informed this approach, which was fundamental to achieving satisfactory haemodynamic performance in this triple-stented configuration. Even with the technical demands posed by three transcatheter valves, the procedure proved feasible and repeatable when driven by comprehensive imaging and predetermined implantation tactics.

The Acurate neo2 device was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet design, relatively short frame, and optimal haemodynamic properties in constricted anatomies. Its construction facilitates commissural alignment, which was particularly important in this scenario to safeguard future coronary access.

A primary technical challenge in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures involves the possibility of compromised coronary access arising from the overlap of multiple stent structures. Although efforts were made to achieve commissural alignment, the accumulation of mesh material may still impede subsequent coronary interventions. This underscores the necessity for thorough procedural planning, especially for patients with established coronary disease or those in whom maintaining coronary access is critical. Preprocedural CT was utilized to assess the landing area, analyze stent interactions, and estimate distances between the valve and the coronaries, though a complete three-dimensional expansion assessment using specialized software was not conducted.

Resolution of the transvalvular gradient after the third device implantation was due to optimized commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut stent frame, which enhanced leaflet movement and promoted central flow. Although deliberate frame fracture was not performed, high-pressure balloon post-dilation likely resulted in additional radial expansion of the Evolut device, thereby improving its function.

The intervention was carried out with institutional authorization for the off-label application of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. Shared decision-making included discussions with the patient and his family, and the case was evaluated by a multidisciplinary heart team comprised of interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging specialists, and anesthesiologists.

If the present prosthesis were to fail prematurely, therapeutic choices would be severely restricted. Surgical explantation would remain technically complex and entail substantial risk. Newer catheter-based techniques, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration—for example, BASILICA (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction)—could potentially be considered, though their applicability and safety in scenarios involving three overlapping stent frames are still unclear.
