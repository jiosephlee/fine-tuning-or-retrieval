Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the varied technical challenges inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, management must be individualized for each patient scenario. Herein, we describe a man in his late seventies with a complicated medical background, including two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures and infective endocarditis, who subsequently developed significant deterioration of his aortic prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The initial implantation utilized a Lotus valve in 2014; in 2022, he underwent a valve-in-valve procedure with an Evolut device, after which endocarditis resulted in further prosthetic degeneration and severe restenosis. A third valve (Acurate neo2) was placed in 2024, employing the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV technique. With the broadening use of TAVI and increasing patient survival, these complex multi-valve interventions are likely to become more prevalent, underscoring the importance of enhanced procedural planning, advanced imaging modalities, and extended patient monitoring. At one-year follow-up, the patient remains without symptoms and retains good prosthetic valve function. This case demonstrates that a tertiary TAVI following treated prosthetic valve endocarditis is possible, utilizing CT-based procedural planning and ongoing follow-up; such instances are rarely documented in the current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely accepted as a treatment option for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, encompassing those with various surgical risk profiles. With the increasing frequency of TAVI procedures and improvements in patient longevity, structural deterioration of the prosthetic valve and the need for repeat interventions are being encountered more often in clinical practice.

Within this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) has become a feasible approach for addressing failed transcatheter valves. Nonetheless, these procedures present unique anatomical and technical difficulties, such as the potential for coronary artery occlusion and diminished effective orifice area, which can lead to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These complexities become even more pronounced with additional redo procedures like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where the presence of several superimposed valve frames requires careful evaluation.

Data on repeated transcatheter valve interventions are sparse, especially in patients who have previously experienced prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In such intricate cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, personalized risk evaluation, and multidisciplinary team discussions are vital to assess procedural feasibility and enhance outcomes.

This case demonstrates the importance of CT-based procedural planning and an intentional, strategy-focused transcatheter approach for a patient with multiple prior TAVI procedures and successfully managed PVE, emphasizing key aspects in the long-term care of structural valve disease.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was admitted to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center with complaints of shortness of breath, a spasmodic sensation localized to the left side of his chest during exertion (walking 200–300 m), dizziness, and overall fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and a history of multiple percutaneous coronary interventions: in 2009 for the right coronary artery, in 2010 for the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, in 2012 for a repeat procedure in the LAD, and in 2014 for the circumflex artery. Other notable comorbidities comprised a third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating a pacemaker insertion in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

Progressive degeneration and severe stenosis of the aortic valve led to the transcatheter placement of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) in 2014. By December 2021, worsening exertional dyspnoea was evident, and echocardiography demonstrated severe bioprosthetic valve stenosis. Early in 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient rose to 83 mm Hg, with a corresponding decrease in left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) to 42% from 54% in December.

In May 2022, the patient underwent a transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure, during which a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic) was implanted. Post-procedural complications included the formation of a pulsatile femoral haematoma and pseudoaneurysm that required surgical vascular repair.

After the TAV-in-TAV with Evolut, the patient’s left ventricular EF increased from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive early haemodynamic outcome. Heart failure management was continued according to guidelines, utilizing beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted again due to ongoing fever and deteriorating heart failure manifestations. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Targeted antimicrobial therapy was administered for E. faecalis PVE, consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral antibiotics as recommended by infectious disease specialists. By the time of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), the antimicrobial regimen had concluded and long-term suppressive antibiotics were not in use. Clinical evaluation, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) all confirmed eradication of infection without signs of ongoing endocarditis.

After finishing antimicrobial treatment and partial improvement in symptoms, follow-up in January 2024 showed declining function of the prosthetic valve, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient measured 49 mm Hg, while left ventricular ejection fraction had normalized to 66%.

A plan for reintervention was developed. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE revealed persistent grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a substantially diminished aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², indicating severe dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA was determined with the continuity equation, using left ventricular outflow tract dimensions and Doppler-based velocity-time integrals. Visualization indicated two superimposed bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT showed a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, indicating considerable haemodynamic impairment and contributing to procedural planning for a third transcatheter valve placement (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT was performed to evaluate anatomical suitability and assess the risk of coronary artery obstruction, including measurements of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distances, and assessment of previous transcatheter valve frame interactions. Special consideration was given to the expected relationship between the planned neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, and whether high implantation was feasible for maintaining coronary flow and future access. These data influenced valve selection, depth of implantation, and procedural approach, facilitating intervention without the need for coronary protection.

Prior to reintervention, transoesophageal echocardiography was conducted and verified the absence of ongoing endocarditis or paravalvular issues.

Treatment:

Due to the patient’s intricate triple-stented aortic anatomy, previous history of infective endocarditis, and extremely high surgical risk, the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was planned using computed tomography-guided, strategy-focused methods prioritizing coronary protection, precise commissural alignment, and maximization of the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention utilized local anesthesia with left radial and left femoral arterial access points. Severe aortic regurgitation was confirmed through aortography performed with a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

Through the left femoral access, a Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle. Pre-dilation of the aortic valve was carried out with a 22/40 mm balloon catheter during rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography verified unimpeded coronary flow (figure 3).

A self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (Acurate neo2, size S) was deployed at the aortic location. The valve was positioned to align with the leaflets of the previously placed Evolut valve according to CT-derived planning, aiming to enhance the effective orifice area and decrease the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

To achieve optimal valve expansion, post-dilation was conducted using a 20/40 mm balloon with rapid ventricular pacing. Final aortography demonstrated accurate device placement, maintained coronary circulation, and the absence of perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Valve deployment was undertaken with special attention to commissural alignment to facilitate future coronary access and reduce procedural challenges, aided by CT images and fluoroscopic reference points.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural outcomes and prosthetic valve function were evaluated following Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, encompassing assessment of haemodynamic performance, presence of any residual regurgitation, and procedure-associated complications.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) performed directly after the intervention on 7 June 2024 verified that the third transcatheter valve had been successfully positioned. The leaflets exhibited unrestricted movement and full opening, with no detectable aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Nonetheless, haemodynamic measurements revealed significant transvalvular gradients; mean gradient was 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured 0.24, consistent with severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

At 1 month post-procedure, TTE indicated substantial improvement: the mean gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg, and AVA rose to 1.1 cm². Left ventricular chamber volumes were reduced, reflecting evidence of remodelling. No residual regurgitation was identified, and the valve continued to operate in the moderately stenotic range.

By 3.5 months post-implantation, the patient remained free of symptoms and exhibited normal exercise capacity. There were no indications of heart failure or recurrence of infection. The prosthetic valve preserved stable function with ongoing improvement in haemodynamic indices.

One year after implantation (June 2025), the patient continued to be stable and active, without dyspnoea or signs of cardiac decompensation. TTE revealed a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA 1.1 cm², and ejection fraction (EF) at 65%. No valve-related adverse events, regurgitation, or evidence of recurrent endocarditis were identified.

Collectively, these results confirm lasting procedural success and indicate a persistent functional benefit from the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy in this high-risk individual.

Discussion:

This report illustrates the clinical reasoning and procedural difficulties encountered when undertaking a third TAVI in a patient who previously underwent two TAV-in-TAV implantations and has a background of PVE.

Although TAVI is an established modality for managing aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a previous history of endocarditis remains contentious. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis continue to endorse surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach for PVE, especially when cases are complicated by heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines underscore the need for individualized treatment planning that incorporates infection resolution, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

In 2014, TAVI was selected as the initial therapeutic intervention due to the patient’s elevated operative risk and significant comorbid conditions, which rendered standard surgical valve replacement less desirable. With subsequent progression to structural valve degeneration, a repeat transcatheter procedure was performed in 2022 to re-establish prosthetic valve function and circumvent the hazards associated with surgical intervention. Once PVE had been effectively managed and there was no evidence of ongoing infection, the rationale for undertaking a third percutaneous intervention in 2024 centered on the patient’s excessively high surgical risk, anatomic complexities introduced by prior valve implants, and preprocedural CT demonstration supporting the feasibility of preserving coronary access and attaining suitable haemodynamic results.

The underlying causes of prosthetic valve dysfunction in this patient appear to be complex and multifaceted. Although the previous episode of infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage and consequent impairment of mechanical function, ongoing deterioration of the transcatheter valve structures was a major contributor. Notably, after infection resolution and when ongoing suppressive antibiotic therapy was deemed unnecessary, management of the prosthetic valve pathology became similar to that of individuals without persistent or active endocarditis. Thus, the choice of intervention was largely dictated by anatomical suitability, degree of haemodynamic compromise, and overall surgical risk, rather than focusing solely on prior infective endocarditis.

This patient had completed a course of antibiotics, demonstrated neither clinical symptoms nor imaging evidence suggestive of active infection, and underwent comprehensive assessment before intervention, including TEE and MSCT, both of which excluded vegetations or paravalvular abnormalities. Additionally, the patient’s EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, indicating very high operative risk, with further significant comorbidities such as COPD, diabetes, and previous gastrointestinal hemorrhage. The existence of two transcatheter prostheses already integrated extensively into the aortic wall further elevated the risk and complexity of open surgery. Due to the deep anchoring of the prior valves, any surgical approach would likely have required replacement of both the aortic root and the ascending aorta.

Multiple reports documenting TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures have appeared in the literature,37 each illustrating distinct anatomical and technical challenges. Our case further expands upon this limited data, presenting the distinct context of degeneration after endocarditis and maintained valve function at the 1-year mark.

This report highlights the increasing significance of lifetime management approaches for patients with structural valve disease. With each additional transcatheter intervention, the considerations involved—such as cumulative stent frame layers, alignment of valve commissures, ability to access the coronary arteries, and preservation of the effective orifice area—become more intricate. Proactive long-term planning and early discussions about procedural options are therefore essential from the outset, especially for younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

In this patient, the observed structural valve deterioration consisted of leaflet fibrosis attributable to earlier degeneration, along with probable mechanical restriction due to past infection. Based on evaluation by preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modelling, which showed the procedure was possible and that adequate clearance of the coronary arteries could be maintained (see figures 1, 5, and 7), the clinical team elected to implant a third valve—specifically, a self-expanding Acurate neo2 device. The choice to position this third transcatheter valve at a high level was intentional. Placement of the valve at the height of the Evolut prosthesis’ leaflets secured the existing degenerated leaflets against the frame, preventing further decreases in effective orifice area that could result from inward leaflet infolding. Furthermore, this method reduced development of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, thus aiding in the preservation of coronary flow and the potential for coronary intervention in the future. Preprocedural CT imaging informed this approach, which was pivotal in attaining satisfactory haemodynamics in a scenario involving three valve layers. Although triple transcatheter valve placement is inherently complex, this method was both technically achievable and could be replicated with careful advance imaging and pre-planned procedural tactics.

The Acurate neo2 prosthesis was chosen based on its supra-annular leaflet placement, modest frame height, and optimal haemodynamic characteristics suited for narrow anatomies. Its architecture facilitates commissural alignment, an important factor in this case to safeguard future coronary access.

In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures, a major technical challenge is the potential compromise of coronary access caused by the layering of stent frames. Despite efforts towards commissural alignment, the aggregate stent mesh may still impede future coronary interventions, underscoring the necessity for detailed procedural planning in patients with existing coronary artery disease or those where subsequent access might become critical. Preprocedural CT scans assisted in assessing the landing zone, analyzing frame interaction, and gauging distances from valve to coronaries, although a comprehensive three-dimensional expansion assessment using specialized software was not undertaken.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve deployment is likely attributable to enhanced commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the previously implanted Evolut frame, which improved leaflet function and central flow. Although frame fracture was not deliberately performed, high-pressure balloon post-dilation appears to have prompted further radial enlargement of the Evolut frame, contributing to improved valve performance.

The intervention was carried out with institutional approval for the off-label application of the Acurate neo2 device. The patient's care involved shared decision-making with the patient and his family, with multidisciplinary evaluation involving interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging specialists, and anaesthesiologists.

If the current valve were to fail prematurely, the therapeutic options would be markedly limited. Surgical removal would entail considerable technical complexity and elevated risk. Newer catheter-based strategies such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration procedures—including BASILICA (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction)—may be explored, though their practicality and safety in triple-stented scenarios are not well established.
