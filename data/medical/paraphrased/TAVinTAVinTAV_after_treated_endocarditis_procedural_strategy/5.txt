Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Owing to the range of technical challenges encountered in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, management approaches should be individually customized for each patient. We report the case of a male in his late 70s with a complicated medical background that includes two earlier transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures as well as infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced significant deterioration of his aortic prosthesis, necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The patient initially received a Lotus valve in 2014, then underwent a TAV-in-TAV procedure with an Evolut device in 2022, after which endocarditis led to degeneration and pronounced restenosis. In 2024, a third prosthesis—Acurate neo2—was implanted using a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As TAVI indications broaden and the longevity of recipients increases, the prevalence of these intricate multi-valve interventions is expected to rise, emphasizing the importance of enhanced procedural planning, advanced imaging, and prolonged follow-up strategies. At the 1-year mark, the patient has no symptoms and maintains good valve performance. This report demonstrates the practicability of a third TAVI following resolved prosthetic valve endocarditis, guided by CT-based planning and confirmed by 1-year follow-up, a scenario infrequently described in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a recognized therapeutic approach for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, spanning various surgical risk categories. With an expanding population of patients receiving TAVI and enhancements in long-term survival, clinicians are observing a rising incidence of structural valve degeneration and an associated increase in repeat procedures.

Within this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) implantation has become an effective approach for addressing degenerated transcatheter valve prostheses. Nevertheless, these procedures introduce distinctive anatomical and technical difficulties, such as potential coronary artery obstruction and the possibility of a decreased effective orifice area, which can lead to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These issues become even more complex with successive reinterventions like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where consideration must be given to the presence of multiple, overlapping valve stent frames.

There is a scarcity of data on performing repeated transcatheter valve interventions, especially among individuals with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these challenging cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, personalized evaluation of risks, and collaborative, multidisciplinary decision-making play crucial roles in assessing viability and enhancing procedural results.

This report demonstrates how CT-assisted planning and a strategy-focused transcatheter approach were utilized in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI interventions and managed prosthetic valve endocarditis, emphasizing important factors in the lifelong care of structural heart valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center due to symptoms of shortness of breath, a spasmodic feeling localized to the left side of the chest associated with physical activity (notably when walking 200–300 meters), along with episodes of dizziness and a general sense of fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, triple-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions carried out in 2009 (right coronary artery), 2010 (left anterior descending artery), 2012 (repeat intervention of the LAD), and 2014 (circumflex artery). Other coexisting conditions consisted of third-degree atrioventricular block for which a pacemaker was implanted in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and ongoing gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

In 2014, the patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) via transcatheter approach for worsening degeneration and marked stenosis of the aortic valve. By December 2021, exertional dyspnea had intensified, with echocardiography indicating significant stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. Early in 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction had dropped to 42%, compared with 54% recorded in December.

In May 2022, the patient received a transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve procedure, during which a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic) was implanted. Postoperatively, the patient developed a pulsatile hematoma and pseudoaneurysm at the femoral site, necessitating open surgical vascular repair.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient’s left ventricular ejection fraction increased from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive early hemodynamic outcome. The patient received heart failure treatment in accordance with established guidelines, which comprised beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was re-admitted to the hospital due to ongoing fever and aggravation of heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed severe restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete regimen of targeted antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis PVE, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, which was then followed by an oral antibiotic regimen as guided by recommendations from an infectious disease specialist. By the time the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was performed, the antimicrobial course had been finished and no ongoing suppressive antibiotic therapy was in use. Clinical evaluation, repeat blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) demonstrated resolution of infection, with no indications of current endocarditis.

Upon finishing the course of antibiotics and experiencing partial improvement in symptoms, a follow-up conducted in January 2024 indicated deteriorating function of the prosthetic valve, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe aortic regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient measured 49 mm Hg, while the left ventricular ejection fraction had returned to 66%.

The team opted to undertake a repeat intervention. Upon admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated ongoing grade 2–3 regurgitation within the prosthetic aortic valve, a mean transvalvular pressure gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly decreased aortic valve area (AVA) measuring 0.5 cm², indicative of severe malfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA determination utilized the continuity equation, employing measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler-based velocity-time integrals. Imaging also revealed the presence of two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT demonstrated a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, indicating marked haemodynamic impairment and informing the approach for a third transcatheter valve implantation (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT was utilized to evaluate anatomical suitability and estimate the likelihood of coronary artery obstruction. This assessment comprised measurements of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva size, valve-to-coronary distances, and the interaction between the frames of previously implanted transcatheter valves. Special consideration was given to the spatial relationship between the predicted neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, as well as the potential for high valve positioning to maintain coronary perfusion and ensure future coronary access. These insights influenced the choice of valve, positioning depth, and procedural plan, thereby enabling the procedure to proceed without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed prior to the reintervention and confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

In light of the patient’s intricate anatomy involving three stents, a history of infective endocarditis, and an unacceptably high surgical risk, a third TAVI was scheduled. This procedure was to be informed by CT imaging and strategically focused on safeguarding the coronary arteries, achieving appropriate commissural alignment, and maximizing the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the left femoral approach. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilatation using a 22/40 mm catheter while rapid ventricular pacing was applied at a rate of 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography verified that coronary perfusion was not compromised (see figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve, size S, was positioned in the aortic valve location. Preprocedural CT modeling was utilized to align the device with the Evolut prosthetic leaflets, aiming to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was utilized for post-dilation while administering rapid pacing to enhance valve deployment. Subsequent aortography demonstrated proper placement, maintained coronary artery flow, and no evidence of either perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

During valve implantation, commissural alignment was considered in order to maintain coronary accessibility and minimize the complexity of any subsequent procedures, using preoperative CT scans and fluoroscopic reference points for guidance.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural success and the function of the prosthetic valve were evaluated based on the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, encompassing assessment of haemodynamic performance, detection of any residual regurgitation, and identification of complications associated with the procedure.

A transthoracic echocardiogram conducted directly following the procedure on 7 June 2024 demonstrated that the third transcatheter valve had been implanted successfully. The leaflets appeared mobile and opened fully, and there was no aortic regurgitation observed (grade 0). Nevertheless, haemodynamic assessment showed elevated transvalvular gradients, with a mean value of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) measured 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), while the Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24, findings indicative of severe prosthesis–patient mismatch.

At one month post-implantation, transthoracic echocardiography revealed significant progress: the mean transvalvular gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg, and the aortic valve area expanded to 1.1 cm². Remodeling of the left ventricle was apparent, characterized by decreased chamber volumes. No persistent regurgitation was detected, and valve performance continued to be consistent with moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months follow-up, the patient continued to exhibit no symptoms and demonstrated satisfactory exercise capacity. There was no evidence of heart failure or return of infection. The prosthetic device continued to function effectively, with ongoing enhancement in haemodynamic measures.

One year following implantation (June 2025), the patient continued to be clinically stable and active, with no symptoms of dyspnoea or indications of decompensation. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a mean pressure gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area (AVA) measuring 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. There were no detected valve-related issues, regurgitation, or recurrent endocarditis.

These findings confirm the durability of the result and suggest a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case highlights the decision-making process and technical challenges of performing a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV procedures and a history of PVE.

Although TAVI is now a standard therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis management recommend surgical valve replacement as the primary approach for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially when there is concomitant heart failure or ongoing infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines highlight the importance of tailoring treatment decisions by assessing infection resolution, surgical risk, and anatomical suitability.

TAVI was initially selected as the treatment approach in 2014 due to the patient's elevated surgical risk and significant comorbidities, which rendered traditional surgical valve replacement an unsuitable option at that time. After the onset of structural valve deterioration, a repeat transcatheter procedure was conducted in 2022 to re-establish valve function while mitigating the hazards associated with open surgery. Once PVE had been effectively managed and with no signs of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter procedure was carried out in 2024; this decision was informed by the patient's unacceptable surgical risk, challenging anatomical factors arising from previous valve frames, and CT imaging that confirmed the possibility of a technique designed to maintain coronary perfusion and attain satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

In this patient, prosthetic valve failure was probably the result of several factors. The occurrence of infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage and resulting mechanical restriction, but ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter prostheses was also a major contributor. Crucially, after infection was deemed resolved and long-term antibiotic suppression was not necessary, the approach to prosthetic valve dysfunction mirrored that used for patients without current or recurrent endocarditis. Consequently, the procedural decisions were based mainly on anatomical suitability, haemodynamic impact, and operative risk, rather than being dictated solely by the antecedent infection.

In this scenario, the patient had finished a full course of antibiotics and demonstrated no evidence of ongoing infection either clinically or on imaging. Comprehensive preprocedural assessments, including both TEE and MSCT, confirmed there were neither vegetations nor paravalvular issues present. Additionally, the patient's EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, and significant comorbid conditions included COPD, diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding. The presence of two previously placed transcatheter valves, both with extensive stent anchoring in the aortic wall, further increased the risk and technical complexity of surgical intervention. Given that the earlier valves were securely embedded, surgical management would probably have required complete replacement of both the aortic root and the ascending aorta.

A number of reports have described TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 emphasizing diverse anatomical and technical difficulties encountered. The current case contributes to this scarce literature, distinguished by degeneration following endocarditis and maintained valve performance at the 1-year follow-up.

This case highlights the increasing relevance of comprehensive, lifelong management approaches for structural valve disorders. Each subsequent transcatheter procedure brings additional complexities, including issues like multiple stent frames, ensuring proper commissural orientation, maintaining future coronary access, and managing changes in residual effective orifice area. Proactive, long-term planning and prompt conversations regarding future procedural options are essential, especially during the initial intervention, and are particularly important for younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

In this patient, leaflet fibrosis resulting from prior degeneration and likely mechanical restriction secondary to infection led to structural valve failure. The multidisciplinary team chose to implant a third valve, specifically a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis, after preprocedural CT imaging and 3D modeling confirmed procedural viability and sufficient coronary clearance (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The decision to position the third transcatheter valve high was intentional. By deploying the device at the level of the Evolut valve’s leaflets, the team achieved fixation of the degenerated leaflets against the stent frame, thereby mitigating further loss of effective orifice area from leaflet infolding. Additionally, this technique minimized creation of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, thereby helping to maintain coronary perfusion and secure future access to the coronaries. The procedural plan was directly informed by preprocedural CT imaging and was pivotal in obtaining satisfactory haemodynamics within this triple-stented context. Although a triple transcatheter valve scenario is complex, the case showed that it is both technically possible and reproducible when detailed imaging and a clear implantation approach are utilized.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen because its supra-annular leaflet configuration, comparatively short frame, and beneficial haemodynamic properties are suitable for restricted anatomies. Additionally, its structure facilitates commissural alignment, which was an important factor to maintain the potential for future coronary interventions in this patient.

A major technical challenge in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions involves the potential for compromised coronary access resulting from the superimposition of several stent frames. Despite efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the increased density of the stent mesh can complicate subsequent coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of careful procedural planning, especially in individuals with established coronary disease or in whom future coronary access is anticipated. Preprocedural CT was useful for determining the dimensions of the landing area, assessing interactions among the stent frames, and estimating the valve-to-coronary distances; however, specialized software was not utilized to conduct a comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third device implantation is likely due to improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical enlargement of the existing Evolut frame, which facilitated better leaflet movement and central hemodynamics. Although a deliberate frame fracture was not attempted, post-dilation with a high-pressure balloon appears to have led to additional radial expansion of the Evolut, resulting in improved valve performance.

The procedure took place with institutional authorization for the off-label application of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. A collaborative decision-making process included both the patient and his family. Additionally, the case underwent evaluation by a multidisciplinary heart team composed of interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the present prosthetic valve were to fail ahead of schedule, therapeutic avenues would be severely restricted. Removal of the device surgically would continue to pose significant technical challenges and substantial risk. Novel percutaneous interventions—including techniques like bioprosthetic valve fracture or intentional leaflet laceration, such as bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—could potentially be evaluated, though their applicability and safety in settings with three stent layers have yet to be established.
