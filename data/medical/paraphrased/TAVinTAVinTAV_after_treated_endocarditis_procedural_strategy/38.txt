Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the various technical complexities encountered during transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, it is necessary to individualize the therapeutic approach for each patient. We describe the case of a male patient in his late seventies with a complicated medical background, including two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced significant deterioration of his aortic prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The patient first received a Lotus valve in 2014, underwent a TAV-in-TAV procedure with an Evolut device in 2022, and later developed prosthesis degeneration and pronounced restenosis associated with endocarditis. In 2024, a third aortic prosthesis (Acurate neo2) was deployed through a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV method. As TAVI indications broaden and life expectancy of treated patients increases, the occurrence of such complex interventions involving multiple prosthetic valves is likely to rise, underscoring the importance of advancements in procedural preparation, imaging techniques, and long-term follow-up. At one year post-procedure, the patient remains symptom-free with intact valve function. This report demonstrates the practicality of performing a third TAVI following managed prosthetic valve endocarditis, facilitated by CT-based procedural planning and extended follow-up at one year, a scenario with limited documentation in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely accepted for treating severe aortic stenosis in patients representing various levels of surgical risk. With the increasing number of TAVI procedures performed and extended survival following intervention, clinicians are encountering a higher incidence of structural valve degeneration and subsequent need for additional procedures.

In response, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) techniques have been developed as practical solutions to address failing transcatheter valves. Nonetheless, these procedures present unique anatomical and technical issues, such as a heightened risk of obstructing the coronary arteries and the possibility of a diminished effective orifice area that may cause significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. The complexity is compounded in repeat interventions, for example, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where the cumulative layering of prosthetic frames necessitates thorough consideration.

There is still a paucity of data regarding the safety and efficacy of serial transcatheter valve replacements, particularly in individuals with a past history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In such intricate cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, tailored risk stratification, and collaborative decision-making by a multidisciplinary team are vital to assess procedural suitability and enhance clinical results.

This report demonstrates the importance of CT-based procedural planning and a tailored transcatheter approach in a patient who previously underwent multiple TAVI operations and treatment for PVE, emphasizing significant factors pertinent to the long-term management of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after presenting with shortness of breath, a constrictive sensation on the left side of his chest during physical activity (when walking 200–300 m), along with episodes of dizziness and fatigue.

His prior medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: in 2009 (right coronary artery), in 2010 (left anterior descending (LAD) artery), a repeat LAD intervention in 2012, and circumflex artery intervention in 2014. Other comorbid conditions were third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating pacemaker placement in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal hemorrhage episodes since 2022.

Progressive degeneration and severe stenosis of the aortic valve led to the transcatheter implantation of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) in 2014. By December 2021, his exertional dyspnoea had deteriorated, and echocardiography confirmed severe stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. In early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient increased to 83 mm Hg, while left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) dropped to 42% from 54% (recorded in December).

In May 2022, a transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure was performed with placement of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). This was complicated by a pulsatile femoral haematoma and pseudoaneurysm, which necessitated open vascular surgical intervention.

Post-Evolut TAV-in-TAV, his left ventricular EF rose from 42% to 54%, suggesting a positive early haemodynamic result. Management included heart failure therapy according to current guidelines, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was re-admitted to the hospital due to ongoing fever and an exacerbation of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean pressure gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures yielded Enterococcus faecalis, leading to the diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a full regimen of tailored antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis-associated PVE, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment followed by oral antibiotics per guidance from infectious disease specialists. Prior to the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), the antimicrobial course was completed, and the patient was not on any ongoing suppressive antibiotic regimen. Clinical evaluation, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice computed tomography (MSCT) all indicated resolution of the infection, with no signs of active endocarditis.

Following the completion of antibiotics and partial improvement in symptoms, a follow-up evaluation in January 2024 identified deterioration in the function of the prosthetic valve, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient was measured at 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had returned to 66%.

A decision was reached to pursue further intervention. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE demonstrated persistent intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation graded at 2–3, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a substantially decreased aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², indicating severe prosthetic valve dysfunction. The AVA was determined through the continuity equation, utilizing measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. Visualization revealed the presence of two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT identified a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm in the Evolut prosthesis, corroborating the presence of significant haemodynamic impairment and informing the procedural approach for the third transcatheter valve implantation (refer to figure 1). The preprocedural MSCT was used to evaluate anatomical suitability and risk for coronary artery obstruction, including assessments of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva size, valve-to-coronary distance, and the spatial relationship between the frames of the existing transcatheter valves. Attention was directed to the projected neo-skirt’s proximity to the coronary ostia and to whether high implantation could maintain coronary flow and access in the future. These findings were instrumental in determining valve type, implantation depth, and procedural approach, enabling the intervention to proceed without coronary protection.

A transoesophageal echocardiogram was conducted before reintervention, confirming the absence of ongoing endocarditis or paravalvular issues.

Treatment:

Given the patient's intricate anatomy involving three stents, history of infective endocarditis, and unacceptably high surgical risk, the third TAVI was scheduled utilizing CT-guidance, with a focus on strategies for coronary protection, commissural alignment, and maximization of the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention was carried out under local anesthesia, utilizing arterial access via the left radial and left femoral arteries. Severe aortic regurgitation was verified through aortography using a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the left femoral approach. The aortic valve was pre-dilated with a 22/40 mm balloon while rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute was employed. Concurrent aortography demonstrated persistent patency of coronary flow (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (size S) was deployed at the aortic site. Based on preprocedural CT modeling, the prosthesis was positioned in alignment with the Evolut valve leaflets to enhance the effective orifice area and minimize the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was used for post-dilation with rapid pacing to ensure optimal expansion of the prosthetic valve. The final aortography showed proper valve placement, maintained coronary blood flow, and no evidence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Throughout deployment, commissural alignment was deliberately managed to protect coronary access and lessen complexity in potential future interventions, using preprocedural CT and fluoroscopic landmarks for guidance.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural outcomes and prosthetic valve function were evaluated based on the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 standards, encompassing assessment of haemodynamic performance, presence of residual regurgitation, and procedure-associated complications.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) conducted immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 verified appropriate placement of the third transcatheter valve. The valve leaflets exhibited normal mobility, fully opening without any indication of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Nevertheless, haemodynamic data revealed pronounced transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient measuring 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated at 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) registered 0.24, signifying severe prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM).

One month following implantation, TTE demonstrated considerable improvement: the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg, and AVA increased to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodelling was apparent, with chamber volumes reduced. No residual regurgitation was detected, and the prosthetic valve’s function was preserved, consistent with moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months post-procedure, the patient remained symptom-free and exhibited good exercise capacity. There were no indications of heart failure or recurrent infection. The prosthetic valve continued to perform well, with sustained enhancements in haemodynamic profiles.

At the 1-year follow-up in June 2025, the patient was clinically stable and physically active, showing neither dyspnoea nor signs of cardiac decompensation. TTE revealed a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. No valve-related complications, regurgitation, or recurrence of endocarditis were identified.

Collectively, these findings affirm the longevity of the repair and imply ongoing functional improvement attributable to the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV technique in this high-risk individual.

Discussion:

This report underscores the clinical reasoning and procedural complexities encountered when undertaking a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient with a previous history of two TAV-in-TAV procedures and prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI is an established therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with previous endocarditis remains a matter of debate. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis continue to recommend surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach in cases of PVE, especially when complicated by heart failure or persistent infection. Nevertheless, the guidelines stress the importance of tailoring management based on individual infection status, surgical risk, and anatomical considerations.

TAVI was initially selected in 2014 because the patient’s elevated surgical risk and extensive comorbid conditions rendered conventional surgery unsuitable. Upon progression to structural valve failure, a transcatheter valve-in-valve approach was employed in 2022 to reestablish valve function while minimizing the hazards associated with open surgery. After effective resolution of the PVE and with no evidence of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter procedure was chosen in 2024. This decision was informed by the ongoing high surgical risk, intricate anatomical factors resulting from previous valve structures, and CT findings supporting the viability of a technique that would sustain coronary perfusion and deliver satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

The underlying causes of prosthetic valve failure in this patient appeared to be multiple. Although previous infective endocarditis may have resulted in leaflet damage and subsequent mechanical limitation, ongoing deterioration of the transcatheter prosthetic structure was also a major factor. Crucially, once the infection was judged to be cured, and there was no indication for prolonged antibiotic suppression, the approach to treating prosthetic valve malfunction mirrored that used in individuals without persistent or current endocarditis. Thus, intervention decisions were primarily based on anatomical suitability, severity of haemodynamic compromise, and operative risk, rather than being dictated by the earlier infectious episode.

For this patient, antimicrobial therapy had been completed, and there were no clinical or imaging signs suggesting ongoing infection. Comprehensive preprocedural assessment—including both transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT)—confirmed there were no vegetations or paravalvular issues. The patient's European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE II) was calculated at 27.36%. He also had considerable comorbidities, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, and previous gastrointestinal bleeding, along with two earlier transcatheter prosthetic valve placements, characterized by extensive stent incorporation into the aortic wall. All these factors together made open surgical intervention particularly hazardous from both technical and risk perspectives. Due to the deep fixation of earlier devices, any surgical approach would have likely involved complete replacement of the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple case reports have documented TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures,37 emphasizing a range of anatomical and procedural difficulties. Our report expands this scarce evidence base by presenting a scenario characterized by degeneration following endocarditis, with sustained valve function at one year.

This case illustrates the increasing relevance of comprehensive lifetime management in structural valve disorders. With each subsequent transcatheter intervention, challenges such as frame layering, commissural positioning, maintaining coronary access, and preserving sufficient effective orifice area become progressively more intricate. It is crucial to engage in early planning and discussions regarding procedural pathways, beginning with the initial intervention—especially for younger or higher-risk populations.

Structural deterioration in this patient was marked by leaflet fibrosis resulting from prior degeneration and likely mechanical impairment following infectious involvement. The clinical team elected to implant a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—after preprocedural CT imaging and three-dimensional modelling confirmed the procedure's feasibility and adequate clearance of the coronary arteries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The third valve was intentionally positioned high. Placement at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets allowed for fixation of the existing leaflets to the stent frame, averting additional reduction of the effective orifice area by preventing inward leaflet folding. Furthermore, this method reduced the creation of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia and helped maintain coronary perfusion and future accessibility. Preprocedural CT evaluations informed this approach, which was vital for achieving satisfactory haemodynamics in a triple-layered valve setting. Despite the inherent complexities of implanting a third transcatheter valve, this strategy proved both technically achievable and repeatable when guided by thorough imaging and a predetermined implantation plan.

The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen for its supra-annular leaflet placement, its relatively short frame height, and its advantageous haemodynamic characteristics in confined anatomical spaces. The valve’s structure facilitates commissural alignment, which was prioritized in this patient to maintain potential access to the coronaries in the future.

A central technical challenge in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures is the threat to coronary access posed by the presence of multiple, overlapping stent frameworks. Even though commissural alignment was pursued, the accumulated mesh from these implants can still hinder subsequent coronary interventions. This underscores the necessity for thorough planning in patients with pre-existing coronary disease or those likely to require coronary access later. Preprocedural CT imaging allowed assessment of the intended implantation zone, analysis of stent frame relationships, and approximation of valve-to-coronary distances, though a comprehensive three-dimensional expansion study using specialized software was not undertaken.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implantation can be explained by a combination of optimal commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the previously placed Evolut frame, which collectively improved leaflet mobility and central flow. Although deliberate frame fracture was not executed, the application of high-pressure balloon post-dilation probably promoted additional radial expansion of the Evolut frame, further enhancing valve performance.

Institutional authorization was obtained for the off-label implantation of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. A collaborative decision-making process included the patient and family, and the case was presented at a multidisciplinary heart team conference consisting of interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anaesthesiologists.

If the current prosthesis fails ahead of schedule, therapeutic alternatives would be severely constrained. Surgical removal would be technically complex and associated with high risk. Recently developed catheter-based strategies—such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration techniques (for example, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent coronary obstruction (BASILICA))—may be considered, although the practical application and safety of these interventions in a triple-stented aortic configuration remains undetermined.
