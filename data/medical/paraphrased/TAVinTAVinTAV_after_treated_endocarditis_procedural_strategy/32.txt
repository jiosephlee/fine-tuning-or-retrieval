Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the wide range of technical obstacles encountered during transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, the approach to therapy must be individualized for each patient. Here, we describe a man in his late 70s with a complex medical background, including two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) operations and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently developed significant deterioration of the aortic prosthesis, necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. His initial treatment consisted of implantation of a Lotus valve in 2014, followed by a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut valve in 2022, after which he experienced degeneration secondary to endocarditis and severe restenosis. In 2024, a third valve (Acurate neo2) was placed utilizing a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV technique. As the scope of TAVI grows and patient survival increases, the occurrence of these intricate multi-prosthesis cases is likely to rise, underscoring the requirement for advanced procedural planning, optimized imaging modalities, and prolonged follow-up. One year post-procedure, the patient is free of symptoms and maintains good valve performance. This case demonstrates the practicality of a third TAVI following prior management of prosthetic valve endocarditis, facilitated by CT-based procedural planning and supported with 1-year follow-up, a scenario that is infrequently described in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely accepted as a therapeutic option for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, regardless of their surgical risk classification. With the expanding patient population undergoing TAVI and improvements in long-term outcomes, issues such as structural valve deterioration and the need for subsequent procedures are being observed more frequently.

To address the failure of transcatheter prosthetic valves, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) techniques have been introduced as effective solutions. Nonetheless, these procedures present distinctive anatomical and technical obstacles, including the potential for coronary artery blockage and a decrease in the effective orifice area, which can result in marked prosthesis–patient mismatch. These complexities become even greater during repeated interventions, such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where multiple superimposed valve frames necessitate thorough evaluation.

Currently, data on serial transcatheter valve reinterventions are sparse, especially among patients with previous episodes of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Managing such complicated cases requires comprehensive preprocedural imaging, tailored risk analysis, and a multidisciplinary approach to assess procedural feasibility and enhance outcomes.

The case presented demonstrates the application of CT-based planning and targeted transcatheter techniques in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI procedures and resolved PVE, emphasizing important aspects of lifelong structural valve disease management.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after reporting shortness of breath, a spasmodic discomfort on the left side of the chest triggered by exertion (notably when walking 200–300 m), along with dizziness and fatigue.

His prior medical background was significant for arterial hypertension, triple-vessel coronary artery disease, and a history of several percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, repeated intervention on the LAD in 2012, and the circumflex artery in 2014. He also had a third-degree atrioventricular block, for which a pacemaker was placed in 2014, as well as type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurring gastrointestinal hemorrhage episodes since 2022.

In 2014, due to the worsening of aortic valve function and presence of critical stenosis, he underwent transcatheter placement of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, he experienced aggravated exertional dyspnoea, and echocardiography revealed severe stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. At the beginning of 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had increased to 83 mm Hg, and the left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had dropped further to 42%, down from 54% in December.

In May 2022, a transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure was performed using a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). This intervention was complicated by the occurrence of a pulsating hematoma and pseudoaneurysm at the femoral access site, necessitating open vascular repair.

Post-procedure, the patient's left ventricular EF improved from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive initial hemodynamic result. Heart failure was managed with standard guideline-recommended therapies including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics, as clinically appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted again due to ongoing fever and aggravated heart failure signs. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated severe restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures yielded Enterococcus faecalis, and prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) was diagnosed. A complete course of targeted antimicrobial therapy was administered for E. faecalis-associated PVE, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral antibiotics as directed by the infectious disease team. When the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was scheduled, the antimicrobial regimen had concluded, and no ongoing suppressive antibiotic therapy was in place. Repeated clinical evaluation, blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) verified infection resolution, showing no signs of active endocarditis.

After finishing antimicrobial treatment and with partial symptom improvement, follow-up in January 2024 showed deterioration in prosthetic valve performance, presenting severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient increased to 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had returned to 66%.

Reintervention was considered appropriate. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE revealed ongoing grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a notably reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², indicative of severe prosthetic valve dysfunction. AVA was calculated using the continuity equation, based on left ventricular outflow tract measurements and Doppler velocity-time integrals. Imaging identified two superimposed bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT identified a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, corroborating significant haemodynamic impairment and assisting in planning for a third transcatheter valve implantation (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT evaluated anatomical suitability and assessed the potential for coronary artery obstruction. This included measuring coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and the interaction between the previously implanted valve frames. Particular focus was placed on the projected neo-skirt’s proximity to the coronary ostia and on whether high implantation could be achieved to maintain coronary blood flow and ensure future access. These results informed valve type, depth of implantation, and procedural approach, allowing the intervention to proceed without coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was conducted prior to reintervention and confirmed that there were no active endocarditis or paravalvular complications present.

Treatment:

Given the patient's intricate anatomy with three prior stents, history of infective endocarditis, and extremely high surgical risk, a third TAVI procedure was scheduled utilizing CT guidance and a strategy prioritizing coronary protection, commissural alignment, and maximization of the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention was conducted under local anesthesia, with arterial access achieved through the left radial and left femoral routes. Severe aortic regurgitation was confirmed via aortography with a pigtail catheter (see figure 2).

Through left femoral access, a Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle. The aortic valve was pre-dilated using a 22/40 mm balloon catheter during rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography demonstrated that coronary flow was maintained (refer to figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 (size S), a self-expanding bioprosthetic valve, was placed in the aortic position. Device alignment with Evolut valve leaflets was achieved by referencing preprocedural CT modeling, aiming to maximize effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

To further enhance valve expansion, post-dilation was carried out with a 20/40 mm balloon during rapid pacing. Subsequent aortography verified optimal positioning, unimpeded coronary blood flow, and absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (see figure 4).

Commissural alignment was considered during deployment to facilitate future coronary access and minimize procedural challenges, guided by preprocedural CT imaging and fluoroscopic cues.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural outcomes and prosthetic valve function were evaluated following the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 standards, encompassing assessment of haemodynamic performance, detection of residual regurgitation, and identification of any complications related to the procedure.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) carried out immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 verified proper placement of the third transcatheter valve. The valve leaflets displayed good mobility, with full opening and no signs of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Nonetheless, haemodynamic measurements revealed notable transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The calculated aortic valve area (AVA) measured 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), while Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24—findings that indicate severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

One month after valve implantation, TTE demonstrated considerable improvement: the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg, and AVA expanded to 1.1 cm². The left ventricle exhibited signs of remodelling, characterized by reduced chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was detected, and valve function remained stable, within parameters for moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic and demonstrated good exercise capacity. There were no indications of heart failure or recurrent infection. The valve continued to function properly, with ongoing improvement in haemodynamic indices.

By 1 year post-procedure (June 2025), the patient’s clinical status was stable, remaining physically active and free from dyspnoea or decompensation. TTE at that time showed a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) at 65%. No prosthesis-related complications, regurgitation, or recurrent endocarditis were detected.

Collectively, these results demonstrate the long-term effectiveness of the intervention and support a sustained functional benefit from the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach in this high-risk individual.

Discussion:

This case report underscores the complexities of clinical decision-making and procedural execution when conducting a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient with two previous TAV-in-TAV interventions and a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI has gained widespread acceptance as a therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in patients with previous infective endocarditis continues to be debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advocate for surgical valve replacement as the preferred option in PVE, especially in the presence of heart failure or persistent infection. Nevertheless, the guidelines stress the necessity for an individualized approach, factoring in infection resolution, operative risk, and the feasibility based on anatomy.

TAVI was initially selected in 2014 for this patient due to elevated surgical risk and significant comorbid conditions, rendering conventional surgery inappropriate. Upon subsequent structural valve deterioration, a transcatheter redo procedure was opted for in 2022 to reinstate valve competence while circumventing the hazards associated with open-heart surgery. After successful management of PVE and absence of ongoing infection, the decision for a third transcatheter valve intervention in 2024 was influenced by high operative risk, challenging anatomy secondary to prior valve scaffolds, and CT verification of a plan focused on preserving coronary patency and achieving satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

The failure of the prosthetic valve in this individual appeared to result from multiple factors. Although infective endocarditis may have caused damage to the leaflets and subsequent mechanical impairment, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter prostheses also contributed substantially. Crucially, after confirming resolution of the infection and the absence of need for prolonged suppressive antibiotic treatment, management of prosthetic valve dysfunction was approached as it would be in patients without active endocarditis. As such, procedural planning was influenced mainly by anatomical suitability, hemodynamic impact, and operative risk, rather than by the patient’s previous history of infection.

For this patient, antimicrobial treatment had been completed, with no evidence of persistent infection either clinically or on imaging, and comprehensive preprocedural assessment was performed using TEE and MSCT, both verifying the lack of vegetations or paravalvular pathology. Additionally, the EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, and the patient’s background included significant comorbid conditions such as COPD, diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding, as well as two prior transcatheter prosthetic valves with considerable stent adherence to the aortic wall—making surgical intervention particularly high-risk and technically difficult. Due to the deep fixation of the previous prostheses, surgery would probably have required replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple case reports have described TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures,37 noting diverse anatomical and technical difficulties encountered during such interventions. The present case contributes to this sparse literature, distinguished by valve deterioration following endocarditis and sustained valve function at one-year follow-up.

This report highlights the increasing relevance of lifelong treatment planning for patients with structural valve disorders. With each subsequent transcatheter procedure, issues including layering of stent frames, commissural alignment, future coronary access, and preservation of the residual effective orifice area grow more complicated. Proactive, long-term planning and early discussions regarding procedural pathways are vital—particularly at the initial intervention—for younger and high-risk patient populations.

The structural damage identified in this patient consisted of leaflet fibrosis related to prior degeneration and suspected mechanical limitation after infection. The clinical team elected to implant a third valve, selecting a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis, as preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modeling showed the procedure was feasible and maintained satisfactory coronary clearance (figures 1, 5, and 7). Purposefully high placement of the third transcatheter valve was employed, deploying the device at the height of the Evolut valve’s leaflets to anchor the previously existing leaflets against the stent frame. This technique prevented further narrowing of the effective orifice area from inward folding of leaflets. Furthermore, it limited creation of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, aiding in both preservation of coronary blood flow and access for future coronary interventions. This method was informed by the CT findings before the procedure and played a pivotal role in achieving favorable hemodynamic outcomes in this complex triple-stented scenario. Although a three-valve transcatheter construct is highly intricate, the procedure was shown to be technically viable and reproducible when executed based on detailed preprocedural imaging and an established implantation strategy.

The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen because of its supra-annular leaflet placement, relatively short frame height, and favorable hemodynamics in anatomies with limited space. Its structural features facilitate commissural alignment, which was crucial in this scenario to maintain potential coronary access in the future.

One of the primary technical challenges in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures is the possibility of obstructed coronary access due to multiple overlapping valve frames. Despite efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the accumulation of stent mesh could still impede future coronary interventions. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural planning for individuals with existing coronary disease or when future access may become necessary. Preprocedural CT was utilized to assess the landing zone, analyze interactions between frames, and estimate distances from valve to coronary arteries, though dedicated software was not used to perform comprehensive three-dimensional expansion evaluation.

The elimination of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implantation was likely due to optimized commissural orientation, successful post-dilatation, and partial mechanical expansion of the pre-existing Evolut frame. These factors combined to improve leaflet function and central flow characteristics. Although frame fracture was not deliberately performed, post-dilatation with a high-pressure balloon probably contributed to further radial enlargement of the Evolut, thereby enhancing prosthetic valve performance.

Institutional approval was obtained for the off-label placement of the Acurate neo2 device. The patient and family participated in shared decision-making, and the case was discussed in a multidisciplinary heart team meeting comprised of interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the present prosthesis were to fail early, available treatment options would be very limited. Surgical removal would remain a technically complex and high-risk undertaking. New catheter-based interventions, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration—for example, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—could be contemplated, but their safety and applicability in situations with three overlapping stents have yet to be established.
