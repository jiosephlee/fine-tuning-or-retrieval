Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the varied technical complexities inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures, individualized treatment plans are essential for each patient. We report the case of a man in his late 70s with a complicated clinical background, having previously undergone two TAVI procedures and experienced infective endocarditis, who subsequently developed severe prosthetic valve degeneration necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The patient's initial intervention with a Lotus valve took place in 2014; a second TAV-in-TAV procedure using an Evolut device was performed in 2022, after which he developed prosthesis deterioration and significant restenosis due to endocarditis. In 2024, a third prosthetic valve (Acurate neo2) was placed employing a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As TAVI use widens and patient longevity improves, such challenging multi-valve cases may become increasingly common, underlining the importance of enhanced procedural planning, advanced imaging, and ongoing follow-up. One year after the latest intervention, the patient is asymptomatic with sustained valve function. This report demonstrates the practicality of performing a third TAVI following managed prosthetic valve endocarditis, aided by CT-based planning and 1-year follow-up, an approach that is still infrequently described in published reports.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has been widely adopted for the treatment of severe aortic stenosis among patients with varying degrees of surgical risk. With an increasing number of individuals receiving TAVI and experiencing improved long-term outcomes, structural valve deterioration and repeat procedures are now more frequently observed in clinical settings.

Transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) techniques have developed as effective approaches for addressing malfunctioning transcatheter prostheses. Nevertheless, these interventions present distinct anatomical and technical hurdles, including the possibility of coronary artery occlusion and decreased effective orifice area, which may result in significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These complexities become even more pronounced with successive procedures, such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where the presence of multiple overlapping valve frames must be carefully evaluated.

Current data on repeated transcatheter valve procedures are scarce, especially among patients with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these intricate circumstances, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, tailored risk analysis, and collaborative multidisciplinary planning are crucial to assess procedural viability and improve clinical outcomes.

The present case demonstrates the importance of CT-based procedural planning and strategic transcatheter intervention in a patient who underwent multiple TAVI operations and was treated for PVE, emphasizing vital aspects for lifelong management of structural valve disorders.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was admitted to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after experiencing dyspnoea, a spastic sensation on the left side of the chest during exertion (specifically when walking 200–300 m), as well as dizziness and fatigue.

The patient's medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions: in 2009 for the right coronary artery, in 2010 for the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, in 2012 for a recurrent LAD lesion, and in 2014 for the circumflex artery. Additional diagnoses consisted of third-degree atrioventricular block (which necessitated pacemaker implantation in 2014), type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and repeated gastrointestinal bleeding episodes since 2022.

In 2014, because of progressive degeneration and significant stenosis of the aortic valve, he received transcatheter placement of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, his exertional dyspnoea had deteriorated, and echocardiography revealed pronounced bioprosthetic valve stenosis. By early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient rose to 83 mm Hg and the ejection fraction (EF) decreased further to 42% (having been 54% in December).

In May 2022, he underwent a TAV-in-TAV procedure with placement of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). This intervention was complicated by a pulsatile femoral haematoma and pseudoaneurysm, requiring open vascular repair.

Post-Evolut TAV-in-TAV, the patient’s left ventricular EF increased from 42% to 54%, demonstrating an early positive haemodynamic effect. He was treated with heart failure pharmacotherapy according to guidelines, which included beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted once again due to ongoing fever and aggravation of heart failure signs. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures yielded Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete course of directed antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis PVE, which included 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral therapy as recommended by infectious disease consultants. By the time of the third TAVI, antimicrobial therapy had concluded, with no ongoing long-term suppressive antibiotics. Clinical evaluation, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) confirmed infection resolution, with no signs of active endocarditis.

Following completion of antibiotics and partial improvement of symptoms, a follow-up in January 2024 demonstrated deterioration of prosthetic valve function—severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation were present, with a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg. Left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had improved to 66%.

Reintervention was deemed necessary. Upon hospital admission in June 2024, TTE showed persistent grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a notably reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², indicating severe prosthetic valve dysfunction. The AVA was calculated using the continuity equation, incorporating left ventricular outflow tract measurements and Doppler velocity-time integrals. Visualization revealed two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT identified a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, confirming marked hemodynamic impairment and providing guidance for planning a third transcatheter valve procedure (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT assessed anatomical suitability and coronary artery obstruction risk, with evaluation including coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and the interface between existing transcatheter valve frames. Detailed analysis was made regarding the relationship of the projected neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, as well as the potential for high implantation to maintain coronary flow and facilitate future coronary access. These findings informed valve choice, placement depth, and procedure strategy, allowing intervention to proceed without the need for coronary protection.

Prior to reintervention, transoesophageal echocardiography verified the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular issues.

Treatment:

Due to the patient's intricate anatomy featuring three prior stent placements, history of infective endocarditis, and elevated surgical risk, the third TAVI was designed as a computed tomography (CT)-guided, carefully strategized intervention focusing on coronary safeguarding, commissural orientation, and maximization of the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention was executed under local anesthesia utilizing left radial and left femoral arterial approaches. Severe aortic regurgitation was verified by aortography via a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

Through the left femoral access, a Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle. Pre-dilation of the aortic valve was accomplished with a 22/40 mm balloon catheter during rapid ventricular pacing (180 beats per minute). Concurrent aortography demonstrated sustained coronary patency (figure 3).

A self-expanding bioprosthesis (Acurate neo2, size S) was deployed in the aortic location. The prosthetic device was oriented in relation to the Evolut valve leaflets—as guided by CT simulation prior to the procedure—in order to enhance the effective orifice area and decrease patient–prosthesis mismatch risk.

Valve post-dilation was performed utilizing a 20/40 mm balloon during rapid pacing to improve prosthesis expansion. Follow-up aortography confirmed accurate placement, intact coronary perfusion, and absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

During implantation, commissural alignment was prioritized to safeguard future coronary access and limit procedural complexity, using preprocedural CT assessments and fluoroscopic reference points.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural outcomes and prosthetic valve function were evaluated in line with the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, which encompass assessments of haemodynamic performance, presence of any residual regurgitation, and procedure-associated complications.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) conducted immediately following the intervention on 7 June 2024 demonstrated successful placement of the third transcatheter valve. The valve cusps exhibited adequate mobility and full opening, without evidence of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Despite this, haemodynamic measurements revealed elevated transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was measured at 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) registered at 0.24, indicating severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

At the 1-month follow-up, TTE demonstrated substantial improvement: the mean gradient declined to 27 mm Hg, and AVA increased to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodelling was apparent, characterized by reduced chamber dimensions. No residual regurgitation was detected, and valve function remained consistent within the moderately stenotic range.

By 3.5 months post procedure, the patient continued to be asymptomatic with preserved exercise tolerance. No clinical or echocardiographic signs of heart failure or recurrent infection were present. The prosthetic valve sustained favourable function with ongoing improvement in haemodynamic indices.

At the 1-year mark following implantation (June 2025), the patient maintained clinical stability and physical activity, experiencing neither dyspnoea nor decompensatory symptoms. TTE recorded a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. There were no valve-specific complications, regurgitation, or evidence of renewed endocarditis.

Collectively, these observations support the durability of the outcome and indicate persistent clinical and functional benefits of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure in this high-risk individual.

Discussion:

This case underscores the complexities in clinical judgment and procedural execution encountered during a third TAVI for a patient with previous TAV-in-TAV implants and a history of PVE.

Although TAVI has become a well-recognized approach to manage aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with previous episodes of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines addressing valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis recommend surgery as the preferred option in PVE, particularly when heart failure or persistent infection is present. Nonetheless, the guidelines advocate for a tailored approach, considering infection management, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

The selection of TAVI as the initial management in 2014 was influenced by the patient’s elevated operative risk and numerous comorbid conditions, rendering traditional surgery inappropriate. After subsequent structural valve failure, a transcatheter redo procedure was carried out in 2022 to restore valve function and avoid the hazards associated with conventional surgery. Once PVE had been effectively managed and no signs of ongoing infection were noted, a third transcatheter procedure was undertaken in 2024. This decision was supported by the ongoing unacceptable surgical risk, the anatomical complexity resulting from prior valve frames, and confirmation via CT imaging that a plan maintaining coronary access and satisfactory haemodynamics was feasible.

The patient’s prosthetic valve dysfunction was attributed to multiple factors. Although the prior episode of infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage and mechanical impairment, the ongoing structural degradation of the transcatheter valves was also a prominent contributor. Crucially, after confirmation that the infection had resolved and no extended antibiotic therapy was warranted, management of the prosthetic valve dysfunction followed standard protocols as in cases absent of active endocarditis. Consequently, procedural choices were determined chiefly by anatomical considerations, hemodynamic compromise, and operative risk, rather than solely by the antecedent infection.

In this scenario, the patient had completed the course of antimicrobials and displayed neither clinical nor imaging evidence of ongoing infection, having undergone comprehensive preprocedure assessment—including TEE and MSCT—that excluded vegetations or paravalvular issues. The patient’s EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, indicating very high surgical risk compounded by serious comorbid conditions (COPD, diabetes mellitus, previous GI bleeding), and the presence of two transcatheter prostheses integrated extensively with the aortic wall. These factors made conventional surgery extremely risky and technically demanding. Given the deep seating of the earlier valves, a surgical approach would likely have required replacement of both the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple reports have described TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 each noting distinct anatomical and procedural hurdles. Our case expands this scarce literature, particularly given the unusual scenario of degeneration following endocarditis, along with sustained valve performance observed at one-year follow-up.

This report highlights the increasing relevance of lifelong management approaches in structural valve disorders. Successive transcatheter interventions present escalating challenges related to frame stacking, commissural positioning, coronary re-entry, and remaining effective orifice area. Proactive, long-range procedural planning—including early discussions about potential intervention pathways—is especially crucial for younger patients or those at elevated risk, beginning with the initial procedure.

The structural valve dysfunction in this patient was characterized by leaflet fibrosis due to prior degeneration, coupled with likely mechanical constriction after infection. The care team opted to place a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 device—after preprocedural CT imaging and three-dimensional modelling confirmed feasibility and satisfactory clearance of the coronary arteries (figures 1, 5 and 7). High deployment of the third transcatheter valve was intentionally chosen. By positioning the device at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets, the procedure secured the existing leaflets against the stent frame, preventing further reduction in effective orifice area caused by leaflet infolding. Additionally, this technique reduced the creation of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, thereby facilitating ongoing coronary perfusion and access for future interventions. Preprocedural CT evaluation informed this strategy, which was pivotal for achieving suitable haemodynamics within a triple-layered stent structure. Although the arrangement of three transcatheter valves is complex, the technique proved to be both achievable and replicable when detailed imaging and a well-defined implantation plan guided the process.

The Acurate neo2 device was chosen for this procedure due to its supra-annular leaflet placement, comparatively short frame height, and beneficial haemodynamic characteristics in confined anatomies. Its structural features facilitate commissural alignment, which was especially important in this patient to maintain potential access to the coronary arteries in the future.

One of the main technical challenges associated with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures is the potential for restricted coronary access as a result of multiple stent frames overlapping. Despite efforts to achieve optimal commissural alignment, the accumulated stent mesh could complicate future coronary interventions. This underscores the importance of careful procedural planning, particularly for patients with pre-existing coronary disease or in whom future coronary access might be needed. Preprocedural CT imaging was utilized to assess the landing zone, analyze stent frame interactions, and estimate the distance from the valve to the coronary ostia, although comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis with specialized software was not carried out.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient observed following the third valve placement can likely be explained by optimized commissural positioning, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the previously implanted Evolut frame, which promoted better leaflet mobility and central hemodynamic flow. Although deliberate frame fracture was not pursued, use of a high-pressure balloon for post-dilation probably aided further radial expansion of the Evolut, thereby improving valve performance.

This intervention was conducted under institutional authorization for the off-label use of the Acurate neo2 device. Shared decision-making included the patient and his family, and the case was evaluated in a multidisciplinary heart team meeting, with input from interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

In the event of early prosthesis failure, management choices would be extremely constrained. Surgical explantation would pose significant technical difficulties and carry considerable risk. Innovative catheter-based interventions, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration (for example, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction [BASILICA]), could be contemplated, but their practicality and safety in scenarios involving triple-layered stent constructs remain uncertain.
