TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the range of technical difficulties inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve interventions, therapeutic approaches must be individualized for each patient. We describe the management of a male patient in his late seventies with a particularly intricate clinical background, encompassing two prior transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures as well as a history of infective endocarditis. The patient later experienced marked deterioration of his aortic prosthesis, necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. His initial intervention involved implantation of a Lotus valve in 2014, which was followed in 2022 by a TAV-in-TAV procedure utilizing an Evolut prosthesis; subsequent to this, he developed endocarditis-associated valve degeneration and severe restenosis. In 2024, a third prosthetic device (Acurate neo2) was inserted using a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As the indications for TAVI broaden and treated individuals live longer, increasingly intricate procedures involving multiple valve prostheses are expected to arise with greater frequency, underscoring the importance of advancements in procedural planning, imaging modalities, and sustained patient monitoring. At one year post-procedure, the patient exhibits no symptoms and maintains adequate valve function. This report demonstrates that a third TAVI can be successfully undertaken after resolution of prosthetic valve endocarditis, with the assistance of CT-based planning and validated by one-year follow-up—an outcome seldom described in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has evolved into a well-recognized therapeutic modality for individuals presenting with severe aortic stenosis, irrespective of their surgical risk stratification. With increasing utilization of TAVI and improvements in patient longevity, clinicians are encountering a rising incidence of structural valve degeneration and consequent requirements for repeat interventions.

Within this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) implantation has become a practical option for addressing dysfunctional transcatheter heart valves. Nevertheless, these repeat procedures entail distinct anatomical and procedural complexities, such as heightened potential for coronary artery occlusion and the possibility of significant prosthesis-patient mismatch due to diminished effective orifice area. The procedural intricacies are further compounded in scenarios involving serial reinterventions, for example, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where the interaction of several superimposed valve frames requires meticulous consideration.

Currently, data on the outcomes of multiple, sequential transcatheter valve reinterventions are sparse, especially among patients who have experienced prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In such complex cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, individualized assessment of risks, and collaborative multidisciplinary planning become crucial for establishing procedural appropriateness and optimizing clinical results.

This report demonstrates the value of CT-based procedural planning and a strategic transcatheter approach in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI procedures and resolved PVE, emphasizing critical elements pertinent to the longitudinal management of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was admitted to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after presenting with shortness of breath, a spasmodic sensation on the left side of the chest provoked by moderate exertion (such as walking 200–300 m), episodes of dizziness, and general fatigue.

The patient’s medical history was significant for arterial hypertension and three-vessel coronary artery disease, for which he underwent multiple percutaneous coronary interventions: to the right coronary artery in 2009, the left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, another intervention on the LAD in 2012, and the circumflex artery in 2014. His comorbidities included third-degree atrioventricular block that necessitated pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and multiple recurrences of gastrointestinal bleeding since 2022.

In response to advancing aortic valve degeneration with pronounced stenosis, the patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) via transcatheter implantation in 2014. However, by December 2021, the severity of exertional dyspnoea increased and echocardiography revealed significant stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. By early 2022, the mean gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had fallen to 42%, down from 54% in December.

In May 2022, a TAV-in-TAV procedure was carried out with the deployment of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). This intervention was complicated by a pulsatile hematoma and pseudoaneurysm at the femoral access site, which required surgical repair.

Post-procedure, there was an improvement in left ventricular EF from 42% to 54%, suggesting an effective initial haemodynamic outcome. The patient subsequently received comprehensive, guideline-recommended pharmacological therapy for heart failure, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months afterwards, in September 2022, the patient required rehospitalisation due to ongoing fever and aggravating symptoms of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) identified significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean pressure gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures yielded Enterococcus faecalis, leading to the diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete regimen of pathogen-specific antimicrobial therapy targeting E. faecalis, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, which was subsequently transitioned to oral antibiotics in accordance with guidance from infectious disease specialists. At the time of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), the antimicrobial regimen had been concluded, with no continuation of long-term suppressive antibiotics. Clinical evaluation, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) all demonstrated clearance of infection and absence of active endocarditis.

Upon completion of antibiotic therapy and with some symptomatic improvement, follow-up assessment in January 2024 detected deterioration in the performance of the prosthetic valve, manifesting as severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient was elevated at 49 mm Hg, while the left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had returned to 66%.

The multidisciplinary team decided to pursue another intervention. At hospital admission in June 2024, TTE continued to show grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a persistent mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly decreased aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², indicating advanced prosthetic valve dysfunction. The AVA measurement was calculated utilizing the continuity equation, informed by left ventricular outflow tract dimensions and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. Imaging also revealed the presence of two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

On MSCT, the Evolut prosthesis was found to have a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm, supporting the diagnosis of profound haemodynamic compromise and serving as a basis for procedural planning for a third transcatheter valve implantation (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT aided in the evaluation of anatomical suitability and potential risk for coronary artery obstruction. The imaging review involved detailed analysis of coronary ostial heights, dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, valve-to-coronary distance, and the structural interactions of the previously implanted valve frames. Special consideration was given to the spatial relationship between the newly anticipated neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, alongside the possibility of high valve implantation to maintain both coronary perfusion and the option for future coronary access. These assessments informed the selection of the transcatheter valve, the depth of implantation, and overall procedural planning, ultimately permitting the intervention to proceed without the necessity for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed in advance of the planned intervention, confirming there were no ongoing signs of endocarditis or paravalvular issues.

Treatment:

Considering the patient’s intricate anatomy, characterized by triple stenting, a history of infective endocarditis, and ineligibility for surgery due to high risk, a third TAVI was conducted following a computed tomography-guided, strategy-oriented approach. This approach prioritized coronary artery preservation, commissural orientation, and optimization of the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention utilized local anaesthesia, with both left radial and left femoral arterial access established. Severe aortic regurgitation was verified by aortography utilizing a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

Through the left femoral artery, a Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle. The aortic valve was then pre-dilated using a 22/40 mm balloon catheter while rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute was maintained. During this step, concurrent aortography demonstrated sustained patency of coronary flow (figure 3).

A self-expanding Acurate neo2 bioprosthetic valve (size S) was positioned at the aortic annulus. Guided by preprocedural CT modeling, alignment with the Evolut prosthesis leaflets was achieved to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the potential for patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Subsequently, a 20/40 mm balloon was used for post-dilation under rapid ventricular pacing to ensure optimal device expansion. Final aortography showed that the valve was correctly placed, coronary flow was preserved, and there was no evidence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Valve implantation was carried out with careful attention to commissural alignment, as informed by preprocedural CT imaging and fluoroscopic markers, to facilitate future coronary access and limit the complexity of possible subsequent procedures.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural success and performance of the prosthetic valve were evaluated in line with Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines. This included comprehensive assessment of haemodynamic status, the existence of any residual regurgitation, and the identification of complications arising from the procedure.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) obtained directly after the intervention on 7 June 2024 verified correct placement of the third transcatheter valve. The prosthetic leaflets demonstrated full mobility and excursion, with no detectable aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Despite this, there were elevated haemodynamic measurements, with a mean transvalvular gradient of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated as 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24—indicative of severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

By the 1-month follow-up, TTE revealed notable improvements: the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg and AVA had expanded to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodelling was apparent, manifested by reduced chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was detected and the prosthesis continued to function appropriately, now classified within the moderately stenotic range.

At 3.5 months post-implant, the patient remained asymptomatic, maintaining good physical endurance. There were no manifestations of heart failure or recurrence of infection. The prosthetic valve continued to exhibit favourable haemodynamic results.

One year after the procedure (June 2025), the patient continued to be clinically stable and physically active, with no signs of dyspnoea or cardiac decompensation. TTE documented a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. There were no prosthesis-related complications, regurgitation, or indications of recurring endocarditis.

Overall, these results demonstrate the long-term effectiveness and persistent functional improvement offered by the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach in this complex, high-risk individual.

Discussion:

This case exemplifies the complexities involved in clinical decision-making and the technical obstacles encountered when conducting a third TAVI in a patient with a history of successive TAV-in-TAV interventions and previous PVE.

Although TAVI is now a well-recognized therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with prior episodes of endocarditis is still subject to debate. As outlined in the 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines regarding valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis, surgical valve replacement remains the standard recommendation for patients with PVE, especially when heart failure or persistent infection is present. Nonetheless, the guidelines underscore the importance of individualized treatment approaches that weigh factors such as infection status, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

The initial selection of TAVI in 2014 was based on the patient’s elevated surgical risk profile and numerous comorbid conditions, rendering surgical valve replacement less suitable. With the occurrence of structural valve degeneration, a repeat transcatheter intervention was performed in 2022 to re-establish valve functionality while minimizing the hazards associated with open-heart surgery. After successfully resolving PVE and confirming the absence of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter procedure was chosen in 2024, guided by persistently high surgical risk, complex anatomy due to existing valve implants, and CT evidence supporting a strategy that would maintain coronary patency and ensure satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

The failure of the prosthetic valve in this individual likely stemmed from a combination of factors. Although a prior episode of infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage and subsequent impairment of mechanical function, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves constituted a major contributor. Once the infection was deemed resolved, and no protracted antibiotic regimen was indicated, the therapeutic approach to prosthetic valve dysfunction mirrored that used in patients with no evidence of persistent endocarditis. Thus, selection of the appropriate intervention was dictated chiefly by anatomical suitability, the degree of haemodynamic compromise, and the patient's surgical risk profile, rather than by prior infectious history in isolation.

Specifically, the patient had successfully completed antimicrobial treatment and presented with neither clinical nor imaging evidence of ongoing infection, as corroborated by extensive preprocedural assessment with TEE and MSCT—both confirming the absence of vegetations or paravalvular pathology. Additionally, the patient exhibited a EuroSCORE II of 27.36% and multiple high-risk comorbidities (COPD, diabetes, antecedent gastrointestinal bleeding), together with two previously implanted transcatheter valves exhibiting substantial stent incorporation into the aortic wall. These factors collectively rendered conventional surgery exceptionally risky and technically complex. In light of the deep anchoring of the prior prostheses, surgical correction would likely have entailed complete replacement of the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple case reports detailing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions have been documented,37 each illustrating distinct anatomical and procedural difficulties. The present report further contributes to this sparse literature, offering the distinct perspective of post-endocarditis valve deterioration alongside sustained valve function observed at the one-year follow-up.

This case emphasizes the necessity for comprehensive lifetime management approaches in patients with structural valve disorders. Each subsequent transcatheter reintervention amplifies the complexity of factors such as cumulative frame layering, commissural orientation, coronary access, and maintenance of the residual effective orifice area. Meticulous forward planning and proactive discussions about possible procedural paths are paramount from the outset, especially in younger individuals or those with elevated procedural risk.

In this instance, the patient’s valve degeneration was characterized by leaflet fibrosis due to prior tissue degeneration and likely mechanical restriction as a consequence of infection. The care team elected to implant a third device—specifically, a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—following preprocedural CT imaging and 3D modelling, which confirmed the technical possibility and ensured satisfactory clearance of the coronary arteries (figures 1, 5, and 7). The high placement of the third transcatheter valve was intentionally planned. By positioning the device at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets, the existing leaflets were anchored against the stent scaffold, thus mitigating further narrowing of the effective orifice area resulting from inward leaflet deflection. Additionally, this strategy reduced the risk of forming a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, facilitating ongoing coronary perfusion and maintaining options for coronary intervention in the future. The approach, directed by comprehensive preprocedural CT analysis, proved fundamental for achieving optimal haemodynamics in a setting with three overlapping valve frames. Although a triple-stented transcatheter arrangement is inherently intricate, the method demonstrated practical feasibility and replicability when supported by detailed imaging and a well-defined procedural plan.

The Acurate neo2 device was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet placement, comparatively short stent frame, and advantageous haemodynamic profile in anatomically narrow situations. Its structure is conducive to achieving commissural alignment—a principal factor in this instance, as maintaining access to the coronary arteries for possible future procedures was of paramount importance.

A major technical issue associated with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions concerns the potential obstruction of coronary access, which may result from the presence of multiple overlapping stent frameworks. Despite efforts to secure commissural alignment, the increased metallic mesh from successive valves may complicate future coronary interventions. This scenario underscores the importance of careful preprocedural planning, particularly in patients with established coronary disease or anticipated future need for coronary interventions. Preprocedural CT was instrumental for assessing the implantation zone, scrutinizing possible stent interactions, and measuring the distances between the implanted valve and the coronary ostia, though comprehensive three-dimensional deployment analysis via specialized software was not conducted.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implantation likely stemmed from an interplay of optimal commissural alignment, thorough post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the existing Evolut frame. These factors collectively improved leaflet kinematics and central blood flow. While intentional fracture of the stent was not executed, the high-pressure balloon post-dilation probably promoted further radial expansion of the Evolut, thereby augmenting the functional performance of the valve.

Institutional approval was obtained for off-label usage of the Acurate neo2 valve in this procedure. Decision-making involved collaboration with the patient and family, and the case underwent detailed assessment by a multidisciplinary heart team composed of interventional cardiologists, cardiothoracic surgeons, imaging experts, and anaesthesiologists.

If premature failure of the current prosthesis were to occur, treatment alternatives would be severely constrained. Surgical removal would present substantial technical complexity and elevated risk. New transcatheter strategies—such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration techniques (for instance, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction [BASILICA])—may be options, though the safety and practicality of these interventions in the context of a triple-stented aortic root remain to be determined.
