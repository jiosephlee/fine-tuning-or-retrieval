Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the varying technical complexities inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, therapeutic approaches should be individualized for each patient. We describe a case involving a male patient in his late 70s with a complicated medical background, including two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently developed significant degeneration of his aortic prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter intervention. The patient first received a Lotus valve in 2014, followed by TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut device in 2022, later complicated by degeneration and pronounced restenosis attributed to endocarditis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 valve was successfully implanted utilizing a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As TAVI indications broaden and patient survival increases, the occurrence of such complex multi-prosthesis procedures is likely to rise, underscoring the importance of enhanced pre-procedural planning, advanced imaging techniques, and ongoing post-procedural monitoring. After one year of follow-up, the patient continues to be asymptomatic and maintains satisfactory valve function. This case demonstrates the practicality of undertaking a third TAVI after resolving prosthetic valve endocarditis, utilizing CT-based planning and confirming 1-year procedural success—an outcome seldom documented in existing literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a recognized therapy for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, regardless of their surgical risk category. With the expanding population of patients receiving TAVI and enhanced long-term survival rates, clinical practice is witnessing a rising incidence of structural valve deterioration and a greater frequency of repeat procedures.

Within this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures have become a practical approach for addressing dysfunctional transcatheter valves. Nevertheless, these interventions present unique anatomical and procedural difficulties, such as heightened risk for coronary artery obstruction and the possibility of a diminished effective orifice area, which can cause significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These issues become even more pronounced in repeated procedures like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where several overlapping stent frames require thorough assessment.

There is a paucity of data on outcomes following multiple transcatheter valve reinterventions, especially among individuals with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these challenging cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, personalized risk evaluation, and collaborative discussions within a multidisciplinary team are critical to assess procedural viability and to enhance the chances of procedural success.

This report demonstrates the importance of CT-based planning and a strategic transcatheter approach in a patient who had undergone multiple previous TAVI interventions and received treatment for PVE, underscoring crucial factors for the long-term management of structural valve disorders.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center due to complaints of shortness of breath, a spasmodic feeling in the left chest region during exertion (notably when walking 200–300 meters), episodes of dizziness, and generalized fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, triple-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: in 2009 for the right coronary artery, in 2010 for the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, another LAD intervention in 2012, and one for the circumflex artery in 2014. Other coexisting conditions comprised third-degree atrioventricular block that necessitated pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes starting in 2022.

In 2014, the patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) via transcatheter approach to address worsening aortic valve deterioration and severe stenosis. By December 2021, the individual reported increased exertional dyspnoea, with echocardiography confirming marked stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. In early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, and the ejection fraction (EF) had dropped to 42%, compared to 54% observed in December.

In May 2022, the patient received a TAV-in-TAV procedure involving placement of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). Following the intervention, a pulsatile hematoma and pseudoaneurysm developed in the femoral region, necessitating open vascular surgical management.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient’s left ventricular ejection fraction increased from 42% to 54%, demonstrating an early positive haemodynamic effect. Management included heart failure therapy in accordance with guidelines, comprising beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted to the hospital due to ongoing fever and a deterioration in heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient was treated with a complete course of antibiotics tailored to E. faecalis PVE, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy, followed by oral antibiotics as advised by infectious disease specialists. At the point of the third TAVI intervention, the antimicrobial regimen had concluded, and there was no ongoing long-term antibiotic suppression. Clinical evaluation, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) demonstrated resolution of the infection, with no signs suggestive of active endocarditis.

Following the conclusion of antimicrobial treatment and some improvement in symptoms, reassessment in January 2024 demonstrated a decline in prosthetic valve performance, characterized by severe aortic stenosis along with moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient measured 49 mm Hg, and the left ventricular ejection fraction had normalized to 66%.

It was determined that a repeat intervention would be undertaken. Upon hospital admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed ongoing grade 2–3 aortic regurgitation originating from the prosthetic valve, a mean transvalvular pressure gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly decreased aortic valve area (AVA) measured at 0.5 cm², indicating pronounced dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. Calculation of the AVA employed the continuity equation using measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and velocity-time integrals obtained via Doppler. Imaging demonstrated the presence of two superimposed bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT demonstrated that the Evolut prosthesis had a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm, indicating marked haemodynamic impairment and informing the strategy for a third transcatheter valve implantation (figure 1). The preprocedural MSCT helped evaluate anatomical suitability and the potential for coronary artery obstruction. Assessment encompassed the height of the coronary ostia, dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, valve-to-coronary distance, and interactions between the frames of prior transcatheter valves. Special consideration was given to the expected positioning of the neo-skirt in relation to the coronary ostia and the possibility of high valve implantation to maintain coronary perfusion and facilitate future access. These assessments determined the choice of prosthesis, depth of implantation, and the overall procedural approach, enabling the procedure to be completed safely without coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed prior to the reintervention and confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

In light of the patient’s intricate anatomy with three prior stents, history of infective endocarditis, and significant risk associated with surgery, a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was scheduled. This approach relied on CT imaging to guide a procedure tailored around strategic planning, prioritizing coronary artery protection, precise commissural positioning, and maximization of the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the left femoral approach. The aortic valve was pre-dilated using a 22/40 mm balloon catheter while rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute was employed. Concurrent aortography demonstrated that coronary perfusion was maintained without obstruction (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve, size S, was positioned within the aortic valve location. Alignment was achieved relative to the Evolut valve’s leaflets, as determined from preprocedural CT modeling, with the intention of optimizing the effective orifice area and minimizing the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was used for post-dilation during rapid pacing to ensure optimal expansion of the valve. A final aortogram verified appropriate placement, maintained coronary flow, and showed no evidence of either perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (see figure 4).

During valve implantation, commissural alignment was considered to maintain coronary accessibility and minimize the complexity of subsequent interventions, utilizing guidance from preprocedural CT scans and fluoroscopic reference points.

Outcome and follow-up:

Assessment of procedural success and prosthetic valve function was conducted based on the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, which encompass analysis of haemodynamic performance, identification of any remaining regurgitation, and monitoring for complications associated with the procedure.

A transthoracic echocardiogram conducted right after the procedure on 7 June 2024 demonstrated appropriate positioning of the third transcatheter valve. The valve's leaflets showed full mobility and complete opening without any signs of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Nevertheless, haemodynamic assessment revealed marked transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated to be 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured 0.24, findings in keeping with severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

One month post-implantation, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated significant improvement: the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg, and the aortic valve area increased to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodeling was noted, as indicated by decreased chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was detected, and valve performance remained stable within the range consistent with moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months follow-up, the patient continued to be without symptoms and demonstrated satisfactory exercise capacity. There were no indications of heart failure or infection recurrence. The prosthetic device continued to operate effectively, with persistently improved haemodynamic values.

One year after device implantation (June 2025), the patient continued to be clinically stable and active, exhibiting neither dyspnoea nor decompensation. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mean transvalvular gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area measuring 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction of 65%. There were no detected valve-related issues, regurgitation, or recurrence of endocarditis.

These findings confirm the durability of the result and suggest a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case highlights the decision-making process and technical challenges of performing a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV procedures and a history of PVE.

Although TAVI is now a standard therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis recommend surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach in PVE, especially when there is associated heart failure or persistent infection. Nevertheless, the guidelines highlight the importance of tailoring treatment plans to each patient, considering factors such as infection resolution, surgical risk, and anatomical considerations.

TAVI was initially selected in 2014 due to the patient’s elevated surgical risk and numerous comorbidities, which rendered traditional surgical valve replacement an unsuitable option at that juncture. When the patient later experienced structural valve deterioration, a repeat transcatheter approach was implemented in 2022 to reestablish valve functionality while circumventing the hazards associated with open surgery. After successfully treating PVE and with no evidence of ongoing infection, the third transcatheter procedure performed in 2024 was prompted by extreme surgical risk, the technical complexities introduced by the presence of previous valve frames, and CT findings demonstrating that a coronary-preserving technique with satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes was feasible.

In this patient, prosthetic valve failure was probably due to multiple contributing factors. The prior episode of infective endocarditis may have caused damage to the valve leaflets, resulting in impaired mobility, while ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valve also significantly influenced the dysfunction. After confirming resolution of infection and determining that continued suppressive antibiotic treatment was unnecessary, management of the prosthetic valve complication mirrored that used for individuals without current or unresolved endocarditis. Consequently, procedural planning was chiefly dictated by anatomical considerations, severity of haemodynamic compromise, and assessment of surgical risk, rather than being based solely on the previous infection.

In this instance, the patient had finished antibiotic treatment and exhibited neither clinical nor imaging evidence of ongoing infection. Comprehensive preprocedural assessments, including transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT), demonstrated no vegetations or paravalvular complications. Additionally, the patient presented with a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, notable comorbidities such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding, as well as two previously placed transcatheter valves with significant stent incorporation into the aortic wall. These factors collectively made open surgical management exceptionally high-risk and technically challenging. Due to the substantial fixation of earlier prosthetic valves, surgical treatment would have likely required replacement of both the aortic root and the ascending aorta.

Multiple case reports have described TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 emphasizing a range of anatomical and technical difficulties. This case contributes further to this small pool of literature, particularly given its distinct background of degeneration following endocarditis and maintenance of valve performance after one year.

This report highlights the increasing relevance of adopting lifelong management approaches in structural valve pathology. Each subsequent transcatheter procedure brings added complexity regarding factors such as frame stacking, alignment of commissures, future coronary access, and maintaining adequate residual effective orifice area. Early and comprehensive discussions about long-term procedural strategies are essential, beginning with the initial intervention, especially for younger patients or those at elevated risk.

In this case, the patient exhibited leaflet fibrosis resulting from previous degeneration and likely mechanical limitation following an infectious episode. The medical team elected to implant a third prosthetic valve—specifically, a self-expanding Acurate neo2—after preprocedural CT imaging and 3D modeling confirmed that the procedure was technically possible with sufficient clearance of the coronary arteries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The intentional placement of the third transcatheter valve at a high position was part of the procedural plan. By deploying the valve at the location of the Evolut prosthesis’ leaflets, the existing degenerated leaflets were pressed against the stent frame, thereby averting additional narrowing of the effective orifice area due to inward leaflet folding. This technique also helped to limit creation of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, maintaining both coronary perfusion and the ability to access the coronaries in the future. The choice of this technique was informed by meticulous preprocedural CT evaluation and proved critical for optimizing haemodynamics in the context of three layered stents. While managing a triple-layer transcatheter valve was challenging, the approach was technically successful and could be consistently applied when preceded by thorough preprocedural imaging and use of a planned deployment technique.

The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet location, comparatively short frame, and advantageous haemodynamic characteristics in constricted anatomies. The device is engineered to facilitate commissural alignment, an important factor in this case to maintain access to the coronary arteries in the future.

An important technical issue in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions involves the potential obstruction of coronary access caused by several superimposed stent frames. Despite efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the total mesh density may hinder subsequent coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural planning in patients with established coronary pathology or those likely to require coronary interventions in the future. Preprocedural CT was utilized to measure the landing zone, assess interactions between stent frames, and estimate the distances from the valve to the coronary arteries, though comprehensive three-dimensional expansion evaluation using specialized software was not carried out.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third device placement is likely due to improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the existing Evolut frame, which facilitated better leaflet mobility and central flow patterns. Although deliberate frame fracture was not carried out, the application of high-pressure balloon post-dilation probably led to additional radial expansion of the Evolut, thereby augmenting the function of the valve.

Institutional authorization was obtained to utilize the Acurate neo2 prosthesis outside its approved indications. The patient and his family participated in shared decision-making, and the case underwent assessment during a multidisciplinary heart team conference attended by interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anaesthesiologists.

If the present prosthesis were to fail earlier than expected, therapeutic avenues would be notably restricted. Surgical removal of the device would continue to pose significant technical challenges and carry considerable risk. Newer catheter-directed interventions, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or intentional leaflet laceration procedures—such as bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—could potentially be explored, though the practicality and safety of these methods in the setting of three overlapping stents are still unclear.
