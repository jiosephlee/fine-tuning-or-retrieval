Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V. Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the varied technical complexities involved in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, management strategies must be individualized for each patient. We describe the case of a male patient in his late seventies with a complicated medical background, including two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced severe degeneration of his aortic prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The patient first received a Lotus valve in 2014, underwent a TAV-in-TAV intervention with an Evolut prosthesis in 2022, and later developed endocarditis-related deterioration and pronounced restenosis. In 2024, a third valve (Acurate neo2) was placed using a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV technique. With broader indications for TAVI and patients living longer after the procedure, complex scenarios involving multiple valve implants may become more common, underscoring the importance of enhanced procedural planning, advanced imaging, and extended surveillance. At one year after the most recent intervention, the patient shows no symptoms and retains adequate valve function. This case demonstrates that performing a third TAVI following resolved prosthetic valve endocarditis is achievable, aided by computed tomography-based procedural guidance and documented by one-year follow-up, a situation infrequently detailed in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a recognized therapeutic approach for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, regardless of their surgical risk category. With the expanding use of TAVI and enhancements in long-term patient outcomes, issues such as structural degeneration of the implanted valve and the subsequent necessity for additional procedures are being observed more frequently in everyday practice.

Within this context, performing a transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure has become a feasible approach to address malfunctioning transcatheter aortic valve prostheses. Nonetheless, these procedures present unique anatomical and procedural difficulties, such as the potential for obstructing the coronary arteries and diminishing the effective orifice area, potentially leading to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These complexities are heightened during successive reinterventions like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where the presence of multiple overlapping stent frames necessitates meticulous consideration.

There is a scarcity of data concerning repeated transcatheter valve interventions, especially among individuals with previous prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these challenging cases, comprehensive pre-procedural imaging, patient-specific risk evaluation, and collaborative input from a multidisciplinary team are crucial for assessing procedural suitability and enhancing the likelihood of successful outcomes.

This report demonstrates the importance of CT-based planning and a strategic approach to transcatheter intervention in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI procedures and managed PVE, underscoring essential factors in the long-term management of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center due to complaints of shortness of breath, a spasmodic feeling on the left side of the chest upon exertion (such as walking 200–300 meters), as well as episodes of dizziness and general fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, triple-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: in 2009 for the right coronary artery, in 2010 for the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, again in 2012 for a repeat LAD procedure, and in 2014 involving the circumflex artery. Other health issues consisted of a third-degree atrioventricular block that necessitated pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes since 2022.

In 2014, following worsening degeneration of the aortic valve and critical stenosis, a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) was implanted via transcatheter approach. By December 2021, the patient experienced increased exertional dyspnoea, and echocardiography revealed significant stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. In early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient rose to 83 mm Hg, accompanied by a further reduction in ejection fraction (EF) to 42%, down from 54% recorded in December.

In May 2022, the patient had a transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve procedure using a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). Post-procedure, a pulsatile hematoma and femoral pseudoaneurysm developed, necessitating open surgical vascular intervention.

After undergoing the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient’s left ventricular ejection fraction increased from 42% to 54%, suggesting an early positive haemodynamic effect. Heart failure management was initiated according to guidelines, comprising beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as clinically appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted once more due to ongoing fever and a deterioration in heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures yielded Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient completed an entire course of tailored antibiotic therapy for E. faecalis-related PVE, which comprised six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, then transitioned to oral antibiotics as directed by infectious disease specialists. At the time of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), antimicrobial therapy had been finished, and there was no ongoing requirement for long-term suppressive antibiotics. Clinical evaluation, repeat blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice computed tomography (MSCT) all demonstrated infection resolution with no indications of persistent endocarditis.

Upon finishing antibiotic treatment and noting some improvement in symptoms, reassessment in January 2024 demonstrated deteriorating prosthetic valve performance, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The measured mean gradient was 49 mm Hg, and the left ventricular ejection fraction had returned to 66%.

A reintervention was planned. Upon admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed ongoing grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient measuring 49 mm Hg, and a significantly diminished aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², findings indicative of severe prosthetic valve malfunction. Calculation of the AVA was performed via the continuity equation, utilizing measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. Imaging demonstrated two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT identified that the Evolut prosthesis had a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm, demonstrating marked haemodynamic impairment and informing the strategy for a third transcatheter valve implantation (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT was employed to evaluate the suitability of the anatomy and estimate the potential for coronary artery obstruction. This evaluation included assessments of coronary ostial height, the dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, valve-to-coronary distance, and interactions among the frames of the previously placed transcatheter valves. Special consideration was given to the predicted configuration of the neo-skirt in relation to the coronary ostia, as well as to the possibility of a high implantation to maintain coronary perfusion and facilitate subsequent coronary access. These assessments influenced the choices regarding valve type, depth of implantation, and overall procedural plan, ultimately enabling the procedure to be conducted without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed prior to the reintervention and confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Due to the patient’s intricate anatomy with three stents in place, history of infective endocarditis, and elevated surgical risk, a third TAVI was scheduled, utilizing CT imaging for planning. The approach prioritized coronary protection, proper commissural alignment, and maximization of the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the left femoral approach. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilation using a 22/40 mm catheter while rapid ventricular pacing was maintained at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography verified that coronary blood flow was not impeded (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve, size S, was deployed in the aortic position. Preprocedural CT modeling guided alignment of this valve with the existing Evolut leaflets to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Post-dilation was carried out utilizing a 20/40 mm balloon while rapid pacing was employed to enhance expansion of the valve. Subsequent aortography verified appropriate placement, maintained coronary blood flow, and demonstrated no evidence of either perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

During valve implantation, attention was given to commissural alignment to maintain access to the coronary arteries and minimize the complexity of potential future interventions. This was achieved using guidance from preprocedural CT scans and fluoroscopic reference points.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural efficacy and prosthetic valve function were evaluated based on the criteria established by the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3, encompassing assessment of haemodynamic parameters, detection of any residual regurgitation, and identification of complications associated with the procedure.

A transthoracic echocardiogram conducted promptly post-procedure on 7 June 2024 verified appropriate positioning of the third transcatheter valve. The valve’s leaflets appeared to open fully and moved freely, with no aortic regurgitation observed (grade 0). Despite this, haemodynamic assessment revealed considerable transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient measuring 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area was calculated as 0.9 cm² (with an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index was determined to be 0.24—findings indicative of severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

At one month post-implantation, transthoracic echocardiography revealed significant progress: the mean pressure gradient measured 27 mm Hg, and aortic valve area expanded to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodeling was apparent, as chamber volumes had decreased. No persistent regurgitation was detected, and valve function continued to be consistent within the range of moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months of follow-up, the patient exhibited no symptoms and demonstrated adequate exercise capacity. There were no indications of heart failure or repeat infection. The prosthetic device continued to function effectively, with persistently favorable haemodynamic measurements.

One year following implantation (June 2025), the patient continued to be clinically stable and physically active, experiencing no shortness of breath or indications of decompensation. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mean pressure gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction of 65%. There were no detected valve-related complications, regurgitation, or recurrence of endocarditis.

These findings confirm the durability of the result and suggest a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case highlights the decision-making process and technical challenges of performing a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV procedures and a history of PVE.

Although TAVI is now a well-recognized therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) regarding valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis recommend surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially when associated with heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines highlight that treatment decisions should be tailored to the patient, considering factors such as infection eradication, surgical risk, and anatomical suitability.

TAVI was initially selected in 2014 due to the patient’s elevated surgical risk and significant comorbid conditions, rendering standard surgical valve replacement less suitable at that juncture. When the patient later experienced structural valve failure, a repeat transcatheter intervention was undertaken in 2022 to re-establish valve competence, thereby circumventing the hazards associated with open-heart surgery. After effective management of PVE and with no evidence of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter procedure was chosen in 2024 in view of continued high surgical risk, challenging anatomical issues stemming from existing valve prostheses, and preprocedural CT confirmation that a coronary flow–preserving, haemodynamically sound approach was feasible.

The underlying causes of this patient’s prosthetic valve failure were probably multiple. Although the previous infective endocarditis episode could have led to leaflet damage and mechanical impairment, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves also significantly contributed. Notably, after confirming resolution of infection and determining that prolonged suppressive antibiotics were unnecessary, the approach to treating prosthetic valve dysfunction paralleled that used for patients who did not have current or persistent endocarditis. Consequently, procedural planning was based chiefly on anatomical suitability, degree of haemodynamic compromise, and the patient’s operative risk, rather than the prior occurrence of infection by itself.

In this instance, the patient had finished a full course of antibiotics, demonstrated neither clinical symptoms nor imaging evidence of ongoing infection, and underwent comprehensive preprocedural assessment with both TEE and MSCT, which excluded the presence of vegetations and paravalvular complications. Additionally, the patient’s EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, with notable comorbidities including COPD, diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding, as well as two previously implanted transcatheter valves with substantial stent incorporation into the aortic wall. These factors made open-heart surgery extremely high risk and technically challenging. The deep anchoring of the prior prostheses meant that surgical management would have likely required complete replacement of both the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple published case reports have described TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 detailing distinct anatomical and technical difficulties encountered. The present case further contributes to this small pool of evidence, distinguished by degeneration following endocarditis and maintained valve performance at one-year follow-up.

This case highlights the increasing significance of developing lifelong management plans for structural valve disease. Repeated transcatheter interventions add complexity to issues like frame overlap, commissural orientation, coronary access, and maintaining adequate effective orifice area. Proactive, long-term planning and initiating conversations about possible procedural pathways should begin as early as the initial treatment—especially for younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

In this case, the patient exhibited leaflet fibrosis stemming from previous degeneration as well as likely mechanical restriction following infection. After reviewing preprocedural CT scans and 3D modeling, which showed sufficient coronary clearance and the practicality of further intervention (figures 1, 5, and 7), the medical team proceeded with a third valve implantation using a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis. A high implantation position was intentionally chosen for the third transcatheter valve. Placing the device at the level of the existing Evolut valve’s leaflets allowed for stabilization of these leaflets against the stent frame, which helped to prevent further decrease of the effective orifice area resulting from leaflet infolding. Additionally, this method reduced the likelihood of neo-skirt formation beneath the coronary ostia, thereby helping to maintain coronary blood flow and ensuring the possibility of future coronary access. The procedural plan was informed by preprocedural CT analysis and was integral in attaining satisfactory hemodynamics within a triple-stented valve anatomy. Although the presence of three transcatheter valves introduces significant procedural complexity, this technique was both technically practical and reproducible when executed with comprehensive preprocedural imaging and a well-defined implantation plan.

The Acurate neo2 device was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet placement, modest frame height, and advantageous haemodynamic characteristics suited to constricted anatomies. The device’s architecture also facilitates commissural alignment, which was especially important in this scenario to maintain the potential for subsequent coronary access.

An important technical issue in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions involves the potential for compromised coronary access caused by several superimposed stent frames. Even with attempts at commissural alignment, the total amount of stent material can still complicate subsequent coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural planning, particularly in individuals with established coronary artery disease or when preserving future access is necessary. Pre-procedural CT was utilized to assess the landing area, analyze stent frame interactions, and estimate distances between the valve and coronary arteries; however, comprehensive three-dimensional expansion assessment using specialized software was not conducted.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implantation is likely due to improved commissural orientation, successful post-dilatation, and partial mechanical widening of the pre-existing Evolut stent, all of which enhanced leaflet mobility and central hemodynamics. Although a deliberate frame fracture was not attempted, post-dilation with a high-pressure balloon probably resulted in additional radial expansion of the Evolut frame, thereby optimizing valve performance.

The procedure was carried out with institutional authorization for the off-label application of the Acurate neo2 valve. The patient and his family participated in shared decision-making, and the case underwent discussion by a multidisciplinary heart team comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the present prosthesis experiences early failure, available therapeutic choices would be quite restricted. Operative removal would continue to pose significant technical challenges and carry substantial risk. Newer transcatheter techniques, including procedures like bioprosthetic valve fracture or intentional leaflet laceration—such as bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—could potentially be evaluated, although it is not yet clear whether these methods are practical or safe in cases involving three stent layers.
