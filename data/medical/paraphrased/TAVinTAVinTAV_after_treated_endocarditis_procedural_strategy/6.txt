Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V. Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Owing to the variety of technical obstacles inherent to transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve interventions, management should be individualized for each patient. We describe a male in his late seventies with a complex background, including two prior transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced significant deterioration of his aortic prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The patient first received a Lotus valve in 2014, then underwent a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis in 2022, after which degenerative changes and severe restenosis related to prior endocarditis emerged. In 2024, a third valve (Acurate neo2) was placed utilizing a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As indications for TAVI continue to broaden and longevity improves in this population, the occurrence of such complex, multi-valve interventions is expected to rise, underscoring the need for enhanced procedural planning, imaging modalities, and ongoing monitoring. At one-year follow-up, the patient was asymptomatic with sustained valve function. This report demonstrates the practicality of a third TAVI following management of prosthetic valve endocarditis, guided by CT-based planning and confirmed by one-year follow-up, a scenario with limited representation in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a recognized therapeutic approach for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, encompassing patients with various levels of surgical risk. With a rising volume of TAVI procedures and greater long-term patient survival, clinicians are more frequently managing cases of structural valve degeneration and facing subsequent requirements for repeat interventions.

Within this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures have become an increasingly utilized option for treating failed transcatheter valves. Nevertheless, these interventions present distinct anatomic and procedural complexities, including heightened risk of coronary obstruction and possible reduction in the effective orifice area, which may precipitate significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These issues are compounded when considering repeat procedures, such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where multiple superimposed valve structures necessitate meticulous evaluation.

There is a scarcity of data on repeated transcatheter valve interventions, especially among individuals with previous prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these challenging cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, tailored risk evaluation, and collaborative input from multiple specialties are crucial for assessing procedural viability and enhancing results.

This case demonstrates the importance of CT-based procedural planning and a strategy-focused transcatheter approach in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI interventions and previously managed PVE, underscoring significant factors in the long-term care of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center following complaints of shortness of breath, a spastic feeling on the left side of the chest elicited by physical activity such as walking 200–300 meters, along with dizziness and generalized fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, repeated intervention of the LAD in 2012, and circumflex artery in 2014. Other comorbid conditions consisted of a third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), as well as recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

In 2014, as a result of ongoing degeneration of the aortic valve and marked stenosis, the patient received a transcatheter placement of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, the patient's exertional dyspnoea had intensified, and echocardiography indicated significant stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. In early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient rose to 83 mm Hg, and the ejection fraction decreased further to 42%, down from 54% measured in December.

In May 2022, the patient received a transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve procedure using a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). Postoperatively, the patient developed a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm, which necessitated open vascular surgical intervention.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient’s left ventricular ejection fraction increased from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive initial haemodynamic effect. Management included heart failure therapy in accordance with established guidelines, incorporating beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted to the hospital again due to ongoing fever and a deterioration of heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures returned positive for Enterococcus faecalis, establishing a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete course of pathogen-directed antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis-associated PVE, which consisted of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, subsequently followed by an oral regimen as advised by an infectious disease specialist. At the time of the third TAVI intervention, the antimicrobial course had concluded and no suppressive long-term antibiotic therapy was being administered. Comprehensive clinical evaluation, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice computed tomography (MSCT) collectively demonstrated infection resolution without indications of ongoing endocarditis.

Following the conclusion of antibiotic treatment and some improvement in symptoms, reassessment in January 2024 demonstrated deterioration of the prosthetic valve, exhibiting severe aortic stenosis accompanied by moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient measured 49 mm Hg, and the left ventricular ejection fraction had normalized to 66%.

A reintervention was planned. Upon admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated ongoing moderate-to-moderately severe (grade 2–3) aortic regurgitation originating from the prosthetic valve, a mean transvalvular pressure gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly diminished aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², indicating pronounced prosthetic valve dysfunction. The AVA was obtained using the continuity equation, utilizing measurements of the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler-based velocity-time integrals. Imaging revealed the presence of two bioprosthetic valves in an overlapping position.

MSCT identified a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut valve, indicating marked haemodynamic impairment and informing the strategy for a third transcatheter valve procedure (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT also served to evaluate the anatomical suitability of the intervention and the likelihood of coronary artery obstruction. This assessment entailed reviewing coronary ostial height, dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, valve-to-coronary distances, and the interactions among frames of the previously deployed transcatheter valves. Special emphasis was given to how the predicted neo-skirt would relate to the coronary ostia, as well as assessing the practicality of high valve placement to maintain coronary perfusion and ensure accessibility for any future coronary interventions. The comprehensive analysis informed choices regarding valve type, implant position, and overall procedural plan, allowing the procedure to be conducted safely without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed prior to the reintervention and confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Considering the patient's intricate anatomy with three stents, history of infective endocarditis, and excessive risk associated with surgery, a third TAVI procedure was scheduled. This intervention was to be guided by CT imaging and tailored strategy, prioritizing coronary protection, precise commissural alignment, and enhancement of the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the left femoral approach. The aortic valve was pre-dilated using a 22/40 mm balloon catheter while rapid ventricular pacing was administered at a rate of 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography demonstrated that coronary perfusion was maintained without obstruction (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (size S) was placed at the aortic site. Alignment of the device with the Evolut valve leaflets was achieved according to preprocedural CT modeling, aiming to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was utilized for post-dilation with rapid pacing to ensure optimal expansion of the valve. Subsequent aortography demonstrated accurate valve placement, maintained coronary flow, and no evidence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (see figure 4).

Valve positioning was performed with consideration of commissural alignment in order to maintain coronary access and minimize potential challenges in subsequent interventions, utilizing preprocedural CT scans and fluoroscopic reference points for guidance.

Outcome and follow-up:

The assessment of procedural success and prosthetic valve function followed the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 standards, encompassing analysis of haemodynamic parameters, detection of any residual regurgitation, and identification of complications linked to the procedure.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) performed immediately following the procedure on 7 June 2024 verified that the third transcatheter valve had been successfully implanted. The prosthetic valve leaflets demonstrated normal mobility, showing complete excursion without any detectable aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Nevertheless, hemodynamic assessment revealed marked transvalvular pressure gradients, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The measured aortic valve area (AVA) was 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24, findings compatible with severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

One month post-implantation, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated significant progress: the mean transvalvular gradient measured 27 mm Hg and the aortic valve area had risen to 1.1 cm². Left ventricular remodeling was evident by decreased chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was detected, and the prosthetic valve maintained stable function within the range of moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months follow-up, the patient exhibited no symptoms and demonstrated adequate exercise capacity. There was no evidence of heart failure or infection recurrence. The prosthetic device continued to function effectively, with ongoing favorable haemodynamic status.

One year after the procedure (June 2025), the patient continued to demonstrate clinical stability and maintained an active lifestyle, with no complaints of dyspnoea or indications of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mean transvalvular gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area (AVA) measuring 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. There were no complications associated with the valve, no regurgitation, and no signs of recurrent endocarditis detected.

These findings confirm the durability of the result and suggest a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case highlights the decision-making process and technical challenges of performing a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV procedures and a history of PVE.

Although TAVI is now a standard therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advocate for surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach in prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially when complicated by heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines highlight the importance of tailoring management to the individual, considering factors such as infection resolution, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

The selection of TAVI as the primary therapeutic approach in 2014 was influenced by the patient's elevated surgical risk profile and significant comorbid conditions, rendering traditional surgical valve replacement less suitable at that point. When the patient later experienced structural degeneration of the valve, a repeat transcatheter approach was undertaken in 2022 to restore valvular function while circumventing the hazards associated with open surgery. After effective management of PVE and with no evidence of ongoing infection, the rationale for a third transcatheter procedure in 2024 centered on the patient’s prohibitive surgical risk, the technical complexities arising from pre-existing valve stent frames, and pre-procedural CT imaging that verified the practicality of an approach focused on maintaining coronary artery patency and securing satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

The prosthetic valve dysfunction in this patient was probably due to several factors. Although the previous episode of infective endocarditis might have led to leaflet damage and resulting mechanical limitation, ongoing deterioration of the transcatheter prostheses also significantly contributed. Notably, after confirming resolution of the infection and determining that prolonged antibiotic suppression was unnecessary, the approach to managing prosthetic valve dysfunction was similar to that used in cases without active or persistent endocarditis. As a result, procedural choices were chiefly informed by anatomical suitability, the degree of haemodynamic compromise, and surgical risk, rather than being influenced solely by the patient’s history of infection.

In this instance, the patient had finished a course of antibiotics and exhibited neither clinical symptoms nor imaging evidence indicative of ongoing infection. A comprehensive preprocedural workup, incorporating TEE and MSCT, verified the lack of vegetations or paravalvular issues. Additionally, the patient’s EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, and she had notable comorbidities, including COPD, diabetes, and a previous episode of gastrointestinal bleeding, along with two transcatheter valve prostheses showing considerable stent incorporation into the aortic wall. These factors made open surgical intervention exceptionally risky and technically challenging. Owing to the firm anchoring of the existing valves, surgery would probably have required complete replacement of both the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple case reports describing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions have been documented,37 illustrating a range of anatomical complexities and technical difficulties. The present case contributes further to this sparse literature, notable for its occurrence following endocarditis-related degeneration and for demonstrating maintained prosthetic valve function at the one-year follow-up.

This case highlights the increasing significance of comprehensive, lifelong management approaches in structural valve pathology. As the number of transcatheter reinterventions rises, issues like overlapping stent frames, alignment of commissures, subsequent coronary access, and maintenance of adequate effective orifice area grow more intricate. Early establishment of long-term strategies and discussion of possible procedural pathways are essential, starting with the initial intervention—especially for younger individuals or those considered high-risk.

In this case, the patient exhibited leaflet fibrosis stemming from previous degenerative changes, along with likely mechanical restriction as a consequence of infection. The medical team elected to implant a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—after preprocedural CT imaging and 3D modelling confirmed that the procedure was technically possible and that there was sufficient clearance of the coronary arteries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The decision to deploy the third transcatheter valve at a higher position was intentional. Positioning the device at the plane of the existing Evolut valve leaflets allowed the team to pin these leaflets against the stent frame, which helped avert additional narrowing of the effective orifice area due to folding of the leaflets inward. Moreover, this implantation method reduced the likelihood of generating a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia, thereby aiding in the maintenance of coronary perfusion and facilitating future access to the coronaries. The adoption of this technique was informed by careful preprocedural CT evaluation and was fundamental for achieving satisfactory haemodynamic performance within the context of three overlapping stented valves. While the triple transcatheter valve construct presented technical challenges, this method demonstrated both technical viability and reproducibility when executed with comprehensive preprocedural imaging and an established implantation plan.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet placement, comparatively short stent frame, and beneficial haemodynamics in confined anatomical settings. Additionally, its structure facilitates commissural alignment, an important factor in this scenario to maintain the possibility of subsequent coronary intervention.

A significant technical issue associated with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures is the potential for compromised coronary access as a result of several superimposed stent frames. Even though efforts were made to achieve commissural alignment, the aggregate stent mesh may continue to hinder subsequent coronary interventions. This underscores the importance of careful procedural planning, particularly in individuals with existing coronary artery disease or when future coronary access is likely to be necessary. Preprocedural CT allowed for assessment of the landing zone, examination of interactions between frames, and estimation of valve-to-coronary distances, though comprehensive three-dimensional expansion assessment using specialized software was not undertaken.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implantation is likely due to improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the pre-existing Evolut frame, which enhanced leaflet mobility and facilitated central blood flow. Although a deliberate frame fracture was not carried out, the application of high-pressure balloon post-dilation probably resulted in additional radial expansion of the Evolut, thereby optimizing valve performance.

Institutional approval was obtained to proceed with off-label application of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis during the procedure. The patient and his family participated in shared decision-making, and the case underwent evaluation in a multidisciplinary heart team meeting comprised of interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the present prosthetic valve were to experience early failure, available therapeutic strategies would be severely restricted. Surgical removal would still present significant technical challenges and carry considerable risk. Newer transcatheter interventions, such as bioprosthetic valve fracturing or leaflet laceration—specifically, techniques like bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—could be contemplated; however, their practicality and safety in the context of triple-stented valves remain unclear.
