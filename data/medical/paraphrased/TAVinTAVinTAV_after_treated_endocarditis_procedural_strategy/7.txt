Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V. Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the varied technical complexities inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, therapeutic approaches should be individualized for each patient. We describe the case of a man in his late 70s with a multifaceted medical background, which included two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced significant structural deterioration of his aortic bioprosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter intervention. His initial TAVI in 2014 involved a Lotus valve, followed in 2022 by a TAV-in-TAV implantation utilizing an Evolut device; this was complicated by infective endocarditis, leading to substantial prosthetic dysfunction and high-grade restenosis. In 2024, the patient underwent placement of a third valve (Acurate neo2) employing a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV strategy. As TAVI indications broaden and patient survival extends, increasingly complex procedures involving multiple prosthetic valves may become more prevalent, underlining the importance of meticulous procedural planning, advanced imaging, and diligent long-term monitoring. At the 1-year evaluation, the patient remained asymptomatic with well-functioning prosthetic valves. This report demonstrates the practical implementation of a third TAVI following endocarditis-associated prosthesis degeneration, guided by CT-based preprocedural planning and 1-year post-procedure follow-up, an occurrence that is infrequently described in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a recognized therapeutic approach for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, irrespective of their surgical risk category. With rising TAVI case numbers and enhanced long-term patient survival, clinicians are more frequently managing structural deterioration of the implanted valve and facing the necessity for subsequent procedures.

In this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures have become an effective approach for addressing the dysfunction of prior transcatheter aortic valve implants. Nevertheless, these procedures present distinct anatomical and technical obstacles, notably an increased likelihood of obstructing the coronary arteries and a potential decrease in the effective orifice area, leading to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These complexities are exacerbated in cases involving repeated interventions, such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where careful management of the interplay among multiple overlapping valve stent frames is required.

There is a scarcity of data on repeated transcatheter valve interventions, especially among individuals with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these challenging cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, tailored risk evaluation, and collaborative multidisciplinary discussions are critical for assessing the viability of the procedure and enhancing clinical results.

This report demonstrates how CT-based planning and a strategic approach to transcatheter intervention were utilized in a patient who had undergone multiple previous TAVI operations and was managed for PVE, underscoring important aspects for ongoing care in structural valve disorders.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center due to complaints of shortness of breath, a tightening feeling on the left side of the chest triggered by physical activity (walking 200–300 meters), along with dizziness and tiredness.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: the right coronary artery in 2009, the left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, a repeat intervention on the LAD in 2012, and the circumflex artery in 2014. Other significant comorbidities were third-degree atrioventricular block for which a pacemaker was implanted in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

In 2014, the patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) via transcatheter approach because of worsening aortic valve degeneration accompanied by severe stenosis. By December 2021, the patient experienced increased exertional dyspnoea, and echocardiography demonstrated significant bioprosthetic valve stenosis. In early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, and the ejection fraction had fallen to 42%, compared to 54% the previous December.

In May 2022, the patient received a transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure utilizing a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). The intervention was complicated by the occurrence of a pulsatile hematoma and pseudoaneurysm at the femoral site, necessitating open surgical vascular repair.

After undergoing Evolut TAV-in-TAV, the patient's left ventricular ejection fraction increased from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive initial haemodynamic improvement. He received standard heart failure management per guidelines, which included beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was re-admitted with ongoing fever and an exacerbation of heart failure signs. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) identified severe restenosis of the prosthetic aortic valve, with a mean transvalvular gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete course of pathogen-directed antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis-associated PVE, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral antibiotics as per infectious disease specialist guidance. At the time of the third TAVI intervention, the antimicrobial regimen had been concluded, with no maintenance suppressive antibiotics in use. Clinical evaluation, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice computed tomography (MSCT) demonstrated clearance of infection, with no indications of ongoing endocarditis.

Upon finishing the course of antimicrobial treatment and experiencing some improvement in symptoms, a follow-up assessment in January 2024 demonstrated deteriorating function of the prosthetic valve, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient measured 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction had normalized to 66%.

A plan for repeat intervention was established. Upon admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed ongoing moderate-to-moderately severe intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation (grade 2–3), an elevated mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly diminished aortic valve area (AVA) measuring 0.5 cm², findings indicative of severe prosthetic valve dysfunction. The AVA was determined utilizing the continuity equation, employing measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract along with Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. Imaging also identified two superimposed bioprosthetic valves.

Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) identified a narrowest lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, indicating marked haemodynamic impairment and aiding in the procedural planning for a third transcatheter valve placement (see figure 1). MSCT prior to the procedure was utilized to determine the anatomical suitability and to assess the potential for coronary artery obstruction. This assessment comprised measurements of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva size, valve-to-coronary distances, and the interaction among the frames of the existing transcatheter valves. Careful evaluation focused on the predicted positioning of the neo-skirt in relation to the coronary ostia and on the appropriateness of high valve implantation to ensure continued coronary perfusion and possible future coronary interventions. The information derived from these analyses informed the selection of valve type, the depth of implantation, and the approach to the procedure, which allowed the team to proceed without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed prior to the reintervention and confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

In light of the patient’s intricate anatomy with three prior stents, a history of infective endocarditis, and an elevated risk associated with surgery, the team opted for a third TAVI guided by CT imaging. The approach prioritized strategies for safeguarding the coronary arteries, achieving commissural alignment, and maximizing the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the left femoral approach. The aortic valve was initially dilated using a 22/40 mm balloon catheter while rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute was applied. Concurrent aortography verified that coronary perfusion was not compromised (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (size S) was placed at the aortic position. Alignment of the new device with the existing Evolut valve leaflets was achieved using preprocedural CT modeling, aiming to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was utilized for post-dilation during rapid pacing to ensure optimal deployment of the valve. Subsequent aortography demonstrated proper device placement, maintained coronary blood flow, and no evidence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (see figure 4).

During valve implantation, attention was given to commissural alignment in order to maintain coronary accessibility and minimize the intricacies of potential subsequent interventions, utilizing information from preprocedural CT scans and fluoroscopic reference points.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural success and the function of the prosthetic valve were evaluated following the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 standards, which included assessment of haemodynamic performance, detection of any residual regurgitation, and identification of complications related to the procedure.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) obtained immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 verified the third transcatheter valve had been implanted successfully. The leaflets demonstrated full mobility and opening, and there was no detectable aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Despite this, the haemodynamic measurements revealed elevated transvalvular gradients, with a mean value of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was measured at 0.9 cm², corresponding to an indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m², and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24—findings that align with severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

At 1 month post-implantation, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated significant improvement, with a mean gradient of 27 mm Hg and an increase in aortic valve area to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodeling was present, as chamber volumes had decreased. No regurgitation persisted, and valve performance stayed stable, falling within the range consistent with moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months follow-up, the patient continued to be free of symptoms and demonstrated satisfactory exercise capacity. There was no evidence of heart failure or repeat infection. The prosthetic device continued to function effectively, and haemodynamic measurements remained improved.

One year after the implantation procedure (June 2025), the patient continued to be clinically stable and active, exhibiting no shortness of breath or indications of heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mean transvalvular gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction of 65%. There were no valve-associated complications, no regurgitation, and no signs of recurring endocarditis.

These findings confirm the durability of the result and suggest a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case highlights the decision-making process and technical challenges of performing a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV procedures and a history of PVE.

Although TAVI is now a well-accepted approach for treating aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines addressing valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advise surgical valve replacement as the preferred option for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially when cases are complicated by heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines highlight the importance of tailoring management decisions based on factors such as infection control, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

The initial decision to proceed with TAVI in 2014 was influenced by the patient’s elevated surgical risk profile and the presence of several comorbid conditions, which rendered traditional surgical valve replacement an unsuitable option. When structural valve degeneration occurred subsequently, a transcatheter redo procedure was selected in 2022 to reestablish valve function and circumvent the hazards associated with open-heart surgery. After PVE was effectively managed and there was no evidence of persistent infection, a third transcatheter intervention was undertaken in 2024 due to the patient’s prohibitive surgical risk, complex anatomy involving prior valve frames, and confirmation on CT imaging that this approach would maintain coronary perfusion and deliver satisfactory haemodynamic results.

The prosthetic valve malfunction in this patient was probably due to multiple contributing factors. Although the prior episode of infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage and resulting mechanical limitations, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves was also a key contributor. Once infection was deemed cleared and there was no indication for chronic suppressive antibiotic treatment, the approach to treating prosthetic valve dysfunction followed standard protocols for patients without current endocarditis. Thus, choices regarding intervention were based chiefly on anatomical suitability, the degree of haemodynamic impairment, and operative risk rather than solely on the antecedent infection.

In this scenario, the patient had already completed antimicrobial treatment, demonstrated no ongoing infection clinically or radiologically, and underwent comprehensive pre-procedural assessment, including TEE and MSCT, both of which verified the lack of vegetations or paravalvular complications. The individual's EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, with notable comorbid conditions such as COPD, diabetes, a history of gastrointestinal bleeding, and had two previously placed transcatheter valves with substantial stent incorporation into the aortic wall, collectively making open-heart surgery exceedingly risky and technically complex. Due to the deep anchoring of the earlier prosthetic valves, a surgical approach would probably have required replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple reports have documented TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 noting a range of anatomical and technical difficulties. This case further contributes to the scarce literature, particularly given the background of degeneration following endocarditis and maintained prosthetic function one year post-procedure.

This case highlights the increasing significance of lifelong management approaches for structural valve disease. Each subsequent transcatheter intervention introduces additional challenges, including concerns about frame stacking, alignment of commissures, coronary reaccess, and maintenance of residual effective orifice area. Careful anticipation and timely discussion of procedural plans are essential, starting with the initial intervention—especially for younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

In this case, the patient’s structural deterioration included leaflet fibrosis resulting from previous degeneration and likely mechanical limitation subsequent to infection. The team chose to implant a third valve—the self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—after preprocedural CT imaging and 3D modeling confirmed its suitability and adequate coronary clearance (see figures 1, 5, and 7). A key aspect of the procedure was the intentional high placement of the third transcatheter valve. Positioning the prosthesis at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets secured the existing leaflets against the stent frame, thereby averting additional narrowing of the effective orifice area due to inward leaflet collapse. Furthermore, this technique reduced the likelihood of forming a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, which helped maintain coronary perfusion and preserved the option for future coronary interventions. The entire approach was closely informed by preprocedural CT imaging and was vital in ensuring an acceptable hemodynamic result within a triple-stented setting. Even with the increased procedural complexity presented by placing a third transcatheter valve, this method was technically possible and could be replicated when comprehensive preprocedural imaging and a defined implantation plan were employed.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet placement, modest frame height, and advantageous haemodynamic properties in confined anatomies. Additionally, its structure facilitates commissural alignment, a critical factor in this case to maintain options for subsequent coronary access.

An important technical issue in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions is the potential for restricted coronary access as a result of several superimposed stent frames. Even when commissural alignment is pursued, the aggregate mesh density can complicate subsequent coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of careful procedural strategy, particularly in patients with established coronary pathology or anticipated need for future access. Preprocedural computed tomography facilitated assessment of the target implantation area, appraisal of stent frame relationships, and measurement of the distance from valve to coronary ostia; however, comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis with specialized software was not conducted.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implantation is likely due to improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilatation, and some mechanical widening of the pre-existing Evolut frame, which together enhanced leaflet mobility and central hemodynamics. Although the frame was not intentionally fractured, post-dilation with a high-pressure balloon probably promoted additional radial expansion of the Evolut frame, resulting in better valve performance.

Institutional authorization was obtained for the off-label application of the Acurate neo2 valve during the intervention. A collaborative decision-making process was conducted with the patient and his family, and the case underwent discussion by a multidisciplinary heart team comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the existing prosthesis were to experience early failure, available therapeutic alternatives would be quite restricted. Surgical removal would continue to pose substantial technical challenges and carry significant risk. Newer transcatheter techniques, including bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration procedures (for example, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)), could be contemplated, but the practicality and safety of these approaches in the context of triple-stented anatomies is still unclear.
