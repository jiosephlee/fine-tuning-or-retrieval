Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the various technical complexities of transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve approaches, the therapeutic plan for each patient should be individualized. We describe a male patient in his late seventies with a challenging clinical course, marked by two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently developed significant prosthetic aortic valve deterioration necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The patient first received a Lotus valve in 2014, underwent a TAV-in-TAV using an Evolut device in 2022, and then experienced degeneration and marked restenosis associated with endocarditis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 valve was implanted via a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV method. As TAVI indications expand and patient survival increases, these intricate multi-valve procedures are likely to occur more often, underscoring the importance of enhanced procedural planning, advanced imaging, and extended monitoring. At one-year post-procedure, the patient shows no symptoms and maintains stable valve performance. This case demonstrates the practicality of performing a third TAVI following prosthetic valve endocarditis, utilizing CT-based planning and one-year follow-up, a scenario that remains seldom documented in the existing literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely accepted for patients with severe aortic stenosis across various levels of surgical risk. With increasing numbers of TAVI procedures being performed and enhanced long-term survival rates, structural valve failure and the subsequent requirement for repeat procedures are being seen more frequently in practice.

In this context, performing transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) interventions has gained recognition as an effective option to address failed transcatheter heart valves. Nonetheless, these procedures are linked to distinctive anatomical and technical issues, such as potential obstruction of the coronary arteries and a decrease in effective orifice area, which can result in pronounced prosthesis-patient mismatch. These issues are further complicated in cases of repeated interventions, such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where several overlapping prosthetic frames need to be precisely evaluated.

There is scant data regarding repeated transcatheter valve interventions, especially among individuals with a prior history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In particularly intricate cases like these, thorough preprocedural imaging, personalised risk evaluation, and collaborative, multidisciplinary decision-making are critical to assess procedure feasibility and achieve optimal results.

This case underscores the importance of CT-based procedural planning and targeted transcatheter approaches in a patient with multiple previous TAVI interventions and managed PVE, offering important insights into the long-term care of structural valve abnormalities.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was admitted to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after experiencing shortness of breath, a spastic discomfort localized to the left side of his chest with physical activity (walking 200–300 meters), dizziness, and fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: one to the right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, another LAD procedure in 2012, and treatment of the circumflex artery in 2014. He also had third-degree atrioventricular block, for which a pacemaker was implanted in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and had experienced recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes since 2022.

In 2014, the patient underwent transcatheter aortic valve replacement with placement of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) as a result of progressive degeneration and severe stenosis of the aortic valve. By December 2021, his symptoms of exertional dyspnea increased, and echocardiography revealed severe stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. In early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient rose to 83 mm Hg, and his left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) decreased to 42% from 54% as recorded in December.

In May 2022, he received a TAV-in-TAV procedure with deployment of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). The intervention was complicated by a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm, which necessitated open vascular surgical repair.

Post-procedurally, following the Evolut TAV-in-TAV, the patient’s left ventricular EF improved from 42% to 54%, suggesting a positive initial hemodynamic outcome. He was treated according to heart failure management guidelines, which included beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as clinically appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted once more due to ongoing fever and exacerbation of heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) identified severe restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Targeted antimicrobial treatment for E. faecalis PVE was administered, comprising a 6-week course of intravenous ampicillin-based therapy, subsequently transitioning to oral therapy as advised by infectious disease specialists. By the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedure, antimicrobial therapy had concluded, and no ongoing suppressive antibiotic regimen was in place. Clinical evaluation, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) all demonstrated resolution of infection, with no evidence for active endocarditis.

Upon completion of antimicrobial treatment and partial alleviation of symptoms, follow-up in January 2024 demonstrated deteriorating function of the prosthetic valve, with severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient had risen to 49 mm Hg, while left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) improved to 66%.

A decision was reached to pursue another intervention. At admission in June 2024, TTE revealed sustained grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and significantly decreased aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², confirming severe dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA was determined using the continuity equation, utilizing measurements of the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. Two overlapping bioprosthetic valves were noted.

MSCT identified a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, substantiating severe haemodynamic impairment and facilitating procedural planning for the third transcatheter valve placement (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT was utilized to evaluate anatomical suitability and potential risk of coronary artery obstruction. This included assessment of coronary ostial height, dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, valve-to-coronary distance, and the spatial interaction of the frames from prior transcatheter valves. Special emphasis was placed on the predicted neo-skirt's relation to the coronary ostia and whether high implantation could preserve coronary flow and ensure future coronary access. These assessments informed decisions regarding valve choice, implantation depth, and procedural approach, allowing the procedure to be carried out without coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography before reintervention confirmed absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular pathology.

Treatment:

Due to the patient’s intricate triple-stented vascular anatomy, history of infective endocarditis, and a surgical risk considered prohibitive, the third TAVI was undertaken as a procedure guided by CT imaging, with strategic priorities placed on protecting coronary circulation, achieving commissural alignment, and optimizing the residual effective orifice area.

The intervention was conducted under local anaesthesia, utilizing arterial access through the left radial and left femoral arteries. Severe aortic regurgitation was corroborated with aortography employing a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

Through the left femoral approach, a Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle. Pre-dilation of the aortic valve was accomplished using a 22/40 mm balloon catheter while the patient underwent rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography ensured that coronary blood flow was not compromised (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve, size S, was subsequently positioned in the aortic location. Deployment was coordinated with alignment to the Evolut valve leaflets, as determined by preprocedural CT modeling, to maximize the valve's effective orifice area and minimize patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Post-dilation involved a 20/40 mm balloon under continued rapid ventricular pacing to enhance expansion of the prosthetic valve. The concluding aortogram verified proper valve placement, maintained coronary circulation, and absence of both paravalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Valve deployment accounted for commissural alignment, preserving future coronary access and simplifying subsequent procedures, guided by both preprocedural CT and fluoroscopic landmarks.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural outcomes and prosthetic valve function were evaluated based on Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 standards, including assessment of haemodynamics, residual regurgitation, and any procedure-associated complications.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) conducted immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 verified proper placement of the third transcatheter valve. The prosthetic leaflets demonstrated normal motion and full excursion, with no evidence of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Despite this, haemodynamic measurements showed substantial transvalvular gradients: the mean gradient measured 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated at 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24, indicating severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

One month following the procedure, TTE revealed significant improvement: mean gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg, and AVA expanded to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodelling was present, including decreased chamber size. No residual regurgitation was observed, and valve function remained stable, consistent with moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months, the patient continued to be asymptomatic with satisfactory exercise capacity. There were no manifestations of heart failure or recurrent infectious processes. Prosthetic valve performance remained robust, with ongoing improvement in haemodynamic metrics.

At the 1-year follow-up in June 2025, the patient maintained clinical stability and physical activity, without shortness of breath or signs of cardiac decompensation. TTE demonstrated a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. No prosthetic complications, regurgitation, or evidence of recurrent endocarditis were apparent.

Collectively, these observations highlight the durability and persistent functional advantage provided by the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure in this high-risk individual.

Discussion:

This report underscores the complexities and procedural challenges associated with undertaking a third TAVI in an individual with prior transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve (TAV-in-TAV) replacements and a background of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although transcatheter aortic valve implantation is a well-established approach for aortic stenosis, its application in patients with previous endocarditis remains debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis continue to advocate for surgical valve replacement as the preferred treatment in PVE, particularly when complicated by heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines recommend tailoring decisions to the individual, considering infection status, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

In this patient, the initial TAVI performed in 2014 was selected due to elevated surgical risk and the presence of several comorbidities, which made traditional surgery less suitable. Following progression to structural valve deterioration, a repeat transcatheter approach was adopted in 2022 to reestablish valve function while minimizing the dangers associated with open surgery. After successful management of PVE and in the absence of ongoing infection, the selection of a third percutaneous intervention in 2024 was justified by exceptionally high surgical risk, challenging anatomical features from existing valve stents, and computed tomography findings supporting the technical possibility of a strategy that would maintain coronary perfusion and deliver satisfactory hemodynamic results.

The failure of the prosthetic valve in this patient appears to have arisen from several contributing factors. Although the episode of infective endocarditis may have caused leaflet damage and subsequent mechanical impairment, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves was also significant. Notably, after confirming resolution of infection and determining that continued antibiotic suppression was unnecessary, the approach to managing prosthetic valve dysfunction mirrored that used in patients without current or persistent endocarditis. As a result, procedural choices were predominantly influenced by anatomical suitability, haemodynamic compromise, and operative risk, rather than the patient’s prior infectious history.

This patient completed antimicrobial therapy and exhibited neither clinical symptoms nor imaging evidence of persistent infection; a comprehensive preprocedural assessment, including transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT), demonstrated absence of vegetations or paravalvular abnormalities. Additionally, the patient had a European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation (EuroSCORE II) of 27.36%, with significant comorbid conditions (COPD, diabetes mellitus, prior gastrointestinal hemorrhage) and two previously implanted transcatheter valves extensively integrated into the aortic wall, which rendered conventional surgery especially risky and technically complex. Owing to the deep anchoring of the preceding prostheses, surgical management would have likely required complete replacement of the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple reports have documented TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 describing distinct anatomical and technical hurdles encountered during these procedures. This case contributes to the small pool of published evidence, distinguished by degeneration subsequent to endocarditis and sustained valve function at one-year follow-up.

The case emphasizes the increasing significance of long-term care strategies for structural valve disease. Each transcatheter reintervention introduces additional complexities, including frame stacking, commissural orientation, accessibility of the coronary arteries, and preservation of residual effective orifice area. Initiating thorough planning and discussing procedural options early, even at the initial intervention, is crucial—especially for patients who are younger or considered high-risk.

In this patient, structural deterioration consisted of leaflet fibrosis from earlier degeneration, compounded by likely mechanical constraint related to prior infection. The team elected to deploy a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—after preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modelling confirmed its suitability and showed sufficient clearance for the coronaries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The valve was intentionally implanted high within the anatomy; placement at the height of the Evolut valve leaflets secured the existing leaflets against the frame, preventing further compromise of the effective orifice area due to inward folding of the leaflets. This method also minimized the creation of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, thereby supporting uninterrupted coronary flow and future coronary access. Preprocedural CT evaluation informed this approach, which was integral to achieving satisfactory haemodynamics in a three-stent configuration. Although the triple transcatheter valve anatomy poses considerable technical challenges, this method was successfully executed and could be replicated when based on detailed preprocedural imaging and an established implantation plan.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen because its supra-annular leaflet placement, relatively short frame, and suitable haemodynamic properties make it advantageous for patients with narrow anatomical structures. Furthermore, its design facilitates commissural alignment, which was especially important in this instance to maintain potential coronary access in the future.

A major technical issue encountered in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV cases is the possibility of compromised coronary access from multiple overlapping stent structures. Despite attempts at achieving commissural alignment, the collective mesh density could still hinder subsequent coronary interventions. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural planning for patients with existing coronary artery disease or those for whom future access might be critical. Preprocedural CT provided measurements for the landing site, assessed potential interactions between frames, and helped estimate the distance between the valve and coronaries, though comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis using specialized software was not undertaken.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implant is likely the result of improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the earlier Evolut frame, all contributing to enhanced leaflet movement and central flow. Although deliberate frame fracture was not carried out, high-pressure balloon post-dilation is presumed to have further expanded the Evolut frame radially, thereby optimizing valve performance.

Institutional approval was granted for the off-label use of the Acurate neo2 valve during this procedure. Shared decision-making included discussions with the patient and his family, and the strategy was reviewed during a multidisciplinary heart team meeting involving interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anaesthesiologists.

If the current prosthesis were to fail early, available therapeutic options would be severely restricted. Surgical removal would be both technically complex and associated with high risk. Novel catheter-based interventions, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration (for example, BASILICA), might be considered; however, their effectiveness and safety in cases involving triple-stent layers remain unproven.
