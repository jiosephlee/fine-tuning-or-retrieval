TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the range of technical complexities inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, therapeutic approaches should be individualized for each patient. We describe the clinical management of a male patient in his late 70s with a multifaceted medical history, marked by two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and a prior episode of infective endocarditis, who subsequently developed pronounced degeneration of his aortic bioprosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The patient first received a Lotus valve in 2014 and underwent a TAV-in-TAV procedure with an Evolut prosthesis in 2022. This was followed by prosthetic valve degeneration attributed to endocarditis, resulting in significant restenosis. In 2024, a third aortic valve (Acurate neo2) was successfully implanted using the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV method. As the criteria for TAVI continue to broaden and patients experience increased survival, complex interventions involving multiple prosthetic valves are likely to arise more commonly. This trend underlines the importance of advancing procedural preparation, imaging modalities, and extended post-procedural monitoring. The patient demonstrated no symptoms and maintained normal valve function at 1-year post-intervention. This report underscores the practicability of performing a third TAVI following successfully managed prosthetic valve endocarditis, utilizing CT-based planning and providing follow-up data out to 1 year—an approach rarely documented in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely recognized as a standard therapeutic approach for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, irrespective of their surgical risk category. With the expanding application of TAVI and improved longevity among recipients, clinicians are encountering a greater incidence of structural valve failure and repeated intervention requirements.

Within this context, the transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) technique has gained prominence as an effective solution for addressing degenerated transcatheter prosthetic valves. Nonetheless, this approach presents unique anatomic and procedural complexities, such as heightened risks for coronary obstruction and reductions in effective orifice area, potentially leading to significant prosthesis–patient mismatch. These issues become even more pronounced with subsequent interventions—like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV)—that necessitate managing several concentric valve frames.

Current data on the outcomes and technical nuances of repeated transcatheter valve procedures are scarce, especially among populations previously affected by prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Consequently, in such high-risk and intricate cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, thorough individualised risk evaluation, and collaborative multidisciplinary discussions are crucial to assess procedural suitability and to maximize the probability of procedural success.

The present case underscores the importance of CT-guided preprocedural planning and a strategic approach to transcatheter intervention in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI implants and managed PVE, drawing attention to essential considerations for the longitudinal management of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was admitted to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after reporting shortness of breath, spasmodic sensations on the left side of his chest during exertion (notably when walking 200–300 m), as well as episodes of dizziness and general fatigue.

The patient's prior medical history included arterial hypertension, triple-vessel coronary artery disease, and a series of percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery (2009), left anterior descending (LAD) artery (2010), a repeat LAD intervention (2012), and circumflex artery (2014). Further comorbidities encompassed third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating pacemaker placement in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal hemorrhages since 2022.

In response to progressive degeneration and severe stenosis of the aortic valve, a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) was implanted via transcatheter approach in 2014. By December 2021, the patient exhibited aggravated exertional dyspnoea, and echocardiography identified severe stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. By early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, accompanied by a reduction in ejection fraction (EF) from 54% (recorded in December) to 42%.

In May 2022, a TAV-in-TAV procedure was conducted with the deployment of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). This intervention was complicated by the formation of a pulsatile hematoma and pseudoaneurysm at the femoral access site, which necessitated open surgical repair.

Post-procedure, the patient experienced an improvement in left ventricular EF from 42% to 54%, signifying a positive early hemodynamic outcome. His management was aligned with heart failure guidelines, incorporating beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretic therapy as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted to hospital due to ongoing fever and an escalation of heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) identified significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, evidenced by a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures isolated Enterococcus faecalis, leading to the diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a comprehensive regimen of pathogen-directed antimicrobial therapy targeting E. faecalis, comprising a 6-week intravenous course with ampicillin-based antibiotics, subsequently transitioning to oral therapy in line with infectious disease specialist advice. By the time of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), antimicrobial therapy was concluded, with no ongoing requirement for chronic suppressive antibiotics. Comprehensive clinical reassessment, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) demonstrated infection clearance, with no indications of active endocarditis.

Following the conclusion of antibiotic treatment and partial improvement in symptoms, a follow-up evaluation in January 2024 disclosed deterioration of the bioprosthetic valve’s function, including severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe aortic regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient had increased to 49 mm Hg, while the left ventricular ejection fraction had normalized at 66%.

Subsequently, reintervention was deemed necessary. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE showed sustained grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a substantially reduced aortic valve area (AVA) measured at 0.5 cm², confirming severe prosthetic dysfunction. The AVA was derived by the continuity equation, utilizing measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler-based velocity-time integrals. Imaging displayed two superimposed bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT identified the minimal lumen diameter within the Evolut prosthesis as 13.7 mm, corroborating the presence of pronounced haemodynamic impairment and informing procedural planning for a third transcatheter valve procedure (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT served to appraise anatomic suitability and evaluate the potential for coronary artery obstruction, including analysis of coronary ostial heights, dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, valve-to-coronary proximity, and the relationship of frames from prior transcatheter valves. Special consideration was given to the spatial relationship between the predicted neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, as well as the feasibility of higher valve deployment to sustain coronary perfusion and preserve future coronary access. This thorough assessment informed the selection of the replacement valve, determination of implantation depth, and the overall procedural approach, which ultimately obviated the need for coronary protection.

Pre-interventional transoesophageal echocardiography excluded both active endocarditis and paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Given the patient’s intricate anatomy featuring triple stents, a history of infective endocarditis, and an excessively high risk associated with surgical intervention, a third TAVI was planned as a computed tomography-guided, strategy-tailored procedure. Particular attention was dedicated to coronary protection, achieving commissural alignment, and maximising the residual effective orifice area.

The intervention was conducted under local anaesthesia via arterial access through both the left radial and left femoral arteries. Severe aortic regurgitation was corroborated by aortography using a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was navigated into the left ventricle via the left femoral approach. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilation utilising a 22/40 mm catheter with rapid ventricular pacing set at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography verified that coronary flow was maintained (figure 3).

A self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (Acurate neo2, size S) was then delivered to the aortic position. Deployment was performed with alignment to the Evolut valve leaflets, based on preprocedural CT simulation, with the dual goal of optimising the effective orifice area and minimising the likelihood of patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

To further optimise valve expansion, post-dilation was carried out using a 20/40 mm balloon during rapid pacing. Final aortography confirmed accurate valve placement, sustained coronary flow, and absence of both paravalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

During valve deployment, commissural alignment was specifically addressed to preserve coronary accessibility and facilitate future interventions, using guidance from preprocedural CT and fluoroscopic landmarks.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural outcomes and prosthetic valve performance were evaluated in accordance with Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, incorporating assessment of haemodynamic function, detection of any residual regurgitation, and identification of complications related to the procedure.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) conducted immediately after the intervention on 7 June 2024 verified that the third transcatheter valve had been successfully positioned. The prosthetic leaflets demonstrated normal mobility and full excursion, with no signs of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Nevertheless, haemodynamic measurements revealed pronounced transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was measured at 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), while the Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24, findings in line with severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

At one month post-procedure, repeat TTE demonstrated significant haemodynamic improvement: the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg and the AVA had risen to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular reverse remodelling was apparent, as reflected by reduced chamber volumes. There was no recurrence of regurgitation, and valve function was stable within the range consistent with moderate stenosis.

Three and a half months following valve implantation, the patient exhibited no symptoms and maintained good exercise capacity. No indications of heart failure or repeat infection were present. The prosthesis continued to function well, with persistently improved haemodynamic indicators.

At one-year follow-up (June 2025), the patient remained clinically well and physically active, without dyspnoea or clinical signs of cardiac decompensation. TTE indicated a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. No device-related complications, regurgitation, or evidence of recurrent endocarditis were observed.

Taken together, these data support the lasting efficacy and functional advantage of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach in this high-risk clinical context.

Discussion:

This case exemplifies the complexities involved in clinical decision-making and technical execution when undertaking a third TAVI procedure in a patient previously treated with TAV-in-TAV interventions and a background of PVE.

Although TAVI is now a well-established modality for managing aortic stenosis, its application in patients with a history of endocarditis remains a topic of debate. The 2025 European society of cardiology (ESC) guidelines for valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis advocate surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach for PVE, especially in the presence of heart failure or persistent infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines underscore the necessity of tailoring the therapeutic approach to each individual, weighing infection control, the risks associated with surgery, and anatomical suitability.

In 2014, TAVI was chosen as the primary intervention due to the patient’s elevated surgical risk and significant comorbidities, circumstances that rendered standard surgical valve replacement inadvisable. When the patient later developed degeneration of the valve prosthesis, a repeat transcatheter procedure was carried out in 2022 to re-establish valve function while circumventing the hazards of open-heart surgery. After resolution of PVE and in the context of no evidence of ongoing infection, the multidisciplinary team opted for a third transcatheter intervention in 2024. This choice was informed by the patient’s continued high surgical risk, intricate anatomy resulting from previously implanted valve structures, and pre-procedural CT imaging that verified both the viability of the proposed intervention and the potential to maintain coronary patency alongside satisfactory haemodynamic results.

In this patient, the causes underlying prosthetic valve failure appeared to be complex and multifactorial. Although the prior episode of infective endocarditis could have resulted in leaflet damage and subsequent mechanical dysfunction, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves substantially contributed to the dysfunction. Notably, after the infection was deemed eradicated and there was no indication for extended suppressive antibiotic use, the approach to managing prosthetic valve failure paralleled that for patients without active or residual endocarditis. As such, procedural choices were dictated primarily by anatomical suitability, the severity of haemodynamic impairment, and surgical risk profile, rather than the patient’s prior infectious history.

Specifically, this patient had completed the indicated course of antimicrobial treatment, demonstrated no clinical or imaging evidence of persistent infection, and underwent comprehensive preprocedural assessment—incorporating both TEE and MSCT—which verified the absence of vegetations or paravalvular pathology. In addition, the patient's EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, and multiple significant comorbid conditions were present (COPD, diabetes, history of gastrointestinal bleeding), alongside two previously implanted transcatheter valves with pronounced stent ingrowth into the aortic wall. Collectively, these factors rendered open surgical intervention exceptionally high risk and technically demanding. The firm fixation of the previous valves suggested that surgical management would likely entail replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple case reports have documented TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 each illustrating distinct anatomical and procedural obstacles. The present case contributes further to this limited literature, notably due to its occurrence in the setting of post-endocarditis degeneration with maintained valve function observed at the 1-year mark.

This example highlights the increasing relevance of comprehensive lifetime management plans in patients with structural valve disorders. As additional transcatheter procedures are performed, factors such as layered stent frames, alignment of commissures, accessibility of the coronary arteries, and preservation of effective orifice area introduce greater complexity. It is therefore imperative to initiate long-term planning and engage in early procedural strategy discussions, starting from the initial intervention—particularly in younger cohorts or individuals at heightened risk.

The degenerative changes identified in this patient consisted of leaflet fibrosis attributable to previous structural deterioration and likely exacerbated by mechanical limitations following infection. The clinical team elected to deploy a third prosthesis—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve—following preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modelling which confirmed procedural viability and sufficient clearance for the coronary arteries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). A high deployment position was purposefully chosen for the third valve. Positioning the device at the same level as the Evolut valve leaflets enabled the existing degenerated leaflets to be secured against the stent frame, thereby mitigating further decreases in effective orifice area that might arise from leaflet infolding. Moreover, this implantation approach minimized the creation of a new skirt below the coronary ostia, facilitating the preservation of coronary perfusion and future coronary interventions. The decision-making process relied heavily on preprocedural CT data and was crucial for achieving a satisfactory haemodynamic profile within the context of triple valve stenting. Although a triple transcatheter configuration is inherently complex, this method proved to be technically achievable and consistent, provided it was informed by comprehensive preprocedural imaging and a clear procedural plan.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen for its supra-annular leaflet configuration, its comparatively short frame, and its advantageous haemodynamic properties in constrained anatomical settings. The device’s design also facilitates commissural alignment, which was essential in this scenario to ensure the preservation of future coronary access.

One of the principal technical challenges in performing TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions is the potential for compromised coronary access arising from the superimposition of multiple stent frameworks. Despite deliberate efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the additive mesh density may still hinder subsequent coronary procedures. This underlines the importance of thorough procedural planning in individuals with established coronary pathology or those for whom preserved access may be crucial. Preprocedural CT assessment provided measurements of the landing zone, insights into interactions between frames, and estimates of valve-to-coronary distances; however, a comprehensive three-dimensional expansion assessment using specialized software was not conducted.

Resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third device implantation likely resulted from a combination of successful commissural orientation, effective post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the existing Evolut frame, leading to improved leaflet kinetics and central hemodynamics. Although no intentional frame fracture was performed, aggressive balloon post-dilation probably led to additional radial expansion of the Evolut, thereby enhancing the functional capacity of the prosthetic valve.

Institutional approval for off-label application of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis was obtained for the intervention. The patient and his family actively participated in shared decision-making, and the case underwent evaluation by a multidisciplinary heart team comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging specialists, and anaesthesiologists.

In the event of premature prosthesis failure, available therapeutic options would be exceedingly constrained. Surgical explantation would remain a technically complex and high-risk undertaking. Innovative catheter-based strategies, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or intentional leaflet laceration (for example, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)), could be contemplated, though their practicality and safety in the context of triple-stented valves are still indeterminate.
