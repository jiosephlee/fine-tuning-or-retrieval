Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the variety of technical difficulties encountered in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, the therapeutic approach must be individualized for each patient. We report the case of a male patient in his late 70s with an intricate medical background, which includes two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures and a history of infective endocarditis. He subsequently developed advanced deterioration of the aortic prosthesis, necessitating a third transcatheter intervention. His initial treatment in 2014 involved a Lotus valve, followed by a TAV-in-TAV procedure with an Evolut prosthesis in 2022, after which infective endocarditis caused degeneration and significant restenosis of the valve. In 2024, a third prosthetic valve (Acurate neo2) was inserted utilizing a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV method. As TAVI indications broaden and patients live longer after intervention, these intricate multi-prosthesis procedures are expected to become increasingly common, emphasizing the importance of better procedural planning, advanced imaging techniques, and extended follow-up protocols. At one year after the third procedure, the patient shows no symptoms, and valve performance is preserved. This case demonstrates the practicality of performing a third TAVI following managed prosthetic valve endocarditis, facilitated by CT-guided planning and one-year outcome assessment—an approach seldom documented in existing literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become a well-established therapeutic approach for individuals with severe aortic stenosis spanning various surgical risk categories. With an increasing patient population undergoing TAVI and enhanced long-term survival, structural valve deterioration and the necessity for repeat procedures are being observed more frequently in clinical practice.

Within this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) techniques have developed as a practical solution for addressing malfunctioning transcatheter valves. Nonetheless, these interventions present distinct anatomical and procedural complexities, such as the potential for coronary artery obstruction and a diminished effective orifice area that can result in significant prosthesis–patient mismatch. These complications become even more pronounced during sequential repeat procedures—like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV)—where the presence of multiple overlapping valve frames must be taken into account.

The literature addressing repeated transcatheter valve interventions is sparse, especially for patients with previous prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these challenging cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, personalised risk evaluation, and collaborative multidisciplinary discussion are imperative to establish procedural suitability and enhance outcomes.

This case report demonstrates the importance of CT-based planning and strategic transcatheter intervention in a patient who had undergone multiple TAVI procedures and was treated for PVE, underscoring critical aspects for lifelong management of structural valve disorders.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was admitted to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center following the onset of dyspnea, a spasmodic sensation on the left side of his chest during exertion (walking 200–300 m), accompanied by dizziness and fatigue.

His history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, repeated LAD intervention in 2012, and circumflex artery in 2014. The patient had additional conditions such as third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating pacemaker placement (2014), type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and ongoing episodes of gastrointestinal bleeding beginning in 2022.

In 2014, increasing aortic valve degeneration with severe stenosis led to transcatheter placement of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, worsening exertional dyspnea was noted, with echocardiography showing severe bioprosthetic valve stenosis. At the start of 2022, the mean gradient reached 83 mm Hg and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had dropped to 42% (down from 54% in December).

In May 2022, the patient received a TAV-in-TAV procedure, implanting a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). The intervention was complicated by a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm, necessitating open vascular surgery.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient’s left ventricular EF improved to 54% from 42%, indicating an initial positive haemodynamic effect. Management included guideline-directed therapy for heart failure with beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as required.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was re-admitted due to ongoing fever and exacerbation of heart failure manifestations. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) identified pronounced restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures isolated Enterococcus faecalis, leading to the diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete regimen of directed antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis-associated PVE, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, succeeded by oral antibiotics as advised by infectious disease specialists. Prior to the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), antimicrobial therapy had concluded and the patient was not receiving ongoing suppressive antibiotics. Repeated clinical assessment, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice computed tomography (MSCT) confirmed resolution of the infection with no signs of active endocarditis.

Upon completion of antibiotic treatment and partial improvement in symptoms, follow-up in January 2024 demonstrated deteriorating prosthetic valve performance, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient increased to 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction returned to 66%.

A reintervention was planned. When the patient was admitted in June 2024, TTE indicated continued grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², denoting severe dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA was derived using the continuity equation, relying on measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler velocity-time integrals. Imaging showed two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT documented a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, affirming critical haemodynamic impairment and informing procedural strategies for a third transcatheter valve placement (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT facilitated evaluation of anatomical suitability and coronary artery obstruction risk. This assessment encompassed coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distance, and interaction between the previously placed transcatheter valve frames. Focus was placed on the spatial relationship between the predicted neo-skirt and coronary ostia, as well as the practicality of high valve positioning to maintain coronary flow and enable future coronary interventions. These analyses determined valve type, implantation depth, and intervention approach, permitting execution of the procedure without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography performed before the procedure confirmed there was no evidence of active endocarditis or paravalvular pathology.

Treatment:

Considering the patient's challenging anatomy with three prior stents, history of infective endocarditis, and unsuitability for surgery, the third TAVI was approached as a CT-guided, strategy-focused intervention, prioritizing coronary protection, commissural alignment, and enhancement of the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was carried out under local anesthesia utilizing both left radial and left femoral arterial access. Severe aortic regurgitation was confirmed through aortography with a pigtail catheter (see figure 2).

Via the left femoral approach, a Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle. Balloon pre-dilation of the aortic valve utilized a 22/40 mm catheter, conducted under rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography demonstrated continued patency of the coronary arteries (refer to figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthesis (size S) was implanted at the aortic valve position. Deployment of the device was coordinated with the leaflet position of the pre-existing Evolut valve, informed by preprocedural CT modeling, to maximize the effective orifice area and minimize patient-prosthesis mismatch risk.

A 20/40 mm balloon was used for post-dilation under rapid pacing to ensure optimal expansion of the valve. Subsequent aortography showed proper device placement, maintained coronary circulation, and the absence of both paravalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (see figure 4).

Commissural alignment was addressed during the valve implantation in order to preserve coronary access and facilitate potential future interventions, guided by preprocedural CT scans and fluoroscopic imaging.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural efficacy and the functioning of the prosthetic valve were evaluated in alignment with the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 standards, encompassing assessment of haemodynamics, detection of residual regurgitation, and identification of complications related to the procedure.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) performed directly after the procedure on 7 June 2024 verified appropriate placement of the third transcatheter valve. The prosthetic leaflets exhibited mobility with full opening, and aortic regurgitation was absent (grade 0). Nevertheless, haemodynamic measurements demonstrated pronounced transvalvular gradients, with a mean of 67 mm Hg. The calculated aortic valve area (AVA) measured 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24—indicative of severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

One month following implantation, TTE revealed substantial improvement: the mean gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg, and AVA rose to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodelling was seen, including reduced chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was present, and the valve’s performance remained stable within the range of moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months post-procedure, the patient reported no symptoms and exhibited good exercise capacity. There were no clinical signs of heart failure or recurrent infection. Prosthetic valve function remained intact, with continued enhancement of haemodynamic indices.

At one year after implantation (June 2025), the patient continued to be clinically well and active, experiencing no dyspnoea or signs of cardiac decompensation. TTE showed a mean transvalvular gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. There were no complications related to the valve, no regurgitation, and no evidence of recurrent endocarditis.

Collectively, these results indicate sustained durability and ongoing functional benefit from the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure in this patient at elevated risk.

Discussion:

This report illustrates the considerations and procedural difficulties encountered when performing a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient with a history of prior transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures and previous prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI is an established therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in patients with prior endocarditis remains debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis recommend surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach in PVE, especially in situations complicated by heart failure or uncontrolled infection. Nonetheless, the guidelines underline that therapeutic decisions should be tailored individually, taking into consideration factors such as control of infection, surgical risk, and anatomical suitability.

In 2014, TAVI was selected as the initial treatment due to the patient’s elevated surgical risk and extensive comorbidities, making standard surgical valve replacement an unsuitable option. When structural valve dysfunction occurred subsequently, a transcatheter redo intervention was performed in 2022 to restore valve functionality while circumventing the dangers associated with open surgery. After PVE was successfully managed and in the absence of ongoing infection, the rationale for undertaking a third transcatheter procedure in 2024 involved excessive surgical risk, intricate anatomical features from prior valve frames, and computed tomography confirmation of a strategy that would preserve coronary flow and deliver satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

The underlying causes of prosthetic valve failure in this patient appeared to be multiple. Although the prior episode of infective endocarditis may have resulted in leaflet damage and ensuing mechanical restriction, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter prosthetic valves was also a substantial factor. Notably, once infection had been declared resolved and long-term suppressive antibiotics were unnecessary, the approach to managing prosthetic valve dysfunction paralleled that for patients without current or persistent endocarditis. Consequently, procedural planning prioritized anatomical suitability, haemodynamic significance, and operative risk, rather than being driven solely by the prior infectious history.

For this case, the patient had completed antimicrobial treatment, displayed no evidence of active infection clinically or on imaging, and underwent comprehensive preprocedural assessment—including TEE and MSCT—which verified the absence of vegetations or paravalvular complications. Additionally, the patient's EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, with notable comorbidities such as COPD, diabetes mellitus, and prior gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Two previously placed transcatheter prostheses demonstrated substantial stent incorporation into the aortic wall, rendering open surgical repair exceptionally high-risk and technically complex. Due to the deep anchoring of earlier valves, surgical management would probably have required replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple published case reports have described TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 illustrating diverse anatomical and technical difficulties encountered during such procedures. This report contributes further to the scarce literature, with the distinct scenario of degeneration following endocarditis and maintained valve functionality at the one-year mark.

The present case highlights the increasing relevance of comprehensive lifetime management in structural valve disorders. With each subsequent transcatheter intervention, issues such as layering of stent frames, alignment of commissures, access to coronary arteries, and maintenance of an effective orifice area become progressively more intricate. Careful long-term planning and early conversations regarding procedural pathways are essential, even during the initial intervention—especially for younger or high-risk individuals.

The structural failure in this patient was characterized by leaflet fibrosis resulting from previous degeneration, along with suspected mechanical restriction post-infection. The team decided to implant a third prosthesis—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve—guided by preprocedural CT scans and three-dimensional modelling that confirmed procedural viability and sufficient clearance for the coronaries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). Purposefully positioning the third valve in a high location was a strategic choice. By placing it at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets, the intervention secured the prior leaflets against the stent frame, thus mitigating the risk of further decrease in the effective orifice area from inward folding. This method also reduced the creation of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, helping maintain coronary circulation and potential future access. This procedure relied on preprocedural CT evaluation and was key to obtaining a satisfactory haemodynamic outcome in the context of three overlapping stented valves. Although managing a triple-layer transcatheter valve anatomy presents considerable complexity, the technique was achievable and could be repeated, provided it was supported by detailed preprocedural imaging and a planned implantation strategy.

The Acurate neo2 device was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet arrangement, comparatively short frame height, and favorable hemodynamic outcomes in constricted anatomies. Its structural features facilitate commissural alignment, which was particularly important in this case to maintain access to the coronary arteries for possible future interventions.

One major technical issue in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures is the potential loss of coronary access, as multiple overlapping stent frames can obstruct intervention pathways. Despite efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the accumulated stent mesh may continue to hinder future coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of thorough procedural planning for individuals with pre-existing coronary disease or those who may require subsequent access. Preprocedural CT allowed for measurement of the landing zone, assessment of stent frame interplay, and calculation of distances between the valve and coronary arteries, though dedicated software for comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis was not utilized.

Resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve placement was likely due to a combination of optimal commissural alignment, efficient post-dilatation, and partial mechanical expansion of the previously implanted Evolut stent frame, which enhanced leaflet mobility and improved central blood flow. Although intentional fracture of the stent frame was not performed, the use of high-pressure balloon post-dilatation probably resulted in greater radial expansion of the Evolut frame, thereby improving valve performance.

The procedure was conducted under the institution's approval for off-label use of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. Decisions regarding treatment were made collaboratively with the patient and his family, and the case underwent comprehensive review by a multidisciplinary heart team consisting of interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

In the event of premature prosthesis failure, therapeutic alternatives would be extremely restricted. Surgical explantation would pose significant technical difficulties and risks. Catheter-based approaches such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration—for instance, BASILICA (bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction)—could be contemplated, though the practicality and safety of these techniques in scenarios involving three overlapping stent frames remain uncertain.
