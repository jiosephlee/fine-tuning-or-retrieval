Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the varied technical complexities inherent to transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, management must be individualized for each patient. Here, we describe a man in his late seventies whose extensive medical history included two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures and infective endocarditis, subsequently leading to severe deterioration of his aortic prosthesis and the necessity for a third transcatheter intervention. The patient first received a Lotus valve in 2014, underwent TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut prosthesis in 2022, and later developed degeneration due to endocarditis resulting in significant restenosis. In 2024, a third prosthetic valve (Acurate neo2) was successfully implanted using a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As TAVI indications broaden and patients experience longer survival, challenging cases involving multiple valve prostheses are likely to increase, emphasizing the importance of advanced procedural planning, enhanced imaging techniques, and robust long-term monitoring. At one-year post-procedure, the patient remains asymptomatic with sustained valve performance. This report demonstrates the viability of a third TAVI following treated prosthetic valve endocarditis, utilizing CT-guided procedural planning and documented one-year follow-up, a scenario rarely described in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a well-established therapeutic option for individuals with severe aortic stenosis spanning various levels of surgical risk. With the growing population of patients undergoing TAVI and extended survival rates, clinicians are increasingly confronted with structural valve deterioration and the necessity for repeat procedures.

In this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) approaches have become an effective means of addressing failure of transcatheter prosthetic valves. Nevertheless, these interventions carry distinct anatomical and technical complexities, such as an elevated risk for coronary artery occlusion and a decreased effective orifice area, which may result in significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These difficulties are compounded when performing sequential reinterventions like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where multiple, layered valve frames present additional considerations.

Current data on multiple transcatheter valve reinterventions are sparse, especially regarding patients with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these particularly intricate situations, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, tailored risk evaluation, and multidisciplinary deliberation are vital for assessing procedural feasibility and achieving optimal outcomes.

This case demonstrates how CT-based planning and a strategy-focused transcatheter approach can be employed for a patient who has undergone several TAVI procedures and received treatment for PVE, underlining crucial factors for the long-term management of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late 70s was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after reporting shortness of breath, a spasmodic feeling in the left chest during exertion (noted after walking 200–300 m), as well as dizziness and generalized fatigue.

His past medical history was significant for arterial hypertension, coronary artery disease involving three vessels, and multiple prior percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, repeat LAD intervention in 2012, and circumflex artery in 2014. Other comorbidities included third-degree atrioventricular block requiring pacemaker placement in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal hemorrhage starting in 2022.

In response to advancing aortic valve deterioration and pronounced stenosis, the patient received a transcatheter Lotus valve (27 mm, Boston Scientific) in 2014. By December 2021, his exertional dyspnoea had worsened, and echocardiography revealed significant bioprosthetic valve stenosis. By early 2022, his mean valve gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, while his left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had dropped to 42% (a decline from 54% noted the previous December).

In May 2022, he underwent a transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure with placement of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). The intervention was complicated by the occurrence of a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm, necessitating open vascular repair.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV, the patient’s left ventricular EF improved from 42% back to 54%, reflecting an encouraging early haemodynamic improvement. He was prescribed medical management consistent with guidelines for heart failure, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted once again due to ongoing fever and an exacerbation of heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated pronounced restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measuring 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, establishing the diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). Targeted antimicrobial therapy was administered for PVE related to E. faecalis, consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based regimen, followed by oral treatment per infectious disease specialist guidance. By the time of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), antimicrobial therapy had concluded and the patient was not receiving any ongoing suppressive antibiotic regimen. Serial clinical evaluations, repeat blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) all indicated the infection had resolved, without any findings of active endocarditis.

Subsequent to the completion of antibiotic treatment and partial alleviation of symptoms, follow-up in January 2024 revealed deterioration in the functioning of the prosthetic valve. Severe aortic stenosis and moderate to severe regurgitation were observed, with a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a restored left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) of 66%.

Reintervention was recommended. Upon presentation in June 2024, TTE showed ongoing grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a sustained mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a notably reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², reflecting severe dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA was computed using the continuity equation, based on measurements of the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. Imaging revealed two bioprosthetic valves overlapped.

MSCT demonstrated a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, supporting significant haemodynamic impairment and informing planning for a third transcatheter valve implantation (see figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT assisted in evaluating anatomical suitability and the risk of coronary artery obstruction. This assessment involved measurements of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva dimensions, valve-to-coronary distances, and the interaction between the existing transcatheter valve frames. Attention was devoted to the anticipated relationship between the new valve skirt and the coronary ostia, as well as to the feasibility of performing a high implantation to safeguard coronary flow and facilitate future coronary access. These findings influenced valve choice, implantation depth, and procedural strategy, allowing the intervention to proceed without coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography performed before the reintervention confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Due to the patient's intricate triple-stented anatomy, history of infective endocarditis, and the elevated surgical risk, the third TAVI procedure was approached as a CT-guided, strategy-focused intervention with particular consideration for coronary protection, commissural alignment, and enhancement of the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention was carried out under local anesthesia, utilizing left radial and left femoral arterial access. Severe aortic regurgitation was verified by aortography with a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

Using the left femoral access, a Safari guidewire was introduced into the left ventricle. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilation with a 22/40 mm catheter, facilitated by rapid ventricular pacing at 180 bpm. Concurrent aortography showed that the coronary arteries maintained adequate flow (figure 3).

A self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (Acurate neo2, size S) was placed at the aortic site. Preprocedural CT modelling was employed to align the device with the leaflets of the Evolut valve, aiming to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Following implantation, post-dilation with a 20/40 mm balloon during rapid pacing was performed to achieve optimal expansion of the valve. The concluding aortography confirmed accurate valve placement, continued coronary perfusion, and no evidence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Commissural alignment was considered throughout valve delivery to maintain coronary access and reduce the complexity of any subsequent interventions, utilizing preprocedural CT and fluoroscopic markers as guidance.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural success and the function of the prosthetic valve were evaluated using the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 (VARC-3) standards, which include assessment of haemodynamic performance, detection of residual regurgitation, and identification of complications related to the procedure.

On 7 June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) conducted immediately following the intervention verified correct placement of the third transcatheter valve. The prosthetic valve leaflets exhibited normal movement with full opening, and there was no aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Despite this, haemodynamic measurements showed elevated transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated at 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24, all findings indicative of severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

One month after the procedure, TTE revealed substantial improvement: the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg, and AVA had increased to 1.1 cm². There was evidence of left ventricular remodelling, seen as a reduction in chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was detected, and the prosthetic valve continued to function within the range of moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months post-procedure, the patient was asymptomatic with preserved exercise capacity. There were no clinical indications of heart failure or recurrence of infection. The prosthetic valve demonstrated ongoing functional stability, with continued improvement in haemodynamic measures.

One year following implantation (June 2025), the patient maintained clinical stability and remained physically active, with no complaints of dyspnoea or evidence of decompensation. TTE results included a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. There were no complications related to the valve, no regurgitation, and no recurrence of endocarditis.

Altogether, these findings demonstrate both the lasting efficacy and durable benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure in this high-risk individual.

Discussion:

This case underscores the complexities involved in both the clinical decision-making and procedural aspects of performing a third TAVI for a patient previously treated with TAV-in-TAV and with a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI has become a standard approach for managing aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a prior history of endocarditis remains debated. The 2025 guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) for the management of valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis recommend surgical valve replacement as the preferred treatment for PVE, especially in situations complicated by heart failure or persistent infection. Nevertheless, these guidelines stress the necessity of tailoring management to each patient, considering factors such as infection control, surgical risk, and anatomical suitability.

TAVI was initially selected in 2014 for this patient due to their elevated surgical risk and multiple comorbidities, rendering standard surgical valve replacement unsuitable at that time. Upon development of structural valve deterioration, a transcatheter redo procedure was implemented in 2022 to restore valve function and mitigate surgical complications. After successful resolution of PVE and in the absence of ongoing infection, the third transcatheter intervention performed in 2024 was prompted by prohibitive surgical risk, intricate anatomical constraints associated with previous valve frameworks, and CT-based confirmation of a strategy designed to maintain coronary flow and ensure satisfactory haemodynamics.

The prosthetic valve failure observed in this patient appeared to result from multiple contributing factors. Although a previous episode of infective endocarditis may have caused leaflet damage leading to mechanical dysfunction, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves was also a major component. Once infection was deemed resolved—with no indication for prolonged antibiotic suppression—the approach to managing valve dysfunction was consistent with protocols for patients who do not have active endocarditis. Accordingly, procedural choices were primarily dictated by anatomical suitability, severity of haemodynamic compromise, and surgical risk rather than the history of endocarditis.

For this patient, antimicrobial treatment had been completed, and both clinical evaluation and imaging failed to demonstrate any evidence of persistent infection. Comprehensive preprocedural assessment, including transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT), confirmed the absence of vegetations and paravalvular pathology. The patient’s EuroSCORE II was calculated at 27.36%, and he was burdened by considerable comorbidities (COPD, diabetes mellitus, previous gastrointestinal hemorrhage), in addition to two previously placed transcatheter valves with substantial integration of the stent frames into the aortic wall. These factors rendered conventional surgery exceptionally risky and technically complex. Given the extensive anchoring of the earlier prostheses, a surgical approach would likely require replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple reports have documented TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 illustrating the anatomical and procedural complexities associated with such cases. The present report expands this limited literature, particularly due to its context of post-endocarditis degeneration, as well as maintenance of valve function at one-year follow-up.

This case highlights the increasing relevance of comprehensive, lifetime structural valve management. Successive transcatheter reinterventions raise multifaceted issues, including layered frames, commissural orientation, coronary access, and preservation of the residual effective orifice area. Early discussion of procedural sequencing and long-term planning are essential from the time of initial treatment, especially for younger individuals or those considered high-risk.

The structural valve deterioration in this patient was characterized by leaflet fibrosis from prior degeneration and probable mechanical restriction resulting from infection. The team opted to implant a third prosthesis—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve—following evaluation with preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modelling, which confirmed both technical feasibility and satisfactory coronary clearance (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The decision for high placement of the third device was intentional; situating the prosthesis at the same level as the Evolut valve leaflets facilitated stabilization of the degenerated leaflets against the stent frame, avoiding additional loss of effective orifice area caused by inward folding. Furthermore, this approach minimized creation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia, thereby maintaining coronary perfusion and access for possible future interventions. The procedure was guided by detailed preprocedural CT imaging, which proved critical for obtaining favorable haemodynamic outcomes in the context of a triple-stented valve structure. Although triple transcatheter valve placements are inherently complex, this technique was practical and reproducible when supported by comprehensive imaging assessment and an established implantation protocol.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen because its leaflet placement above the annulus, comparatively short frame, and favorable hemodynamic profile are well-suited for narrow anatomical spaces. Additionally, its structure allows for commissural alignment, which was essential in this patient to maintain future coronary access.

A significant technical challenge in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures is the potential compromise of coronary access due to multiple stent frames overlapping. Even though commissural alignment was pursued, the accumulation of mesh from successive valves may complicate subsequent coronary interventions. This underscores the necessity for careful procedural strategy in individuals with existing coronary disease or those where maintaining coronary access is critical. Preprocedural computed tomography facilitated assessment of the landing zone, evaluated the interaction between frames, and estimated valve-to-coronary distances; however, a comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis using specialized software was not carried out.

Resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve placement likely resulted from improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilatation, and partial expansion of the earlier Evolut frame, which enhanced leaflet mobility and optimized central flow. Although frame fracture was not deliberately induced, the high-pressure balloon post-dilation likely led to additional radial enlargement of the Evolut frame, further improving valve function.

Institutional approval was obtained for off-label use of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis in this procedure. Decision-making was collaborative, involving the patient and his family, and the case was deliberated in a multidisciplinary heart team conference, comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the current prosthesis were to fail earlier than anticipated, management options would be markedly restricted. Surgical explantation would remain technically complex and associated with considerable risk. Novel transcatheter interventions, such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration (for example, BASILICA, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction), might be contemplated, though their practicality and safety in triple-stented configurations are yet to be established.
