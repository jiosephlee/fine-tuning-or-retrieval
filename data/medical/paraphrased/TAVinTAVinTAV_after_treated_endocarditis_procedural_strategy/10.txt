Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V. Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the variety of technical complexities inherent to transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve interventions, management decisions should be individualized for each patient. We describe a case involving a man in his late 70s with a complicated past medical history, notable for two prior transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and an episode of infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced marked deterioration of his aortic prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The patient initially received a Lotus valve in 2014, followed by a valve-in-valve TAVI using an Evolut prosthesis in 2022, after which he developed endocarditis-associated prosthetic degeneration and significant restenosis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 device was deployed as the third prosthetic valve employing a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As TAVI indications broaden and patient longevity post-procedure increases, these intricate, multi-prosthesis cases are likely to become more common, underscoring the necessity for enhanced procedural planning, advanced imaging modalities, and ongoing long-term monitoring. One year after the latest intervention, the patient remains symptom-free with sustained valve performance. This report demonstrates the practicality of performing a third TAVI following resolution of prosthetic valve endocarditis, utilizing CT-based procedural planning and providing 1-year post-procedural follow-up, a scenario infrequently documented in current medical literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a recognized therapeutic approach for individuals with severe aortic stenosis spanning various surgical risk categories. With a rising number of TAVI procedures being performed and enhanced long-term survival rates, clinicians are observing an increased incidence of structural valve degeneration and a corresponding rise in repeat interventions.

Within this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures have become an established option for treating failing transcatheter valves. Nonetheless, these approaches present particular anatomical and procedural difficulties, notably the potential for coronary artery occlusion and a diminished effective orifice area that can lead to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These complexities are intensified in successive redo procedures, such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where careful attention is required due to the presence of multiple overlapping prosthetic frames.

There is a paucity of data on repeated transcatheter valve interventions, especially in individuals with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these challenging cases, comprehensive preprocedural imaging, patient-specific risk evaluation, and a collaborative multidisciplinary approach are critical to assess procedural viability and enhance outcomes.

This report demonstrates the importance of CT-based procedural planning and strategic transcatheter intervention in an individual with a history of multiple TAVI implants and prior treatment for PVE, underscoring essential factors in the long-term management of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center due to complaints of shortness of breath, a spastic feeling in the left chest area brought on by physical activity (specifically when walking 200–300 m), as well as dizziness and feelings of fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and a series of percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, repeated intervention on the LAD in 2012, and circumflex artery in 2014. Other notable comorbidities were third-degree atrioventricular block managed with pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurring gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

In 2014, the patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) via transcatheter approach due to worsening degeneration and significant stenosis of the aortic valve. By December 2021, there was a notable increase in exertional dyspnoea, and echocardiography indicated marked stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. By early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, while the ejection fraction (EF) had dropped further to 42%, compared to 54% in December.

In May 2022, the patient received a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic) through a TAV-in-TAV procedure. Postoperatively, a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm occurred, necessitating open vascular intervention.

After undergoing the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient’s left ventricular ejection fraction increased from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive initial haemodynamic outcome. The patient received heart failure management in accordance with clinical guidelines, which included beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was readmitted with ongoing fever and aggravated heart failure manifestations. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed marked restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient of 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to the diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete regimen of pathogen-directed antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis-associated PVE, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral antibiotics as advised by the infectious disease team. By the time of the third TAVI procedure, the antibiotic course had concluded, with no ongoing suppressive therapy. Clinical evaluation, repeat blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) all indicated infection resolution and absence of active endocarditis.

Following the conclusion of antibiotic treatment and some improvement in symptoms, reassessment in January 2024 demonstrated a decline in prosthetic valve performance, presenting with significant aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The recorded mean transvalvular gradient was 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction had improved to 66%.

A plan for repeat intervention was established. Upon admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated ongoing grade 2–3 regurgitation within the prosthetic aortic valve, a mean gradient across the valve of 49 mm Hg, and a notably diminished aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², findings indicative of severe prosthetic valve malfunction. The AVA calculation utilized the continuity equation, incorporating measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. Imaging revealed two superimposed bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT demonstrated that the Evolut prosthesis had a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm, indicating significant haemodynamic impairment and informing the planning of a third transcatheter valve implantation (figure 1). Prior to the procedure, MSCT was employed to evaluate anatomical suitability and to estimate the likelihood of coronary artery obstruction. The assessment considered factors such as the height of the coronary ostia, dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, the distance from valve to coronary arteries, and the interplay between the frameworks of the previously deployed transcatheter valves. Careful consideration was given to the prospective relationship between the expected neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, as well as whether a high implantation approach could sustain coronary perfusion and maintain future coronary access. These imaging results informed decisions regarding the type of valve, placement depth, and overall procedural approach, allowing the procedure to proceed without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed prior to the reintervention and confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

In light of the patient’s intricate anatomy involving three prior stents, a history of infective endocarditis, and a surgical risk deemed too high, a third TAVI was scheduled, utilizing CT guidance and a strategic approach focusing on safeguarding the coronary arteries, achieving commissural alignment, and maximizing the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the left femoral approach. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilatation using a 22/40 mm catheter while rapid ventricular pacing was conducted at a rate of 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortographic imaging demonstrated maintained coronary perfusion without obstruction (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (size S) was placed in the aortic position. Preprocedural CT modeling guided alignment of the device with the leaflets of the Evolut valve to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was utilized for post-dilation while rapid pacing was maintained in order to achieve optimal valve expansion. Subsequent aortography demonstrated appropriate valve placement, intact coronary blood flow, and no evidence of either perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (see figure 4).

Valve deployment was performed with attention to commissural alignment, using preprocedural CT imaging and fluoroscopic reference points to maintain coronary accessibility and minimize potential challenges for subsequent interventions.

Outcome and follow-up:

Evaluation of procedural success and prosthetic valve function was carried out in line with the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, encompassing assessment of haemodynamic performance, detection of any remaining regurgitation, and identification of complications related to the procedure.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) conducted immediately following the procedure on 7 June 2024 verified that the third transcatheter valve had been properly positioned. The valve leaflets exhibited good mobility with full opening, and there was no detectable aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Despite this, haemodynamic assessment revealed substantial transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was measured at 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), while the Doppler velocity index (DVI) was 0.24—findings compatible with severe prosthesis–patient mismatch (PPM).

At 1 month post-implantation, transthoracic echocardiography revealed significant progress: the mean gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg, and the aortic valve area expanded to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodeling was present, as indicated by decreased chamber volumes. No remaining regurgitation was detected, and the valve maintained consistent function classified as moderately stenotic.

At 3.5 months follow-up, the patient continued to exhibit no symptoms and maintained satisfactory exercise capacity. There were no indications of heart failure or infection recurrence. The prosthetic device functioned well, with persistently enhanced haemodynamic measurements.

One year after the implantation procedure (June 2025), the patient continued to do well clinically, exhibiting no dyspnea or indications of decompensated heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mean transvalvular gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area of 1.1 cm², and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%. There were no complications associated with the valve, no regurgitation, and no signs of recurrent endocarditis.

These findings confirm the durability of the result and suggest a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case highlights the decision-making process and technical challenges of performing a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV procedures and a history of PVE.

Although transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a well-accepted therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with a history of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines addressing valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis state that surgical valve replacement continues to be the preferred approach for prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially when there is heart failure or infection that cannot be controlled. Nonetheless, these guidelines stress the need for a tailored management strategy based on infection eradication, operative risk assessment, and anatomical suitability.

TAVI was initially selected in 2014 due to the patient’s elevated surgical risk and the presence of several comorbidities, which rendered traditional surgical valve replacement less suitable at that point. When the patient later experienced structural valve degeneration, a repeat transcatheter approach was undertaken in 2022 to restore valve function while mitigating the hazards associated with open-heart surgery. After effective management of PVE and with no evidence of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter procedure was performed in 2024, guided by persistent high surgical risk, challenging anatomy due to existing valve frames, and CT findings supporting the feasibility of an approach that would maintain coronary patency and provide satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

The failure of the prosthetic valve in this patient was probably due to multiple contributing factors. Although the past episode of infective endocarditis may have resulted in leaflet damage leading to mechanical impairment, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter prosthesis was also a key factor. Notably, after confirming resolution of the infection and determining that prolonged suppressive antibiotic treatment was unnecessary, the approach to treating prosthetic valve dysfunction became similar to management in individuals without current or persistent endocarditis. Consequently, anatomical suitability, haemodynamic impact, and surgical risk primarily influenced procedural choices, rather than the patient’s previous infection history.

In this instance, the patient had finished the prescribed antimicrobial regimen and demonstrated neither clinical nor radiological evidence of ongoing infection. Comprehensive preoperative assessment was performed, incorporating transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT), both of which verified the absence of vegetations or paravalvular pathology. The patient's EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, and their medical history was notable for multiple comorbidities, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes mellitus, and previous episodes of gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Additionally, the presence of two pre-existing transcatheter aortic prostheses, with marked stent incorporation into the aortic wall, significantly increased both the procedural risk and technical complexity of conventional surgical approaches. Due to the substantial anchoring of these prior valves, surgical management would most likely have required replacement of both the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple published case reports have described TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 each illustrating different anatomical and procedural complexities. The present case further contributes to this small pool of data, distinguished by degeneration following endocarditis and maintained valve performance one year post-procedure.

This report highlights the increasing relevance of long-term management approaches in patients with structural valve disease. Each subsequent transcatheter procedure adds further complexity to factors like multiple frame layers, proper commissural orientation, access to the coronary arteries, and maintaining sufficient effective orifice area. Early and comprehensive planning of future procedural pathways is essential, beginning with the initial intervention, especially for younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

In this patient, structural deterioration was characterized by leaflet fibrosis due to previous degeneration and likely mechanical limitation after infection. The multidisciplinary team opted to implant a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—guided by preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modeling, which confirmed the procedure's viability and sufficient coronary clearance (see figures 1, 5, and 7). A high implantation of the third transcatheter valve was intentionally chosen. Positioning the device at the height of the Evolut valve’s leaflets secured the existing leaflets against the stent frame, which helped avert additional decrease in effective orifice area from leaflet infolding. Additionally, this method limited the development of a neo-skirt beneath the coronary ostia, which supported the maintenance of coronary blood flow and facilitated potential future coronary access. The decision-making process was underpinned by preprocedural CT imaging and was critical for achieving suitable haemodynamic performance within the context of triple-stented anatomy. Although introducing a third transcatheter valve presents technical challenges, the approach was both feasible and reproducible when based on comprehensive preprocedural imaging and a clearly established implantation strategy.

The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet location, modest frame height, and beneficial haemodynamic properties in restricted anatomies. Its construction also enables commissural alignment, an important factor in this scenario to maintain potential access to the coronary arteries.

A major technical issue in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions is the potential for obstructed coronary access resulting from several superimposed stent structures. Even with efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the increased density of the stent mesh could complicate future coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of careful preprocedural strategy, especially for patients with established coronary artery disease or when maintaining coronary access is critical. Preprocedural CT facilitated assessment of the implantation site, appraisal of stent frame interactions, and estimation of the valve-to-coronary distances; however, a complete analysis of three-dimensional valve expansion with specialized software was not conducted.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third implantation is likely due to improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilatation, and some degree of mechanical expansion of the existing Evolut frame, all of which enhanced leaflet movement and central flow characteristics. Although the procedure did not involve deliberate frame fracture, post-dilation with a high-pressure balloon appears to have promoted additional radial expansion of the Evolut device, leading to better valve performance.

The intervention was conducted with institutional authorization for off-label application of the Acurate neo2 valve. Decision-making was collaborative, engaging both the patient and his family, and the case underwent assessment at a multidisciplinary heart team conference attended by interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the present prosthesis were to experience early failure, there would be very few therapeutic alternatives available. Surgical removal would continue to be a complex procedure associated with significant risk. New transcatheter interventions, including bioprosthetic valve fracture or techniques such as leaflet laceration (for example, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction [BASILICA]), could be evaluated as potential strategies; however, the practicality and safety of these approaches in cases involving three stents have yet to be established.
