Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the various technical complexities inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, management approaches must be individualized for each patient. We describe the case of a male in his late 70s with a complicated medical background—having undergone two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) operations and experienced infective endocarditis—who later presented with severe deterioration of his aortic prosthesis, necessitating a third transcatheter intervention. The patient was first treated with a Lotus valve in 2014, then received a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut device in 2022, after which degeneration and significant restenosis developed due to endocarditis. In 2024, he received a third prosthetic valve (Acurate neo2) employing a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV technique. As indications for TAVI broaden and treated patients' life expectancy increases, these complex interventions involving multiple prostheses may occur more frequently, underscoring the need for enhanced procedural planning, advanced imaging modalities, and diligent long-term monitoring. At the 1-year mark, the patient continues to be asymptomatic and maintains good valve function. This case demonstrates the practicality of performing a third TAVI post-treatment of prosthetic valve endocarditis, using CT-based procedural planning and 1-year follow-up, which is rarely documented in existing literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely accepted as a therapeutic approach for patients presenting with severe aortic stenosis, regardless of their surgical risk categories. With increasing numbers of TAVI procedures performed and enhanced longevity among recipients, clinicians are more frequently confronted with cases of structural valve deterioration and subsequent requirements for repeat intervention.

Transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) techniques have thus emerged as practical solutions for addressing failure of transcatheter bioprostheses. Nonetheless, these procedures pose distinct anatomical and technical obstacles, such as the potential for coronary artery blockage and diminished effective orifice area, which can lead to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. These complexities are compounded during repeat procedures—such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV)—where management of multiple superimposed valve frames presents additional challenges.

Data pertaining to successive transcatheter valve reinterventions remains sparse, especially among individuals with previous prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In such intricate cases, thorough preprocedural imaging, tailored risk evaluation, and collaborative multidisciplinary discussions are crucial in assessing procedural suitability and enhancing outcome quality.

This case demonstrates how computed tomography-guided planning and a deliberate, strategic approach to transcatheter intervention were applied in a patient who underwent multiple TAVI procedures and previously received treatment for PVE, underscoring important aspects for the longitudinal management of structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center due to shortness of breath, a spastic feeling localized to the left chest during exertion such as walking 200–300 meters, along with dizziness and tiredness.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, triple-vessel coronary artery disease, and several prior percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, repeat LAD intervention in 2012, and circumflex artery in 2014. Other significant comorbid conditions comprised third-degree atrioventricular block requiring pacemaker placement (2014), type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurring gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

In 2014, due to advanced aortic valve degeneration and marked stenosis, he underwent transcatheter placement of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, his exertional dyspnoea had intensified, and echocardiography showed severe bioprosthetic valve stenosis. At the start of 2022, the mean valve gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) decreased to 42% (down from 54% the previous December).

During May 2022, a transcatheter valve-in-valve procedure (TAV-in-TAV) was performed with a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). This was complicated by the occurrence of a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm, necessitating surgical vascular intervention.

After receiving the Evolut TAV-in-TAV, the patient’s left ventricular ejection fraction improved from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive early haemodynamic outcome. He was treated according to established heart failure guidelines, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretic therapy where clinically indicated.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was re-admitted due to ongoing fever and deterioration of heart failure manifestations. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed severe restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete course of directed antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis PVE, comprising six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral therapy as per guidance from infectious disease consultants. At the time of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), the antimicrobial regimen had concluded and no ongoing suppressive antibiotic treatment was maintained. Subsequent clinical evaluation, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) demonstrated resolution of infection without signs of active endocarditis.

Following antimicrobial treatment and partial improvement in symptoms, surveillance in January 2024 showed further deterioration in prosthetic valve performance, marked by severe aortic stenosis with moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient had increased to 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) returned to 66%.

Reintervention was planned. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE demonstrated ongoing grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly reduced aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², consistent with pronounced prosthetic valve dysfunction. The AVA determination utilized the continuity equation, employing measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and Doppler velocity-time integrals. Imaging revealed two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT identified a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, affirming severe haemodynamic compromise and informing procedural planning for a third transcatheter valve implantation (see figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT facilitated anatomical assessment and evaluation of coronary artery obstruction risk, including measurements of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva size, valve-to-coronary distance, and interaction between the prior transcatheter valve frames. Attention focused on the spatial relationship between the projected neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, as well as the suitability of high valve placement to maintain coronary perfusion and access. These parameters shaped decisions regarding valve type, implantation depth, and intervention strategy, enabling the procedure to proceed without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography before reintervention verified the absence of ongoing endocarditis or paravalvular defects.

Treatment:

Considering the patient’s intricate triple-stent anatomy, history of infective endocarditis, and elevated operative risk, a third TAVI was scheduled with guidance from computed tomography. The procedural approach emphasized coronary protection, achieving commissural alignment, and optimizing the remaining effective orifice area.

Local anesthesia was administered, with arterial access obtained via the left radial and left femoral sites. Severe aortic regurgitation was verified on aortography utilizing a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was introduced into the left ventricle through the left femoral approach. The aortic valve underwent pre-dilation using a 22/40 mm balloon catheter while rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute was applied. Concurrent aortography established that coronary flow remained unaffected (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (size S) was deployed in the aortic position. Leveraging preprocedural CT modeling, the valve was oriented in alignment with the Evolut valve leaflets to enhance the effective orifice area and mitigate patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Post-dilation was executed with a 20/40 mm balloon under continued rapid pacing for optimal device expansion. Subsequent aortography demonstrated accurate valve placement, ongoing coronary perfusion, and absence of perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Valve deployment incorporated commissural alignment, assisted by preprocedural CT scans and fluoroscopic references, to maintain coronary access and reduce the complexity of potential future interventions.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural success and the functioning of the prosthetic valve were evaluated according to the criteria established by the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3, including assessment of haemodynamics, presence of any residual regurgitation, and procedure-associated complications.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) performed immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 verified proper positioning of the third transcatheter valve. The leaflets exhibited normal mobility, demonstrated complete opening, and no signs of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Nonetheless, haemodynamic measurements revealed a marked transvalvular gradient, with a mean value of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated at 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA of 0.4 cm²/m²) and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured 0.24, findings indicative of severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

One month after the procedure, TTE indicated considerable improvement: the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg, and the AVA increased to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodelling was noted, with a decline in chamber volumes. Valve function remained stable within the moderately stenotic range, with no residual regurgitation.

At 3.5 months post-implantation, the patient exhibited no symptoms and tolerated physical activity well. There were no clinical signs of heart failure or recurrence of infection. The prosthetic valve continued to perform well, with ongoing enhancement of haemodynamic indices.

At the 1-year follow-up (June 2025), the patient was clinically stable and maintained normal activity levels, with no dyspnoea or indications of decompensation. TTE revealed a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. No prosthesis-related complications, regurgitation, or recurrence of endocarditis were detected.

Collectively, these results demonstrate the lasting efficacy of the intervention and indicate continued functional advantage from the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case demonstrates the complexity of clinical judgement and the procedural difficulties encountered when undertaking a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient previously managed with TAV-in-TAV approaches and a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI is now routinely used for aortic stenosis, its application in patients with prior endocarditis remains subject to ongoing debate. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis management continue to advocate for surgical valve replacement as the preferred option in cases of PVE, especially when associated with heart failure or persistent infection. Nevertheless, the guidelines underscore the necessity for individualized management, factoring in infection status, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

The original decision to proceed with TAVI in 2014 was influenced by the patient's elevated surgical risk profile and numerous comorbidities, rendering standard surgical intervention inadvisable. Later, the emergence of structural valve deterioration led to a transcatheter re-intervention in 2022 to reestablish valve functionality and circumvent surgical complications. After successful resolution of PVE and in the absence of ongoing infection, a third percutaneous procedure was chosen in 2024 due to excessive surgical risk, intricate anatomical challenges involving previous valve frameworks, and CT verification of a plan supporting maintained coronary flow and satisfactory haemodynamic outcomes.

The etiology of prosthetic valve failure in this patient appeared to involve several factors. The prior episode of infective endocarditis might have resulted in leaflet damage and subsequent mechanical limitation, while ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves substantially contributed as well. Crucially, after infection was deemed resolved and there was no indication for extended suppressive antibiotic treatment, the therapeutic approach to prosthetic valve dysfunction mirrored that taken with patients free from active endocarditis. As such, the procedural strategy was based mainly on anatomical suitability, haemodynamic impact, and operative risk, rather than being influenced solely by previous infective events.

For this patient, antimicrobial treatment had been completed, with no evidence of active infection either clinically or on imaging; an extensive preprocedural work-up—including TEE and MSCT—confirmed the absence of vegetations or paravalvular abnormalities. The patient’s EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, with multiple comorbidities such as COPD, diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding. Additionally, two transcatheter valves had been previously implanted, both with robust stent integration within the aortic wall. Collectively, these factors made conventional surgery extremely risky and technically challenging. The prior valve’s deep fixation meant that surgical management would probably have required comprehensive replacement of both the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple reports documenting TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures have been described,37 emphasizing a range of anatomical and procedural obstacles. Our report contributes to this scarce literature by presenting a case with the distinctive circumstance of degeneration following endocarditis and maintained valve performance at the 1-year mark.

This case illustrates the increasing relevance of comprehensive, lifelong management in structural valve pathology. With repeated transcatheter interventions, complexities such as the layering of stent frames, proper commissural positioning, continued access to the coronaries, and maintenance of an effective orifice area must be addressed. Thorough long-term planning and early conversations about procedural directions are essential, especially from the initial procedure—this is particularly true for younger or high-risk patients.

In this patient, structural deterioration consisted of leaflet fibrosis resulting from previous degeneration and likely mechanical restriction related to earlier infection. The clinical team elected to implant a third prosthetic valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2—guided by preprocedural CT imaging and 3D modeling which confirmed both procedural viability and sufficient coronary clearance (see figures 1, 5, and 7). Purposeful high placement of the third transcatheter valve served as a procedural tactic. Positioning the device at the level of the Evolut valve leaflets enabled fixation of the existing leaflets against the stent structure, thus avoiding additional reduction in effective orifice area that could occur from leaflet folding inward. Furthermore, this technique reduced the creation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia, supporting preservation of coronary flow and facilitating future coronary access. The selection of this approach was informed by CT imaging before the procedure and was crucial to achieve satisfactory haemodynamics within the triple-stented setting. Despite the inherent complexity associated with implanting three transcatheter valves, this method proved technically possible and reproducible when based on thorough imaging evaluation and a carefully established implantation plan.

The Acurate neo2 device was chosen due to its supra-annular leaflet arrangement, comparatively short frame, and favorable hemodynamic properties in narrow anatomies. Its features facilitate commissural alignment, which was particularly important in this case to maintain future coronary access.

A significant technical issue with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures is the possibility of compromised coronary access resulting from multiple overlapping stent frames. Even though efforts were made to achieve commissural alignment, the accumulation of mesh from successive frames could still create difficulties for subsequent coronary interventions. This underscores the importance of careful procedural strategy for patients with existing coronary disease or those in whom future coronary access may be crucial. Preprocedural CT was employed to measure the landing zone, assess interactions between frames, and estimate valve-to-coronary distances; however, comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis using specialized software was not undertaken.

The elimination of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implantation is likely a result of improved commissural alignment, efficient post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the earlier Evolut frame, which enhanced leaflet movement and central flow patterns. Although deliberate frame fracture was not performed, high-pressure balloon post-dilation appears to have facilitated further radial expansion of the Evolut device, optimizing valve performance.

This intervention was carried out with institutional authorization for off-label application of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. The decision-making process involved collaboration with the patient and his family and was discussed at a multidisciplinary heart team meeting comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging professionals, and anesthesiologists.

If the present prosthesis were to fail earlier than expected, therapeutic options would be severely restricted. Surgical explantation would pose considerable technical challenges and substantial risks. Novel catheter-based approaches—such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or intentional leaflet laceration techniques (e.g., bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction [BASILICA])—could potentially be considered, but their practicality and safety in triple-stented situations are yet to be established.
