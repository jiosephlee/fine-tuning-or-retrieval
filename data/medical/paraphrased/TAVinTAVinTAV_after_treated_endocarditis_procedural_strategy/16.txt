Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the various technical complexities inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, therapeutic approaches should be customized to each patient's unique situation. We report the case of a man in his late 70s with a complicated medical background that included two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) interventions and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently developed significant deterioration of his aortic prosthesis necessitating a third percutaneous procedure. The patient initially received a Lotus valve in 2014, later underwent a valve-in-valve TAVI with an Evolut device in 2022, and subsequently experienced prosthetic degeneration and severe restenosis related to endocarditis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 valve was deployed using a valve-in-valve-in-valve approach. As indications for TAVI continue to broaden and patient longevity after TAVI rises, such complicated cases involving multiple sequential prostheses may become increasingly common, underscoring the importance of enhanced procedural planning, advanced imaging, and extended post-procedural monitoring. One year after the third procedure, the patient continues to do well, demonstrating preserved prosthetic function and no symptoms. This report demonstrates the possibility of performing a third TAVI following resolved prosthetic valve endocarditis, with procedure planned using CT imaging and follow-up over 12 months, a scenario that is rarely described in the existing literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now widely recognized as a standard therapy for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, regardless of their surgical risk category. With more patients receiving TAVI and improvements in long-term outcomes, clinicians are more frequently observing structural valve deterioration and facing the necessity for subsequent procedures.

Under these circumstances, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures have become a practical option for addressing failed transcatheter valve implants. Nonetheless, these procedures present distinct anatomical and procedural complexities, notably the potential for coronary artery obstruction and diminished effective orifice area, which may cause significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. Such issues are further intensified during repeated interventions—such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV) procedures—where multiple, overlapping stent frames necessitate careful consideration.

There is a paucity of data on repeated transcatheter valve interventions, especially among individuals with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these challenging cases, comprehensive pre-procedural imaging, tailored evaluation of risks, and collaborative multidisciplinary planning are critical for assessing procedural suitability and enhancing outcomes.

This report demonstrates how CT-guided procedural planning and a strategic approach to transcatheter intervention were utilized in a patient with a history of multiple previous TAVI procedures and managed prosthetic valve endocarditis, emphasizing important factors in the lifelong treatment of structural heart valve conditions.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late 70s was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center following complaints of shortness of breath, a spastic feeling on the left side of his chest during physical activity (specifically when walking 200–300 meters), as well as episodes of dizziness and general fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, repeat LAD intervention in 2012, and circumflex artery in 2014. Other comorbidities comprised third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

In 2014, the patient received a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific) via transcatheter approach for worsening aortic valve disease and critical stenosis. By December 2021, she experienced increased exertional dyspnoea, with echocardiography revealing significant stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. In early 2022, her mean transvalvular gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, and her ejection fraction had dropped to 42%, compared to 54% noted in December.

In May 2022, the patient received a transcatheter aortic valve-in-valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedure, during which a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic) was implanted. The intervention was complicated by the onset of a pulsatile hematoma and pseudoaneurysm at the femoral site, necessitating open surgical vascular repair.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV procedure, the patient’s left ventricular ejection fraction increased from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive early haemodynamic effect. Management included heart failure therapy consistent with current guidelines, incorporating beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was re-admitted due to ongoing fever and exacerbation of heart failure manifestations. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, leading to the diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete course of pathogen-specific antibiotic therapy for E. faecalis PVE, consisting of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral antibiotics as advised by infectious disease consultants. By the time of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), antimicrobial therapy had been finalized, and no chronic suppressive antibiotics were being administered. Clinical evaluation, follow-up blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) all demonstrated infection resolution without indications of ongoing endocarditis.

Subsequent evaluation in January 2024, following the end of antibiotic treatment and some improvement of symptoms, demonstrated deteriorating function of the prosthetic valve, now exhibiting severe aortic stenosis along with moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient measured 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction had improved to 66%.

It was determined that a repeat intervention was necessary. Upon hospital admission in June 2024, transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed ongoing grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient measuring 49 mm Hg, and a significantly decreased aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², findings which are indicative of severe dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA was derived using the continuity equation, utilizing measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and velocity-time integrals obtained by Doppler. Imaging identified two superimposed bioprosthetic valves.

Multislice computed tomography (MSCT) demonstrated a minimal lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, indicating significant haemodynamic impairment and informing the strategy for a third transcatheter valve implantation (see figure 1). The preprocedural MSCT evaluation focused on determining anatomic suitability and evaluating the potential for coronary artery obstruction. This assessment included measurements of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva size, the distance from the valve to the coronaries, and analysis of how the frames of the previously placed transcatheter valves interacted. Special emphasis was placed on the spatial relationship between the projected neo-skirt and the coronary ostia, as well as on the potential for high valve implantation to maintain coronary perfusion and allow for future coronary access. These results influenced the choice of valve, implantation depth, and overall procedural approach, permitting the intervention to proceed without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed prior to the reintervention and confirmed the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Considering the patient's intricate anatomy with three prior stents, a history of infective endocarditis, and a surgical risk deemed excessively high, the third TAVI was scheduled utilizing CT imaging to inform a strategy focused on safeguarding coronary arteries, achieving proper commissural orientation, and maximizing the remaining effective orifice area.

The procedure was performed under local anaesthesia using left radial and left femoral arterial access. Aortography with a pigtail catheter confirmed severe aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through access obtained in the left femoral artery. The aortic valve was then pre-dilated using a 22/40 mm balloon catheter while rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute was applied. Concurrent aortographic imaging demonstrated that coronary blood flow was not impeded (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (size S) was placed in the aortic position. Preprocedural CT modelling was used to align the new device with the Evolut valve leaflets, with the aim of optimizing the effective orifice area and minimizing the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

A 20/40 mm balloon was utilized for post-dilatation under rapid pacing in order to achieve optimal valve deployment. Subsequent aortography demonstrated accurate valve placement, maintained coronary flow, and no evidence of perivalvular or transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Valve deployment was performed with attention to commissural alignment, utilizing preprocedural CT scans and fluoroscopic reference points to maintain coronary accessibility and minimize the complexity of subsequent interventions.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural success and function of the prosthetic valve were evaluated based on the criteria established by the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3, which encompassed assessment of haemodynamic parameters, detection of any remaining regurgitation, and identification of complications associated with the procedure.

A transthoracic echocardiogram conducted directly following the procedure on 7 June 2024 verified that the third transcatheter valve had been correctly positioned. The valve cusps displayed normal motion with full excursion, and there was no sign of aortic regurgitation (graded as 0). Nonetheless, hemodynamic assessment revealed elevated transvalvular pressure gradients, with a mean gradient measured at 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area was calculated at 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index was 0.24, findings that indicate severe prosthesis–patient mismatch.

At one month post-implantation, transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated significant improvement, with the mean transvalvular gradient measuring 27 mm Hg and aortic valve area expanding to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodeling was present, indicated by decreased chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was detected, and the valve continued to function stably within the parameters of moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months of follow-up, the patient continued to exhibit no symptoms and demonstrated adequate exercise capacity. There were no indications of heart failure or a return of infection. The prosthetic device continued to function well, with persistent enhancement of haemodynamic measurements.

One year following implantation in June 2025, the patient continued to demonstrate clinical stability and maintained an active lifestyle, experiencing no shortness of breath or indicators of cardiac decompensation. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a mean transvalvular gradient of 26 mm Hg, an aortic valve area measuring 1.1 cm², and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%. There were no complications associated with the valve, no regurgitation detected, and no signs of recurrent endocarditis.

These findings confirm the durability of the result and suggest a sustained functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case highlights the decision-making process and technical challenges of performing a third TAVI in a patient with prior TAV-in-TAV procedures and a history of PVE.

Although transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) is now a recognized therapy for aortic stenosis, its role in individuals with a history of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines addressing valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis continue to advocate for surgical valve replacement as the preferred approach in prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE), especially when the condition is complicated by heart failure or ongoing infection. Nevertheless, the guidelines highlight the importance of tailoring management to each patient, with consideration given to factors such as infection control, the risks associated with surgery, and the suitability of the patient’s anatomy.

In 2014, TAVI was selected as the initial management approach owing to the patient’s elevated surgical risk and presence of several comorbidities, which rendered conventional surgical valve replacement inadvisable at that stage. When the patient later experienced structural valve degeneration, a repeat transcatheter procedure was undertaken in 2022 to reestablish valve function and minimize the hazards associated with open surgery. After effective management of PVE and with no ongoing infection detected, a third transcatheter intervention was planned in 2024 due to the patient's prohibitive surgical risk, the technical challenges posed by previously implanted valve frames, and CT evidence demonstrating that coronary flow could be maintained and satisfactory haemodynamic results achieved with this approach.

In this patient, prosthetic valve dysfunction was probably due to several factors. The prior episode of infective endocarditis may have led to damage of the valve leaflets and subsequent mechanical limitation, but ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves also significantly contributed. Notably, after the infection was deemed resolved and prolonged antibiotic suppression was not indicated, the approach to treating prosthetic valve malfunction mirrored that for individuals without active or persistent endocarditis. Consequently, procedural planning was driven mainly by anatomical considerations, severity of haemodynamic impairment, and surgical risk assessment, rather than solely by the previous infection history.

In this instance, the patient had finished a course of antibiotics, with no evidence of ongoing infection on clinical assessment or imaging studies. Comprehensive preprocedural workup, which included transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT), verified the lack of vegetations and paravalvular issues. The patient's calculated EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, and he had notable comorbidities such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, and a history of gastrointestinal bleeding, as well as two previously implanted transcatheter valves that demonstrated substantial stent integration into the aortic wall. These factors collectively made conventional surgery extremely risky and technically demanding. The prior valve positioning suggested that open surgery would likely involve complete replacement of the aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple case reports have described TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 emphasizing different technical and anatomical difficulties encountered. This case contributes further to the scarce literature, presenting the distinct scenario of valve degeneration following endocarditis and demonstrating maintained prosthetic valve performance at one year follow-up.

This case highlights the increasing significance of comprehensive, lifelong management approaches for structural valve disease. Successive transcatheter reinterventions add complexity to factors such as cumulative stent frame layers, commissural orientation, coronary access, and the maintenance of adequate effective orifice area. Early planning for long-term outcomes and prompt discussions regarding procedural sequences are essential, especially at the initial intervention, and are particularly vital for younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

The patient’s leaflet structural deterioration was characterized by fibrosis secondary to earlier degeneration, coupled with likely mechanical constraint after infection. The care team elected to implant a third valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 prosthesis—guided by preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modeling that confirmed both the procedural feasibility and sufficient clearance of the coronary arteries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The valve was intentionally implanted high within the anatomy. Positioning the prosthesis at the level of the existing Evolut valve leaflets enabled the fixation of the native leaflets to the stent frame, effectively mitigating additional reductions of the effective orifice area due to leaflet folding inward. This high implantation also limited the creation of a neo-skirt below the coronary ostia, helping to preserve coronary flow and future access for coronary interventions. The procedural plan was based on comprehensive preprocedural CT analysis and proved critical in optimizing haemodynamic results in the context of triple valve stenting. Even though triple transcatheter valve deployment is complex, the technique was both technically possible and reproducible, provided it was directed by meticulous preprocedural imaging and a well-defined implantation plan.

The Acurate neo2 device was chosen due to its supra-annular placement of the leaflets, comparatively short stent frame, and optimal haemodynamic outcomes in constricted anatomies. Additionally, its design facilitates commissural alignment, a critical factor in this scenario to maintain accessibility to the coronary arteries in the future.

An important technical issue associated with TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions is the potential for compromised coronary access arising from several superimposed stent structures. While efforts were made to achieve commissural alignment, the aggregate mesh density could still hinder subsequent coronary procedures. This underscores the importance of careful procedural strategy, especially in patients with established coronary pathology or in whom later coronary access could be critical. Preprocedural CT was used to assess the implantation site, analyze interactions between valve frames, and approximate distances from the valve to coronary ostia, though comprehensive three-dimensional expansion assessment with specialized software was not undertaken.

The resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve placement is likely due to improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical enlargement of the existing Evolut stent, all of which enhanced leaflet movement and central hemodynamics. Although deliberate fracturing of the valve frame was not conducted, the use of high-pressure balloon post-dilation probably resulted in additional radial expansion of the Evolut device, further improving valve performance.

Institutional authorization was obtained to utilize the Acurate neo2 prosthesis in an off-label manner during the procedure. The decision-making process was collaborative, involving the patient and his relatives, and the case underwent discussion within a multidisciplinary heart team comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

If the present prosthetic valve were to fail sooner than anticipated, the available therapeutic options would be highly constrained. Surgical removal of the device would continue to pose significant technical challenges and carry substantial risk. Novel catheter-based approaches—including bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration procedures such as bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—could potentially be explored, but their practicality and safety in the setting of a triple-stented anatomy are still undetermined.
