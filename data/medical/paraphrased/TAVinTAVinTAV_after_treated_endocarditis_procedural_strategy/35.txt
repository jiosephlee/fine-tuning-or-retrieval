Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the array of technical complexities inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve interventions, management must be individualized for each patient. Here, we describe a man in his late 70s with a complicated medical background, including two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures and a history of infective endocarditis, who subsequently presented with significant aortic prosthesis deterioration, necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The initial TAVI involved a Lotus valve in 2014; in 2022, he received a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut device, which was complicated by endocarditis-induced deterioration and marked restenosis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 valve was deployed using a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. With broader indications for TAVI and increasing life expectancy among recipients, intricate multi-prosthesis procedures are likely to become more common, underscoring the importance of enhanced procedural planning, advanced imaging, and extended follow-up. At the 1-year mark, the patient remains symptom-free and retains adequate valve function. This case demonstrates the practicality of performing a third TAVI following managed prosthetic valve endocarditis, with support from CT-based planning and one-year follow-up—a scenario rarely documented in published reports.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become a widely accepted therapy for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, regardless of surgical risk category. As TAVI procedures become more prevalent and patients experience increased long-term survival, structural valve dysfunction and the subsequent need for repeat procedures are being encountered with greater frequency in clinical settings.

Within this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) interventions have surfaced as an effective approach for addressing the failure of previous transcatheter valves. Nevertheless, these procedures bring distinct anatomic and technical complexities, such as the potential for coronary artery blockage and a diminished effective orifice area, which may lead to pronounced prosthesis-patient mismatch. These issues become even more pronounced with successive procedures—such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV)—due to the additional overlapping of prosthetic valve frames that must be factored into procedural planning.

The literature addressing repeated transcatheter valve procedures is sparse, especially in patients who have previously experienced prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these challenging situations, comprehensive imaging before the procedure, personalized risk evaluation, and involvement of a multidisciplinary team are imperative to assess procedural feasibility and improve outcomes.

The present case demonstrates the importance of CT-based planning and a strategic transcatheter approach in a patient with multiple prior TAVI interventions and resolved PVE, underscoring critical aspects of long-term management in structural valve pathology.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center, presenting with dyspnoea, a constricting sensation localized to the left side of the chest upon exertion (noted while walking distances of 200–300 m), as well as dizziness and fatigue.

His prior medical history included arterial hypertension, triple-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: right coronary artery in 2009, left anterior descending (LAD) artery in 2010, repeated LAD intervention in 2012, and circumflex artery in 2014. He also had a third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating pacemaker placement in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes beginning in 2022.

In 2014, following progressive aortic valve degeneration and severe stenosis, he underwent transcatheter implantation of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, his exertional dyspnoea worsened, and echocardiography indicated severe stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. By early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had escalated to 83 mm Hg, and his left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) further declined to 42% (from 54% in December).

Subsequently, in May 2022, he received a TAV-in-TAV procedure with placement of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). This intervention was complicated by the emergence of a pulsatile femoral haematoma and pseudoaneurysm, necessitating open vascular surgery for management.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV, his left ventricular EF increased from 42% to 54%, demonstrating a positive early haemodynamic outcome. His heart failure was treated in accordance with guideline-directed medical therapy, including beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics, as clinically indicated.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted again due to ongoing fever and aggravation of heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures returned positive for Enterococcus faecalis, leading to the diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a full course of tailored antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis-induced PVE, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, subsequently followed by oral antibiotics as advised by infectious disease specialists. By the time the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was scheduled, antimicrobial treatment had concluded, and no maintenance suppressive antibiotic regimen was in use. Clinical examination, repeated blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) all confirmed infection resolution, with no signs indicative of ongoing endocarditis.

Upon completion of antimicrobial therapy and after partial alleviation of symptoms, follow-up in January 2024 showed further deterioration in the prosthetic valve, characterized by severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean transvalvular gradient had increased to 49 mm Hg, and the left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) had improved to 66%.

A plan was made for another reintervention. On admission in June 2024, TTE revealed sustained grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a notably decreased aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², consistent with advanced prosthetic valve dysfunction. AVA was determined using the continuity equation, incorporating measurements from the left ventricular outflow tract and velocity-time integrals from Doppler imaging. Visualization identified two superimposed bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT measured the minimum lumen diameter in the Evolut prosthesis at 13.7 mm, confirming significant haemodynamic impairment and aiding procedural planning for a third transcatheter valve placement (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT was employed to evaluate anatomical suitability and assess the potential for coronary artery obstruction, with specific measurements including the height of the coronary ostia, the sinus of Valsalva diameter, valve-to-coronary distance, and interactions among the structural frames of the previously implanted valves. The anticipated neo-skirt’s relationship to the coronary ostia, as well as the viability of high implantation to ensure sustained coronary flow and future coronary access, were particularly examined. These assessments determined valve choice, implantation depth, and procedure strategy, ultimately allowing the intervention without the need for coronary protection.

Prior to the reintervention, transoesophageal echocardiography was conducted and confirmed there was no ongoing endocarditis or paravalvular complication.

Treatment:

Owing to the patient’s intricate anatomy with three previous stents, history of infective endocarditis, and extremely high surgical risk, the third TAVI was scheduled as a computed tomography-guided and strategically focused intervention prioritising coronary protection, precise commissural orientation, and enhancement of the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention utilized local anaesthesia, employing both left radial and left femoral arterial entry sites. Aortography performed with a pigtail catheter demonstrated significant aortic regurgitation (figure 2).

Using left femoral access, a Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle. Balloon pre-dilatation of the aortic valve with a 22/40 mm catheter occurred during rapid ventricular pacing set at 180 bpm. Concurrent aortography verified that coronary circulation was unaffected (figure 3).

An Acurate neo2 (size S) self-expanding bioprosthetic valve was delivered at the aortic valve location. Positioning of the device was matched to the orientation of the Evolut valve leaflets based on preprocedural CT planning, aiming to optimise the effective orifice area and minimise the possibility of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Following valve implantation, post-dilation using a 20/40 mm balloon was carried out under rapid pacing to further expand the device. A final aortogram demonstrated proper valve placement, maintained coronary blood flow, and confirmed the absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Commissural alignment was considered throughout valve deployment to maintain access to coronary arteries and simplify any potential future procedures, guided by preoperative CT imaging and fluoroscopic landmarks.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural success and performance of the prosthetic valve were evaluated in accordance with the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, incorporating assessment of haemodynamic parameters, identification of residual regurgitation, and any procedure-related adverse events.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) conducted immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 verified correct placement of the third transcatheter valve. Valve leaflet mobility was noted, with full opening and absence of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Despite this, haemodynamic measurements revealed high transvalvular gradients, with a mean of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated as 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), while the Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured 0.24, in keeping with severe patient–prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

At 1 month after valve placement, TTE demonstrated significant improvement; the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg and AVA had risen to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodelling was seen, with diminished chamber volumes. There remained no residual regurgitation, and prosthetic valve function was stable, now within the moderately stenotic classification.

After 3.5 months, the patient was symptom-free and exhibited robust exercise capacity. There was no evidence of heart failure or recurrent infection. The valve continued to function effectively with persistent improvement in haemodynamic parameters.

By 1 year post-implantation (June 2025), the patient was clinically stable and physically active, with neither dyspnoea nor signs of decompensation. TTE indicated a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, an AVA of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. No complications related to the valve, regurgitation, or recurrence of endocarditis were detected.

Overall, these results support the durability of this intervention and indicate a prolonged functional benefit of the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach for this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This report underscores the clinical reasoning and procedural difficulties encountered when undertaking a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient who had previously undergone TAV-in-TAV procedures and presented with a background of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI is now widely accepted for the management of aortic stenosis, its application in patients with a prior history of endocarditis is still debated. The 2025 guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) concerning valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis recommend surgical valve replacement as the preferred intervention for PVE, especially in the presence of heart failure or infection that is not controlled. Nonetheless, the guidelines also highlight the necessity of tailoring management to the individual, considering factors such as infection eradication, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

In 2014, TAVI was selected as the initial therapy because the patient was determined to be at high surgical risk with several comorbidities, making traditional surgical valve replacement less suitable. When the patient later experienced degeneration of the bioprosthesis, a repeat transcatheter intervention was chosen in 2022 to re-establish valve competence while circumventing the dangers associated with repeat open heart surgery. After successful resolution of PVE and with no signs of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter procedure was elected in 2024 based on persistently high operative risk, challenging anatomy due to previous implanted valves, and CT evidence supporting the procedural feasibility to maintain coronary perfusion and attain satisfactory haemodynamic results.

The underlying cause of prosthetic valve dysfunction in this patient appeared to involve multiple factors. Although the previous episode of infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage and subsequent mechanical limitation, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter prostheses was also a major contributor. Crucially, after infection resolution and with no need for prolonged suppressive antibiotics, management of prosthetic valve complications was similar to that for patients who did not have active endocarditis. As a result, procedural decisions were made primarily based on anatomical suitability, severity of haemodynamic impairment, and surgical risk, rather than on the historical presence of infection.

For this patient, antimicrobial treatment had been completed, with no evidence of active infection on clinical assessment or imaging. Preprocedural investigations, including transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) and multislice computed tomography (MSCT), confirmed there were no vegetations or paravalvular abnormalities. Additionally, a EuroSCORE II of 27.36%, along with significant comorbidities such as COPD, diabetes, and prior gastrointestinal hemorrhage, and the presence of two previously placed transcatheter valves that were extensively integrated into the aortic wall, made conventional surgery extremely high-risk and technically complex. Due to the deep anchoring of earlier prostheses, surgical repair would most likely require replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple case reports have documented TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 outlining distinct anatomical and technical hurdles. Our report contributes to this limited literature by presenting a scenario characterized by post-endocarditis degeneration, with maintained valve functionality at 1-year follow-up.

This example highlights the increasing relevance of long-term management plans in patients with structural valve pathology. Repeated transcatheter interventions add layers of complexity involving frame superimposition, commissural orientation, coronary access preservation, and sustaining sufficient effective orifice area. Therefore, establishing a comprehensive procedural roadmap and discussing the overall trajectory early—including at the initial intervention—is vital, especially for younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

In the present patient, structural deterioration was attributed to fibrotic thickening of the leaflets due to prior degeneration, combined with presumed mechanical leaflet restriction resulting from infection. The multidisciplinary team opted to implant a third prosthesis—a self-expanding Acurate neo2 valve—following preprocedural CT imaging and 3D reconstruction, which confirmed procedural feasibility and suitable clearance for the coronaries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The decision to position the third device at a higher location was intentional. Implanting the prosthesis at the plane of the Evolut valve’s leaflets secured the degenerated leaflets against the stent structure, thus preventing further compromise of the effective orifice area by avoiding inward leaflet prolapse. Moreover, this tactic limited the creation of a new stent skirt below the coronary ostia, supporting maintenance of coronary patency and access for future interventions. Preprocedural CT analysis was integral in planning this approach and essential for optimizing haemodynamic performance in a triple-layered stent construct. Despite the challenges inherent in deploying three transcatheter valves, careful imaging and predetermined procedural planning allowed for a technically successful and reproducible result.

The Acurate neo2 valve was chosen because of its supra-annular leaflet configuration, comparatively short frame height, and its favourable haemodynamic characteristics in narrower anatomical settings. Its structural features facilitate commissural alignment, which was an important factor in this case to maintain future accessibility to the coronary arteries.

A major technical challenge in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions is the potential for compromised coronary access caused by overlapping stent frames. Despite attempts at achieving optimal commissural alignment, the accumulation of stent mesh may still make subsequent coronary procedures more difficult. This underscores the importance of careful procedural planning for patients with established coronary pathology or for those in whom preserved coronary access may be critical. Preprocedural CT scans allowed for assessment of the landing area, review of frame interactions, and approximation of the valve-to-coronary distances, although a complete three-dimensional expansion analysis utilizing specialized software was not conducted.

Resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implantation likely resulted from a combination of improved commissural alignment, effective post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the pre-existing Evolut frame, which promoted better leaflet mobility and central flow patterns. Although deliberate frame fracture was not carried out, balloon post-dilation at high pressure probably provided additional radial expansion of the Evolut device, thereby improving valvular performance.

This intervention proceeded under institutional authorization for off-label utilization of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. Decisions regarding treatment were made collaboratively with the patient and his family, and the case underwent review during a multidisciplinary heart team discussion, including interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging professionals, and anaesthesia providers.

If the newly implanted prosthesis fails sooner than expected, options for further management would be severely restricted. Surgical removal would be associated with considerable technical difficulty and elevated risk. Novel catheter-based techniques such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration—for example, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction (BASILICA)—could potentially be contemplated, though their efficacy and safety in scenarios involving three overlapping stent frames remain unclear.
