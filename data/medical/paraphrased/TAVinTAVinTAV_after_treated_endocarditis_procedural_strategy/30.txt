Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Due to the varying technical complexities inherent in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve procedures, therapeutic decisions must be individualized for each patient. Here, we describe a case involving a male in his late seventies who had a complicated clinical background, including two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantations (TAVI) and a history of infective endocarditis, subsequently developing severe deterioration of the aortic prosthesis that necessitated a third transcatheter procedure. The patient initially received a Lotus valve in 2014, followed by a TAV-in-TAV intervention using an Evolut valve in 2022, after which degeneration secondary to endocarditis and severe restenosis occurred. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 prosthesis was placed using the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As indications for TAVI broaden and treated patients live longer, complex interventions involving multiple prostheses are expected to occur more frequently, emphasizing the importance of refined procedural planning, advanced imaging, and diligent long-term monitoring. One year post-procedure, the patient continues to be asymptomatic with maintained prosthetic valve function. This report demonstrates that a third TAVI after managed prosthetic valve endocarditis is achievable, supported by CT-based procedural guidance and one-year follow-up, a scenario that is rarely documented in existing literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become a well-established therapeutic approach for severe aortic stenosis, applicable to patients across a wide spectrum of surgical risk categories. With an expanding population of individuals undergoing TAVI and extended survival durations, clinicians are increasingly encountering structural valve degeneration and the necessity for subsequent interventions.

In this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) procedures have arisen as an effective solution for managing failed transcatheter bioprostheses. Nevertheless, these procedures present unique anatomical and procedural complexities, such as the potential for coronary artery obstruction and a diminished effective orifice area, which may lead to significant prosthesis-patient mismatch. The complexity of these issues is further heightened when multiple sequential interventions are performed—such as transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV)—necessitating careful consideration of the cumulative effects of overlapping stent frames.

There remains a paucity of data regarding repeat transcatheter valve interventions, particularly in patients with prior episodes of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these intricate cases, thorough pre-procedural imaging, individualized risk stratification, and collaborative, multidisciplinary planning are vital for assessing procedural feasibility and optimizing outcomes.

This report demonstrates how computed tomography-assisted planning and a tailored transcatheter strategy were utilized in a patient with a history of multiple TAVI interventions and previous PVE, emphasizing important aspects in the ongoing management of structural valve disease.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was admitted to the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after experiencing dyspnoea, a sensation of spasm localized to the left chest during exertion (while walking 200–300 m), dizziness, and fatigue.

His prior medical history included arterial hypertension, three-vessel coronary artery disease, and several percutaneous coronary interventions: in 2009 for the right coronary artery, 2010 for the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, 2012 for recurrent LAD lesions, and 2014 for the circumflex artery. He also had comorbidities such as third-degree atrioventricular block, for which a pacemaker was placed in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding episodes starting in 2022.

Due to advancing degeneration and severe stenosis of the aortic valve in 2014, he underwent transcatheter implantation of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, his exertional dyspnoea had worsened, with echocardiography demonstrating severe stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. In early 2022, the mean pressure gradient had increased to 83 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction decreased to 42% (from 54% in December).

In May 2022, transcatheter valve-in-valve (TAV-in-TAV) implantation of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic) was performed. The intervention was complicated by a pulsatile femoral haematoma and pseudoaneurysm, necessitating open surgical repair.

Post-Evolut TAV-in-TAV implantation, left ventricular ejection fraction improved from 42% to 54%, representing a positive early haemodynamic outcome. Medical management for heart failure was continued in accordance with guidelines, utilizing beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted once more due to ongoing fever and deteriorating heart failure. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated significant restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures grew Enterococcus faecalis, resulting in a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient underwent a complete regimen of targeted antimicrobial therapy for E. faecalis-associated PVE, which consisted of six weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, followed by oral therapy as recommended by infectious disease specialists. By the time of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), the antimicrobial course had ended and no ongoing suppressive antibiotic regimen was being administered. Repeat clinical evaluation, serial blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice CT (MSCT) indicated that infection had resolved, with no signs of active endocarditis detected.

Following completion of antimicrobial therapy and partial improvement in symptoms, a follow-up assessment in January 2024 showed deteriorating function of the prosthetic valve, including severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean gradient was recorded at 49 mm Hg, and left ventricular ejection fraction had improved to 66%.

The clinical team opted to pursue another intervention. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE identified sustained grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a notably decreased aortic valve area (AVA) at 0.5 cm², all indicative of pronounced dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA was estimated using the continuity equation, utilizing left ventricular outflow tract data and Doppler velocity-time integrals. Imaging revealed two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT detected a minimum lumen diameter of 13.7 mm within the Evolut prosthesis, verifying severe haemodynamic compromise and informing plans for a third transcatheter valve insertion (see figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT was utilized to evaluate anatomical suitability and risk of coronary artery obstruction, including assessment of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva size, valve-to-coronary distance, and the interaction between the frames of previously implanted transcatheter valves. Special consideration was given to the position of the expected neo-skirt in relation to the coronary ostia and the feasibility of high implantation to maintain coronary flow and facilitate future coronary access. These results shaped choices regarding valve selection, implantation depth, and procedural approach, enabling intervention without coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography was performed before the reintervention and verified the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular pathology.

Treatment:

Due to the patient's intricate triple-stented aortic anatomy, history of infective endocarditis, and extremely high surgical risk, a third TAVI was scheduled utilizing CT guidance and a strategic approach prioritizing coronary preservation, commissural alignment, and optimal maintenance of the remaining effective orifice area.

The intervention was carried out under local anesthesia, employing both left radial and left femoral arterial access. Severe aortic regurgitation was demonstrated on aortography with a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

A Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle through the left femoral route. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilation using a 22/40 mm catheter, with rapid ventricular pacing at 180 beats per minute. Concurrent aortography verified uninterrupted coronary flow (figure 3).

A self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (Acurate neo2, size S) was deployed in the aortic position. Precise device alignment with the Evolut valve leaflets—guided by preprocedural CT simulation—was performed to increase effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Post-dilation followed, utilizing a 20/40 mm balloon during rapid pacing, to ensure optimal valve expansion. The final aortogram demonstrated accurate device placement, maintained coronary perfusion, and absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Attention was paid to commissural alignment during the valve insertion to facilitate future coronary access and limit complexity in subsequent interventions, informed by preprocedure CT images and fluoroscopic references.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural efficacy and the function of the prosthetic valve were evaluated utilizing the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 guidelines, including assessment of haemodynamic performance, the presence of residual regurgitation, and procedure-associated complications.

A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) performed directly following the intervention on 7 June 2024 verified the successful implantation of the third transcatheter valve. The valve leaflets exhibited unrestricted mobility with full opening, and no evidence of aortic regurgitation was detected (grade 0). Despite this, haemodynamic measurements showed significant transvalvular gradients with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The aortic valve area (AVA) was calculated as 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured at 0.24, indicating severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

One month post-procedure, TTE demonstrated substantial improvement: the mean gradient had decreased to 27 mm Hg, and the AVA had risen to 1.1 cm². Evidence of left ventricular remodelling was present, as indicated by reduced chamber volumes. No residual regurgitation was observed, and the prosthetic valve function remained consistent with moderate stenosis.

At 3.5 months, the patient exhibited no symptoms and was able to tolerate exercise well. There were no manifestations of heart failure or recurring infection. The valve prosthesis continued to function effectively, with stable haemodynamic findings.

By the 1-year follow-up (June 2025), the patient persisted in a clinically stable and active state, with no signs of dyspnoea or decompensation. TTE revealed a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and an ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. There was no evidence of valve-related complications, regurgitation, or recurrent endocarditis.

Collectively, these results validate the durability of the outcome and indicate continued functional improvement following the TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedure in this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This report illustrates the complexities and technical considerations involved in carrying out a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient who previously underwent two TAV-in-TAV procedures and had a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI is now a well-established approach for treating aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with previous endocarditis remains debated. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines on valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis still advocate for surgical valve replacement as the preferred option in cases of PVE, especially when heart failure or ongoing infection is present. Nonetheless, the guidelines stress that treatment should be tailored to the individual, factoring in infection resolution, operative risk, and suitability from an anatomical standpoint.

The initial selection of TAVI for this patient in 2014 was largely influenced by an elevated surgical risk and multiple coexisting conditions that rendered standard surgical valve replacement less suitable. When the patient subsequently experienced structural valve failure, a transcatheter re-intervention was performed in 2022 to restore valvular function and avoid the dangers of open-heart surgery. After resolution of PVE and with no active infection identified, a third transcatheter intervention was deemed appropriate in 2024, prompted by excessive surgical risk, challenging anatomy due to earlier valve placements, and CT confirmation that this approach would maintain coronary perfusion and provide acceptable haemodynamic results.

The cause of prosthetic valve failure in this patient was likely due to multiple factors. Although the prior episode of infective endocarditis may have led to leaflet damage and subsequent impaired mobility, ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter valves was also a major contributor. Notably, after infection had been deemed cured and no extended antibiotic therapy was warranted, management of the prosthetic valve abnormality followed standard protocols used for patients without persistent or active endocarditis. Thus, decisions regarding intervention were based chiefly on anatomical considerations, the severity of haemodynamic compromise, and surgical risk assessment rather than solely the patient’s infectious history.

In this scenario, the patient had finished antimicrobial treatment, exhibited neither clinical nor imaging evidence of current infection, and underwent comprehensive preprocedural workup—including TEE and MSCT—which confirmed the absence of vegetations and paravalvular pathology. In addition, the patient’s EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, with notable comorbid conditions (COPD, diabetes mellitus, history of gastrointestinal bleeding) and two previously inserted transcatheter valves showing considerable stent incorporation within the aortic wall. These features made conventional surgery exceptionally risky and technically challenging. Owing to the deep anchoring of the existing valves, a surgical approach would likely have required replacement of both the entire aortic root and the ascending aorta.

Multiple reports have described TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 illustrating a range of anatomical and procedural difficulties. Our case expands this limited literature by presenting a scenario characterized by valve degeneration following endocarditis, with valve functionality maintained at the 1-year follow-up.

This report emphasizes the increasing need for comprehensive lifetime management approaches in structural valve disorders. Subsequent transcatheter procedures introduce heightened challenges regarding aspects such as overlapping frames, alignment of commissures, accessibility of coronary arteries, and maintenance of the effective orifice area. It is essential to initiate long-term planning and discuss possible procedural pathways early—ideally at the time of initial intervention—especially for younger patients or those at elevated risk.

In this patient, structural valve deterioration included leaflet fibrosis from prior degeneration as well as likely mechanical restriction stemming from infection. The medical team elected to place a third prosthetic valve—a self-expanding Acurate neo2—after preprocedural CT and three-dimensional modelling confirmed both procedural feasibility and sufficient clearance of coronary arteries (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The choice to implant the third valve at a high position was intentional. By deploying the device at the level of the Evolut valve’s leaflets, the prior leaflets were fixed against the stent frame, which avoided further narrowing of the effective orifice due to inward folding. Moreover, this method reduced the risk of neo-skirt formation below the coronary ostia, thus supporting maintenance of coronary flow and future access. This strategy, informed by preprocedural CT evaluation, was critical to achieving satisfactory haemodynamics within a triple-stented configuration. Although a triple transcatheter valve anatomy poses considerable complexity, the procedure was successfully executed and shown to be replicable when guided by comprehensive preprocedural imaging and an established implantation plan.

The Acurate neo2 device was chosen for this patient due to its supra-annular leaflet position, comparatively short frame height, and advantageous haemodynamic properties in restricted anatomical spaces. Its structural features allow for commissural alignment, which was of particular importance in this situation to ensure potential future coronary access.

A major technical issue encountered in TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV procedures is the possibility of compromised coronary access resulting from multiple superimposed stent frames. Despite efforts to achieve commissural alignment, the cumulative presence of stent mesh can still complicate subsequent coronary interventions. This emphasizes the necessity for careful procedural planning, especially for individuals with existing coronary disease or those who may require future access. Preprocedural CT imaging facilitated assessment of the intended landing area, evaluation of frame interactions, and estimation of distances between valve and coronaries, though comprehensive three-dimensional expansion analysis was not performed with specialized software.

Resolution of the transvalvular gradient after the third device implantation is likely due to optimal commissural alignment, thorough post-dilatation, and partial mechanical expansion of the earlier Evolut frame, which enhanced leaflet mobility and central flow. Although deliberate frame fracturing was not undertaken, high-pressure balloon post-dilatation most likely further increased radial expansion of the Evolut, resulting in improved valve performance.

The intervention was conducted under the institution’s approval for the off-label application of the Acurate neo2 prosthesis. Collaborative decision-making involved the patient and his family, and the case underwent review by a multidisciplinary heart team consisting of interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anesthesiologists.

In the event that the current prosthesis fails prematurely, available therapeutic strategies would be markedly constrained. Surgical explantation would remain complex and carry significant risk. Newer transcatheter options, such as bioprosthetic valve fracturing or leaflet laceration techniques (for example, bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction [BASILICA]), could be explored; however, their viability and safety in cases with triple stent layers are yet to be clearly established.
