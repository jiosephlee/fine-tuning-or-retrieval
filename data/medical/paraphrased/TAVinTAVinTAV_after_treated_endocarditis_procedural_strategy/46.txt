Title: TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV after treated endocarditis: procedural strategy, imaging planning and 1-year outcome

Evgeny Vladimirovich Merkulov, Goar Kimovna Arutyunyan, Andrey Sergeevich Tereshchenko, Dmitriy V Pevzner, Iiliya Tarasovich Zyuryaev, Mariya Alekseevna Terenicheva, Olga Nickolaevna Ganeeva, Alexey Vladimirovich Knigin

Abstract:

Summary: Due to the various technical complexities involved in transcatheter valve-in-transcatheter valve interventions, management approaches should be individualized for each patient. We describe a case involving a man in his late seventies with a complicated history, including two previous transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedures as well as infective endocarditis, who subsequently experienced significant degeneration of the aortic valve prosthesis necessitating a third transcatheter procedure. The patient originally received a Lotus valve in 2014, underwent a TAV-in-TAV with an Evolut device in 2022, and subsequently suffered prosthetic degeneration with severe restenosis secondary to endocarditis. In 2024, an Acurate neo2 prosthesis was deployed as a third valve utilizing a TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV approach. As indications for TAVI broaden and treated patients experience longer survival, the incidence of such complex, multi-prosthetic interventions may rise, emphasizing the importance of enhanced procedural planning, optimized imaging protocols, and vigilant long-term follow-up. At one year post-procedure, the patient is asymptomatic and maintains satisfactory valve performance. This case demonstrates the practicality of performing a third TAVI after previous prosthetic valve endocarditis, with the use of CT-based procedural guidance and 12-month follow-up—an occurrence rarely documented in current literature.

Background:

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has become a well-recognized therapy for individuals with severe aortic stenosis, encompassing a wide spectrum of surgical risk categories. As TAVI is increasingly performed and patient longevity improves, clinicians are facing more cases of structural valve deterioration and are more frequently required to perform reintervention procedures.

Within this context, transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV) techniques have arisen as an effective method for addressing malfunctioning transcatheter valves. Nonetheless, these interventions pose unique anatomical and procedural difficulties, such as the possibility of coronary artery occlusion and a diminished effective orifice area, leading to pronounced prosthesis-patient mismatch. These issues become more pronounced during subsequent interventions like transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve-in-transcatheter aortic valve (TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV), where multiple layers of valve frames must be evaluated carefully.

Current data on consecutive transcatheter valve redo procedures remain sparse, especially in patients with prior prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). In these complicated cases, comprehensive preprocedure imaging, personalised assessment of risk, and collaborative multidisciplinary discussion are critical to ascertain procedural viability and enhance outcomes.

The present case demonstrates the importance of CT-based planning and deliberate transcatheter strategy in a patient with several previous TAVI interventions and managed PVE, underscoring essential factors in the lifelong approach to structural valve disease.

Case presentation:

A male patient in his late seventies was hospitalized at the Academician E.I. Chazov National Medical Research Center after reporting shortness of breath, a spastic sensation localized to the left chest during exertion (walking 200–300 m), as well as episodes of dizziness and general fatigue.

His past medical history included arterial hypertension, coronary artery disease affecting three vessels, and multiple percutaneous coronary interventions: in 2009 for the right coronary artery, in 2010 for the left anterior descending (LAD) artery, in 2012 for a recurrent LAD lesion, and in 2014 for the circumflex artery. Additional diagnoses were third-degree atrioventricular block necessitating a pacemaker implantation in 2014, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and recurrent gastrointestinal bleeding beginning in 2022.

In 2014, after experiencing progressive degeneration and severe stenosis of the aortic valve, he underwent transcatheter placement of a 27 mm Lotus valve (Boston Scientific). By December 2021, his exertional dyspnoea had intensified, and echocardiographic evaluation showed marked stenosis of the bioprosthetic valve. In early 2022, the mean transvalvular gradient had risen to 83 mm Hg, and his ejection fraction (EF) further dropped to 42% from a previous value of 54% in December.

In May 2022, he underwent a transcatheter valve-in-valve procedure (TAV-in-TAV) with implantation of a 23 mm Evolut valve (Medtronic). The intervention was complicated by the formation of a pulsatile femoral hematoma and pseudoaneurysm, which necessitated open vascular surgical repair.

After the Evolut TAV-in-TAV intervention, the patient’s left ventricular ejection fraction improved from 42% to 54%, reflecting a positive initial haemodynamic outcome. He subsequently received guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure, which consisted of beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, and diuretics, tailored as appropriate.

Three months later, in September 2022, the patient was admitted again due to persistent fever and aggravated heart failure symptoms. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed pronounced restenosis of the aortic valve prosthesis, with a mean gradient measured at 35 mm Hg. Blood cultures identified Enterococcus faecalis, establishing a diagnosis of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE). The patient was administered a complete course of targeted antimicrobial therapy for PVE attributed to E. faecalis, consisting of 6 weeks of intravenous ampicillin-based treatment, succeeded by oral therapy as per recommendations from infectious disease consultants. By the time of the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), antimicrobial therapy was concluded, and there was no ongoing long-term suppressive antibiotic treatment. Clinical evaluation, multiple blood cultures, transoesophageal echocardiography, and multislice computed tomography (MSCT) demonstrated resolution of the infection, with no signs of active endocarditis.

Following antimicrobial therapy completion and partial improvement of symptoms, follow-up in January 2024 indicated further deterioration of the prosthetic valve, presenting as severe aortic stenosis and moderate-to-severe regurgitation. The mean pressure gradient was 49 mm Hg, while left ventricular ejection fraction had recovered to 66%.

Reintervention was subsequently planned. Upon admission in June 2024, TTE demonstrated ongoing grade 2–3 intraprosthetic aortic regurgitation, a mean transvalvular gradient of 49 mm Hg, and a significantly diminished aortic valve area (AVA) of 0.5 cm², indicative of pronounced dysfunction of the prosthetic valve. The AVA was established using the continuity equation, incorporating left ventricular outflow tract measurements and Doppler-derived velocity-time integrals. Imaging displayed two overlapping bioprosthetic valves.

MSCT measured the minimum lumen diameter of the Evolut prosthesis at 13.7 mm, confirming severe hemodynamic impairment and informing procedural plans for a third transcatheter valve placement (figure 1). Preprocedural MSCT was employed to evaluate anatomical suitability and the risk of coronary artery obstruction, including analysis of coronary ostial height, sinus of Valsalva size, valve-to-coronary distances, and the interplay between the frames of the existing transcatheter valves. Special consideration was given to the relationship between the predicted neo-skirt and coronary ostia, as well as the practicality of high implantation in order to preserve coronary flow and access for future coronary interventions. These assessments determined the choice of valve, depth of implantation, and procedural approach, enabling the procedure to proceed without the need for coronary protection.

Transoesophageal echocardiography prior to the reintervention corroborated the absence of active endocarditis or paravalvular complications.

Treatment:

Considering the patient’s intricate anatomy due to three prior stents, history of infective endocarditis, and significant contraindications to surgery, the third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) was undertaken as a computed tomography (CT)-guided, protocol-driven intervention, prioritizing coronary protection, commissural orientation, and enhancement of the effective orifice area.

The intervention was carried out with local anesthesia, utilizing left radial and left femoral arterial access points. Severe aortic regurgitation was corroborated by aortography using a pigtail catheter (figure 2).

Through the left femoral access, a Safari guidewire was advanced into the left ventricle. The aortic valve underwent balloon pre-dilation with a 22/40 mm balloon during rapid ventricular pacing at 180 bpm. Concurrent aortographic imaging confirmed uninterrupted coronary blood flow (figure 3).

A self-expanding bioprosthetic valve (Acurate neo2, size S) was placed in the aortic location. Placement was oriented to align with the leaflet positions of the Evolut valve, as determined by preprocedural CT analysis, aiming to optimize the effective orifice area and minimize the likelihood of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

Further balloon dilation was performed with a 20/40 mm catheter under rapid pacing to ensure optimal expansion of the valve. Concluding aortography demonstrated correct device positioning, sustained coronary circulation, and absence of both perivalvular and transvalvular regurgitation (figure 4).

Commissural alignment was specifically addressed during implantation to maintain coronary access and simplify any future procedures, aided by preprocedural CT evaluation and fluoroscopic guidance.

Outcome and follow-up:

Procedural outcomes and prosthetic valve function were evaluated based on the Valve Academic Research Consortium-3 standards, encompassing assessment of haemodynamic status, detection of any residual regurgitation, and documentation of procedure-associated complications.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) performed immediately post-procedure on 7 June 2024 demonstrated correct placement of the third transcatheter valve. The leaflets exhibited full mobility and opened completely, with absence of aortic regurgitation (grade 0). Despite these findings, haemodynamic measurements revealed pronounced transvalvular gradients, with a mean gradient of 67 mm Hg. The calculated aortic valve area (AVA) was 0.9 cm² (indexed AVA 0.4 cm²/m²), and the Doppler velocity index (DVI) measured 0.24, findings in keeping with severe patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM).

After one month, TTE revealed substantial improvement: mean gradient decreased to 27 mm Hg, AVA increased to 1.1 cm², and left ventricular chamber volumes were reduced, indicating remodelling. No residual regurgitation was present, and prosthetic valve function was stable within the moderately stenotic range.

At 3.5 months, the patient continued to have no symptoms and demonstrated robust exercise capacity. There was no clinical or echocardiographic evidence of heart failure or recurrent infection. The prosthetic valve maintained adequate performance with persistently improved haemodynamic measures.

By one year following implantation (June 2025), the patient remained clinically well and physically active, free from dyspnoea or signs of cardiac decompensation. TTE revealed a mean gradient of 26 mm Hg, AVA of 1.1 cm², and left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) of 65%. No valve-related adverse events, regurgitation, or recurrent endocarditis were detected.

Taken together, these results demonstrate ongoing durability and indicate lasting functional improvement following TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV intervention for this high-risk patient.

Discussion:

This case illustrates both the complexity of clinical decision-making and the technical difficulties encountered when performing a third transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) in a patient with two previous TAV-in-TAV interventions and a history of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE).

Although TAVI has become an established therapy for aortic stenosis, its application in individuals with prior endocarditis remains subject to debate. The 2025 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines for managing valvular heart disease and infective endocarditis continue to recommend surgical valve replacement in cases of PVE, especially when complicated by heart failure or persistent infection. Nevertheless, the guidelines stress the need to tailor management plans, factoring in infection status, operative risk, and anatomical suitability.

In 2014, the patient’s elevated surgical risk and extensive comorbidities led to the selection of TAVI as the preferred initial approach, as traditional surgery was deemed unsuitable. When structural valve deterioration occurred subsequently, a transcatheter repeat intervention was chosen in 2022 to restore valve functionality and avert the hazards of open surgery. After successful resolution of PVE and with no evidence of ongoing infection, a third transcatheter procedure was undertaken in 2024, guided by excessively high surgical risk, intricate anatomical issues related to the existing valve structures, and CT confirmation of a plan compatible with preserved coronary perfusion and satisfactory haemodynamics.

The failure of the prosthetic valve in this patient appeared to result from multiple contributing factors. The previous episode of infective endocarditis may have caused damage to the leaflets, resulting in mechanical impairment, but ongoing structural deterioration of the transcatheter devices was also an important contributor. After infection had been judged resolved and long-term suppressive antibiotics were no longer necessary, the approach to treating prosthetic valve dysfunction followed protocols typically applied to patients without current or persistent endocarditis. As a result, decisions regarding intervention were chiefly informed by anatomical considerations, haemodynamic severity, and operative risk rather than the patient’s history of infection.

For this patient, antimicrobial treatment had been completed, with no evidence of active infection clinically or on imaging. Comprehensive preprocedural assessment included TEE and MSCT, which verified that vegetations and paravalvular pathology were absent. Additionally, the patient’s EuroSCORE II was 27.36%, and comorbid conditions such as COPD, diabetes, and previous gastrointestinal bleeding, combined with the presence of two transcatheter valves with extensive stent incorporation into the aortic wall, made surgical intervention particularly hazardous and technically challenging. Due to the profound fixation of the previously placed prostheses, a surgical approach would likely have involved replacement of the entire aortic root and ascending aorta.

Multiple published case reports detail TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions,37 describing distinct anatomical and procedural difficulties. Our report contributes to this sparse literature, presenting a case with post-endocarditis deterioration and sustained valve performance at one-year follow-up.

This case highlights the increasing relevance of comprehensive lifetime management for patients with structural valve disease. Every subsequent transcatheter procedure brings heightened complexity regarding factors such as frame stacking, commissural positioning, coronary access, and maintaining effective orifice area. Thoughtful long-term strategy and early discussion of procedural pathways are necessary—ideally initiated with the first valve intervention, particularly in younger individuals or those at elevated risk.

Structural valve degradation in this patient included leaflet fibrosis resulting from previous deterioration as well as likely mechanical impairment secondary to infection. The clinical team selected a third prosthesis, implanting a self-expanding Acurate neo2 device, after preprocedural CT scans and three-dimensional modelling confirmed suitability and ensured sufficient coronary clearance (see figures 1, 5, and 7). The third valve was intentionally deployed at a high position, aligning with the leaflet level of the Evolut prosthesis. This method secured the pre-existing leaflets against the stent structure, preventing additional compromise of the effective orifice area from leaflet infolding. Furthermore, this approach reduced neo-skirt development below the coronary ostia, thereby supporting preserved coronary flow and facilitating subsequent coronary access. Decisions were informed by preprocedural CT evaluation and were pivotal to attaining satisfactory haemodynamics in this triple-layer valve anatomy. Although a three-valve configuration poses considerable procedural challenge, the method proved practical and reproducible with detailed imaging and a predetermined implant plan guiding the intervention.

The Acurate neo2 was chosen because its supra-annular leaflet placement, comparatively low frame height, and advantageous haemodynamic properties are particularly suited to narrow anatomies. Its structure facilitates commissural alignment, an important factor in this situation to maintain potential for future coronary access.

In TAV-in-TAV-in-TAV interventions, a major technical issue is the possibility of restricted coronary access resulting from multiple overlapping stent frames. Although efforts were made to achieve commissural alignment, the cumulative mesh complexity could nonetheless create difficulties for subsequent coronary procedures. This emphasizes the importance of careful procedural planning for patients with pre-existing coronary disease or those for whom future access may be critical. Preprocedural CT allowed for assessment of the landing zone, evaluation of frame interplay, and estimation of distances between the valve and coronaries, although dedicated three-dimensional expansion analysis software was not employed.

Resolution of the transvalvular gradient following the third valve implantation is likely due to a combination of improved commissural alignment, successful post-dilation, and partial mechanical expansion of the prior Evolut stent, which enhanced leaflet movement and central flow patterns. Although deliberate frame fracture was not utilized, high-pressure balloon post-dilation probably contributed additional radial expansion of the Evolut frame, thereby optimizing valve performance.

This case was managed with institutional authorization for off-label use of the Acurate neo2 device. The approach involved shared decision-making with the patient and his family and was deliberated by a multidisciplinary heart team comprising interventional cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, imaging experts, and anaesthesiologists.

In the event of premature failure of the present prosthesis, therapeutic avenues would be severely restricted. Surgical removal would pose significant technical difficulty and carry considerable risk. Novel catheter-based approaches such as bioprosthetic valve fracture or leaflet laceration (for example, BASILICA—bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent iatrogenic coronary artery obstruction) could be contemplated, but their practicality and safety within a triple-stent construct remain unproven.
