Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported a 5-year duration of worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical assessment demonstrated features of accelerated ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluation identified pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although treatment of her nutritional deficits was initiated, the combination of cytopenias, premature greying of hair, osteoporosis, liver and lung fibrosis prompted evaluation for a hereditary condition. Clinical exome analysis revealed a heterozygous PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg) categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN have previously been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. In this patient, alignment of her clinical features and genetic findings indicated a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs in younger adults who present with unexplained multisystem involvement, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous findings. Early diagnosis is essential to facilitate appropriate genetic counselling, monitoring, and potential consideration for bone marrow transplantation in progressive cases.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes associated with accelerated aging, resulting from Mendelian mutations in genes involved in telomere maintenance, which cause telomeres to be unusually short or nonfunctional. The primary tissues impacted are those containing cells with high mitotic activity, such as bone marrow, epidermis, mucosal tissues, hair follicles, and hepatocytes. TBD encompasses a range of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the archetypal condition. Other related disorders include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of disorders, PARN mutations are associated with a unique clinical presentation. Individuals with two mutated alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with a single mutated allele are predominantly linked to telomere-mediated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a female patient in her late twenties presenting with persistent fatigue and manifestations affecting multiple organ systems, who was eventually found to have a telomere biology disorder.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary facility after experiencing generalised weakness and increasing fatigue over the preceding five years. During this period, her ability to engage in routine activities diminished, and she was less capable than others her age. In the most recent three months, her weakness escalated, significantly restricting self-care and making her dependent on others. She additionally described shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest pain, loss of weight, and decreased appetite.

Upon physical assessment, the patient was found to be haemodynamically stable. Significant observations included pallor, jaundice, premature greying of the hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, bald tongue with atrophy of the lingual papillae.

Evaluation of the musculoskeletal system identified thoracic scoliosis and wasting of the thenar muscles bilaterally. The abdominal assessment detected hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. Respiratory examination demonstrated diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. No abnormalities were detected on cardiovascular examination.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessments indicated pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, presence of Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation, which pointed toward ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum assays demonstrated pronounced deficiencies in vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results indicated increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, findings that aligned with ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Evaluation of the bone marrow displayed trilineage hematopoiesis, prominent erythroid hyperplasia, and augmented iron stores. There were no signs indicative of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative processes. Cytogenetic testing yielded normal results.

Indicators of hemolysis included raised lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) returned positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. In spite of these results, the subdued reticulocyte production and marrow findings primarily supported the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral immune-mediated hemolysis.

To investigate possible gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was undertaken and appeared normal on gross inspection. Histological analysis of a duodenal biopsy indicated mild chronic gastritis with a decreased number of parietal cells, potentially leading to vitamin B12 malabsorption as a result of reduced intrinsic factor synthesis. Nevertheless, the patient’s clinical presentation seemed more severe than would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

The presence of hepatic impairment warranted additional imaging and evaluation. Abdominal ultrasonography detected both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography demonstrated grade II hepatic steatosis, as indicated by a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, along with evidence of cirrhosis based on a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. Elevated inflammatory markers were noted. Testing for autoimmune etiologies was unremarkable, as antinuclear antibodies and markers for autoimmune hepatitis tested negative. Assessment for infections including viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was predominantly negative, with the exception of positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for concealed infiltrative or inflammatory conditions. This imaging revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake throughout the bone marrow in both the axial and appendicular skeleton. Both lungs demonstrated widespread ground-glass opacities accompanied by various fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic alterations. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were identified; however, there were no signs of clear malignancy or granulomatous pathology (figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation revealed secondary hyperparathyroidism, disruptions in electrolytes (notably hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), a deficiency of vitamin D, and osteoporosis as demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, all of which accounted for her frequent episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography together with nerve conduction studies identified an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, accompanied by chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The combination of cytopenia, hepatic impairment, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy raised suspicion for a syndromic or genetic origin.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg in exon 8). This missense mutation affects a highly conserved amino acid and is anticipated to disrupt protein function. Nonetheless, its relationship to the disease could not be confirmed, and it was classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the precise pathogenic nature of this variant has not been established, the association of PARN with disease and the patient’s phenotype aligns with the telomere biology disorder spectrum.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient's clinical findings of pancytopenia, unintended weight loss, early onset greying of hair, and fatigue prompted consideration of hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. A substantially reduced reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, leading to an initial focus on nutritional deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Early testing confirmed pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, which explained several clinical manifestations and was corroborated by bone marrow findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite correcting these deficiencies, the patient's persistent and disproportionate multisystem dysfunction could not be explained.

An immune-mediated mechanism was contemplated for a short period in light of a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and elevated markers of inflammation. However, the lack of evidence for hemolysis, negative results from autoimmune serologic testing, and an absence of other systemic symptoms effectively excluded both autoimmune cytopenias and connective tissue disorders.

There was no evident cause for hepatic fibrosis (HF), as autoimmune and infectious origins had been ruled out. The combination of premature ageing signs, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both the lungs and liver in a young adult prompted consideration of a concealed genetic condition. Even though there was no family history to suggest a hereditary disorder, the potential for a de novo mutation could not be dismissed.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD), known to imitate acquired diseases and demonstrate variable degrees of penetrance. Although uncommon, such disorders may not exhibit the classic mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), but may instead present more inconspicuously with bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs of premature aging.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by detecting a pathogenic single-allele mutation in the PARN gene, which accounted for the diverse clinical manifestations observed in the patient.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg daily for 5 days, then once weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance therapy to address severe deficiency.

Supportive management consisted of whole blood transfusions for symptomatic anemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, and vitamin D at 60,000 IU weekly, along with elemental calcium 1000 mg daily to manage osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed using standard supplementation protocols.

Additionally, the patient participated in physiotherapy, and her family was provided with genetic counseling after the diagnosis of a TBD was made.

Outcome and follow-up:

After deficiencies were corrected and supportive measures provided, the patient experienced relief of symptoms. At the two-week follow-up visit, her hematologic values remained steady, she reported decreased fatigue, and no additional clinical issues were identified.

Ongoing outpatient follow-up is being maintained to monitor her liver and lung function, as well as to assess for any further development of bone marrow or systemic involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA elements located at chromosome termini that function to maintain genomic stability by protecting chromosomes from degradation. During normal cellular replication, telomere sequences progressively diminish until they reach a threshold length, prompting the onset of cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Under typical physiological conditions, telomere length is preserved by the action of telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein enzyme comprised of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—which elongates telomeres. Mutations in genes that code for components of the telomerase machinery compromise its activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and giving rise to a group of inherited disorders collectively termed telomere biology disorders (TBD).

Pathogenic variants in DKC1, inherited in an X-linked recessive manner, were the first to demonstrate the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, establishing DC as the prototypical telomere biology disorder (TBD). DC is traditionally characterized by the mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation of the skin, or by the presence of any two of these manifestations accompanied by bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognised clinical features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), as well as an elevated risk for both hematologic and solid organ malignancies. Several severe TBD subtypes with onset in childhood, such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, are also caused by genetic variants affecting genes associated with DC.

TBDs can appear with isolated mucocutaneous symptoms, organ-specific findings, or haematological abnormalities, and may be detected in adulthood even in the absence of the traditional DC triad. Commonly, the underlying genetic abnormality is concealed by diagnoses such as aplastic anaemia, BMFS, IPF, HF, or various gastrointestinal disorders. When the classical triad is missing and clinical features emerge late, misdiagnosis is common, particularly outside specialised centres. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are usually found in TBDs, with presentations frequently resembling IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of an evolutionarily conserved group of exoribonucleases, functions by trimming the poly(A) tails of mRNA via deadenylation, thereby modulating gene expression. High-throughput sequencing of the 3' end of TERC RNA revealed that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which direct nuclear RNAs towards degradation and are necessary for proper 3'-end processing of TERC.

PARN is ranked within the bottom 20% of genes with respect to tolerance for rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical function and supporting the notion of haploinsufficiency among carriers. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have been identified as causative in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as within the broader clinical spectrum of DC/TBD. Instances of HH syndrome have been linked to biallelic PARN mutations. Monoallelic germline pathogenic alterations are associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was first suggested through the discovery of novel monoallelic variants, and later studies noted monoallelic rare variants of PARN in five out of 262 sporadic IPF patients who underwent whole-exome sequencing, establishing a growing link that now encompasses more than 65 cases cited in the literature.1517 Nonetheless, the comprehensive clinical manifestations linked to PARN mutations are not yet well characterized owing to the limited number of documented cases.

Importantly, this patient possessed a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), impacting a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (spanning residues 178–245). According to four out of five in silico missense prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), the variant is anticipated to have a deleterious effect on protein function. Based on ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation, several supporting criteria are met: PM1—this amino acid change lies in a key, well-recognized functional region (R3H domain) lacking documented benign variation; PM2—population database screening shows the variant is either absent or extremely uncommon; PP3—multiple computational methods forecast detrimental consequences for the gene or its product; PP2—PARN has a low incidence of benign missense changes and missense mutations are frequently implicated in disease within this gene. While the clinical relevance of this specific variant is still undetermined, its potential importance to TBDs is underscored by PARN’s recognized function as well as similarities with other PARN missense changes, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), both listed as 'likely pathogenic' in the ClinVar database.

Multiple genes, varying modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and the phenomenon of somatic genetic rescue may contribute differently to organ involvement in TBDs. Environmental influences and lifestyle choices can also differently affect the replication abilities of various organ systems. In the case of our patient, these factors may help account for the clinical features observed, even though only a single mutated PARN allele was detected.

Monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF rather than primarily to hematological symptoms; however, the finding of cytopenia in this case suggests the possibility of an underlying TBD, potentially predisposing the bone marrow to increased vulnerability under hematopoietic stress, as with severe vitamin B12 deficiency. The process of telomere shortening impairs the self-renewal of hematopoietic stem cells, leading to earlier cellular aging and a heightened risk for BMFS. Although the patient's cytopenia was most likely a result of pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, identifying a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematologic surveillance to detect any progression towards marrow failure in an individual with genetic susceptibility.

Cirrhosis in TBD has frequently been overlooked, but recent data indicate it may occur more often than was previously thought. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry employing transient elastography demonstrated that 88.8% of adult individuals with disease-causing TERC or TERT mutations exhibited at least moderate HF, while 33.3% progressed to cirrhosis. Another similar case reported in the literature detailed a patient who presented with pancytopenia and HF and was found to possess single allelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, the patient described here had only a single allelic mutation in PARN, yet manifested both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF, but their effect on telomerase may be responsible for the HF commonly associated with TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations reinforce the expanding clinical range of PARN-related TBDs, highlighting the need to recognize HF as a component of its phenotype.

The definitive methods for diagnosing presumed TBD are genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing and assessment of telomere length by flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation. Current management focuses on supportive care, addressing symptom burden, determining the need for organ transplantation, and reducing modifiable risk factors; hematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a potential cure for individuals with hematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical research and small-scale clinical trials have indicated that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, the long-term consequences of this approach have not yet been established.

The patient exhibited manifestations consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough assessment ruled out alternative prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the pathogenic gene responsible. A comprehensive review of the literature further expanded our knowledge regarding this uncommon clinical scenario. Supportive care was offered, along with genetic counseling for her relatives. She acknowledged the prognosis and recognized that definitive treatments were unavailable. During her follow-up two weeks later, her hematologic values were unchanged and there was no evidence of new or worsening clinical findings.
