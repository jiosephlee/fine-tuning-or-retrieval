Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year history marked by worsening fatigue and diffuse weakness. On physical examination, she displayed features suggestive of premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlarged liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluation identified pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, and evidence of fibrosis in both hepatic and pulmonary tissues. Although her nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the combination of cytopenias, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, and liver and lung fibrosis prompted suspicion for a genetic etiology. Clinical exome analysis uncovered a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN have been linked with dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The concordance between her clinical presentation and genetic findings supported the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underlines the necessity of considering TBDs in young adults presenting with unexplained, multisystem involvement even if typical mucocutaneous signs are not observed. Prompt identification is essential for informing genetic counseling, surveillance strategies, and for evaluating the need for bone marrow transplantation in cases with advancing disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes characterized by early aging, resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere function and yielding telomeres that are excessively short or ineffective. Cell populations with high proliferative activity—such as those in the bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and liver—are mainly impacted. TBDs manifest through a variety of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the prototypical condition. Other related disorders include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this group of conditions, PARN mutations are associated with a unique clinical presentation: individuals harboring mutations in both alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single-allele mutations are more frequently linked to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a female patient in her late twenties who presented with longstanding fatigue and involvement of multiple organ systems, and who was ultimately diagnosed with a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary centre, citing a 5-year progression of generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. During this period, she experienced a gradual decrease in her functional abilities, limiting her participation in everyday tasks relative to individuals her age. In the preceding three months, her symptoms intensified, leading to significant difficulty with self-care and necessitating help. Additionally, she described shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest pain, unintended weight loss, and diminished appetite.

Upon physical assessment, the patient’s hemodynamic parameters were stable. Significant observations included pallor, jaundice, early greying of scalp hair, increased pigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, bald tongue with loss of lingual papillae.

Musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic scoliosis as well as atrophy of both thenar eminences. The abdominal exam indicated hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and a span of 18 cm. The respiratory evaluation showed diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. No abnormalities were detected on cardiovascular assessment.

Investigations:

The initial lab workup identified pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation, all indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum assays showed marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron parameters demonstrated increased serum iron and ferritin levels, further supporting the presence of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Assessment of the bone marrow showed proliferation across all three cell lines, notable erythroid hyperplasia, and augmented iron accumulation. No signs of malignancy, dysplastic features, or infiltrative pathology were detected, and cytogenetic analysis returned within normal limits.

Laboratory indicators of hemolysis included heightened lactate dehydrogenase and unconjugated bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test produced a negative result. Nonetheless, given the diminished reticulocyte production and marrow findings, ineffective erythropoiesis appeared more likely than peripheral immune-mediated cell destruction.

In order to assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted, demonstrating a normal appearance on gross examination. Biopsy samples taken from the duodenum revealed mild chronic gastritis with diminished parietal cells, which may have contributed to vitamin B12 malabsorption by limiting intrinsic factor production. Nonetheless, the extent of clinical manifestations seemed excessive in relation to nutritional insufficiency as the sole cause.

Because of abnormal hepatic function, additional imaging and evaluation were undertaken. An abdominal ultrasound identified enlargement of both liver and spleen. Transient elastography demonstrated grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) along with findings consistent with cirrhosis (liver stiffness measured at 18.1 kPa). There was elevation of inflammatory markers. Serologic tests for autoimmunity, including antinuclear antibodies and markers for autoimmune hepatitis, were found to be negative. The infectious disease panel for viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus yielded predominantly normal results, except for the detection of Epstein-Barr virus IgG (refer to table 2).

To investigate possible hidden infiltrative or inflammatory processes, positron emission tomography–CT was conducted. The scan revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose absorption throughout the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Both lungs demonstrated widespread ground-glass opacity accompanied by several fibrotic alterations and subpleural atelectasis. Hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were also present, yet there were no signs of obvious malignancy or granulomatous pathology (figures 1 and 2).

Further assessment uncovered secondary hyperparathyroidism, disruptions in electrolytes including hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, as well as osteoporosis, which was established by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These findings accounted for her persistent bone pain and frequent fractures (see table 3). Electromyography along with nerve conduction studies demonstrated asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy accompanied by chronic neurogenic alterations involving both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Considering the aggregate of clinical manifestations—cytopenia, hepatic impairment, early onset greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic origin was considered likely.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This missense change affects a residue that is highly conserved and is anticipated to negatively impact protein functionality. Nonetheless, the contribution of this variant to the disease could not be determined; thus, it was classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the exact pathogenicity of this variant is still unclear, the association of PARN and the observed clinical characteristics in the patient correspond to those seen in the TBD spectrum.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, weight loss, early greying, and fatigue prompted an evaluation for hematological, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. A profoundly decreased reticulocyte count indicated insufficient red blood cell production, suggesting nutrient deficits and bone marrow failure disorders as possible causes.

Early testing confirmed a significant vitamin B12 deficiency, which explained multiple clinical findings and was corroborated by bone marrow evidence of defective erythropoiesis. Despite repletion of nutritional deficits, the broad dysfunction persisted and remained out of proportion to the corrected abnormalities.

Given a positive DAT and elevated inflammatory markers, an immune-mediated mechanism was considered. However, the lack of hemolysis, negative results on autoimmune serologies, and absence of other systemic manifestations argued against autoimmune cytopenias or connective tissue disease.

The origin of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was indeterminate, as both autoimmune and infectious etiologies had been ruled out. The unusual combination of premature ageing symptoms, sustained cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young adult prompted consideration of an obscure genetic condition. Despite the lack of relevant family history which appeared to contradict a hereditary syndrome, the potential for a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of imitating acquired disorders and displaying variable penetrance. While uncommon, such conditions may not exhibit the classic mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), instead presenting more insidiously with bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs of premature aging.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous variant in the PARN gene, thereby offering a cohesive explanation for her diverse clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg once daily for 5 days, followed by weekly administrations over 4 weeks, and subsequently transitioned to monthly maintenance therapy to address the severe deficiency.

Additional supportive therapies consisted of whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, and vitamin D at 60,000 IU weekly together with elemental calcium 1000 mg per day to treat osteoporosis. Standard supplementation was used to correct electrolyte disturbances.

Physiotherapy was initiated, and genetic counselling was provided to her family after a diagnosis of a TBD was established.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive management, the patient experienced alleviation of symptoms. At her 2-week follow-up appointment, her hematologic parameters remained unchanged, her fatigue lessened, and she did not present with any additional clinical issues.

She is maintained on routine outpatient monitoring to assess liver and lung function, as well as to observe for any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres consist of repetitive DNA motifs at the termini of chromosomes, functioning to maintain genomic stability by protecting chromosome ends from degradation. Each round of cellular replication leads to progressive telomere shortening, ultimately reaching a threshold that induces cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein that comprises telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts telomere erosion by lengthening telomeres. Mutations in genes coding for telomerase complex components disrupt its activity, causing early telomere shortening and giving rise to a group of hereditary disorders referred to as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 inherited in an X-linked recessive manner initially demonstrated the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, establishing DC as the prototypical telomere biology disorder (TBD). DC is traditionally characterized by the presence of a mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or by the occurrence of two of these signs along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional manifestations include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and an elevated risk for both hematological and solid tumors. Severe forms of childhood-onset TBDs—such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—also arise from mutations in genes associated with DC.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can manifest as isolated mucocutaneous findings, organ-specific symptoms, or hematologic abnormalities, and may appear in adulthood lacking the traditional dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad. Often, the inherited genetic abnormality is concealed by presentations such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic failure (HF), as well as gastrointestinal involvement. In the absence of the classic triad and with delayed onset of symptoms, these cases are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly outside specialized medical centers. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most frequently encountered in TBDs, where they commonly present as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is part of a conserved exoribonuclease family and is responsible for trimming the poly(A) tail of mRNA via deadenylation, thereby influencing gene expression. Extensive sequencing of the TERC RNA 3’ terminal has demonstrated that PARN is necessary to eliminate oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation and are essential for proper 3’-end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks among the top 20% of genes least tolerant to infrequent, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical biological function and supporting the hypothesis of haploinsufficiency in heterozygous individuals. Germline pathogenic variants in PARN are recognized as etiologic factors in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as in the wider DC/TBD phenotypic spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been documented with biallelic PARN alterations. Monoallelic germline pathogenic changes in PARN have also been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The first connection between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) arose following the detection of novel monoallelic mutations, and additional research identified five monoallelic rare PARN variants among 262 sporadic IPF patients evaluated by whole-exome sequencing, establishing a preliminary correlation which has since been observed in over 65 cases in published reports.1517 Nevertheless, the full clinical spectrum linked to PARN mutations remains unclear, as only a limited number of cases have been described to date.

Of particular interest, this individual carried a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), located at a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (positions 178–245). Four out of five computational tools for missense variant analysis (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) predict this change to have a detrimental impact on protein function. According to the ACMG/AMP guidelines, the variant fulfills several supportive evidence categories: PM1—this amino acid substitution occurs in a crucial and well-established functional region (R3H domain) without known benign changes; PM2—the alteration is either absent or exceedingly rare in population databases; PP3—numerous predictive algorithms indicate it is damaging to the gene or its protein product; PP2—benign missense changes are infrequent in PARN, and pathogenic missense variants are a recognized disease-causing mechanism. While the clinical importance of this variant has not been definitively established, its possible association with TBDs is highlighted by the gene’s known function and by similarities to other PARN missense variants, including p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), both listed as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar repository.

A range of genes, differing modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue may uniquely impact various organ systems involved in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Moreover, external environmental conditions and individual lifestyle choices may influence the ability of different organs to replicate. These factors could help account for the patient’s clinical presentation, even though only a single PARN allele variant was detected.

Monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than primary hematological abnormalities; however, our patient’s cytopenia suggests a potential underlying TBD that could make bone marrow more vulnerable to hematopoietic stressors such as significant vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres impair the proliferative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, promoting premature cell aging and an increased probability of bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the cytopenia in this patient was most likely caused by marked vitamin B12 deficiency, the identification of a PARN variant emphasizes the necessity for continued clinical and hematological evaluation to monitor for any emerging marrow failure phenotype in a genetically susceptible individual.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has frequently gone unrecognized, yet recent data indicate it may occur more often than was previously believed. A retrospective study conducted by the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, utilizing transient elastography, showed that 88.8% of adults carrying pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), while 33.3% met criteria for cirrhosis. Similarly, a case published elsewhere reported a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who had monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our case involved a patient with only a monoallelic PARN mutation who nonetheless developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN variants are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their downstream effects on telomerase activity might explain the hepatic fibrosis more commonly seen in the context of TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations highlight a broadening clinical phenotype for PARN-related TBDs and emphasize the need to recognize hepatic fibrosis as part of its manifestation.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis utilizing next-generation sequencing as well as telomere length assessment via flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation techniques. Management predominantly focuses on supportive measures, addressing symptom burden, organ transplantation as indicated, and minimization of risk factors; hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option for individuals with hematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical and early clinical data indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, their long-term impact has yet to be determined.

The patient exhibited signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. A thorough assessment ruled out typical etiologies, after which whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible gene. A comprehensive review of the literature helped elucidate this uncommon manifestation. Supportive care was administered, and genetic counseling was extended to her relatives. The patient was informed about her diagnosis and recognized that curative treatments were unavailable. At the 2-week follow-up appointment, her hematologic values were unchanged and there were no indications of worsening clinical status.
