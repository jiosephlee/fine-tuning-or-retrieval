Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

A female patient in her late twenties presented with a five-year history of increasing fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical assessment revealed features of early aging, anemia, neuropathy, as well as enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory and imaging investigations demonstrated pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Correction of her nutritional deficiencies did not resolve the underlying constellation of cytopenias, premature greying of the hair, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs, prompting consideration of an underlying genetic condition. Clinical exome analysis detected a monoallelic PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have previously been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The correlation between the patient's clinical presentation and genetic findings supported the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to contemplate TBDs in younger adults presenting with unexplained multisystem involvement, even when classical mucocutaneous manifestations are missing. Early identification is vital for informing genetic counseling, surveillance strategies, and consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in progressive cases.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated ageing, resulting from inherited mutations in genes responsible for telomere maintenance that cause telomeres to be unusually short or impaired. This primarily impacts cell populations with high turnover rates, such as bone marrow, skin, mucosal tissues, hair follicles, and liver cells. TBDs comprise a spectrum of clinical manifestations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the archetypal condition. Additional forms include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this class of conditions, mutations in PARN are associated with a unique clinical presentation; biallelic PARN variants result in autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single-allele mutations are more commonly linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report the case of a female patient in her late twenties who presented with persistent fatigue and multi-organ symptoms, and was eventually found to have a telomere biology disorder.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary centre after experiencing generalized weakness and early fatigue that had gradually worsened over five years. This gradual decline led to a significant reduction in her ability to carry out routine tasks compared to others her age. In the past three months, her weakness further deteriorated, to the extent that she was unable to manage self-care independently and needed help. Additionally, she complained of shortness of breath with exertion, episodic chest discomfort, unintended weight loss, and loss of appetite.

Upon evaluation, her hemodynamic status was stable. Significant examination findings comprised pallor, jaundice, early greying of the hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, depapillated tongue.

Assessment of the musculoskeletal system identified thoracic scoliosis and wasting of the thenar muscles bilaterally. Abdominal assessment revealed an enlarged liver, palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin with a total span of 18 cm. The respiratory exam detected diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. Cardiovascular assessment did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessments indicated pancytopenia accompanied by a reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). The peripheral blood smear showed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, findings which are indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum assays revealed significant deficiencies in vitamin B12 and folate. Evaluation of iron parameters showed increased serum iron and ferritin, further supporting a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow analysis demonstrated haematopoiesis involving all three cell lines, with marked erythroid hyperplasia and augmented iron reserves; there was no indication of malignant transformation, dysplasia, or infiltrative pathology. Cytogenetic studies yielded normal results.

Indicators of haemolysis included raised lactate dehydrogenase levels and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. Direct antiglobulin test (DAT) results were positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect test was negative. Nevertheless, the suppressed reticulocyte response and bone marrow characteristics were more consistent with a disorder of ineffective erythropoiesis than with peripheral immune-mediated destruction.

To investigate potential gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was carried out and showed no abnormal gross findings. Biopsy from the duodenum demonstrated mild chronic gastritis with a reduction in parietal cell count, which could result in impaired intrinsic factor secretion and subsequent B12 malabsorption. Despite this, the degree of clinical manifestations seemed excessive when compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

Given evidence of hepatic impairment, further imaging was pursued. Abdominal ultrasonography identified hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography revealed grade II hepatic steatosis, with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, as well as cirrhosis, indicated by a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. There was an elevation in inflammatory markers. Nonetheless, screening for autoimmune disorders (including antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies) yielded negative results. Testing for infections such as viral hepatitis, HIV, cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was also negative, except for a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was conducted to investigate possible hidden infiltrative or inflammatory conditions. The scan revealed irregular ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Both lungs displayed widespread ground-glass opacity, accompanied by numerous fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectasis. Additional findings included hepatosplenomegaly and ascites. However, no definitive signs of malignancy or granulomatous disease were observed (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results comprised secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes including hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, deficiency in vitamin D, and osteoporosis as demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry; these factors accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyographic and nerve conduction testing identified an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The combination of clinical features—cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early hair greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—raised suspicion for a syndromic or genetic origin.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This variant affects a residue that is highly conserved and is predicted to have a detrimental effect on protein function. Nonetheless, the clinical relevance of this mutation remains undetermined; as such, it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite uncertainty regarding the pathogenicity of this particular variant, both the presence of PARN involvement and the patient's clinical presentation align with features characteristic of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, premature greying of hair, and fatigue prompted evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. A significantly reduced reticulocyte count indicated ineffective erythropoiesis and initially shifted focus toward possible nutritional deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Early diagnostic work-up confirmed pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, which explained many clinical findings; this was corroborated by bone marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Despite correcting the nutrient deficiency, the widespread systemic abnormalities persisted and remained disproportionate to what would be expected.

Although an immune-mediated mechanism was considered briefly due to a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and elevated inflammatory markers, the lack of hemolysis, negative results on autoimmune serologies, and absence of other systemic symptoms made autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disorders unlikely.

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) was of unclear origin, as both infectious and autoimmune etiologies had been ruled out. The distinctive combination of early-onset ageing characteristics, sustained cytopenias, and fibrotic changes affecting both the lungs and liver in a young adult prompted consideration of a concealed genetic disease. While the lack of family history seemed to disfavor a hereditary disorder, the potential for a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted consideration of a TBD that can imitate acquired disorders and demonstrate variable penetrance. Although uncommon, it may not display the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in DC, and can present more insidiously with bone marrow failure, fibrotic changes in organs, and signs of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion, revealing a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, which accounted for the entirety of her clinical presentation.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for five days, transitioned to weekly administration for four weeks, and subsequently maintained with monthly dosing to address her profound deficiency.

Adjunctive therapies comprised whole blood transfusions due to symptomatic anemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, and vitamin D at 60 000 IU weekly along with elemental calcium at 1000 mg daily for management of osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed by conventional supplementation.

Additionally, she participated in physiotherapy, and her family was provided with genetic counselling after she was diagnosed with a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient’s symptoms improved after addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive treatment. At the 2-week follow-up appointment, her blood counts remained within stable ranges, her fatigue had lessened, and there were no additional clinical issues identified.

She is ongoing with routine outpatient monitoring to assess liver and lung function as well as to observe for any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repeated DNA sequences located at chromosome termini that protect chromosomes against degradation, thereby maintaining genome stability. During normal cellular replication, telomeres progressively decrease in length until a threshold is reached, prompting cell senescence or apoptosis. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein comprised of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—acts to extend telomeres and uphold their length. Nevertheless, pathogenic mutations in the genes responsible for telomerase complex constituents disrupt enzyme activity, cause accelerated telomere shortening, and result in an array of inherited disorders collectively referred to as TBD.

Pathogenic variants of DKC1 inherited in an X-linked recessive manner initially established the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, positioning DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). DC is traditionally characterized by a mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, dystrophic nails, and reticulated skin pigmentation, or the presence of two of these symptoms accompanied by bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and elevated susceptibility to both hematologic and solid cancers. Severe TBD subtypes presenting in childhood, such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, also arise from mutations in genes involved in DC.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can manifest as isolated involvement of the mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or haematological abnormalities, and may appear in adulthood without the characteristic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad. Often, the underlying genetic abnormality is concealed by presentations such as aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), or gastrointestinal disorders. The absence of the classical triad and delayed onset of symptoms contribute to frequent misdiagnosis, particularly in patients who do not receive care at specialized facilities. Typically, monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are detected in TBDs and are commonly associated with IPF or BMFS.

PARN, a member of a well-conserved exoribonuclease gene family, functions by reducing the length of mRNA poly(A) tails via deadenylation, thereby controlling gene expression. Comprehensive sequencing of the TERC RNA 3’ terminus revealed that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails acquired after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation and are necessary for proper 3’-end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks within the least 20% of genes for tolerance to uncommon, disruptive mutations, indicating its crucial biological function and lending support to the haploinsufficiency hypothesis in individuals carrying these variants. Germline pathogenic alterations in PARN are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader category of DC/TBD. Cases of HH syndrome have been associated with biallelic variants in PARN. Monoallelic germline pathogenic variants have also been connected to telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was first proposed following the discovery of new monoallelic variants, and follow-up investigations detected monoallelic rare PARN variants in five out of 262 sporadic IPF patients assessed via whole-exome sequencing, thereby indicating a developing link that has now been noted in more than 65 reported instances in published literature.1517 Nevertheless, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations linked to PARN mutations remains unclear, largely because the number of documented cases is still limited.

Of particular significance, this patient possessed a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), altering a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (positions 178–245). According to four out of five computational missense prediction models (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), this variant is anticipated to be functionally harmful. Based on ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation, the following criteria apply: PM1—the amino acid change occurs in a vital and extensively characterized functional region (R3H domain) without documented benign variants. PM2—the alteration is either absent or extremely infrequent in population data sets. PP3—several predictive algorithms indicate a detrimental effect on gene function or product. PP2—PARN demonstrates a scarcity of benign missense alterations and disease-causing missense variants are frequently described. Although the clinical impact remains uncertain, its potential relevance to TBDs is underscored by the known role of PARN and comparisons with other missense changes such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), both classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

The manifestation of TBDs can be influenced by numerous genes, differing modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue, with these factors variably impacting multiple organ systems. Furthermore, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices may affect the proliferative abilities of individual organs to differing extents. These intricate interactions may account for our patient's clinical presentation, notwithstanding the detection of only a monoallelic PARN variant.

Monoallelic PARN mutations tend to be linked with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) more frequently than primary hematologic complications; nonetheless, the presence of cytopenia in this patient suggests the possibility of an underlying TBD, potentially rendering the bone marrow more vulnerable to hematopoietic stressors such as marked vitamin B12 deficiency. Reduced telomere length diminishes the replicative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in accelerated cellular aging and elevated risk for bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the cytopenia in this case was most likely due to the significant vitamin B12 deficiency, the identification of the PARN variant highlights the necessity for ongoing clinical and hematological surveillance to detect any emerging marrow failure manifestations in a genetically at-risk individual.

Cirrhosis in TBD has historically been overlooked, but recent studies indicate it may occur more frequently than previously thought. A retrospective review conducted by the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilized transient elastography and revealed that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate HF, while 33.3% were diagnosed with cirrhosis. Another published case described a patient who presented with pancytopenia and HF, and possessed monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our case involved a patient carrying a single monoallelic PARN mutation, yet developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Though monoallelic PARN mutations are usually linked to IPF, their effect on telomerase activity could explain the HF more commonly associated with TERT or TERC alterations. The observations contribute to the evolving clinical spectrum of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the need to recognize HF as a feature within this phenotype.

Definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing and assessment of telomere length via flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays. Management is largely supportive, addressing the severity of symptoms, the requirement for organ transplantation, and modifying risk factors; however, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option for individuals presenting with haematologic involvement. Although androgen therapies such as danazol and nandrolone have demonstrated telomere extension in preclinical models and limited clinical studies, the durability and long-term impact of these treatments have yet to be established.

The patient exhibited symptoms indicative of TBD, HF, and IPF. A comprehensive workup eliminated more typical etiologies, and whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the pathogenic gene. Further review of existing literature broadened insight into this unusual case. Supportive care was initiated, and her family received genetic counseling. The patient comprehended her diagnosis and recognized that curative treatments were unavailable. At a follow-up visit two weeks later, her hematological parameters were unchanged, and no additional signs of worsening were observed.
