Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported a five-year progression of persistent fatigue and diffuse muscle weakness. Physical examination uncovered manifestations suggestive of accelerated aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory findings included pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Correction of nutritional deficiencies did not resolve the combination of symptoms—namely, cytopenia, premature greying of hair, osteoporosis, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis—which pointed toward an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing uncovered a heterozygous PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), designated as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN are linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and to telomere-related disorders involving pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (subtype 4). The genotype–phenotype association in this instance indicated a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report emphasizes the necessity of suspecting TBDs in young individuals exhibiting unexplained multisystem involvement, even when classic mucocutaneous hallmarks are lacking. Early identification plays a pivotal role in enabling appropriate genetic counseling, clinical monitoring, and evaluation of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in progressive presentations.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a group of uncommon premature ageing conditions resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, ultimately giving rise to telomeres that are unusually short or dysfunctional. These disorders primarily impact tissues characterized by high cellular turnover, such as the bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and hepatocytes. TBDs encompass a wide spectrum of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the archetypal example. Additional recognized disorders under the TBD umbrella include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of disorders, PARN mutations exhibit a unique clinical presentation: individuals with biallelic variants present with autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those harboring monoallelic variants are more commonly associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a woman in her late 20s who experienced persistent fatigue alongside symptoms affecting multiple organ systems, ultimately receiving a diagnosis of a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care facility, reporting a five-year progression of generalized weakness and increased susceptibility to fatigue. During this period, her capacity to engage in routine activities steadily diminished in comparison to others her age. In the most recent three months, her symptoms had intensified, limiting her ability to manage personal care and necessitating help from others. She additionally experienced shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest discomfort, unintended weight loss, and a diminished appetite.

On physical assessment, her haemodynamic parameters were within normal limits. Significant observations comprised pallor, icterus, early onset of hair greying, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a depapillated (bald) tongue demonstrating lingual papillae atrophy.

Evaluation of the musculoskeletal system identified thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of the thenar muscles on both sides. The abdominal exam revealed hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. Pulmonary assessment indicated decreased breath sounds in the bilateral infra-scapular areas. Cardiovascular evaluation did not reveal any abnormal findings.

Investigations:

The initial set of laboratory investigations indicated pancytopenia accompanied by a markedly decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, the presence of Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—findings that collectively point to impaired erythropoiesis. Biochemical assays confirmed profound deficiencies of both vitamin B12 and folate. Assessment of iron metabolism demonstrated elevated levels of both serum iron and ferritin, further supporting the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (table 1).

Analysis of bone marrow aspirate showed active trilineage haematopoiesis with prominent erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron reserves, with no indication of neoplastic, dysplastic, or infiltrative processes; cytogenetic evaluation returned normal results.

Laboratory indicators of haemolysis included raised lactate dehydrogenase and increased indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG at a strength of 2+, while the indirect antiglobulin assay remained negative. Nevertheless, the observed low reticulocyte production alongside the bone marrow findings was more suggestive of ineffective erythropoiesis, as opposed to destruction due to peripheral immune mechanisms.

In order to investigate the possibility of gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted and found to be grossly unremarkable. Histopathological examination of the duodenal mucosa demonstrated mild chronic gastritis accompanied by a reduction in parietal cell number, which may have resulted in decreased intrinsic factor synthesis and subsequently contributed to B12 deficiency. Nevertheless, the clinical manifestations appeared to exceed what would be anticipated from isolated nutritional deficits.

Given the evidence of hepatic dysfunction, further diagnostic imaging and evaluation were undertaken. Abdominal ultrasonography identified both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis, as shown by a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, alongside findings consistent with cirrhosis, with a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. Levels of inflammatory markers were elevated. Despite this, results from autoimmune screening—including assays for antinuclear antibodies and markers of autoimmune hepatitis—were negative. Infectious disease investigations for hepatitis viruses, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus yielded negative results except for the detection of Epstein-Barr virus IgG positivity (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory diseases. The scan revealed irregular ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation throughout the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Both lungs exhibited widespread ground-glass opacity accompanied by multiple fibrotic alterations and subpleural atelectasis. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were present, but there were no signs indicating clear malignancy or granulomatous processes (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results comprised secondary hyperparathyroidism, imbalances in electrolytes (notably hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), deficiency in vitamin D, as well as osteoporosis, which was verified through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry; these factors accounted for her recurrent episodes of bone pain and multiple fractures (see table 3). Electromyographic analysis and nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, with evidence of chronic neurogenic alterations involving both proximal and distal muscles.

The aggregation of clinical features—including cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—raised suspicion for a syndromic or genetic origin.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This alteration affects an amino acid residue that is highly conserved across species and is anticipated to negatively impact protein function. Nevertheless, its contribution to the disease phenotype could not be definitively determined, leading to its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite the ambiguous pathogenic nature of this particular variant, both PARN involvement and the clinical presentation of the patient align with characteristics observed in the TBD spectrum.

Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, premature greying, and persistent fatigue initially prompted evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The pronounced reduction in reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, leading clinicians to prioritize diagnoses such as nutritional deficiencies and various marrow failure syndromes.

Laboratory findings soon established severe vitamin B12 deficiency, which explained several clinical manifestations and was further corroborated by bone marrow findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite correction of these deficiencies, the patient's broad systemic impairment remained unusually severe and persisted over time.

Given a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and elevated inflammatory markers, an immune-mediated mechanism was considered; however, this possibility was deemphasized due to a lack of evidence for haemolysis, negative results from autoimmune serological testing, and an absence of characteristic systemic symptoms, making autoimmune cytopenia and connective tissue disorders less likely.

The origin of hepatic fibrosis (HF) remained undefined, as autoimmune and infectious etiologies had been ruled out. The coexistence of early-onset ageing characteristics, ongoing cytopenias, and fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young individual raised the possibility of an obscure inherited condition. While the lack of familial precedent seemed to lessen the likelihood of a genetic syndrome, the occurrence of a de novo mutation could not be discounted.

This prompted the consideration of a TBD capable of imitating acquired disorders and exhibiting diverse penetrance. While uncommon, such a presentation may not display the traditional mucocutaneous triad seen in DC, and may instead emerge more inconspicuously with bone marrow insufficiency, organ fibrosis, and signs of premature ageing.

Genetic testing reinforced this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing monoallelic mutation in the PARN gene, offering a cohesive account for the observed spectrum of her clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg each day for 5 days, subsequently once per week over a 4-week period, and thereafter continued on monthly doses to address the marked deficiency.

Additional management comprised whole blood transfusions to treat her symptomatic anaemia, along with daily oral folic acid (1 mg) and weekly vitamin D (60 000 IU), accompanied by elemental calcium supplementation (1000 mg per day) for the management of osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed through conventional supplementation protocols.

Physiotherapy was initiated, and the patient’s family was provided with genetic counselling after the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing the identified deficiencies and providing supportive management, the patient experienced alleviation of her symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up appointment, her hematologic parameters remained within normal limits, her fatigue had diminished, and no additional clinical issues were identified.

She remains under routine outpatient observation to assess hepatic and pulmonary status, as well as to track any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres consist of repetitive nucleotide sequences that protect the termini of chromosomes, thereby maintaining genome stability and preventing chromosomal deterioration. During successive rounds of cell replication, these telomeric sequences gradually erode, and upon reaching a minimal threshold, initiate cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomere length homeostasis is achieved by the action of telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein enzyme made up of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which extends telomeric DNA. Nonetheless, deleterious mutations in the genes encoding telomerase subunits compromise telomerase activity, resulting in accelerated telomere shortening and ultimately causing a spectrum of heritable disorders collectively classified as TBD.

The identification of X-linked recessive pathogenic mutations in DKC1 was pivotal in demonstrating the association between DC and significantly shortened telomeres, positioning DC as the quintessential telomere biology disorder (TBD). Traditionally, DC is characterized by the mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, dystrophic nails, and reticular hyperpigmentation of the skin; alternatively, a diagnosis may be considered when two of these clinical features present alongside bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional clinical manifestations encompass idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and an elevated susceptibility to both haematological and solid tumors. Notably, the more severe, early-onset TBD subtypes—including HH syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—also arise due to mutations in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can manifest through isolated mucocutaneous symptoms, specific organ involvement, or haematological abnormalities, and onset may occur in adulthood without the characteristic DC triad. In many cases, the underlying genetic defect is obscured by overlapping disorders such as aplastic anaemia, BMFS, IPF, HF, or gastrointestinal conditions. Owing to the lack of the classic triad and delayed appearance of symptoms, misdiagnosis is common, particularly outside of expert centres. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most commonly observed in TBDs, where presentations often include IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of a widely conserved exoribonuclease family, functions by catalyzing deadenylation, which reduces the poly(A) tail length on mRNA and modulates gene expression. High-throughput sequencing of the TERC RNA 3-prime terminus demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation; this step is crucial for proper 3-prime-end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks within the top 20% of genes showing the greatest intolerance to rare, loss-of-function variants, underscoring its critical biological significance and supporting the notion of haploinsufficiency among heterozygous carriers. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN have been identified as causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the wider DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been associated with biallelic variants in PARN, while monoallelic pathogenic germline mutations have been linked to telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was first recognized following the discovery of new monoallelic mutations, with subsequent analyses revealing rare monoallelic PARN variants in five of 262 sporadic IPF patients who underwent whole-exome sequencing. This link has since been reinforced, as over 65 cases have now been described in the published literature.1517 Nonetheless, a comprehensive characterization of the phenotypic manifestations arising from PARN mutations remains elusive, primarily due to the relatively small cohort of documented cases.

Of particular note, our patient carried a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which disrupts a highly conserved amino acid located within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). Four out of five in silico prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) suggest that this variant is likely to impair protein function. According to the ACMG/AMP guidelines for interpreting sequence variants, the c.613T>C change satisfies several supporting criteria: PM1—this alteration lies within a functionally significant and well-characterized domain (the R3H domain) that does not tolerate benign variation; PM2—it is either not present or extremely infrequent in large-scale population datasets; PP3—numerous computational models indicate a detrimental effect on the gene or its product; and PP2—the gene PARN has a low incidence of benign missense variation, with pathogenic missense mutations commonly implicated in disease. Although the precise clinical impact of this variant remains unclear, its possible association with TBDs is strengthened by the known biological role of PARN and by reported pathogenic missense variants in the same gene—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which have been designated as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

A range of factors, including the involvement of multiple genes, varying patterns of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and the occurrence of somatic genetic rescue, can uniquely influence different organs in the setting of TBDs. Moreover, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices can also contribute to differences in the replicative abilities of various organ systems. For our patient, such intricate mechanisms could potentially account for the clinical features observed, even though only a monoallelic PARN variant was detected.

While heterozygous PARN mutations are predominantly linked to IPF rather than to primary hematologic disorders, our patient’s cytopenia raises suspicion of an underlying TBD, possibly predisposing the bone marrow to hematopoietic stress, as exemplified by severe vitamin B12 deficiency. The reduction in telomere length diminishes the proliferative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, which may result in premature senescence and a heightened likelihood of developing BMFS. Although it is most probable that our patient’s cytopenia resulted from profound vitamin B12 deficiency, the detection of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematological surveillance, given the genetic predisposition to potential progression towards marrow failure.

Cirrhosis has historically been underdiagnosed in TBD; nonetheless, recent studies indicate it may occur more frequently than once assumed. Data from a retrospective review of the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, utilizing transient elastography, revealed that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic mutations in either TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate HF, with cirrhosis present in 33.3% of these cases. Additionally, the literature documents a similar case involving a patient affected by pancytopenia and HF, carrying heterozygous variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Conversely, in our case, the patient possessed only a monoallelic PARN variant but developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic mutations in PARN are most often linked to IPF, the resulting impairment of telomerase function may contribute to HF in a fashion akin to what is seen with TERC or TERT mutations. Collectively, these observations highlight the broadening clinical presentation associated with PARN-related TBDs, emphasizing the necessity of recognizing HF as a component of their manifestation.

The definitive method for diagnosing suspected TBD involves genetic analyses using next-generation sequencing along with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays for telomere length assessment. Management is primarily supportive, customized according to symptom intensity, necessity for organ transplantation, and mitigation of risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option in individuals exhibiting haematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical and early clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may extend telomere length, the durability and long-term outcomes of these interventions have yet to be established.

The patient exhibited clinical signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough assessment ruled out more frequent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the underlying genetic defect. A comprehensive review of the existing literature further clarified the rarity of this manifestation. Supportive therapy was implemented, and her relatives received genetic counselling. The patient demonstrated understanding of her diagnosis and the lack of curative interventions. At the 2-week follow-up appointment, her hematologic parameters were unchanged and no additional clinical decline was observed.
