Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported a five-year progression of fatigue and diffuse weakness. Physical assessment demonstrated features of premature aging, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory testing revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were treated, the combination of cytopenia, early-onset greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs suggested an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome analysis detected a heterozygous missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Inherited pathogenic variants in PARN have previously been associated with dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure type 4. The clinical and molecular findings in this patient were consistent with a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underscores the necessity to consider TBDs in young adults presenting with unexplained, multisystem involvement, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous symptoms. Prompt diagnosis is essential for appropriate genetic counseling, ongoing monitoring, and early evaluation for bone marrow transplantation in patients with progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon premature aging syndromes that arise from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere function, resulting in telomeres that are unusually short or impaired. These disorders predominantly impact cells that undergo frequent division, including those found in the bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver. TBDs encompass a variety of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the principal example. Additional conditions within this spectrum include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of diseases, mutations in the PARN gene are associated with a unique clinical presentation; individuals with two mutated alleles exhibit autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with a single mutated allele are more frequently found to have telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a female patient, aged in her late 20s, presenting with sustained fatigue and multiple organ system involvement, who was eventually diagnosed with a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care facility, reporting a 5-year course of gradually intensifying generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. During this time, her physical abilities deteriorated, limiting her participation in daily tasks relative to individuals of similar age. In the most recent 3 months, her symptoms escalated, severely impacting her independence with personal care and necessitating help. Additionally, she described experiencing shortness of breath with exertion, occasional chest pain, unintended weight loss, and reduced appetite.

Upon physical assessment, her haemodynamic parameters were within normal limits. Significant observations consisted of pallor, jaundice, early greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, bald tongue lacking lingual papillae.

Examination of the musculoskeletal system demonstrated thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of the thenar muscles bilaterally. The abdominal evaluation identified an enlarged liver extending 5 cm below the right costal margin, with a hepatic span of 18 cm. On respiratory examination, breath sounds were diminished in the infra-scapular regions bilaterally. The cardiovascular assessment did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment revealed pancytopenia accompanied by a reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, indicating ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum assays demonstrated pronounced deficiencies of vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results showed increased serum iron and ferritin, supporting a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow evaluation demonstrated trilineage haematopoiesis, erythroid hyperplasia, and augmented iron reserves, with no signs of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative processes; cytogenetic studies yielded normal findings.

Hemolysis markers included raised levels of lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) returned positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect test was negative. Nonetheless, both the diminished reticulocyte production and marrow findings favored ineffective erythropoiesis rather than immune-mediated destruction in the peripheral circulation.

To investigate possible gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was conducted, which revealed no gross abnormalities. Histological examination of duodenal biopsies demonstrated mild chronic gastritis with a decrease in parietal cell population, which may have led to impaired intrinsic factor secretion and consequent B12 deficiency. Nevertheless, the degree of the patient’s clinical presentation seemed excessive for nutritional deficiency as the sole explanation.

Given the presence of hepatic dysfunction, further imaging was pursued. Abdominal ultrasonography showed enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography identified grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) along with features of cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Elevated inflammatory markers were detected. Yet, autoimmune panels—including antinuclear antibodies and those for autoimmune hepatitis—were negative. Additional infectious disease screening for viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus yielded no significant findings aside from positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was conducted to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease. The scan revealed uneven uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose throughout the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Both lungs exhibited widespread ground-glass opacity along with numerous fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic alterations. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were observed, yet there was no indication of definite malignancy or granulomatous pathology (figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation revealed secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis which was verified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These findings accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyographic and nerve conduction studies indicated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, with evidence of chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Given the combination of cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy, a syndromic or genetic origin was considered as a possible underlying cause.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single allele missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This missense change affects a highly conserved amino acid and is anticipated to negatively impact protein function. Nonetheless, its contribution to the disease has not been established, leading to its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite unclear pathogenicity for this particular variant, both PARN involvement and the patient’s clinical presentation align with the spectrum associated with TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, premature hair greying, and persistent fatigue, prompting evaluation for hematological, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. A significantly reduced reticulocyte count suggested inadequate erythropoiesis, which led clinicians to initially suspect nutrient deficiencies and possible marrow failure syndromes.

Early testing established a pronounced deficiency in vitamin B12, which explained several clinical manifestations, corroborated by bone marrow findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite correction of the vitamin deficiencies, the patient’s broad systemic symptoms continued, remaining out of proportion and unresolved.

A transient consideration was given to immune-mediated mechanisms in light of a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and raised inflammatory markers. However, the lack of hemolysis, negative results from autoimmune panels, and the absence of other systemic signs weighed against the likelihood of autoimmune cytopenia or underlying connective tissue disease.

The origin of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was uncertain, as both autoimmune and infectious etiologies had been ruled out. The unusual presentation of premature aging signs, sustained cytopenia, along with fibrosis affecting both the liver and lungs in a young adult, prompted consideration of an obscure genetic disorder. While no familial background supported a hereditary syndrome, the potential for a de novo mutation could not be dismissed.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder that may resemble acquired disorders and demonstrate variable penetrance. While uncommon, it can present without the typical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita, instead appearing more subtly as bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs of premature ageing.

Genetic testing supported this, revealing a disease-causing heterozygous variant in the PARN gene, which accounted for her diverse clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg once daily for 5 days, subsequently once weekly for 4 weeks, and transitioned to monthly maintenance therapy to address the profound deficiency.

Additional supportive interventions comprised whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, vitamin D 60 000 IU weekly along with elemental calcium 1000 mg daily for osteoporosis. Standard supplementation was used to correct electrolyte disturbances.

Physiotherapy was initiated for the patient, and genetic counselling was provided to her family after confirming a diagnosis of TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After replenishing deficiencies and providing supportive treatment, the patient experienced alleviation of her symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up appointment, her hematologic parameters remained steady, her fatigue subsided, and no additional clinical issues arose.

She remains under routine outpatient observation to assess hepatic and pulmonary status, as well as to evaluate any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres consist of repeated DNA sequences situated at chromosome termini and function to safeguard chromosomes against deterioration, thereby maintaining genomic stability. During successive cell divisions, telomeres progressively shorten until they reach a threshold that induces cellular senescence or apoptosis. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein enzyme comprising telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—is responsible for the elongation of telomeres, sustaining their length. Mutations in genes coding for telomerase components compromise enzyme activity, which accelerates telomere shortening and gives rise to a category of hereditary disorders known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 inherited in an X-linked recessive manner were the initial discovery connecting dyskeratosis congenita (DC) with significantly shortened telomeres, establishing DC as the archetype of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). The classical diagnostic criteria for DC consist of the mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular hyperpigmentation of the skin, or the presence of two of these findings in conjunction with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional commonly observed features encompass idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and elevated susceptibility to both hematological and solid tumors. Severe forms of childhood-onset TBDs—including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—also arise due to variants in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can exhibit isolated involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or hematologic abnormalities, and may arise in adults absent the characteristic triad of DC. Commonly, the hereditary defect is concealed by disorders like aplastic anemia, BMFS, IPF, or HF as well as gastrointestinal conditions. In the absence of the classic triad and because the onset is often delayed, misdiagnosis is common, particularly outside specialty centres. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are frequently found in TBDs, with patients typically presenting as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is part of a well-conserved group of exoribonucleases and functions to reduce the poly(A) tail of mRNAs via deadenylation, thereby controlling gene expression. Comprehensive sequencing of TERC RNA at the 3' end revealed that PARN is necessary for eliminating oligo(A) tails, which are added after transcription and direct nuclear RNAs toward degradation; this process is essential for proper maturation of the 3'-end of TERC.

PARN is among the 20% of genes exhibiting the least tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, highlighting its critical function and the relevance of haploinsufficiency in heterozygous individuals. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have been identified as causative in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as in the wider DC/TBD disease spectrum. Cases of Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome have been associated with biallelic PARN mutations. Telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4) have been linked to monoallelic germline pathogenic variants. The association between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was initially noted following the discovery of novel monoallelic variants, with subsequent studies finding rare monoallelic PARN variants in five out of 262 patients with sporadic IPF subjected to whole-exome sequencing, thereby establishing an emerging connection now described in more than 65 cases in published reports.1517 Nevertheless, the complete spectrum of clinical manifestations attributable to PARN mutations has not been thoroughly characterized due to the small number of documented cases.

Of particular note, the patient was found to carry a monoallelic missense alteration in the PARN gene, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which affects a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (positions 178–245). Four out of five in silico tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) predict that this variant has a detrimental effect on protein function. Applying the ACMG/AMP variant classification framework, this variant satisfies several supporting criteria: PM1—it is located within a vital and well-characterized functional region (the R3H domain) without known benign variation; PM2—it is absent or found at very low frequency in population databases; PP3—multiple computational algorithms suggest that it is harmful to the gene or gene product; and PP2—missense changes are a frequent disease mechanism for PARN, a gene with few benign missense variants. Although its clinical impact has not yet been established, its possible connection to TBDs is supported both by PARN’s recognized functional importance and by similarities to other PARN missense changes—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which are listed as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database.

TBDs may involve multiple genes, exhibit varied patterns of inheritance, show incomplete penetrance, and feature somatic genetic rescue, resulting in differential impacts across organ systems. Moreover, environmental influences and lifestyle factors can modulate the replicative abilities of distinct organs. In relation to our patient, these factors may help clarify the clinical presentation, even though only a single PARN allele variant was detected.

While single-allele PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF and less often to primary hematologic disorders, our patient's cytopenia suggests an underlying TBD that could make the bone marrow more vulnerable to hematopoietic stressors, such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Reduced telomere length impairs the replication capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, promoting earlier cellular aging and heightening the risk for BMFS. Although the cytopenia in this case was probably due to marked vitamin B12 deficiency, the identification of a PARN variant emphasizes the need for continuous clinical and hematological surveillance to monitor for potential development of marrow failure in this patient with a genetic predisposition.

Cirrhosis associated with TBD has frequently been underdiagnosed; nonetheless, recent data indicate it may occur more often than once thought. In a retrospective review from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography, 88.8% of adults carrying pathogenic TERC or TERT mutations exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis, while cirrhosis was observed in 33.3%. A similar case report detailed a patient presenting with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who possessed monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our case involved a patient with only a monoallelic PARN variant who nonetheless developed hepatic as well as pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most frequently linked to IPF, their downstream effects on telomerase activity may explain the hepatic fibrosis more commonly seen in TERT or TERC mutation carriers. These observations highlight a widening phenotypic range for PARN-related TBDs and emphasize the need to recognize hepatic fibrosis as a component of its clinical features.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, as well as telomere length assessment with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation. Management is primarily supportive, individualized according to symptom severity, requirements for organ transplantation, and efforts to minimize risk factors. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a cure for patients exhibiting hematologic symptoms. Although limited preclinical and clinical studies indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, the prolonged consequences of these treatments are not yet established.

The patient exhibited signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough assessment ruled out other prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the pathogenic gene, and a comprehensive review of the literature broadened knowledge regarding this uncommon manifestation. Supportive care was administered, and her family received genetic counseling. She comprehended the details of her diagnosis and recognized that curative treatments were unattainable. At her two-week review, hematologic parameters were unchanged, with no evidence of new clinical decline.
