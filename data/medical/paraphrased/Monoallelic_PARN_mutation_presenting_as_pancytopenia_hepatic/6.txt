Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported a five-year progression of persistent fatigue and widespread weakness. On examination, there were indications of early-onset ageing, anaemia, neuropathic changes, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory findings demonstrated pancytopenia, a mixed-type anaemia, pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the combination of cytopenias, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, liver and lung fibrosis suggested an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome analysis revealed a heterozygous missense alteration in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), designated as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic variants in PARN are recognized causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and are also implicated in telomere-related disorders such as pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The alignment of clinical and genetic findings in this patient was consistent with a diagnosis of telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to include TBDs in the differential diagnosis for young individuals presenting with unexplained, multisystem disease—even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous manifestations. Timely diagnosis is vital for directing genetic evaluation, ongoing monitoring, and potential early intervention with bone marrow transplantation in advancing disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) constitute a group of uncommon premature ageing syndromes arising from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, resulting in excessively shortened or impaired telomeres. Cell populations characterized by high proliferative activity—including bone marrow, skin, mucosal tissues, hair follicles, and liver hepatocytes—are particularly susceptible to these disorders. The spectrum of TBDs encompasses a wide array of clinical manifestations, among which dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serves as the classic example. Additional conditions under this category are Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of disorders, PARN mutations are associated with a unique clinical presentation. Specifically, biallelic alterations in PARN result in autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single allelic mutations are predominantly linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report the case of a woman in her late 20s presenting with persistent fatigue and involvement of multiple organ systems, who was ultimately found to have a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care facility with a five-year progression of generalized weakness and increasing susceptibility to fatigue. Throughout this period, she experienced a notable decline in functional ability, which limited her participation in daily activities relative to others her age. In the most recent three months, her weakness had further intensified, significantly affecting her capacity for self-care and necessitating help. Additionally, she described experiencing shortness of breath with exertion, occasional chest pain, involuntary weight loss, and diminished appetite.

Upon assessment, her haemodynamic status was stable. Significant physical signs included pallor, icterus, early onset greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, bald tongue due to atrophy of the lingual papillae.

Evaluation of the musculoskeletal system demonstrated thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of both thenar eminences. On abdominal examination, hepatomegaly was observed, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. Respiratory assessment revealed decreased breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular examination did not show any abnormalities.

Investigations:

On initial laboratory assessment, the patient was found to have pancytopenia accompanied by a markedly reduced reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed a dimorphic red cell population, presence of basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmented nuclei, all of which pointed towards ineffective erythropoiesis. Biochemical analysis indicated pronounced deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Assessment of iron parameters showed increased serum iron and ferritin levels, which aligns with a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow analysis demonstrated active trilineage haematopoiesis, prominent erythroid hyperplasia, and elevated iron stores, with no signs of malignant transformation, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative pathology. Cytogenetic testing yielded normal results.

Indicators of haemolysis included raised lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) yielded a positive result for IgG (2+), while the indirect antiglobulin test remained negative. Nevertheless, the observed poor reticulocyte production and the bone marrow findings supported a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than haemolysis driven by immune-mediated processes in the peripheral blood.

To investigate possible malabsorption in the gastrointestinal tract, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted, revealing no abnormal findings on gross examination. Histological analysis of a duodenal biopsy identified mild chronic gastritis and a reduction in parietal cell numbers, which may hinder intrinsic factor production and thereby contribute to vitamin B12 malabsorption. Nonetheless, the extent of the patient’s symptoms seemed excessive when compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiencies alone.

The presence of liver dysfunction warranted further diagnostic imaging and laboratory studies. Abdominal ultrasonography indicated the presence of hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography findings demonstrated grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) as well as cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Elevated inflammatory markers were observed. Yet, testing for autoimmune conditions—including antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies—yielded negative results. Comprehensive infectious screening for viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus did not reveal significant abnormalities, except for positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

To assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease, positron emission tomography–CT was utilized. This imaging revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within both the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Additionally, there were widespread ground-glass opacities in both lungs accompanied by numerous fibrotic alterations and areas of subpleural atelectasis. The examination also identified hepatosplenomegaly and the presence of ascites; however, there were no signs indicating clear malignancy or granulomatous pathology (figures 1 and 2).

Further investigations identified secondary hyperparathyroidism, various electrolyte imbalances (notably hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis as evidenced by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These abnormalities accounted for her persistent bone pain and repeated fractures (see table 3). Electromyography and nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy accompanied by chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal musculature.

The combination of findings—including cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early onset greying, osteoporosis, and peripheral neuropathy—raised suspicion for an underlying syndromic or genetic disorder.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This substitution affects an evolutionarily conserved amino acid and is predicted to impair protein function. Despite these findings, the contribution of this variant to the disease phenotype remains unclear and it has thus been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While definitive pathogenicity for this particular variant is lacking, the association of PARN and the patient’s clinical presentation align with the recognized range of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, early onset greying of hair, and persistent fatigue initially suggested the possibility of underlying haematologic, nutritional, or autoimmune etiologies. A significantly reduced reticulocyte count indicated that erythropoiesis was impaired, thereby leading to early focus on potential nutrient deficits and various marrow failure syndromes.

Laboratory confirmation of severe vitamin B12 deficiency explained several clinical manifestations and was corroborated by bone marrow findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, after nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the patient’s multisystem abnormalities persisted in a manner that was disproportionate to the identified deficiencies.

Although the presence of a positive DAT and elevated markers of inflammation briefly raised the possibility of an immune-driven disorder, the lack of clinical or laboratory evidence of haemolysis, together with negative autoimmune serologies and the absence of other systemic symptoms, made diagnoses such as autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease unlikely.

The cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was undetermined, as both autoimmune and infectious origins were ruled out. The combination of early-onset ageing characteristics, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrotic changes affecting both the lungs and liver in a young adult led clinicians to consider an obscure genetic disorder. While the lack of familial cases initially made an inherited syndrome seem unlikely, the possibility of a de novo genetic mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted the suggestion of a TBD that resembles acquired disorders, exhibiting incomplete penetrance and variable clinical expression. While uncommon, such cases may not display the typical mucocutaneous triad observed in DC, but instead reveal more understated symptoms such as bone marrow failure, fibrotic changes in organs, and indicators of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this theory by detecting a disease-causing monoallelic mutation in the PARN gene, thereby offering a coherent account for the range of her clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg each day for 5 days, followed by weekly administration over 4 weeks, and subsequently transitioned to monthly doses for maintenance to address the pronounced deficiency.

Adjunctive therapy comprised whole blood transfusions for management of symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid (1 mg per day), and vitamin D (60 000 IU weekly) together with elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) for osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed through standard supplementation protocols.

In addition, she participated in physiotherapy, and her family was offered genetic counselling after the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive therapy, the patient experienced relief of her symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters remained stable, her fatigue had lessened, and there were no additional clinical issues observed.

She is presently under ongoing outpatient monitoring to assess hepatic and pulmonary function, as well as to track for any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA motifs located at the termini of chromosomes, serving a protective function that safeguards chromosomal DNA from deterioration. As somatic cells undergo successive rounds of replication, telomeres gradually erode until reaching a threshold length that induces either senescence or programmed cell death. In normal physiological states, the maintenance of telomere length is orchestrated by telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein enzyme complex composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which is responsible for adding telomeric repeats to chromosome ends. Mutations in genes encoding key elements of this telomerase complex disrupt telomerase activity, hastening telomere shortening and leading to a spectrum of hereditary disorders collectively known as TBD.

The association between DC and significantly shortened telomeres was initially identified through the discovery of X-linked recessive pathogenic mutations in DKC1, thereby establishing DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). The clinical diagnosis of DC traditionally involves the mucocutaneous triad comprising nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular skin pigmentation; alternatively, the presence of any two of these features along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS) is sufficient. Additional well-documented clinical features encompass idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), HF, and a heightened susceptibility to both haematological and solid tumors. Furthermore, severe forms of childhood-onset TBD—such as HH syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are also attributable to genetic alterations affecting DC-associated genes.

TBDs can exhibit a range of clinical features, including isolated involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or the hematopoietic system, and may become apparent during adulthood without the presence of the traditional DC triad. Frequently, underlying genetic abnormalities are obscured by other clinical syndromes such as aplastic anaemia, BMFS, IPF, HF, or disorders of the gastrointestinal tract. In the absence of the characteristic triad and because of late onset, accurate diagnosis is often missed, particularly outside of specialized healthcare settings. Typically, monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are found in TBDs and are commonly associated with phenotypes such as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is a member of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family and functions by reducing the length of poly(A) tails on mRNA molecules via deadenylation, thereby modulating gene expression. Analysis using deep sequencing of the TERC RNA 3-prime terminal revealed that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added post-transcriptionally, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation; this activity is crucial for proper 3-prime-end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks within the lowest 20% of genes in terms of tolerance to rare, deleterious variants, underscoring its crucial biological function and providing evidence for haploinsufficiency among carriers. Pathogenic germline alterations in PARN have been established as causative factors for dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as for the more extensive DC/TBD phenotypes. Instances of biallelic mutations in PARN have been documented in individuals with HH syndrome. Additionally, heterozygous pathogenic germline variants in PARN have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The first correlation between PARN and IPF was revealed through the identification of novel heterozygous variants; further research has since detected rare monoallelic PARN variants in five out of 262 individuals with sporadic IPF subjected to whole-exome sequencing. This expanding evidence base now encompasses over 65 reported cases in the literature.1517 Nevertheless, our comprehension of the full phenotypic spectrum linked to PARN mutations is still limited, primarily because of the small number of observed cases.

Importantly, our patient possessed a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which impacts a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). This variant is predicted to have a damaging functional consequence by four out of five computational missense assessment tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT). According to the ACMG/AMP guidelines for classifying variants, this alteration satisfies multiple supporting criteria: PM1—since it alters a crucial residue within the R3H domain, a region critical for function and lacking known benign variants; PM2—its presence is absent or exceedingly rare in large-scale population data; PP3—several in silico algorithms consistently predict a detrimental effect on gene function; and PP2—PARN is characterized by infrequent benign missense variation and a tendency for missense mutations to underlie disease phenotypes. While the clinical impact of this specific variant is still undetermined, its possible association with TBDs is highlighted both by the recognized function of PARN and by similar missense substitutions in the gene, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which ClinVar classifies as ‘likely pathogenic’.

In TBDs, diverse genetic factors, variable inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and the phenomenon of somatic genetic rescue may influence organ systems in distinct ways. Furthermore, non-genetic influences, such as environmental exposures and lifestyle choices, can variably modulate the replicative limits of specific organs. For our patient, these multifaceted elements could account for the clinical presentation, even though only a single heterozygous PARN variant was detected.

While heterozygous PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF rather than primary haematological disorders, the cytopenia exhibited by our patient suggests the possibility of a subclinical TBD that could predispose the bone marrow to increased vulnerability under haematopoietic stressors like profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres restrict the self-renewal ability of haematopoietic stem cells, resulting in premature cellular aging and heightened susceptibility to BMFS. Although the patient’s cytopenia was most plausibly due to the marked vitamin B12 deficiency, identification of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continuous clinical and haematological evaluation to monitor for the development of a bone marrow failure phenotype in this individual with genetic risk.

Cirrhosis in the context of TBD has historically been underdiagnosed; nonetheless, accumulating data indicate that its occurrence may be higher than once thought. In a retrospective study conducted through the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, which employed transient elastography, it was observed that 88.8% of adult individuals carrying pathogenic variants in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate HF, while cirrhosis was detected in 33.3% of cases. Similarly, another report detailed an individual presenting with pancytopenia and HF, who possessed monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By contrast, our case involved a patient with only a monoallelic PARN mutation, yet who still manifested both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF, potential downstream effects on telomerase activity could explain the HF more frequently associated with TERT or TERC mutations. Altogether, these observations broaden the clinical understanding of PARN-associated TBDs and emphasize the necessity of recognizing HF as part of its clinical presentation.

The definitive approach for diagnosing suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis utilizing next-generation sequencing, as well as telomere length assessment via flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays. Current management is primarily supportive and individualized according to the degree of symptoms, requirements for organ transplantation, and strategies for risk reduction. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may provide a cure for individuals exhibiting haematologic involvement. Although early-stage and limited clinical trials have indicated that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone might promote telomere extension, their long-term safety and efficacy have yet to be established.

The patient exhibited symptoms indicative of TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough diagnostic workup that ruled out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the underlying genetic cause. A comprehensive review of existing literature further informed our comprehension of this uncommon clinical presentation. Management was primarily supportive, and her relatives received genetic counseling. The patient demonstrated an understanding of her diagnosis and recognized that curative treatments were unavailable. At a follow-up visit two weeks later, her hematological parameters had not declined, and she showed no additional clinical worsening.
