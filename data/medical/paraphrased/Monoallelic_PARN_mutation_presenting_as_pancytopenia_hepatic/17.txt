Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported experiencing worsening fatigue and widespread weakness over five years. Physical examination noted premature ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory tests revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Correction of nutritional deficits did not resolve her symptoms, and the combination of cytopenias, premature greying, osteoporosis, and evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis suggested an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome analysis identified a heterozygous missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN are recognized in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and in cases of telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The clinical and genetic findings in this patient were consistent with a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs in young adults with unexplained disease affecting multiple organ systems, even when classic mucocutaneous signs are lacking. Timely identification is important for appropriate genetic counseling, monitoring, and evaluating the need for bone marrow transplantation in patients with disease progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a group of uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated aging, which arise due to inherited mutations in genes involved in telomere maintenance, resulting in excessively short or impaired telomeres. These disorders chiefly impact cells with high proliferative rates, such as those in the bone marrow, skin, mucosal tissues, hair follicles, and liver. A variety of clinical presentations are encompassed within TBDs, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional related disorders include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of disorders, mutations in PARN are associated with a characteristic clinical presentation: individuals with mutations in both alleles exhibit autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single-allele mutations are more frequently implicated in telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We describe a woman in her late 20s with prolonged fatigue and multisystem involvement who was ultimately diagnosed with a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties arrived at our tertiary facility with a five-year progression of increasing generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. Her physical abilities gradually diminished during this period, leading to difficulty carrying out routine tasks relative to others her age. In the preceding three months, her weakness intensified further, significantly affecting her self-care and necessitating support. Additional symptoms included shortness of breath with exertion, sporadic chest discomfort, unintentional weight loss, and loss of appetite.

On examination, she was haemodynamically stable. Notable findings included pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae.

On musculoskeletal assessment, thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of both thenar eminences were noted. The abdominal exam identified hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin and a total span of 18 cm. On respiratory evaluation, breath sounds were diminished in both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular assessment found no abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial lab results revealed pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified dimorphic red cells, presence of basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—findings indicative of inefficient erythropoiesis. Further laboratory evaluation showed marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate levels. Iron panel results indicated increased serum iron and ferritin concentrations, which align with a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow analysis showed active production of all three cell lines, marked erythroid proliferation, and increased iron reserves, with no signs of cancer, abnormal cell development, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetics yielded normal results.

Laboratory markers of haemolysis demonstrated raised lactate dehydrogenase and increased indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) yielded a 2+ positivity for IgG, while the indirect antiglobulin assay was negative. Nevertheless, the reticulocyte count was inappropriately low, and marrow assessment indicated that the findings were indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than destruction of red cells in the periphery due to immune mechanisms.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was carried out to assess for malabsorption in the gastrointestinal tract and revealed no significant abnormalities on gross inspection. Histologic examination of the duodenal biopsy indicated mild chronic gastritis and a decreased number of parietal cells, which may play a role in vitamin B12 malabsorption through reduced intrinsic factor synthesis. Nonetheless, the extent of the patient’s clinical manifestations seemed greater than what would be expected from nutritional deficiency by itself.

Liver impairment led to additional diagnostic imaging and evaluation. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography identified grade II hepatic steatosis with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m and findings consistent with cirrhosis, as indicated by a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. Elevated inflammatory markers were present. Nevertheless, autoimmune serologic testing—including assays for antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies—yielded negative results. Infectious screening for viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was essentially negative, with the exception of a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG result (see table 2).

To investigate potential hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease, a positron emission tomography–CT scan was obtained. This revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake within both the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Both lungs demonstrated widespread ground-glass opacities, as well as several areas of fibrosis and subpleural atelectasis. Additionally, there was hepatosplenomegaly and the presence of ascites. No definitive signs of malignancy or granulomatous process were identified (see figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results were secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes—including hypocalcemia and hypokalemia—as well as vitamin D deficiency and osteoporosis, which was verified through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These findings accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography combined with nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, accompanied by chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Given the constellation of findings—cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic aetiology was suspected.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This variant affects an evolutionarily conserved amino acid and is predicted to negatively impact protein function. Nevertheless, its direct contribution to the patient’s disease could not be determined and it has been classified as a 'Variant of Uncertain Significance'. While the precise pathogenicity of this mutation is unclear, both the association with PARN and the clinical presentation of the patient align with the features observed in the TBD spectrum.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, early onset greying of hair, and fatigue prompted evaluation for potential haematological, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The observation of a significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, resulting in a differential that included nutritional deficiencies as well as bone marrow failure syndromes.

A profound deficiency of vitamin B12 was promptly identified and explained multiple clinical manifestations, as corroborated by bone marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite appropriate correction of these deficits, the widespread dysfunction persisted and was out of proportion to what would be expected.

An immune-mediated mechanism was momentarily contemplated in light of a positive direct antiglobulin test and raised inflammatory markers. However, given the lack of haemolysis, negative results on autoimmune serologies, and no systemic manifestations, autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease was deemed unlikely.

The cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was not evident, as both autoimmune and infectious origins were ruled out. The combination of early onset ageing characteristics, sustained cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young adult suggested the possibility of an unrecognized genetic disorder. While the lack of similar conditions in family members initially made a hereditary syndrome seem unlikely, the potential for a de novo genetic mutation was considered.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD), known to simulate acquired diseases and exhibit heterogenous penetrance. Although uncommon, such disorders may not show the classic mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), and instead present more subtly with bone marrow failure, visceral fibrosis, and signs of premature ageing.

This was substantiated by genetic analysis, which identified a pathogenic monoallelic variant in the PARN gene, providing a unifying explanation for the constellation of her clinical features.

Treatment:

The patient was treated with intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance to correct severe deficiency.

Supportive management comprised transfusion of whole blood to address symptomatic anemia, administration of oral folic acid at 1 mg per day, and supplementation with vitamin D at 60,000 IU weekly along with 1,000 mg of elemental calcium daily for osteoporosis. Disturbances in electrolytes were managed using routine replacement therapy.

She also underwent physiotherapy, and her family received genetic counselling following diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After her deficiencies were corrected and she received supportive treatment, the patient experienced relief from her symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her blood cell counts remained steady, her fatigue had lessened, and no additional clinical issues were identified.

She continues to receive regular outpatient follow-up for surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function and to monitor the progression of bone marrow or systemic involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres consist of repeated DNA motifs that form protective caps at chromosome termini, safeguarding chromosomes from degradation and maintaining genomic stability. During successive cellular replications, telomeres progressively shorten until they reach a critical threshold, at which point cells enter senescence or undergo apoptosis. Normally, telomerase—an enzyme complex made up of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts this process by adding telomeric repeats, thereby sustaining telomere length. Pathogenic mutations in genes encoding elements of the telomerase complex compromise telomerase activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and giving rise to a spectrum of hereditary disorders collectively known as telomere biology disorders (TBD).

The association between DC and significantly shortened telomeres was first identified through pathogenic X-linked recessive mutations in DKC1, positioning DC as the prototype for telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Traditionally, DC is characterized by a mucocutaneous triad—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation—or by the presence of any two of these clinical findings together with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized features comprise idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), and a heightened risk for both hematologic and solid tumors. Rare, severe childhood-onset TBD forms such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome also stem from genetic alterations in genes associated with DC.

Telomere biology disorders can manifest as isolated involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or hematologic abnormalities, and may arise in adulthood absent the hallmark triad of dyskeratosis congenita. Commonly, the underlying genetic defect is obscured by the presence of disorders such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic fibrosis, or gastrointestinal conditions. The absence of the classical triad, combined with delayed onset of symptoms, often leads to frequent misdiagnosis, particularly when patients are not evaluated at specialized facilities. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 genes are most often detected in telomere biology disorders, which frequently present clinically as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

The PARN gene encodes a member of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family that functions to regulate gene expression by trimming the poly(A) tails of mRNA via deadenylation. High-throughput sequencing of the 3′ terminus of TERC RNA has demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating post-transcriptional oligo(A) additions, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation, a step necessary for the maturation of TERC at its 3′ end.

PARN ranks among the 20% of genes most intolerant to rare, disruptive mutations, emphasizing its critical function and supporting the notion of haploinsufficiency among carriers. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and the broader DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been associated with biallelic PARN variants. Heterozygous germline pathogenic alterations have been linked to telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was first suggested by the discovery of novel heterozygous variants; later investigations revealed rare heterozygous PARN variants in five of 262 sporadic IPF patients undergoing whole-exome sequencing, thus establishing an expanding association now described in more than 65 patients in published reports.1517 Nonetheless, the full clinical phenotype linked to PARN mutations is not yet clearly defined due to the small number of described cases.

Importantly, our patient possessed a monoallelic missense mutation in the PARN gene, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). This alteration is predicted to have a harmful impact on protein function by four out of five in silico missense prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT). According to ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines, this change satisfies several supporting criteria: PM1—the missense occurs within a critical and well-characterized functional domain (R3H domain) without reported benign variation; PM2—the variant is either absent or extremely uncommon in population genetic databases; PP3—several computational analyses indicate a deleterious effect on gene function or gene product; and PP2—PARN is a gene known to have few benign missense changes, with disease often resulting from such variants. While the clinical significance of this specific variant remains unclear, its possible connection to TBDs is underscored by the recognized function of the gene and by comparable PARN missense substitutions, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which ClinVar has classified as ‘likely pathogenic’.

A range of genes, diverse modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can uniquely impact individual organ systems in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Moreover, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices may variably affect the replicative potential across organ systems. In our patient's case, these intricate factors could account for the clinical presentation seen, even though only a single PARN allele variant was found.

While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF rather than primary hematologic abnormalities, the cytopenia noted in our patient suggests the possibility of an underlying TBD, which could make the bone marrow more vulnerable to hematopoietic stressors like profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Reduced telomere length impairs the proliferative ability of hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in premature cellular aging and a heightened risk of developing BMFS. Although severe vitamin B12 deficiency was the most probable cause of cytopenia in this case, the identification of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematological surveillance to detect any emerging features of marrow failure in this genetically at-risk individual.

Cirrhosis associated with telomere biology disorders (TBD) has historically been overlooked, but new data indicate it may be more common than formerly recognized. In a retrospective study conducted through the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography, 88.8% of adult participants with pathogenic variants in TERC or TERT demonstrated at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), while 33.3% exhibited cirrhosis. Additionally, a similar published case described a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis possessing monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our patient carried a solitary monoallelic PARN mutation but still developed hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their effect on telomerase activity may explain the occurrence of hepatic fibrosis often observed with TERT or TERC variants. These observations broaden the clinical manifestations associated with PARN-related TBDs, emphasizing the need to recognize hepatic fibrosis within its clinical phenotype.

Definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing and assessment of telomere length using flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assays. Management is primarily supportive, individualized according to the severity of symptoms, requirement for organ transplantation, and modification of risk factors; however, hematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a potential cure for patients presenting with hematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical data and small clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, the long-term outcomes of this approach are still uncertain.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough workup that ruled out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible genetic abnormality, and an in-depth literature review enhanced insight into this uncommon clinical scenario. Management was supportive in nature, and her family received genetic counseling. The patient demonstrated understanding regarding the disease course and recognized that definitive curative therapies were not possible. At a two-week follow-up, laboratory findings showed her blood counts were unchanged, and no additional clinical decline was observed.
