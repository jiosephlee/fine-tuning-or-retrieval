Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her late twenties presented with a five-year progression of fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination showed evidence of early-onset ageing, anemia, neuropathy, and both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as findings of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Even after treating nutritional deficiencies, the combination of cytopenias, premature greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs suggested the possibility of an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing uncovered a heterozygous missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), identified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Inherited pathogenic alterations in PARN are linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The overall clinical and genetic findings in this patient indicated a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs as a differential diagnosis in younger adults with unexplained, multisystem involvement—even in the absence of typical mucocutaneous signs. Early detection is essential to inform genetic counseling, surveillance strategies, and the potential need for bone marrow transplantation in patients with progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are uncommon syndromes of accelerated aging arising from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, resulting in unusually short or impaired telomeres. Tissues with rapid cell turnover—such as bone marrow, epidermis, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and hepatocytes—are chiefly impacted. The spectrum of TBD encompasses various clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) representing the classic example. Additional conditions under TBD include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of diseases, mutations in PARN are associated with unique clinical features: individuals harboring biallelic mutations present with autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas heterozygous mutations tend to be linked to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a woman in her late twenties who experienced persistent fatigue and signs affecting multiple organ systems, leading to a definitive diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary hospital due to a five-year progression of generalized weakness and increasing fatigue. Her physical capabilities had gradually diminished over time, leading to reduced participation in daily tasks relative to individuals of similar age. During the past three months, her muscle weakness had intensified significantly, resulting in difficulty managing personal care and necessitating help from others. She further described experiencing shortness of breath with exertion, occasional chest pain, unintentional weight loss, and a decreased appetite.

Upon assessment, her vital signs were within normal limits. Significant clinical features observed were pallor, jaundice, early onset hair greying, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth tongue indicating loss of lingual papillae.

The musculoskeletal evaluation demonstrated thoracic spinal curvature and atrophy of the muscles in both thenar regions. Abdominal examination identified liver enlargement, palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin with an overall liver span of 18 cm. Pulmonary assessment showed diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular system examination did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory workup revealed pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Review of the peripheral blood smear showed the presence of dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—findings indicative of defective erythrocyte maturation. Serum analysis demonstrated pronounced deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Assessment of iron metabolism showed elevated serum iron and ferritin concentrations, supporting the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Examination of the bone marrow indicated trilineage hematopoiesis with a predominance of erythroid cells and increased iron content, in the absence of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic results were normal.

Indicators of hemolysis included increased levels of lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG at a 2+ intensity, whereas the indirect test yielded a negative result. Nevertheless, the reduced reticulocyte production and bone marrow findings aligned more closely with a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than peripheral destruction by immune mechanisms.

To investigate the possibility of gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was carried out, revealing no gross abnormalities. Histopathological analysis of the duodenal mucosa identified mild chronic gastritis with decreased parietal cell population, which could lead to impaired intrinsic factor secretion and subsequent B12 deficiency. Nevertheless, the extent of clinical symptoms seemed excessive compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

Given the presence of hepatic dysfunction, additional imaging and evaluation were pursued. Abdominal ultrasonography detected hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) and evidence of cirrhosis (liver stiffness measurement: 18.1 kPa). Inflammatory markers were elevated. Autoimmune serological testing—including antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies—yielded negative results. Investigation for infectious causes such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus did not reveal abnormalities apart from Epstein-Barr virus IgG positivity (table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for undetected infiltrative or inflammatory conditions. The scan demonstrated uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Both lungs exhibited widespread ground-glass opacities accompanied by several fibrotic alterations and subpleural atelectasis. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were present. There was no indication of frank malignancy or granulomatous disease (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable observations were secondary hyperparathyroidism, abnormalities in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis, which was verified via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry; these findings accounted for her frequent bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyography together with nerve conduction studies identified asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The aggregate of findings—including cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early onset greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—raised suspicion for a syndromic or genetic origin.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This missense change affects an amino acid that is highly conserved and is predicted to have a deleterious effect on protein function. Nonetheless, its contribution to the disease could not be determined, and thus it was classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the specific pathogenicity of this variant is unclear, the implication of PARN combined with the patient's presentation aligns with features characteristic of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintended weight loss, early greying of hair, and fatigue prompted evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. Profoundly decreased reticulocyte levels indicated ineffective erythropoiesis, which initially led to consideration of nutrient deficits and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Severe deficiency of vitamin B12 was promptly identified and explained multiple clinical findings, corroborated by bone marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Despite appropriate correction of nutritional deficiencies, the patient’s persistent and disproportionately widespread dysfunction remained.

Due to a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and increased inflammatory markers, the possibility of an immune-mediated process was explored. Nevertheless, the absence of evidence for hemolysis, negative autoimmune serologies, and lack of extrahematologic manifestations diminished the likelihood of autoimmune cytopenia or a connective tissue disorder.

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) was not attributed to any specific cause, as autoimmune and infectious etiologies were ruled out. The rare combination of signs indicative of accelerated ageing, sustained cytopenia, and fibrotic changes in both the liver and lungs in a young adult raised suspicion for an underlying genetic disorder. While there was no family history suggestive of a hereditary condition, the possibility of a de novo mutation was still considered.

This raised the suspicion of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of imitating acquired diseases and exhibiting diverse penetrance. While uncommon, such disorders may present without the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), instead displaying more inconspicuous signs such as bone marrow failure, fibrotic changes in organs, and symptoms reflecting premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing monoallelic variant in the PARN gene, which offered a cohesive rationale for her array of clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day over 5 days, then once weekly for four weeks, and subsequently transitioned to monthly maintenance therapy to address the marked deficiency.

Supportive measures consisted of whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, and vitamin D (60 000 IU weekly) together with 1000 mg daily of elemental calcium for osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed using routine supplementation.

In addition, she participated in physiotherapy, and genetic counseling was provided to her family after the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After deficiencies were addressed and supportive measures provided, the patient experienced alleviation of symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up, her hematologic parameters remained steady, fatigue was lessened, and no additional clinical issues were observed.

She remains under ongoing outpatient observation to assess hepatic and pulmonary functions and to track any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences that serve as protective caps at the ends of chromosomes, helping to maintain genomic stability by preventing chromosome degradation. During cell proliferation, these telomeric regions progressively shorten until they reach a threshold length, which induces cellular senescence or apoptosis. Normally, telomere length is preserved by the activity of telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein consisting of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which facilitates telomere elongation. Nevertheless, pathogenic mutations affecting genes responsible for telomerase components compromise enzyme activity, causing early telomere shortening and giving rise to a spectrum of inherited disorders referred to as TBD.

Pathogenic DKC1 variants inherited in an X-linked recessive manner were the first to establish an association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, rendering DC the archetype of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). The classic clinical diagnosis of DC involves the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, dystrophic nails, and a reticular pigmentation pattern of the skin, or alternatively, the presence of two of these symptoms in conjunction with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional well-described features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), as well as an elevated risk for developing both hematological and solid tumors. More severe forms of TBD presenting in childhood, such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, arise from mutations in genes also associated with DC.

TBDs can appear with only mucocutaneous, organ-specific, or haematological features, and often develop during adulthood in the absence of the classic DC triad. Frequently, the underlying inherited abnormality is concealed by disorders including aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, or hepatic and gastrointestinal conditions. The lack of classical findings and delayed onset of symptoms commonly leads to misdiagnosis, especially outside of specialist clinics. Single-allele mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are regularly found in TBD cases, commonly manifesting as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

The PARN gene is part of a well-preserved exoribonuclease family and modulates gene expression by decreasing the poly(A) tail length on mRNA via deadenylation. Advanced sequencing analyses of the 3-prime end of TERC RNA demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails acquired after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation and are necessary for the 3-prime-end maturation of TERC.

PARN ranks among the top 20% of genes with low tolerance for rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical function and lending support to haploinsufficiency as a mechanism in carriers. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN are established causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader group of DC/TBD disorders. Cases of HH syndrome have been linked to biallelic PARN alterations. Telomere-associated PFBMFT4 has been connected with monoallelic pathogenic germline PARN variants. The initial association between PARN and IPF was noted following the discovery of novel monoallelic mutations, with further reports describing monoallelic rare PARN variants in five out of 262 patients with sporadic IPF subjected to whole-exome sequencing, thereby contributing to a growing body of evidence—now comprising over 65 cases in published studies.1517 Nevertheless, comprehensive understanding of the clinical manifestations associated with PARN mutations remains limited due to the small cohort of cases described to date.

Of particular interest, the patient carried a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid located within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). Four out of five computational prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) indicate this variant is likely to impair protein function. According to the ACMG/AMP guidelines for classifying variants, several criteria apply to this mutation: PM1—the alteration occurs within a vital and well-characterized functional domain (R3H domain) without any reported benign variants; PM2—the mutation is either absent or extremely uncommon in population frequency databases; PP3—various in silico approaches suggest the variant is damaging to the gene or protein; PP2—PARN demonstrates a low prevalence of benign missense mutations, and disease-causing missense changes are often seen in this gene. Despite its clinical significance remaining unclear, this variant may be relevant to TBDs, based on the gene’s recognized function and similarity to other PARN missense mutations, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), both listed as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

The involvement of multiple genes, varied inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can have differing impacts on organ systems in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Moreover, environmental and lifestyle elements may variably affect the replicative abilities of individual organs. In our patient's case, these factors may account for the clinical presentation, even though only a monoallelic PARN mutation was detected.

Monoallelic PARN variants are generally linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than primary hematologic manifestations; however, our patient’s cytopenia raises the possibility of an underlying TBD that could make the bone marrow more vulnerable to hematopoietic challenges, including severe vitamin B12 deficiency. Telomere attrition impairs hematopoietic stem cell replicative capacity, resulting in premature cellular senescence and heightened risk of bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Although the patient’s cytopenia was likely due to profound vitamin B12 deficiency, the presence of the PARN variant emphasizes the necessity for continued clinical and hematologic surveillance to monitor for potential marrow failure features in an individual at genetic risk.

Cirrhosis associated with TBD has frequently gone unrecognized; nonetheless, recent data indicate it may be more widespread than once thought. In a retrospective study utilizing transient elastography from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, 88.8% of adults with pathogenic variants in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), while 33.3% met criteria for cirrhosis. The literature also documents a similar case involving a patient who had pancytopenia and HF, possessing monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Conversely, our case featured a patient with only a monoallelic PARN variant but who developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Monoallelic PARN mutations are often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), yet their impact on telomerase activity may explain the presence of HF more typically observed in those with TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations suggest a broader clinical range for PARN-related TBDs, highlighting the need to include HF within its recognized manifestations.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, along with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays to assess telomere length. Management is primarily supportive, adjusted according to the severity of symptoms, need for organ transplantation, and modification of risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a cure for individuals with haematological involvement. Preliminary preclinical and limited clinical trials have indicated that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may lengthen telomeres; however, the long-term outcomes are not yet established.

The patient exhibited signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. A thorough assessment ruled out more prevalent etiologies, after which whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the causative gene. An in-depth review of the literature augmented comprehension of this uncommon clinical picture. Supportive measures were instituted, along with genetic counseling for her relatives. She comprehended her diagnosis and acknowledged the unavailability of curative therapies. Two weeks post initial evaluation, her hematologic indices were unchanged, and no additional clinical decline was noted.
