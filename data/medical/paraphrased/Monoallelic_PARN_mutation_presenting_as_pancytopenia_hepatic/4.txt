Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her late twenties reported a five-year progression of fatigue and widespread weakness. Physical examination demonstrated premature signs of ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, as well as hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory workup revealed pancytopenia, a dimorphic pattern of anaemia, pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. While nutritional deficits were corrected, the continued presence of cytopenias, early-onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis pointed toward an underlying genetic condition. Clinical exome sequencing detected a monoallelic PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline alterations in PARN are recognized in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and telomere-related disorders characterized by pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. In this patient, the clinical findings aligned with the genetic data, supporting a diagnosis of telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the necessity of considering TBDs in young adults presenting with otherwise unexplained multisystemic symptoms, regardless of the absence of classic mucocutaneous manifestations. Prompt identification is essential for appropriate genetic counseling, disease monitoring, and evaluation for bone marrow transplantation in those with a progressive course.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) constitute a group of uncommon premature-aging conditions resulting from inherited mutations in genes involved in telomere maintenance, which produce excessively short or impaired telomeres. The pathological effects are primarily observed in tissues composed of rapidly proliferating cells, including the bone marrow, epidermis, mucosal linings, hair follicles, and liver cells. TBDs manifest in a wide array of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classical example. Additional disorders within this spectrum encompass Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of disorders, PARN mutations are associated with a unique clinical presentation: individuals harboring biallelic variants typically develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with monoallelic variants are more frequently predisposed to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report the case of a woman in her late twenties who presented with longstanding fatigue and manifestations involving multiple organ systems, ultimately leading to a diagnosis of a TBD.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care centre due to a 5-year progression of generalized weakness and increasing susceptibility to fatigue. During this period, her functional abilities gradually diminished, leaving her less capable of managing routine daily tasks relative to her contemporaries. In the most recent 3 months, her symptoms intensified, severely limiting her capacity for self-care and necessitating help from others. Additionally, she described experiencing shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest pain, unintended weight loss, and decreased appetite.

Upon assessment, she maintained stable haemodynamics. Significant observations comprised pallor, icterus, early onset greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a bald tongue marked by lingual papillae atrophy.

The musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of the thenar muscles bilaterally. On abdominal examination, the liver was enlarged, palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin with an 18 cm span. Respiratory assessment indicated diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular regions. The cardiovascular exam yielded no abnormal findings.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment identified pancytopenia accompanied by a markedly reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed a dimorphic population of erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, the presence of Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—findings indicative of defective erythropoiesis. Biochemical analysis of serum demonstrated profound deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron profile testing showed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, further supporting the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Evaluation of the bone marrow demonstrated trilineage haematopoiesis, notable erythroid hyperplasia, and augmented iron storage, with no features suggestive of malignant transformation, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic studies were also within normal limits.

Laboratory indices of haemolysis included raised lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin concentrations. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) yielded a positive result for IgG (graded 2+), while the indirect test was negative. However, given the blunted reticulocyte response and the bone marrow findings, ineffective erythropoiesis was favored over peripheral immune-mediated haemolytic processes.

In order to assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, the patient underwent an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, which demonstrated no gross abnormalities. Histopathological analysis of the duodenal biopsy identified mild chronic gastritis and a reduction in parietal cell population, which may have led to decreased intrinsic factor synthesis and subsequent vitamin B12 malabsorption. Nevertheless, the extent of clinical symptoms appeared excessive compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiencies alone.

Given the presence of hepatic impairment, additional imaging and evaluations were conducted. Abdominal ultrasonography identified enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography findings indicated grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) and established cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Elevation of inflammatory markers was noted. Conversely, autoantibody panels—including tests for antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies—were negative. Screening for infectious etiologies such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus yielded negative results except for the detection of Epstein-Barr virus IgG antibodies (table 2).

In order to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disorders, a positron emission tomography–CT scan was conducted. This imaging revealed irregular ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation throughout the bone marrow in both the axial and appendicular skeleton. Additionally, there was widespread ground-glass opacity along with numerous fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectatic alterations in both lungs. The scan also demonstrated hepatosplenomegaly and the presence of ascites. Notably, there were no findings suggestive of clear malignancy or granulomatous disease (see figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluations identified secondary hyperparathyroidism, imbalances in electrolytes (specifically hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis as demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These abnormalities provided an explanation for her persistent bone pain and frequent fractures (see table 3). Electromyography and nerve conduction assessments demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, with chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The combination of clinical features—namely cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early-onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—raised suspicion for a syndromic or genetic underlying cause.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This variant affects a highly conserved amino acid residue and computational analyses suggest a detrimental impact on protein function. Nonetheless, its direct contribution to the disease phenotype has not been established, leading to its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite the unresolved pathogenicity of this particular variant, both the functional relevance of PARN and the patient’s clinical presentation align with features typical of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient’s symptoms—including pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, premature greying, and persistent fatigue—prompted an evaluation for possible hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The presence of a significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested that erythropoiesis was impaired, guiding the differential initially toward nutritional deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

A diagnosis of profound vitamin B12 deficiency was quickly established, which explained several clinical and laboratory findings, as also reflected in the bone marrow evidence for ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite replenishment of vitamin and nutrient stores, the patient’s systemic impairment persisted and remained disproportionately severe relative to the corrected deficiencies.

Consideration was briefly given to an immune-mediated etiology based on a positive DAT and raised inflammatory indices. Nevertheless, the absence of hemolysis, negative results from autoimmune serologic testing, and the lack of characteristic systemic manifestations made autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disorders unlikely.

The underlying cause of Hepatic fibrosis (HF) remained undetermined, as both autoimmune and infectious origins were ruled out. The distinct combination of early-onset ageing signs, sustained cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both the lungs and liver in a young adult prompted consideration of a rare genetic disorder. While no familial precedent appeared to support an inherited syndrome, the emergence of a de novo mutation could not be discounted.

This observation prompted the proposal of a TBD capable of resembling acquired disorders, exhibiting incomplete penetrance and atypical clinical features. While uncommon, such cases may not display the traditional mucocutaneous triad associated with DC, but rather present with less conspicuous symptoms such as bone marrow insufficiency, fibrotic changes in organs, and signs indicative of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this hypothesis by revealing a pathogenic monoallelic mutation in the PARN gene, thereby offering a cohesive account for the range of her presenting symptoms.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for 5 days, transitioned to weekly administrations for 4 weeks, and subsequently maintained on a monthly schedule to address the profound deficiency.

Additional management comprised transfusion of whole blood to alleviate symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid supplementation (1 mg daily), and vitamin D (60 000 IU weekly) in combination with elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) for treatment of osteoporosis. Any identified electrolyte disturbances were managed with standard supplementation protocols.

Physiotherapy was initiated as part of her rehabilitation, and after a diagnosis of a TBD, genetic counselling was provided to her family.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive management, the patient experienced an alleviation of symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters remained stable, her fatigue had diminished, and there were no additional clinical issues noted.

She remains under ongoing outpatient observation to assess hepatic and pulmonary function as well as to track any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres consist of repeated DNA sequences situated at the termini of chromosomes, serving to safeguard genomic stability by protecting chromosomal ends from degradation. During successive rounds of cell division, these telomeric regions become progressively shorter, and once they fall below a threshold length, cells are prompted to enter senescence or undergo apoptosis. Normally, telomerase—an enzyme complex composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts this shortening by adding telomeric repeats. Mutations in genes encoding elements of the telomerase machinery compromise its activity, accelerating telomere shortening and giving rise to a spectrum of inherited disorders collectively termed TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1, inherited in an X-linked recessive manner, were the first to demonstrate a connection between DC and critically reduced telomere length, establishing DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). Traditionally, DC is characterized by a mucocutaneous triad that includes oral leukoplakia, dystrophic nails, and reticular skin hyperpigmentation; alternatively, a diagnosis can be made with the presence of two of these signs in conjunction with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional known features comprise idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), and a heightened susceptibility to both hematologic and solid tumors. Furthermore, severe forms of childhood-onset TBD—such as HH syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are likewise caused by mutations in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can appear as isolated involvement of the mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or haematological abnormalities, and may only become apparent during adulthood in the absence of the classical DC triad. In many cases, the underlying inherited mutation is obscured by presentations resembling conditions such as aplastic anaemia, BMFS, IPF, HF, or gastrointestinal pathologies. When the classical triad is not evident and clinical manifestations are delayed, misdiagnosis is common, particularly outside of specialised medical centres. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most commonly associated with TBDs, and frequently manifest clinically as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is a member of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family and functions in modulating gene expression by reducing the length of mRNA poly(A) tails via deadenylation. Comprehensive sequencing of the 3-prime terminal region of TERC RNA revealed that PARN is essential for excising oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation. This removal is a necessary step for the proper 3-prime-end maturation of TERC.

PARN ranks within the bottom 20% of genes in terms of tolerance to rare, deleterious variants, highlighting its crucial biological function and underscoring the relevance of haploinsufficiency among carriers. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are established etiologies for dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and the broader clinical spectrum associated with DC/TBD. Cases of HH syndrome have been linked to biallelic PARN variants, while heterozygous germline pathogenic alterations have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The initial association between PARN and IPF emerged following the discovery of novel heterozygous variants, and subsequent investigations have identified rare heterozygous PARN variants in five of 262 individuals with sporadic IPF who underwent whole-exome sequencing, thereby reinforcing this developing connection, which has now been reported in over 65 patients in the scientific literature.1517 Nonetheless, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations resulting from PARN mutations remains incompletely characterized due to the small number of documented cases.

Importantly, the patient described carries a heterozygous missense variant in PARN, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid located within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). Four out of five computational missense variant prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) suggest that this alteration has a harmful effect on protein function. According to ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines, this variant satisfies several criteria supporting pathogenicity: PM1—it is positioned in a crucial and well-defined functional region (R3H domain) where benign variation has not been described; PM2—the variant is either absent or extremely rare in large population reference datasets; PP3—diverse computational predictions concur in indicating a deleterious impact; PP2—PARN is characterized by a low prevalence of benign missense variants and missense changes are known to frequently result in disease. While the precise clinical impact of this variant is not definitively established, its potential involvement in TBDs is consistent with PARN’s known biological function and mirrors other PARN missense changes, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which have been annotated as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar repository.

The expression and inheritance patterns of multiple genes, along with phenomena such as incomplete penetrance and somatic genetic rescue, can produce variable effects across different organ systems in TBDs. Furthermore, environmental exposures and personal habits can distinctly modulate the proliferative capacity of specific organs. In relation to our patient, such multifactorial influences may account for the clinical presentation, even though only a heterozygous PARN variant was detected.

While heterozygous mutations in PARN are most frequently linked to IPF rather than initial blood-related abnormalities, our patient’s cytopenia prompts consideration of a potential underlying TBD, which could heighten bone marrow vulnerability to haematopoietic challenges like marked vitamin B12 deficiency. Progressive telomere attrition reduces the ability of haematopoietic stem cells to proliferate, accelerating cellular aging and heightening susceptibility to BMFS. Although severe vitamin B12 deficiency was likely the primary cause of cytopenia in this case, the identification of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continual clinical and haematological follow-up to detect any developing marrow failure manifestations in this genetically at-risk patient.

Cirrhosis within the context of TBD has historically been under-diagnosed, yet recent studies indicate its occurrence may be higher than once thought. Data from a retrospective review utilizing transient elastography in the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry revealed that 88.8% of adult individuals harboring pathogenic variants in TERC or TERT presented with at least moderate HF, and 33.3% exhibited cirrhosis. Another published report documented a case involving a patient with pancytopenia and HF, who possessed monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Unlike this previously reported case, our subject exhibited only a monoallelic PARN mutation but still developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are more typically linked to IPF, it is plausible that their effects on telomerase function contribute to HF as is frequently observed with TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations broaden the clinical manifestations attributed to PARN-associated TBDs and highlight the necessity of recognizing HF as an integral component of the disease phenotype.

The definitive approach for diagnosing suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing and the use of flow cytometry-coupled fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays for telomere length quantification. Current management strategies are primarily supportive, individualized based on clinical presentation, the requirement for organ transplantation, and reduction of risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a potentially curative option for individuals exhibiting haematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical and early clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may induce telomere extension, their sustained long-term efficacy and safety have yet to be fully elucidated.

The patient exhibited symptoms characteristic of TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough assessment that ruled out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the underlying genetic mutation, and a comprehensive literature review offered greater insight into this uncommon clinical scenario. Management was primarily supportive, and her family received genetic counseling. She demonstrated clear comprehension of her diagnosis and acknowledged that curative therapies were unavailable. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters were unchanged, and there was no evidence of further clinical decline.
