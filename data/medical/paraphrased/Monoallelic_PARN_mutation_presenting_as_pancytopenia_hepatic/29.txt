Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year duration of worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination noted features suggestive of early ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory findings revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the combination of cytopenias, premature hair greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs prompted consideration of a hereditary condition. Clinical exome analysis detected a heterozygous PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was interpreted as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline alterations in PARN are linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and disorders involving telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The observed clinical and genetic findings supported a diagnosis consistent with a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to include TBDs in the differential diagnosis for young adults with unexplained multisystem involvement, even when classic mucocutaneous stigmata are lacking. Prompt diagnosis is essential to inform genetic counseling, ongoing surveillance, and to evaluate candidacy for bone marrow transplantation in progressive cases.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes associated with premature aging that arise from inherited mutations in genes responsible for telomere function, resulting in telomeres that are unusually short or impaired. The condition primarily impacts cell populations that undergo active proliferation, including those of the bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and hepatocytes. TBDs comprise a range of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classical example. Additional forms encompass Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of diseases, mutations in PARN are notable for producing a unique clinical presentation: individuals with two mutated alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single mutated alleles are typically associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report a female patient in her late twenties who experienced persistent fatigue alongside symptoms affecting multiple organ systems and was eventually diagnosed with a telomere biology disorder.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary facility with a five-year progression of generalized weakness and early fatigue. During this period, she experienced a decline in her functional abilities, limiting her capacity to carry out routine tasks relative to individuals of similar age. In the past three months, her weakness intensified further, resulting in difficulty with self-care and necessitating help from others. Additionally, she complained of shortness of breath during physical exertion, occasional chest pain, unintended weight loss, and reduced appetite.

Upon evaluation, her vital signs were within normal limits. Significant physical observations comprised pallor, icterus, early onset grey hair, hyperpigmentation of the knuckles, and a smooth tongue with diminished lingual papillae. 

Assessment of the musculoskeletal system disclosed thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of the thenar eminences on both hands. The abdomen demonstrated an enlarged liver measuring 5 cm below the right costal margin, with a span of 18 cm. Examination of the respiratory system indicated diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. Findings from the cardiovascular examination were normal.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory workup identified pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation, all pointing toward ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum assessments demonstrated pronounced deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron studies indicated increased serum iron and ferritin levels, findings that support the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow assessment showed trilineage hematopoiesis, prominent erythroid hyperplasia, and augmented iron reserves, with no indications of malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic studies yielded normal results.

Indicators of hemolysis included raised levels of lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG at a level of 2+, whereas the indirect test was negative. Nonetheless, the diminished reticulocyte production along with the bone marrow findings suggested ineffective erythropoiesis as the primary cause rather than immune-mediated peripheral destruction.

In order to assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted and appeared macroscopically unremarkable. Histopathological examination of the duodenal biopsy demonstrated mild chronic gastritis with decreased parietal cell count, which may have led to vitamin B12 malabsorption secondary to reduced intrinsic factor secretion. Nonetheless, the extent of the clinical presentation seemed excessive for a pure nutritional deficiency.

The presence of hepatic dysfunction warranted additional imaging and diagnostic studies. Abdominal ultrasonography identified hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography revealed grade II hepatic steatosis, as evidenced by a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, as well as cirrhosis with a measured liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa. Inflammatory markers were elevated. Conversely, serological assays for autoimmunity, including antinuclear antibodies and markers for autoimmune hepatitis, were negative. Evaluation for infectious etiologies such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus yielded negative results, with the exception of a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory conditions. The scan demonstrated uneven uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose within both axial and appendicular bone marrow. Both lung fields exhibited widespread ground-glass opacities accompanied by several fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectasis. Additional findings included hepatosplenomegaly and the presence of ascites; however, there was no indication of clear malignancy or granulomatous disease (figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation revealed secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes (notably hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis confirmed by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, providing an explanation for her ongoing bone pain and frequent fractures (table 3). Electromyography alongside nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Taken together, the combination of cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy raised the suspicion of a syndromic or genetic origin.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single-allele missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This missense change affects a residue that is highly conserved and is anticipated to have a detrimental effect on protein function. Nonetheless, the association between this variant and the disease cannot be established, so it is classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the precise pathogenicity of this variant has not been determined, both the patient's clinical presentation and the involvement of the PARN gene align with characteristics seen in TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintended weight loss, early onset hair greying, and fatigue in this patient prompted evaluation for potential hematological, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The presence of a substantially reduced reticulocyte count suggested a defect in erythropoiesis, directing initial differential diagnosis toward nutritional deficits and syndromes associated with marrow failure.

Early confirmation of severe vitamin B12 deficiency explained multiple clinical manifestations and was corroborated by bone marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, after replacement of deficient nutrients, the patient continued to exhibit persistent and disproportionate systemic dysfunction.

Brief consideration was given to immune-mediated disease in light of a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and raised inflammatory markers. However, lack of clinical and laboratory evidence for hemolysis, negative results for autoimmune serologies, and absence of additional systemic involvement excluded both autoimmune cytopenia and connective tissue disorders from the working diagnosis.

The etiology of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was indeterminate, as both infectious and autoimmune origins were ruled out. The combination of premature aging signs, sustained cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young adult raised the likelihood of an unrecognized genetic condition. Despite the lack of relevant family history, the potential for a de novo genetic alteration was still considered.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD), which may imitate acquired disorders and demonstrate variable penetrance. While uncommon, it may not exhibit the typical mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and can instead present insidiously with bone marrow failure, fibrotic organ involvement, and signs of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous variant in the PARN gene, offering a cohesive explanation for the patient’s clinical findings.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg each day for 5 days, then once weekly for 4 weeks, after which she was placed on monthly maintenance therapy to address her marked deficiency.

Supportive therapies consisted of whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg per day, and vitamin D 60,000 IU weekly together with elemental calcium 1000 mg daily to treat osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed using conventional supplementation methods.

Physiotherapy was initiated, and after a diagnosis of a TBD, genetic counseling was provided to her family.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive treatment, the patient experienced an improvement in symptoms. At the two-week follow-up, her hematologic parameters remained consistent, fatigue had lessened, and no additional clinical issues had emerged.

She is undergoing ongoing outpatient monitoring to assess hepatic and pulmonary function and observe for any changes related to bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres consist of repetitive nucleotide sequences at the termini of chromosomes and function to maintain genomic stability by protecting chromosomal ends from deterioration. With ongoing cellular replication, these telomeric sequences progressively shorten until they reach a threshold that induces cell cycle arrest or programmed cell death. In normal physiological states, telomerase—an enzyme complex composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts this shortening by extending telomeres. Nevertheless, deleterious mutations in genes coding for telomerase complex subunits disrupt the enzyme's activity, resulting in accelerated telomere shortening and giving rise to a spectrum of inherited disorders known as telomere biology disorders (TBD).

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 inherited in an X-linked recessive manner were the first to demonstrate a connection between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and extremely short telomeres, positioning DC as the archetype of telomere biology disorders (TBD). DC is traditionally characterized by a mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, dystrophy of the nails, and reticular pigmentation of the skin, or by two of these features together with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical manifestations include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and a heightened susceptibility to both haematological and solid tumors. The more severe subtypes of TBD with childhood onset—such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are likewise caused by mutations in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can appear as isolated mucocutaneous, organ-specific, or hematological abnormalities, and may emerge in adults lacking the characteristic DC triad. Often, the underlying hereditary abnormality is obscured by disorders such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, heart failure, or gastrointestinal conditions. The absence of the classic triad, along with delayed symptom onset, results in frequent misdiagnosis, particularly outside of specialized clinics. In TBDs, monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly encountered, frequently manifesting as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

The PARN gene is part of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family that controls gene expression by trimming the poly(A) tail on mRNA via deadenylation. Analysis through deep sequencing of the 3-prime end of TERC RNA demonstrated that PARN is necessary to eliminate oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation and are essential for the proper maturation of TERC’s 3-prime end.

PARN ranks among the top 20% of genes least accommodating to rare, disruptive alterations, underscoring its critical function and providing evidence for haploinsufficiency in individuals with such variants. Germline pathogenic variants in PARN have been identified as causative for dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been described with biallelic PARN mutations. Pathogenic monoallelic germline changes in PARN have also been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF originated with the discovery of new monoallelic variants, and later studies found rare monoallelic PARN variants in five of 262 sporadic IPF patients undergoing whole-exome sequencing, supporting an emerging link now documented in more than 65 cases in the literature.1517 Nonetheless, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations related to PARN alterations is not yet well defined because of the relatively small number of documented cases.

Importantly, the patient carried a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which involves a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). Four out of five computational tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) predict this variant to have a detrimental impact on protein function. According to ACMG/AMP guidelines for classifying genetic variants, several criteria apply: PM1—the change is located in a crucial, well-established functional domain (R3H domain) that lacks known benign variants; PM2—the variant is not present, or is exceedingly rare, in population reference databases; PP3—multiple computational analyses suggest a harmful effect on the gene or its product; and PP2—PARN has few benign missense variants, with pathogenic missense mutations being a recognized mechanism for disease. Although the clinical significance remains undetermined, the gene’s known function in TBDs, as well as similarities with other PARN missense variants (including p.Lys59Arg, ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2, and p.Ala383Val, ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which are described as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar, underscores its potential relevance.

Various genes, distinct inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue may impact organ systems differently in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Furthermore, environmental and lifestyle elements can modulate the replicative abilities of organs in diverse ways. These intricacies may clarify our patient's clinical presentation, despite the detection of only a single monoallelic PARN variant.

While monoallelic PARN mutations are more frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than primary hematological issues, the patient’s cytopenia suggests a potential underlying TBD that could increase the vulnerability of bone marrow to hematopoietic stressors like severe vitamin B12 deficiency. Telomere attrition diminishes the replicative lifespan of hematopoietic stem cells, thereby hastening cellular senescence and elevating the likelihood of bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). The cytopenia in this patient was most plausibly due to severe vitamin B12 deficiency; however, the finding of a PARN variant highlights the need for continued clinical and hematological surveillance to monitor for any developing marrow failure features in an individual with this genetic predisposition.

Cirrhosis associated with telomere biology disorders (TBD) has frequently gone unrecognized, but new data indicates its occurrence may be higher than once thought. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography showed that 88.8% of adults carrying pathogenic mutations in either TERC or TERT had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), while 33.3% demonstrated cirrhosis. Similarly, one report detailed a case of a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who had monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Our case differed in that the patient possessed only a monoallelic PARN variant, yet manifested both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most often associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their potential effects on telomerase activity could explain the hepatic fibrosis usually attributed to TERC or TERT mutations. Collectively, these observations broaden the recognized clinical manifestations of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the necessity of recognizing hepatic fibrosis as a possible component of this phenotype.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, as well as assessment of telomere length by flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation. Management remains supportive, individualized to address symptom burden, the necessity for organ transplantation, and reduction of contributing risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a potential cure for patients with haematologic involvement. Although early experimental and limited clinical research indicates that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere extension, the sustained impact over time is still uncertain.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After conducting a thorough assessment to rule out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the responsible gene, and a comprehensive review of published cases enhanced our knowledge of this uncommon clinical picture. Supportive care was delivered, along with genetic counseling offered to her relatives. She comprehended her diagnosis and recognized that curative therapies were unavailable. Two weeks later at follow-up, her hematological parameters were unchanged, with no additional evidence of clinical decline.
