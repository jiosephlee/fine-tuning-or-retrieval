Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A female in her late twenties presented with progressive fatigue and widespread weakness over a five-year period. On physical examination, findings included features suggestive of early ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluations revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, liver fibrosis, and fibrosis of the lungs. Although nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the combination of cytopenias, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs indicated a possible underlying genetic disorder. Clinical exome analysis uncovered a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN have been linked with dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure 4. The relationship between the clinical manifestations and genetic findings supported the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the necessity of including TBDs in the differential diagnosis for young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem illness, even if typical mucocutaneous signs are missing. Prompt identification is essential for facilitating genetic counseling, ongoing monitoring, and consideration of bone marrow transplant in cases with disease progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are uncommon syndromes associated with premature ageing, resulting from Mendelian mutations in genes involved in telomere maintenance that produce unusually short or nonfunctional telomeres. Tissues composed of proliferative cells—such as bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and liver cells—are mainly impacted. TBDs encompass a range of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional conditions under this group include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this disorder spectrum, PARN mutations exhibit a unique clinical presentation: individuals with biallelic alterations present with autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with single allele variants are more frequently associated with telomere-mediated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report on a female patient in her late twenties who experienced extended fatigue and involvement of multiple organ systems, eventually receiving a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was evaluated at our tertiary care facility after experiencing generalised weakness and easy fatigability that had gradually intensified over a five-year period. Her functional ability steadily deteriorated during this time, leading to greater difficulty performing routine tasks relative to others her age. In the preceding three months, her symptoms had further progressed, significantly compromising her independence with self-care activities and necessitating help. Additional complaints included shortness of breath with exertion, occasional chest pain, unintended weight loss, and diminished appetite.

Upon assessment, the patient was found to be haemodynamically stable. Significant physical findings comprised pallor, jaundice, early onset greying of the hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, depapillated (bald) tongue.

Evaluation of the musculoskeletal system demonstrated thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of the thenar muscles on both sides. The abdomen was notable for an enlarged liver, palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin with a total span of 18 cm. On respiratory examination, breath sounds were diminished bilaterally in the infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular examination yielded no abnormal results.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory tests indicated pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed two types of erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—findings that are indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum testing demonstrated profound deficiencies of both vitamin B12 and folate. Assessment of iron parameters revealed increased serum iron and ferritin, which supports a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (table 1).

Bone marrow analysis displayed haematopoiesis of all three lineages, prominent erythroid expansion, and augmented iron stores. No signs of malignancy, abnormal development, or infiltration were observed; cytogenetic studies yielded normal results.

Evaluation for haemolysis showed increased levels of lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nevertheless, the diminished reticulocyte response and bone marrow features pointed more convincingly toward ineffective erythropoiesis than to destruction via peripheral immune mechanisms.

To assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was conducted and revealed no abnormalities on gross inspection. Histological analysis from the duodenal biopsy demonstrated mild chronic gastritis with decreased numbers of parietal cells, which could impair intrinsic factor secretion and thereby contribute to vitamin B12 deficiency. Nonetheless, the patient’s pronounced clinical manifestations seemed out of proportion to a nutritional deficit as the sole etiology.

Given the presence of hepatic dysfunction, additional imaging and evaluations were pursued. Abdominal ultrasonography identified both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, and evidence of cirrhosis as reflected by liver stiffness measured at 18.1 kPa. Inflammatory markers were elevated. Autoimmune screening, including tests for antinuclear antibodies and antibodies associated with autoimmune hepatitis, yielded negative results. Evaluation for infectious causes—including viral hepatitis, HIV, cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus—was predominantly negative, apart from a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory processes. The imaging revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation throughout the bone marrow of both the axial and appendicular skeleton. Both lung fields exhibited widespread ground-glass opacities along with numerous fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectasis. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were observed, yet no clear signs of malignancy or granulomatous disease were detected (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results were secondary hyperparathyroidism, disruptions in electrolytes (such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis identified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which accounted for her persistent bone pain and multiple fractures (see table 3). Electromyography along with nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, featuring chronic neurogenic alterations across both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Taken together—cytopenia, liver impairment, early onset greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—the profile suggested a possible syndromic or genetic cause.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This missense change affects a highly conserved amino acid and is anticipated to have a deleterious impact on protein function. Despite this prediction, the significance of this variant in relation to the disease is unclear; thus, it was classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenic status of this particular alteration has not been determined, the association of PARN and the patient’s clinical presentation align with the features observed in TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, weight loss, early onset of hair greying, and fatigue prompted evaluation for hematological, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, directing initial focus to possible nutritional deficiencies and bone marrow failure conditions.

The patient was found to have severe vitamin B12 deficiency, which explained several clinical and laboratory findings, as corroborated by bone marrow results indicating ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, following correction of the vitamin deficiencies, the persistent and disproportionate systemic abnormalities remained.

A transient consideration was given to an immune-mediated mechanism due to a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and elevated markers of inflammation. However, in light of the absence of hemolysis, negative results for autoimmune serologies, and no evidence of systemic autoimmune features, diagnoses such as autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease were deemed unlikely.

There was no identifiable cause for hepatic fibrosis (HF), as both infectious and autoimmune etiologies had been ruled out. The rare combination of early-onset ageing manifestations, sustained cytopenia, and fibrotic changes in both hepatic and pulmonary tissues in this young adult prompted consideration of an underlying, undiagnosed genetic disorder. Despite the initial lack of familial occurrence, the potential for a de novo genetic mutation was still plausible.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of resembling acquired diseases and demonstrating a spectrum of penetrance. Although uncommon, such a disorder may not display the characteristic mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), and instead present with more insidious symptoms including bone marrow failure, fibrosis of organs, and signs suggestive of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, which accounted for her diverse clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg once daily for 5 days, transitioned to weekly administration over 4 weeks, and subsequently moved to monthly maintenance therapy to address her profound deficiency.

Supportive measures comprised whole blood transfusions for symptomatic anemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg per day, and vitamin D at 60,000 IU weekly alongside elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) to manage osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were addressed with conventional supplementation.

She participated in physiotherapy sessions, and genetic counseling was provided to her family after the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive treatment, the patient experienced relief from symptoms. At the two-week follow-up, her hematologic parameters remained steady, her fatigue had lessened, and there were no additional clinical issues identified.

Ongoing, she is monitored in the outpatient setting to assess hepatic and respiratory function and to evaluate for advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the termini of chromosomes that serve to maintain genome stability by preventing chromosomal degradation. During normal cell proliferation, telomeres progressively shorten and, upon reaching a threshold length, induce cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Under homeostatic circumstances, the telomerase enzyme complex—composed of the catalytic subunit telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts this shortening by adding telomeric repeats. Mutations affecting the genes that encode these telomerase complex constituents disrupt enzyme activity, accelerate telomere erosion, and give rise to a spectrum of hereditary disorders known as telomere biology disorders (TBD).

DC was first linked to significantly reduced telomere length by X-linked recessive mutations in DKC1, establishing it as the prototype for telomere biology disorders (TBDs). The classical diagnostic criteria for DC include a mucocutaneous triad comprising oral leukoplakia, dystrophic nails, and reticular skin pigmentation, or the presence of any two of these findings together with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical manifestations encompass idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), and a heightened susceptibility to both hematological and solid tumors. Severe forms of TBD with onset in childhood, including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, are also attributed to mutations in genes associated with DC.

Telomere biology disorders can exhibit isolated mucocutaneous, organ-specific, or hematological symptoms and may become apparent in adulthood in the absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita triad. The underlying hereditary defect is often concealed by conditions such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, or hepatic and gastrointestinal disorders. When the characteristic triad is missing and clinical onset is delayed, affected individuals are often incorrectly diagnosed, particularly outside of specialty centers. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly found in telomere biology disorders, with frequent presentations as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

PARN, a member of a well-preserved exoribonuclease family, modulates gene expression by deadenylating mRNAs, thereby reducing the length of their poly(A) tails. Extensive sequencing of the 3’-end of TERC RNA indicated that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added post-transcriptionally to nuclear RNAs, facilitating their degradation and enabling proper 3’-end maturation of TERC.

PARN is classified among the 20% of genes least permissive to uncommon, disruptive mutations, underscoring its crucial function and supporting the haploinsufficiency hypothesis in heterozygous individuals. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are acknowledged contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have involved biallelic PARN variants. Single-gene pathogenic mutations have also been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The connection between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was initially identified with newly discovered monoallelic variants; subsequent research documented monoallelic PARN rare variants in 5 out of 262 sporadic IPF patients subjected to whole-exome sequencing, thus establishing a relationship that has since been noted in more than 65 cases in the literature.1517 Nevertheless, the full spectrum of phenotypes linked to PARN alterations remains largely uncertain due to the scarcity of case reports.

Importantly, this patient carried a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). Four out of five computational tools for missense prediction (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) suggest that this variant is likely to disrupt function. Based on ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines, several supportive criteria are fulfilled: PM1—the change is located in a key functional domain (R3H) where benign variants are not observed; PM2—this variant is absent or exceedingly rare in population datasets; PP3—multiple algorithms indicate a harmful impact on the gene or its product; PP2—benign missense variants are rare in PARN, and pathogenic missense mutations are a known disease mechanism for this gene. While the clinical significance of the variant is still unclear, it is considered potentially relevant to TBDs due to PARN’s established function and similarities with other PARN missense variants—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—that are listed in ClinVar as ‘likely pathogenic’.

The interplay of multiple genes, diverse inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can affect specific organ systems differently in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Furthermore, environmental exposures and individual lifestyle choices may influence the replicative abilities of various organ systems to differing extents. In the case of our patient, these factors may account for the clinical presentation despite identification of only a single pathogenic variant in PARN.

While monoallelic PARN mutations tend to be more frequently linked with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) than with primary hematologic abnormalities, our patient's cytopenia raises the possibility of an underlying TBD that could increase the bone marrow's vulnerability to hematopoietic stressors such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Telomere attrition impairs the replicative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, promoting premature cellular senescence and heightened susceptibility to bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the cytopenia in this case was most likely due to significant vitamin B12 deficiency, the presence of the PARN variant highlights the need for regular clinical and hematologic surveillance to monitor for the development of any bone marrow failure phenotype in this individual who may have a genetic predisposition.

Cirrhosis associated with telomere biology disorders (TBD) has frequently been overlooked, though new data indicate it could be more common than once thought. In a retrospective study utilizing transient elastography from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, 88.8% of adults with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT were found to have at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with cirrhosis present in 33.3% of cases. The literature also documents a similar case featuring a patient with pancytopenia and HF, who was heterozygous for pathogenic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Conversely, our patient carried a single monoallelic PARN mutation but still manifested both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their downstream effect on telomerase activity might also explain the development of HF typically associated with TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations broaden the recognized clinical manifestations of PARN-related TBD and highlight the necessity of recognizing HF as a feature within this spectrum.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis utilizing next-generation sequencing, along with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assays to assess telomere length. Management at present focuses on supportive care, individualized according to symptom burden, the requirement for organ transplantation, and minimizing risk factors; however, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may provide a curative option for those with haematologic involvement. Although preliminary laboratory and limited clinical data indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere extension, the prolonged consequences of such treatment have yet to be determined.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a comprehensive assessment that ruled out more typical etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the responsible gene. An in-depth review of existing literature expanded our insights into this uncommon clinical scenario. Supportive care was offered, alongside genetic counseling for her relatives. She comprehended the specifics of her illness and recognized that curative treatments were not an option. During her follow-up after two weeks, her hematological parameters were unchanged, and there were no additional indications of worsening clinical status.
