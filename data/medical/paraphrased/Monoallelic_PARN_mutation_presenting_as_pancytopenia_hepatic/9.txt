Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year history characterized by worsening fatigue and widespread weakness. Physical examination revealed evidence of premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory assessment identified pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the combination of cytopenias, early greying, osteoporosis, liver and lung fibrosis led to suspicion of an underlying genetic condition. Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single allele PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), interpreted as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are known to be linked with dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure type 4. The relationship between the patient’s clinical and genetic findings supported the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs in young adults with unexplained, multisystem involvement, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous findings. Timely identification is vital for informing genetic counseling, ongoing monitoring, and evaluation for bone marrow transplantation in cases that show progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are uncommon syndromes characterized by early-onset aging, resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere function, which in turn cause telomeres to become pathologically short or impaired. Tissues with high proliferative activity—including the bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver cells—are most commonly impacted. The spectrum of TBD encompasses various clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional disorders within this category are Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of diseases, mutations in PARN are associated with unique clinical characteristics: individuals with biallelic alterations develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those carrying monoallelic variants are more frequently linked to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We describe a woman in her late 20s with prolonged fatigue and multisystem involvement who was ultimately diagnosed with a TBD.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary center due to a five-year progression of increasing generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. During this period, her functional status deteriorated, limiting her capacity to carry out routine tasks in comparison to individuals of similar age. In the preceding three months, her weakness intensified, leading to difficulty in personal care and necessitating help from others. Additional symptoms included shortness of breath upon exertion, occasional chest pain, unintentional weight loss, and reduced appetite.

On examination, she was haemodynamically stable. Notable findings included pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae.

On musculoskeletal assessment, thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of the thenar muscles on both sides were noted. Examination of the abdomen identified hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. Pulmonary evaluation showed diminished breath sounds at both infra-scapular areas. Cardiovascular findings were within normal limits.

Investigations:

The patient's initial labs revealed pancytopenia along with a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified dimorphic red blood cells, the presence of basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmented nuclei, all findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Additional serum analyses showed marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Assessment of iron parameters revealed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, further supporting the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow analysis demonstrated trilineage hematopoiesis, prominent erythroid hyperplasia, and increased iron deposition, with no signs indicative of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltration; cytogenetic studies returned within normal limits.

Laboratory indicators of haemolysis were present, including raised lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The direct antiglobulin test showed positivity for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nonetheless, the diminished reticulocyte response combined with the marrow findings supported a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than immune-mediated peripheral red cell destruction.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted to investigate possible gastrointestinal malabsorption, and the examination was grossly unremarkable. Biopsy of the duodenum revealed mild chronic gastritis and a decrease in parietal cell numbers, a finding that could explain B12 malabsorption due to compromised intrinsic factor secretion. Nevertheless, the degree of the patient's symptoms seemed excessive when compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency by itself.

Liver dysfunction led to additional imaging and evaluation. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography identified grade II fatty liver disease (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) along with cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). There was an elevation in inflammatory markers. Nevertheless, tests for autoimmune conditions, such as antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies, returned negative. Evaluation for infectious causes—covering viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus—was predominantly negative, except for a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to investigate for underlying infiltrative or inflammatory processes. The imaging revealed patchy uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose throughout both the axial and appendicular bone marrow. There were also widespread ground-glass opacities accompanied by multiple fibrotic regions and subpleural atelectasis observed in both lungs. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were present. No clear indications of malignancy or granulomatous disease were identified (see figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results were secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes (low calcium and potassium), vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis identified via dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which accounted for her ongoing bone pain and repeated fractures (see table 3). Electromyography and nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, with chronic neurogenic alterations detected in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Given the constellation of findings—cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic aetiology was suspected.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense change in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This substitution affects an evolutionarily conserved amino acid and is anticipated to impair protein function. Nonetheless, the pathogenic contribution of this variant to the patient’s condition could not be determined, so it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the exact impact of this specific variant on disease is unclear, alterations in PARN and the patient’s presenting features align with the range seen in TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintended weight loss, early-onset hair greying, and persistent fatigue in this patient prompted consideration of hematological, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. A significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, which directed the differential diagnosis toward causes such as nutritional insufficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

A marked deficiency in vitamin B12 was identified promptly and explained various clinical findings, as corroborated by bone marrow findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite rectification of these deficiencies, the extent and persistence of the systemic dysfunction exceeded what would be expected.

An immune-related mechanism was initially contemplated in light of the positive DAT result and increased inflammatory markers. However, the lack of haemolysis, negative autoimmune serological testing, and no systemic manifestations made autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease unlikely.

The cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) could not be identified, as both autoimmune and infectious origins were ruled out. The unusual combination of early-onset ageing characteristics, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both the liver and lungs in a young adult raised the possibility of an undiagnosed genetic condition. While no family history was present to support a hereditary syndrome, the potential for a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This suggested the possibility of an underlying TBD capable of resembling acquired disorders and displaying a range of penetrance. While uncommon, it may not exhibit the characteristic mucocutaneous triad seen in DC, but rather present insidiously with bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs indicative of premature aging.

This was substantiated by genetic analysis, which identified a pathogenic monoallelic variant in the PARN gene, providing a unifying explanation for the constellation of her clinical features.

Treatment:

The patient was treated with intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance to correct severe deficiency.

Supportive management involved administering whole blood transfusions to address symptomatic anemia, along with daily oral folic acid at 1 mg and vitamin D at a dose of 60,000 IU weekly combined with 1,000 mg of elemental calcium daily to treat osteoporosis. Standard electrolyte supplementation was provided to correct any electrolyte disturbances.

She also underwent physiotherapy, and her family received genetic counselling following diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient experienced symptomatic relief after addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive management. At the 2-week follow-up, her hematologic parameters remained stable, her fatigue had lessened, and no additional clinical issues were identified.

She continues to receive regular outpatient follow-up for surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function and to monitor the progression of bone marrow or systemic involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences at the termini of chromosomes that safeguard genomic stability by protecting chromosomes from deterioration. During cellular proliferation, telomeres progressively diminish in length, ultimately arriving at a threshold that induces cell senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein enzyme complex composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—is responsible for sustaining telomere length by adding repetitive sequences. Mutations in the genes coding for telomerase constituents disrupt its activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and giving rise to a collection of hereditary disorders known as telomere biology disorders (TBD).

The discovery of X-linked recessive pathogenic mutations in DKC1 was the first to connect dyskeratosis congenita (DC) with significantly reduced telomere length, establishing DC as the archetype among telomere biology disorders (TBDs). DC is traditionally characterized by the mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation of the skin, or by the presence of any two of these findings in conjunction with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and a heightened risk for both hematologic and solid tumor malignancies. More severe telomere biology disorder variants with childhood onset—such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—also arise from mutations in genes associated with DC.

Telomere biology disorders can exhibit manifestations limited to mucocutaneous areas, specific organs, or hematologic abnormalities, and may initially appear in adults lacking the hallmark triad of dyskeratosis congenita. Commonly, the underlying genetic abnormality is obscured by diseases such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, heart failure, or gastrointestinal conditions. In the absence of the typical triad and because of delayed symptom onset, misdiagnosis is common, particularly outside of specialized medical centers. Single-copy variants in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most frequently detected in TBDs, with clinical presentations often mimicking idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family, regulates gene expression by reducing the length of the mRNA poly(A) tail via deadenylation. Advanced sequencing of the TERC RNA 3' end has demonstrated that PARN is necessary for eliminating post-transcriptionally added oligo(A) tails, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation and are essential for the proper 3'-end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks among the 20% of genes that show the least tolerance for rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical biological function and supporting haploinsufficiency in heterozygous individuals. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN are established causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as other conditions within the DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome have been documented with biallelic PARN mutations. Additionally, heterozygous germline pathogenic PARN variants have been linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure syndrome type 4 (PFBMFT4). The association between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was first observed with newly identified heterozygous mutations, with follow-up studies detecting rare heterozygous PARN variants in five out of 262 patients with sporadic IPF analyzed by whole-exome sequencing. This connection has since been confirmed in over 65 individuals in the literature.1517 Nonetheless, a comprehensive understanding of the full clinical phenotype linked to PARN mutations is lacking, as only a small number of cases have been described.

Of particular interest, our patient possessed a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which affects a highly conserved amino acid located within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). According to predictions from four out of five in silico missense analysis platforms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), this variant is likely to have a harmful impact on function. Utilizing ACMG/AMP criteria for variant interpretation, this alteration fulfills multiple supporting criteria: PM1—the amino acid change lies within a crucial and well-characterized functional domain (the R3H domain) where no benign variants have been identified; PM2—the variant is either absent or exceptionally infrequent in reference population datasets; PP3—numerous computational algorithms indicate a deleterious effect on gene or protein function; and PP2—PARN demonstrates low benign missense variability, with missense mutations frequently underlying disease. Although the precise clinical implications are undetermined, its potential importance to TBDs is substantiated by PARN’s recognized function and by comparable missense mutations, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which are classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar resource.

A variety of genes, modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue may impact organ systems in telomere biology disorders (TBDs) to varying degrees. Furthermore, factors such as environment and lifestyle can affect the proliferative potential of various organs differently. In our patient, these intricate factors might account for the clinical presentation, even though only a single heterozygous PARN variant was detected.

While heterozygous PARN mutations are predominantly linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis rather than primary hematologic presentations, the cytopenias detected in our patient suggest the possibility of an underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD) that could render the bone marrow more vulnerable to hematopoietic challenges, such as marked vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres diminish the proliferative reserve of hematopoietic stem cells, hastening cellular aging and heightening the likelihood of bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although our patient’s cytopenia was most plausibly due to pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, identification of a PARN variant emphasizes the necessity for continued clinical and hematological surveillance to monitor for the emergence of marrow failure features in this individual with a genetic predisposition.

Cirrhosis associated with TBD has historically been underdiagnosed; nonetheless, recent studies indicate it might occur more frequently than once believed. A retrospective review utilizing transient elastography from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry demonstrated that 88.8% of adult individuals with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with 33.3% presenting with cirrhosis. The literature also details a case involving pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis in a patient heterozygous for pathogenic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, the patient discussed in this report had a single PARN mutation but nonetheless developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While heterozygous PARN mutations are most frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the consequent effect on telomerase activity may explain the development of hepatic fibrosis usually observed with mutations in TERT or TERC. These observations broaden the known clinical manifestations of PARN-associated TBDs and highlight the necessity of including hepatic fibrosis within its phenotypic spectrum.

Definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, along with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays for telomere length assessment. Management is primarily supportive, individualized based on symptom intensity, necessity for organ transplantation, and risk reduction strategies; however, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may provide a curative option for those with haematological involvement. Although limited preclinical and early clinical evidence indicates that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere extension, their long-term impact has yet to be established.

The patient exhibited signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough workup that ruled out more frequent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible gene. Comprehensive literature review further illuminated this uncommon clinical scenario. She received supportive care, and her family was offered genetic counseling. The patient comprehended the character of her illness and recognized that curative therapies were unavailable. On reassessment two weeks later, her blood parameters were unchanged, and she showed no additional evidence of clinical decline.
