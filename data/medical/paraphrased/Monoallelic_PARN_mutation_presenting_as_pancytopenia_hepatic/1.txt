Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported experiencing progressively worsening fatigue and generalized weakness over a period of five years. Clinical evaluation demonstrated premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory assessments revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were corrected, the persistent presence of cytopenias, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs prompted suspicion of an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome analysis revealed a heterozygous missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Germline pathogenic alterations in PARN are linked with dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The concordance between clinical presentation and genetic findings in this case pointed towards a telomere biology disorder (TBD) diagnosis. This report underscores the necessity of evaluating TBDs in younger adults presenting with unexplained multisystem involvement, irrespective of the absence of traditional mucocutaneous indicators. Timely identification is vital for informed genetic counselling, patient monitoring, and considering bone marrow transplantation in individuals with progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) constitute a group of uncommon syndromes associated with accelerated ageing, resulting from inherited mutations in genes implicated in telomere maintenance that produce either unusually short or malfunctioning telomeres. These conditions chiefly impact tissues with high cellular turnover, such as the bone marrow, epidermis, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and hepatocytes. The clinical spectrum of TBDs is broad, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic representative. Additional forms encompass Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this collection of disorders, PARN mutations present a unique clinical picture: individuals harboring biallelic variants develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with monoallelic variants are more commonly affected by telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report on a female patient in her late twenties who exhibited persistent fatigue and symptoms affecting multiple organ systems, leading to a diagnosis of a TBD.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties arrived at our tertiary care center, reporting a five-year progression of generalized weakness accompanied by early-onset fatigue. Her level of functioning had steadily diminished during this period, and she became increasingly unable to manage routine daily tasks relative to individuals her age. In the preceding three months, her symptoms intensified, ultimately limiting her independence with self-care and necessitating external support. Additional complaints included shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest discomfort, unintentional weight loss, and decreased appetite.

Upon assessment, the patient maintained haemodynamic stability. Significant observations comprised pallor, icterus, early onset greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, bald tongue indicative of lingual papillae atrophy.

The musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic scoliosis as well as atrophy of the thenar muscles bilaterally. Abdominal assessment demonstrated hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring a span of 18 cm. Respiratory assessment detected diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular regions. No abnormalities were found on cardiovascular examination.

Investigations:

On initial laboratory assessment, the patient exhibited pancytopenia accompanied by a markedly decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed the presence of dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation, all of which are indicative of disrupted erythropoiesis. Biochemical analyses demonstrated pronounced deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel testing showed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, further corroborating a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (table 1).

Bone marrow analysis demonstrated trilineage hematopoiesis, pronounced erythroid hyperplasia, and heightened iron reserves, while findings did not suggest malignancy, dysplastic changes, or marrow infiltration; cytogenetic studies were within normal limits.

Evaluation of hemolysis revealed elevated lactate dehydrogenase and increased indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) yielded a positive result for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nevertheless, the combination of the poor reticulocyte response and the marrow findings indicated that the underlying process was primarily ineffective erythropoiesis, rather than peripheral immune-mediated destruction.

In order to assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted and appeared grossly unremarkable. Histological analysis of a duodenal biopsy demonstrated mild chronic gastritis with a decrease in parietal cell numbers, which could have led to diminished intrinsic factor and thereby contributed to B12 malabsorption. Nonetheless, the pronounced clinical manifestations seemed excessive when considered in light of nutritional deficiency alone.

Due to evidence of hepatic impairment, additional diagnostic imaging and evaluation were initiated. Abdominal ultrasonography detected the presence of hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography findings were notable for grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) along with features of cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Elevated levels of inflammatory markers were observed. Despite this, serological tests for autoimmunity, including antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies, returned negative results. Screening for infectious etiologies—such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus—was predominantly negative, except for the detection of Epstein-Barr virus IgG positivity (table 2).

To investigate potential hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease, a positron emission tomography–CT scan was carried out. The imaging revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within both the axial and appendicular bone marrow compartments. Additionally, diffuse ground-glass opacities accompanied by several fibrotic alterations and subpleural atelectasis were observed across both pulmonary fields. Hepatosplenomegaly and the presence of ascites were also identified. Notably, there were no findings suggestive of clear malignancy or granulomatous pathology (figures 1 and 2).

Further investigations revealed the presence of secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes (including hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis as evidenced by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry; these abnormalities accounted for her recurrent episodes of bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyography alongside nerve conduction studies identified an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, characterized by chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscles.

In light of this pattern—encompassing cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early-onset greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a genetic or syndromic cause was considered likely.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This variant affects a residue that is highly conserved and is predicted to impair protein function. Nevertheless, its direct contribution to the disease phenotype remains unclear; thus, it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite the unresolved pathogenicity of this particular variant, both the gene implicated and the patient’s observed clinical manifestations align with the typical presentations of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, premature greying, and persistent fatigue in the patient prompted evaluation for underlying hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. A significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested a defect in red cell production, leading the diagnostic approach toward possibilities such as nutrient insufficiency and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was rapidly identified, accounting for many of the clinical and laboratory abnormalities, with bone marrow studies further corroborating ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite appropriate correction of these deficiencies, the patient’s multi-system symptoms continued without adequate resolution.

An immune-mediated pathology was entertained due to the presence of a positive DAT and increased inflammatory markers. However, the lack of hemolysis, negative results on autoimmune serologies, and the absence of features typical of systemic autoimmune conditions ultimately made diagnoses such as autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease unlikely.

The origin of hepatic fibrosis (HF) remained obscure, as both autoimmune and infectious etiologies were ruled out. The combination of early-onset aging characteristics, ongoing cytopenia, and progressive fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young adult prompted consideration of a rare underlying genetic disorder. Despite the lack of familial incidence, the possibility of a de novo genetic alteration could not be discounted.

This prompted consideration of a TBD capable of simulating acquired disorders and exhibiting diverse levels of penetrance. While uncommon, such a disorder might not display the hallmark mucocutaneous triad typical of DC, instead presenting insidiously with bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs indicative of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing monoallelic mutation in the PARN gene, which offered a cohesive rationale for the patient's array of clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for 5 days, then once weekly for 4 weeks, and subsequently transitioned to monthly maintenance dosing to address the pronounced deficiency.

Additional supportive measures involved whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anaemia, daily oral folic acid (1 mg), and weekly vitamin D (60 000 IU) along with elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) to treat osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with conventional supplementation.

She participated in physiotherapy, and her family was provided with genetic counselling after the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive treatment, the patient experienced an alleviation of symptoms. Over the course of the subsequent two-week follow-up period, her blood counts remained steady, her fatigue lessened, and no additional clinical issues were identified.

She remains under routine outpatient observation to monitor hepatic and pulmonary function, as well as to track any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the termini of chromosomes, functioning to safeguard the genome by preventing chromosomal degradation. During successive rounds of cell division, these telomeric regions progressively shorten until a threshold is reached, at which point cells enter senescence or undergo programmed cell death. Normally, telomere maintenance is carried out by telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein enzyme complex comprising telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which acts to elongate telomeres. Mutations in genes coding for elements of the telomerase complex disrupt this maintenance mechanism, thereby accelerating telomere shortening and causing a spectrum of inherited disorders known as TBD.

The initial connection between DC and extremely short telomeres was identified through X-linked recessive pathogenic variants in DKC1, positioning DC as the archetype of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). The classic diagnostic criteria for DC involve the mucocutaneous triad, consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or alternatively, two of these clinical signs accompanied by bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical features of DC include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), HF, as well as a heightened susceptibility to both haematological and solid cancers. Furthermore, certain severe forms of childhood-onset TBD, such as HH syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, also originate from mutations in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can manifest as isolated involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or haematological abnormalities, and may appear in adulthood in the absence of the classical DC triad. The underlying inherited mutations are often obscured by coexisting conditions such as aplastic anaemia, BMFS, IPF, HF, or gastrointestinal disorders. Owing to the lack of characteristic clinical features and the tendency for delayed presentation, these cases are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly outside specialised centres. Monoallelic variants in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly detected in TBDs, with frequent clinical presentations resembling IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family, facilitates deadenylation by trimming the poly(A) tails of mRNA, thereby influencing gene expression. Comprehensive sequencing analyses of the TERC RNA 3-prime end have demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails that are added post-transcriptionally and mark nuclear RNAs for decay, a step necessary for the proper 3-prime-end maturation of TERC.

PARN is classified among the top 20% of genes that exhibit the lowest tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, reinforcing its crucial biological function and supporting the model of haploinsufficiency among carriers. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are established etiological factors in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and the broader disease spectrum encompassing DC/TBD. Additionally, cases of HH syndrome have been associated with biallelic mutations in PARN. Pathogenic monoallelic germline variants in this gene have also been linked to telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was first suggested following the discovery of novel monoallelic variants, and later studies revealed rare monoallelic PARN variants in five of 262 individuals with sporadic IPF subjected to whole-exome sequencing. This relationship has gained further support, with over 65 cases described in published studies.1517 Nevertheless, the full clinical phenotype associated with PARN mutations remains incompletely characterized due to the scarcity of documented cases.

Importantly, our patient carries a heterozygous missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). According to four out of five computational missense variant prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), this variant is likely deleterious in function. Application of ACMG/AMP variant interpretation guidelines supports the following evidence: PM1—this alteration is located in a crucial, well-characterized functional domain (R3H domain) lacking benign variation; PM2—the variant is not present, or present at extremely low frequency, in reference population databases; PP3—multiple in silico algorithms anticipate a detrimental impact on the gene or its product; and PP2—the PARN gene typically shows a low incidence of benign missense variation, while disease is frequently associated with missense changes in this gene. Although the clinical consequences of this specific variant are unresolved, its possible significance in TBDs is reinforced both by the established biological function of PARN and by comparisons to other PARN missense substitutions (such as p.Lys59Arg, ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2, and p.Ala383Val, ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1) that are catalogued as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database.

The involvement of multiple genes, differing modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and phenomena such as somatic genetic rescue can lead to varying impacts on different organ systems in TBDs. Moreover, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices may distinctively alter the proliferative ability of individual organs. These layers of complexity may help account for our patient's clinical presentation, even though only a single heterozygous PARN variant was detected.

Monoallelic PARN variants are most often linked to IPF rather than to primary haematological abnormalities; however, the occurrence of cytopenia in our patient suggests the possibility of an underlying TBD, which could predispose the bone marrow to heightened vulnerability during periods of haematopoietic stress, such as in the case of profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Reduction in telomere length restricts the self-renewal potential of haematopoietic stem cells, promoting premature cellular aging and elevating the likelihood of BMFS. Although the cytopenia in this patient was likely driven primarily by the significant vitamin B12 deficiency, the discovery of a PARN variant highlights the need for continued clinical and haematological evaluation, to monitor for possible progression toward marrow failure in this at-risk individual.

Cirrhosis in the context of TBD has historically been under-diagnosed, but accumulating data indicate it is more widespread than once assumed. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography reported that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate HF, with cirrhosis present in 33.3% of cases. Additionally, a published case described an individual with pancytopenia and HF, harboring monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Notably, our patient possessed a monoallelic PARN mutation alone and still developed hepatic as well as pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most often linked to IPF, they may disrupt telomerase activity and thereby lead to HF, similar to the effect seen in TERT or TERC mutations. These observations broaden the recognized clinical manifestations of PARN-related TBDs, highlighting the necessity of recognizing HF as a potential aspect of its phenotype.

The definitive approach for confirming a diagnosis of suspected TBD involves genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing in conjunction with flow cytometry-assisted fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays for telomere length assessment. Presently, management focuses on supportive care, individualized according to symptom intensity, the necessity for organ transplantation, and addressing relevant risk factors; haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option for those exhibiting haematologic involvement. Although early evidence from preclinical experiments and limited clinical trials indicates that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere lengthening, their long-term impact has yet to be fully established.

The patient exhibited clinical characteristics consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. A thorough workup ruled out more prevalent etiologies, after which whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible gene. An in-depth review of the literature enhanced our insight into this uncommon presentation. Management focused on supportive care, alongside offering genetic counseling to her relatives. She comprehended the prognosis and acknowledged the absence of curative therapies. At her follow-up two weeks later, her hematological parameters were unchanged, and she displayed no additional clinical decline.
