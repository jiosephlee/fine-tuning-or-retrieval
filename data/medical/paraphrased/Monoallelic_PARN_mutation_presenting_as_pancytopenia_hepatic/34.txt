Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported a five-year duration of worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. On examination, she exhibited features of accelerated ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both liver and spleen. Laboratory findings revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the combination of cytopenias, premature hair greying, osteoporosis, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis suggested an underlying genetic condition. Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), designated as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN have been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The observed clinical and genetic findings in this patient were consistent with a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underscores the necessity of considering TBDs in young adults with multisystem disease of unclear etiology, particularly when classic mucocutaneous manifestations are not present. Prompt identification is key to directing genetic counselling, monitoring, and evaluating the potential need for bone marrow transplantation in cases with disease progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes of accelerated aging resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, ultimately causing excessively short or impaired telomeres. Rapidly proliferating tissues—including bone marrow, epidermis, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver cells—are most vulnerable to these changes. TBDs encompass a range of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional conditions in this group are Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of diseases, mutations in PARN are associated with unique clinical presentations; individuals with mutations affecting both alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single allele mutations are typically linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report the case of a female patient in her late twenties who experienced persistent fatigue and symptoms affecting multiple organ systems, ultimately leading to a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was admitted to our tertiary care facility, reporting a five-year progression of generalised weakness and increasing early fatigue. Her functional status had gradually deteriorated, limiting her performance of daily tasks relative to individuals of her own age group. Within the past three months, her symptoms intensified to the extent that basic self-care became difficult, necessitating help from others. Additionally, she described experiencing shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest pain, unintended weight loss, and decreased appetite.

Upon physical assessment, the patient exhibited stable haemodynamic parameters. Significant observations were pallor, icterus, early hair greying, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a depapillated tongue suggestive of lingual papillae atrophy.

The musculoskeletal assessment demonstrated thoracic scoliosis and wasting of both thenar eminences. Abdominal evaluation revealed an enlarged liver palpated 5 cm below the right costal margin with a total span of 18 cm. On respiratory examination, diminished breath sounds were detected in both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular assessment yielded no abnormal findings.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment identified pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, the presence of Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—findings that indicate ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum testing confirmed profound deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results showed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, further supporting the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow evaluation demonstrated trilineage hematopoiesis, marked erythroid proliferation, and heightened iron stores, with no evidence for malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltration; cytogenetic studies returned normal findings.

Evidence of hemolysis included raised lactate dehydrogenase levels and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+), while the indirect test remained negative. In spite of these findings, the reduced reticulocyte response and bone marrow characteristics favored ineffective erythropoiesis over immune-mediated peripheral destruction.

To assess for possible gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was undertaken and found to be grossly unremarkable. Histology from duodenal biopsies demonstrated mild chronic gastritis and a reduction in parietal cell number, which may have contributed to vitamin B12 deficiency by limiting intrinsic factor synthesis. Nonetheless, the clinical manifestations seemed excessively severe to be accounted for solely by nutritional insufficiency.

Given evidence of hepatic impairment, additional imaging and evaluation were pursued. Abdominal ultrasonography identified both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography revealed grade II steatosis of the liver, with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, alongside evidence of cirrhosis indicated by a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. Inflammatory markers were elevated. Autoimmune serological tests, including antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis panels, were negative. Infectious investigations for viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus did not reveal significant abnormalities, except for positive IgG to Epstein-Barr virus (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was carried out to investigate possible hidden infiltrative or inflammatory conditions. The imaging revealed uneven uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose within both axial and appendicular bone marrow. There was widespread ground-glass opacity throughout both lungs, accompanied by several fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectasis. Additional findings included hepatosplenomegaly and ascites. No clear signs of malignancy or granulomatous disease were observed (figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation revealed secondary hyperparathyroidism, abnormalities in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, along with vitamin D deficiency. Osteoporosis was established through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, accounting for her repeated bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography combined with nerve conduction studies showed an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, featuring chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Considering the collective clinical features—cytopenia, impaired hepatic function, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic cause was contemplated.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This mutation affects a residue that is highly conserved, and computational analyses suggest it is detrimental to protein function. Nonetheless, its contribution to the disease has not been determined and it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite uncertainty regarding its pathogenicity, the association of PARN and the patient's phenotype aligns with the telomere biology disorder (TBD) spectrum.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, weight loss, early onset greying, and fatigue prompted an evaluation for haematological, nutritional, and autoimmune conditions. A significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, which guided the initial focus toward possible nutrient depletion and bone marrow failure disorders.

Early testing revealed pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, explaining multiple clinical manifestations and corroborated by bone marrow findings of suboptimal erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, following correction of these deficiencies, persistent and disproportionately extensive systemic dysfunction was noted.

A brief consideration was given to an immune-mediated mechanism in light of a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and increased inflammatory markers. However, the absence of haemolytic evidence, negative autoimmune antibody panels, and lack of further systemic autoimmune signs made autoimmune cytopenias or connective tissue diseases unlikely.

The underlying cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was unclear, as both infectious and autoimmune origins were ruled out. The presence of premature ageing signs, chronic cytopenia, and fibrotic changes in both the lungs and liver in a young adult raised concern for a concealed genetic condition. Even though a family history was not apparent, which might suggest a non-hereditary condition, the potential for a de novo genetic mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder that may resemble acquired diseases and demonstrate diverse penetrance. While uncommon, such conditions might be without the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita, and instead, present more insidiously with bone marrow failure, fibrosis of organs, and signs consistent with premature aging.

Genetic testing supported this hypothesis by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous variant in the PARN gene, accounting for her wide range of symptoms.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dosage of 1000 µg per day for 5 days, continued with weekly administration over 4 weeks, and subsequently switched to monthly maintenance to address the pronounced deficiency.

Additional supportive therapies involved whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anaemia, as well as oral folic acid at 1 mg daily and vitamin D at 60 000 IU weekly alongside elemental calcium at 1000 mg daily to treat osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were resolved using conventional supplementation regimens.

She participated in physiotherapy, and genetic counselling was provided to her family following identification of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive treatment, the patient experienced relief from symptoms. At her 2-week follow-up visit, hematologic parameters remained steady, fatigue had lessened, and no additional clinical issues were identified.

Ongoing outpatient monitoring is maintained to observe hepatic and pulmonary function, as well as to assess for changes in bone marrow or broader systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the termini of chromosomes that function to maintain genome stability by preventing chromosomal degradation. Each time a cell undergoes division, telomeres progressively decrease in length until a threshold is reached, prompting cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts this shortening by extending telomeres. Mutations in the genes responsible for components of the telomerase complex compromise its activity, leading to accelerated telomere shortening and giving rise to a group of inherited disorders referred to as telomere biology disorders (TBD).

X-linked recessive mutations in DKC1 were the first to demonstrate the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, thereby establishing DC as the paradigm among telomere biology disorders (TBDs). DC is traditionally characterized by a mucocutaneous triad comprising oral leukoplakia, dystrophic nails, and reticulate skin pigmentation, or alternatively, by the presence of two of these features along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized complications include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and elevated risks of both hematologic and solid tumors. More severe forms of TBD with early childhood onset—such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—also arise from mutations in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can appear with singular involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or the hematologic system, and may arise in adults without the characteristic DC triad. Commonly, the inherited abnormality is concealed by disorders such as aplastic anemia, BMFS, IPF, or HF, as well as gastrointestinal pathologies. In the absence of the classic triad and because of late onset, affected individuals are often misdiagnosed, particularly when managed outside specialized facilities. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are frequently found in TBDs, which often manifest clinically as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-conserved group of exoribonucleases, functions to regulate gene expression by reducing the length of mRNA poly(A) tails through deadenylation. Extensive sequencing of the 3-prime end of TERC RNA demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails that nuclear RNAs gain after transcription, which mark them for degradation and are necessary for proper maturation of TERC at its 3-prime end.

PARN is classified among the 20% of genes that show minimal tolerance to rare and disruptive mutations, highlighting its critical function and supporting haploinsufficiency in heterozygous individuals. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN are acknowledged as causative for dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as for broader DC/TBD syndromes. Homozygous PARN alterations have been observed in individuals with HH syndrome. Single-copy germline pathogenic mutations have been linked to telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) originated with the discovery of novel monoallelic mutations, and later investigations identified monoallelic rare PARN variants in five out of 262 sporadic IPF cases analyzed through whole-exome sequencing. This relationship, now documented in more than 65 cases, is increasingly recognized.1517 Despite this, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations associated with PARN mutations remains largely undefined due to the relatively small number of published cases.

Importantly, this patient carried a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). Four out of five in silico tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, SIFT) predict this change will negatively impact protein function. According to ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines, the variant fulfills multiple supporting criteria: PM1—the amino acid substitution lies within a critical, well-characterized functional domain (R3H domain) where benign variants have not been described; PM2—the variant is either not detected or extremely rare in large population datasets; PP3—several computational algorithms indicate a detrimental effect on gene function or gene product; PP2—PARN is characterized by low rates of benign missense variation, and disease mechanisms often involve missense changes. While its clinical significance remains unclear, its possible association with TBDs is reinforced by the gene’s known involvement and similarity to other PARN missense variants—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—classified as 'likely pathogenic' within ClinVar.

A range of genetic factors—including multiple genes, diverse inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue—may have distinct impacts on the organ systems involved in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Moreover, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices can variably affect the replication capabilities of these organ systems. Considering these factors, they may account for the clinical presentation observed in our patient, even though only a single heterozygous PARN variant was found.

Monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than primary hematological conditions; however, the patient’s cytopenia suggests an underlying TBD might increase bone marrow vulnerability to hematopoietic stressors, such as severe vitamin B12 deficiency. Reduced telomere length impairs the replicative ability of hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in premature cellular aging and heightened risk for bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the patient’s cytopenia was most likely due to pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, identification of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematologic surveillance to monitor for the possible emergence of bone marrow failure manifestations in this genetically susceptible patient.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has historically been overlooked, yet recent studies indicate it may occur more frequently than initially believed. Data from a retrospective review at the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, utilizing transient elastography, showed that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT demonstrated at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with 33.3% meeting criteria for cirrhosis. A similar report detailed a patient with both pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who carried heterozygous variants in PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our patient possessed only a monoallelic PARN variant and still exhibited both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While heterozygous PARN mutations are most frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their negative effect on telomerase activity may explain the development of HF usually associated with TERC or TERT alterations. Collectively, these observations broaden the recognized phenotype of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the necessity of including hepatic fibrosis in its clinical considerations.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, along with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assessments for telomere length. Current therapeutic strategies focus on supportive care, adapted to the patient's symptom severity, requirements for organ transplantation, and management of risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a potential cure for individuals exhibiting hematological features. Although preliminary animal and limited human studies indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may increase telomere length, the durability and long-term consequences of these interventions are not yet established.

The patient exhibited signs indicative of TBD, HF, and IPF. After conducting a thorough assessment that ruled out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the responsible genetic mutation, and an in-depth review of relevant literature enhanced comprehension of this uncommon case. Supportive therapy was administered, and her family received genetic counseling. She was fully informed regarding her diagnosis and acknowledged that definitive treatment was not possible. At her two-week review, hematological parameters persisted within normal ranges, and she showed no additional evidence of clinical decline.
