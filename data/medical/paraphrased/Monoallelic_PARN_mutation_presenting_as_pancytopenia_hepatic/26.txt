Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with five years of gradually worsening fatigue and widespread muscle weakness. Physical examination demonstrated features suggestive of accelerated ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia, a dimorphic pattern of anaemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as findings consistent with hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Even after addressing the nutritional deficiencies, the co-occurrence of cytopenias, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, and both liver and lung fibrosis suggested an underlying genetic cause. Clinical exome sequencing detected a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which is currently considered a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN have previously been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and also to pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure associated with telomere maintenance defects (syndrome 4). In this case, the combination of clinical findings and genetic data was consistent with a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the necessity of considering TBDs in young adults who present with unexplained multisystem involvement, even if the classic mucocutaneous manifestations are lacking. Prompt identification is essential for informing genetic counselling, ongoing monitoring, and considering bone marrow transplantation in cases where the disease progresses.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes of accelerated ageing resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere function, which produce unusually short or impaired telomeres. Cell populations that divide frequently, including those of the bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and liver, are especially susceptible. TBD encompasses a range of distinct clinical presentations, among which dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serves as the classical example. Additional variants are Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of disorders, PARN mutations exhibit unique clinical characteristics: individuals with mutations in both alleles experience autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single-allele mutations are primarily associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a female patient in her late twenties presenting with persistent fatigue and multisystem manifestations, who was eventually found to have a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary facility due to a five-year progression of generalized weakness and increasing fatigue with minimal effort. During this period, her ability to engage in everyday tasks diminished in comparison to individuals of her age group. In the preceding three months, her muscular weakness further intensified, severely limiting her self-sufficiency and necessitating support for daily care. Additional symptoms included shortness of breath on exertion, occasional episodes of chest discomfort, unintentional weight loss, and decreased appetite.

Upon assessment, her vital signs were stable. Key physical findings included pallor, icterus, early greying of the hair, hyperpigmentation of the knuckles, and a bald tongue displaying atrophied lingual papillae.

Evaluation of the musculoskeletal system demonstrated thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of the thenar muscles bilaterally. Abdominal examination indicated hepatomegaly, palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin with a span of 18 cm. Respiratory assessment showed diminished breath sounds over both infra-scapular areas. Examination of the cardiovascular system yielded normal results.

Investigations:

Initial lab tests indicated pancytopenia accompanied by a reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, presence of Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation, all pointing toward impaired erythropoiesis. Biochemical analyses verified pronounced deficiencies in vitamin B12 and folate. Iron studies demonstrated increased serum iron and ferritin levels, aligning with features of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow assessment displayed haematopoiesis across all three cell lineages, pronounced erythroid expansion, and augmented iron reserves. There were no indications of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative pathology, and cytogenetic evaluation yielded normal results.

Haemolytic indices revealed raised lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin levels. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG at a 2+ intensity, whereas the indirect antiglobulin test yielded a negative result. Nonetheless, the poor reticulocyte output and marrow findings favored a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than immune-mediated destruction in the periphery.

To assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was undertaken and demonstrated a normal gross appearance. Histopathology from the duodenal biopsy indicated mild chronic gastritis with a decreased number of parietal cells, which could have led to vitamin B12 malabsorption due to compromised intrinsic factor production. Nonetheless, the patient’s clinical manifestations seemed excessive when compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

The presence of liver dysfunction necessitated additional imaging and evaluation. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography confirmed the presence of grade II hepatic steatosis, with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, along with evidence of cirrhosis, as indicated by liver stiffness measuring 18.1 kPa. Laboratory studies showed elevated inflammatory markers. Despite this, serologic tests for autoimmune conditions, including antinuclear antibodies and antibodies related to autoimmune hepatitis, were negative. Evaluation for infections such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was negative except for positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

To investigate hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease, positron emission tomography–CT was conducted. The scan revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within the bone marrow of both the axial and appendicular skeleton. Both lungs demonstrated widespread ground-glass opacities accompanied by numerous fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectasis. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were observed; however, there were no findings suggestive of clear malignancy or granulomatous pathology (see figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation identified secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis documented by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which accounted for her frequent bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyography and nerve conduction tests demonstrated asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy accompanied by chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Considering the overall pattern—including cytopenia, hepatic impairment, early onset greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic cause was considered likely.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single-copy missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This mutation affects a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to negatively impact the protein’s function. Nonetheless, its contribution to the disease has not been determined, and thus it is classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the disease-causing potential of this particular variant is not established, both PARN involvement and the patient’s phenotype align with the typical presentation of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, weight loss, premature greying, and fatigue prompted evaluation for haematological, nutritional, and autoimmune causes. The patient’s significantly reduced reticulocyte count suggested deficient erythropoiesis, thereby initially steering suspicion toward nutrient depletion and possible bone marrow failure syndromes.

A profound vitamin B12 deficiency was identified early on, explaining multiple aspects of the clinical picture, and was corroborated by bone marrow findings indicating compromised erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite correction of these deficiencies, systemic disturbances persisted in severity and duration beyond what would be expected.

An immune-related etiology was briefly contemplated given a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and increased inflammatory markers. However, the lack of haemolysis, negative autoimmune panels, and absence of characteristic systemic manifestations did not support autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease.

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) was present without an identifiable cause, as both infectious and autoimmune origins had been ruled out. The distinctive combination of early-onset ageing signs, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrotic changes affecting both the liver and lungs in a young adult prompted consideration of a rare genetic condition. Despite no familial precedent supporting a hereditary disorder, the potential for a de novo mutation was still recognized.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of resembling acquired diseases, with a presentation marked by inconsistent penetrance. Although uncommon, such disorders might not exhibit the classic mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), and instead display milder symptoms like bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs indicative of premature aging.

Genetic testing supported this hypothesis by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, which accounted for her diverse clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for 5 days, subsequently administered once a week for four weeks, and then transitioned to monthly maintenance therapy to address severe deficiency.

Additional supportive measures involved whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, vitamin D at 60 000 IU weekly, and elemental calcium at 1000 mg daily for osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were treated using conventional supplementation protocols.

Physiotherapy was provided, and genetic counselling was offered to her family after a diagnosis of a TBD was established.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive care, the patient experienced relief of symptoms. At her 2-week follow-up appointment, her hematologic parameters remained stable, her fatigue had lessened, and no additional clinical issues were identified.

She remains under routine outpatient monitoring to assess hepatic and pulmonary function and to track any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences at the ends of chromosomes that maintain genomic stability by protecting chromosome termini from deterioration. As cells divide, telomeres progressively become shorter until they reach a threshold that induces cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomerase—an enzyme complex composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts this shortening by elongating telomeres. Nevertheless, disease-causing mutations in genes coding for telomerase complex components disrupt its activity, causing accelerated telomere loss, which underlies a spectrum of inherited disorders known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1, inherited in an X-linked recessive manner, first demonstrated the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly reduced telomere length, establishing DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). The classic clinical definition of DC involves a mucocutaneous triad: reticular skin pigmentation, nail dystrophy, and oral leukoplakia, or alternatively, any two of these findings accompanied by bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized features of DC include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and an elevated risk for both haematologic and solid tumors. Severe childhood-onset TBD variants such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome are also caused by mutations in genes known to play a role in DC.

TBDs can exhibit isolated mucocutaneous signs, single-organ dysfunction, or hematological abnormalities, and may emerge in adulthood without the characteristic DC triad. Commonly, the underlying genetic abnormality is concealed by presentations such as aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic and gastrointestinal disorders. In the absence of the classic triad and with delayed symptom onset, patients are often misdiagnosed, particularly if not evaluated at specialized centers. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are frequently detected in TBDs, frequently manifesting as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is a member of a well-preserved group of exoribonucleases and is responsible for reducing mRNA poly(A) tail length by deadenylation, thereby influencing gene expression. Extensive sequencing of the 3’-end of TERC RNA revealed that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails that are added post-transcriptionally and mark nuclear RNAs for degradation, a process necessary for proper 3’-end maturation of TERC.

PARN ranks within the lowest 20% of genes in terms of tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical function and supporting haploinsufficiency in individuals carrying such variants. Germline pathogenic alterations in PARN have been identified as causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the wider DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have featured biallelic PARN mutations. Monoallelic germline pathogenic variants have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The relationship between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was first revealed through the discovery of novel monoallelic variants, with subsequent research detecting rare monoallelic PARN variants in 5 of 262 patients with sporadic IPF following whole-exome sequencing, thus indicating a growing association now documented in more than 65 cases as reported in the literature.1517 Nevertheless, the full clinical spectrum linked to PARN mutations is not well characterized, due to the relatively few cases described to date.

Importantly, the patient was found to carry a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). This variant is predicted to be deleterious by four out of five computational prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT). According to the ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation, this variant fulfills several supporting criteria: PM1—it is located within a critical and well-established functional domain (the R3H domain) where no benign variation has been observed; PM2—it is absent or exceedingly rare in population-level databases; PP3—multiple in silico algorithms predict a damaging effect on gene function; and PP2—PARN demonstrates low benign missense variation and is known to harbor disease-causing missense mutations. While the clinical implications of this variant are still unclear, its potential connection to TBDs is substantiated by PARN’s known function and by the existence of other reported PARN missense variants—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which are classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

Multiple genetic factors, varied inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can produce differing effects across organ systems in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Furthermore, environmental influences and lifestyle can impact the replicative abilities of particular organ systems to different extents. In our patient, these complexities may account for the clinical presentation, despite the identification of only a single PARN allele variant.

Monoallelic PARN mutations are typically associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than primary hematologic abnormalities. However, the occurrence of cytopenia in this patient raises the potential for an underlying TBD, which could increase bone marrow sensitivity to hematopoietic stressors, such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres diminish the proliferative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in faster cellular aging and a heightened risk for bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the cytopenia was most likely due to marked vitamin B12 deficiency, the existence of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematologic surveillance to monitor for the development of marrow failure features in this genetically predisposed patient.

Cirrhosis related to TBD has historically been overlooked; nonetheless, new data indicate it may occur more frequently than previously believed. In a retrospective study utilizing transient elastography from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, 88.8% of adults with disease-causing mutations in TERC or TERT demonstrated at least moderate HF, while cirrhosis was observed in 33.3% of cases. The literature also reports a similar case involving a patient affected by pancytopenia and HF with monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Conversely, our patient possessed only a single monoallelic PARN mutation yet presented with both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF, their impact on telomerase activity could explain the presence of HF characteristic of TERT or TERC mutations. These results reinforce a widening clinical spectrum for PARN-associated TBDs, emphasizing the need to recognize HF as a feature within its presentation.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing, alongside telomere length measurement by flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays. Management at present focuses on supportive care, addressing symptom severity, the requirement for organ transplantation, and reducing modifiable risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative approach for individuals presenting with hematologic involvement. Although androgen agents such as danazol and nandrolone have demonstrated telomere lengthening in some experimental and limited clinical contexts, the enduring impact of these therapies is still undetermined.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. A thorough diagnostic workup was conducted to rule out frequently encountered etiologies, after which whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the causative gene. A comprehensive review of published cases deepened insight into this uncommon clinical scenario. Supportive care was administered, and genetic counseling offered to her relatives. She demonstrated comprehension regarding her disease and recognized that definitive treatment was unattainable. At the 2-week review, her hematologic parameters were unchanged, and there was no evidence of new or worsening clinical issues.
