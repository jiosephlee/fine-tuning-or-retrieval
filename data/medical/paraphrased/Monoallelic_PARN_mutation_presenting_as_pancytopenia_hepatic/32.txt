Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year history characterized by worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination showed features of premature ageing, anemia, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory studies revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, severe vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic fibrosis and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were corrected, the presence of cytopenias, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, and both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis suggested an underlying genetic cause. Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN have previously been associated with dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The clinical presentation in conjunction with genetic findings pointed towards a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs in younger adults presenting with unexplained multisystem involvement, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous findings. Prompt identification is important for initiating genetic counselling, planning surveillance, and assessing the potential need for bone marrow transplantation in cases that are progressive.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) constitute uncommon syndromes of accelerated aging due to inherited mutations in genes responsible for telomere maintenance, resulting in telomeres that are either unusually short or functionally impaired. Tissues with rapid cell turnover, such as bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and hepatocytes, are primarily impacted. TBD encompasses a range of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the principal disorder. Additional conditions include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of conditions, mutations in PARN are associated with a unique clinical presentation: individuals harboring biallelic PARN variants develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas heterozygous variants are more commonly linked to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a female patient in her late twenties experiencing persistent fatigue and signs of dysfunction in multiple organ systems, who was subsequently found to have a TBD.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary hospital with a 5-year progression of increasing generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. During this period, her level of physical functioning deteriorated, limiting her capacity for routine tasks in comparison to others her age. Within the most recent 3 months, her weakness became severe enough to hinder her independence in self-care and necessitated support from others. Additional symptoms included shortness of breath on exertion, episodic chest pain, unintentional weight loss, and loss of appetite.

On physical assessment, her vital signs were within normal limits. Significant observations comprised pallor, jaundice, early greying of the hair, increased pigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, bald tongue due to atrophy of the lingual papillae.

The musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic scoliosis and wasting of both thenar eminences. Abdominal assessment demonstrated hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring a span of 18 cm. The respiratory exam revealed diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular assessment did not show any abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory workup identified pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation, indicative of defective erythropoiesis. Serum analysis demonstrated marked deficiencies in vitamin B12 and folate. Iron testing showed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, which align with features of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow evaluation showed all three hematopoietic lineages present, with an increase in erythroid cells and excessive iron accumulation. There was no evidence of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative pathology, and cytogenetic studies were within normal limits.

Laboratory markers reflecting haemolysis included raised lactate dehydrogenase and elevated indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) returned positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nonetheless, the reduced reticulocyte output and bone marrow findings were more indicative of defective erythropoiesis rather than an immune-mediated peripheral process.

To investigate the possibility of gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was conducted and found to be macroscopically unremarkable. Histological analysis of a duodenal biopsy revealed mild chronic gastritis and a decreased number of parietal cells, which could have played a role in vitamin B12 malabsorption by reducing intrinsic factor synthesis. Nevertheless, the patient’s pronounced clinical symptoms appeared out of proportion to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

Due to evidence of liver dysfunction, additional imaging and diagnostic evaluations were pursued. Abdominal ultrasonography identified hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis, with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, and evidence of cirrhosis, with liver stiffness measured at 18.1 kPa. The patient also demonstrated elevated inflammatory markers. Despite these findings, autoimmune serological tests, such as antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies, returned negative results. Extensive infectious testing for viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was generally unrevealing, except for the presence of Epstein-Barr virus IgG positivity (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was carried out to investigate for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease. The scan demonstrated uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake within the bone marrow of both the axial and appendicular skeleton. Both lungs exhibited widespread ground-glass opacity, accompanied by numerous fibrotic alterations and subpleural atelectasis. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were observed, but there was no indication of frank malignancy or granulomatous disorder (see figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluations identified secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes including hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis as demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry; these findings accounted for her frequent bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyography combined with nerve conduction studies showed an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, with chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Considering the aggregate of clinical features—cytopenia, hepatic impairment, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a genetic or syndromic cause was considered likely.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This change affects an amino acid residue that is highly conserved and is predicted to negatively impact protein function. Despite this, the contribution of this variant to the patient’s disease is undetermined, and it has therefore been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the exact pathogenicity of this variant is unclear, both the patient’s clinical presentation and the involvement of PARN align with features characteristic of the TBD spectrum.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pancytopenia, weight reduction, early onset greying of hair, and fatigue, prompting evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune conditions. A significantly diminished reticulocyte count indicated defective erythropoiesis, which led to initial suspicions of nutrient depletion and bone marrow failure disorders.

Early findings revealed profound vitamin B12 deficiency, which explained several clinical manifestations, as confirmed by bone marrow evidence supporting ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite replenishing deficient nutrients, the broad systemic impairment persisted and was out of proportion to expectations.

An immune-related etiology was considered temporarily, given a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and raised inflammatory parameters. However, the absence of hemolysis, negative results for autoimmune antibody panels, and lack of systemic signs undermined the possibility of autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease.

The origin of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was indeterminate, as evaluations ruled out both autoimmune and infectious etiologies. The unusual presentation consisting of signs of accelerated ageing, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrotic changes affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young adult raised the likelihood of a rare genetic condition. Despite the lack of familial precedent, the potential for a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of resembling acquired diseases and exhibiting diverse penetrance. Although uncommon, it may be absent of the classic mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), instead presenting with understated findings such as bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs suggestive of premature aging.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a pathogenic heterozygous variant in the PARN gene, thereby offering a cohesive account for the patient’s range of clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for five days, then weekly for four weeks, and subsequently switched to monthly maintenance therapy to address the marked deficiency.

Additional supportive interventions consisted of whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anemia, oral folic acid (1 mg daily), vitamin D (60 000 IU weekly), and elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) for osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with conventional supplementation.

Physiotherapy was initiated, and her family was provided with genetic counseling after the identification of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After her deficiencies were corrected and supportive measures implemented, the patient experienced alleviation of symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up appointment, her hematologic parameters remained stable, she reported decreased fatigue, and there were no emergent clinical issues.

She remains under routine outpatient observation to assess hepatic and pulmonary status, as well as to track any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the termini of chromosomes, serving to protect chromosomes from degradation and thereby maintaining genomic stability. During each round of cellular replication, telomeres become progressively shorter, and once they reach a critically short length, this signals the cell to undergo senescence or programmed cell death. In normal physiologic states, the enzyme telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein comprising the catalytic subunit telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—prevents this shortening by adding telomeric repeats to the chromosome ends. Nevertheless, disease-associated mutations in genes encoding telomerase components compromise its activity, which accelerates telomere shortening and gives rise to a spectrum of hereditary disorders collectively known as telomere biology disorders (TBD).

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 with an X-linked recessive inheritance pattern initially established the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly reduced telomere length, positioning DC as the prototypical telomere biology disorder (TBD). Traditionally, DC is identified by the presence of the mucocutaneous triad, consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or by two of these findings along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and elevated susceptibility to both hematologic and solid tumors. Severe TBD variants presenting in childhood—such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are also caused by mutations in genes that are involved in DC.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can exhibit isolated involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or the hematologic system, and may become clinically apparent in adulthood in the absence of the classic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad. The underlying inherited abnormality is commonly obscured by presentations such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic failure (HF), along with gastrointestinal conditions. Because these cases often lack the classic triad and manifest later, they are frequently misdiagnosed, particularly outside specialized care settings. Monoallelic mutations in genes including TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most often detected in TBDs, and usually present as either IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is part of a well-conserved group of exoribonucleases that shortens the poly(A) tail of mRNA via deadenylation, thereby influencing gene expression. High-throughput sequencing of the 3’-end of TERC RNA demonstrated that PARN facilitates the elimination of oligo(A) tails added after transcription, marking nuclear RNAs for degradation, which is essential for the proper 3’-end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks among the 20% of genes with lowest tolerance to infrequent, disruptive mutations, underscoring its vital function and reinforcing haploinsufficiency in affected individuals. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been described in patients carrying biallelic PARN mutations. Telomere-related PFBMFT4 has been attributed to monoallelic germline pathogenic variants. The association between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was first observed following the discovery of novel monoallelic variants; subsequent research identified rare monoallelic PARN variants in five of 262 sporadic IPF patients who underwent whole-exome sequencing, highlighting a developing connection that has now been reported in more than 65 cases in published literature.1517 Nevertheless, a comprehensive characterization of clinical manifestations resulting from PARN mutations is still lacking, owing to the small number of documented cases.

Importantly, this patient carries a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which involves a highly conserved amino acid located within the R3H domain (positions 178–245). According to four out of five in silico missense prediction models (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), this change is anticipated to adversely impact protein function. Application of ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines indicates several supportive criteria: PM1—this alteration occurs within a critical functional region (R3H domain) lacking benign variation; PM2—the variant is either absent or extremely infrequent in population databases; PP3—several computational tools indicate a deleterious effect; PP2—PARN displays a low rate of benign missense variants and pathogenic missense mutations are a recognized cause of disease in this gene. While the clinical significance of this variant is presently unclear, it may be relevant to TBDs given the known function of PARN and the similarities to other PARN missense variants—including p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which are classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can be influenced by multiple genes, diverse patterns of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and mechanisms such as somatic genetic rescue, all of which may have distinct impacts on different organ systems. Furthermore, factors related to environment and lifestyle may further modulate the proliferative abilities of various tissues. These complexities likely contribute to our patient’s clinical presentation, even though only a single heterozygous PARN variant was detected.

While heterozygous PARN mutations are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than primary hematologic conditions, the development of cytopenia in this patient suggests the possibility of a TBD affecting bone marrow resilience during hematopoietic stress, such as that induced by marked vitamin B12 deficiency. Reduced telomere length impairs the replicative function of hematopoietic stem cells, which can hasten cellular senescence and increase the likelihood of bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the cytopenia in this case was most likely caused by severe vitamin B12 deficiency, the detection of a PARN variant highlights the need for continued clinical and hematologic surveillance to monitor for potential progression toward bone marrow failure in this genetically susceptible individual.

Cirrhosis related to TBD has historically been overlooked, but new studies indicate it may occur more frequently than once thought. In a retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry that employed transient elastography, 88.8% of adult individuals harboring pathogenic TERC or TERT mutations exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), while 33.3% demonstrated cirrhosis. Another report detailed a similar case of a patient with pancytopenia and HF who carried monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our patient possessed only a monoallelic PARN mutation but still developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although monoallelic PARN is most frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), its secondary effects on telomerase activity might explain the occurrence of HF typically observed with mutations in TERT or TERC. Collectively, these observations broaden the clinical manifestations associated with PARN-related TBDs and highlight the need to recognize HF as a potential feature of this condition.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, alongside flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization methods to assess telomere length. Management is primarily supportive and individualized according to symptom burden, indications for organ transplantation, and efforts to reduce risk factors. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may provide a curative option for individuals exhibiting hematologic involvement. Although early preclinical data and limited clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone might lengthen telomeres, the long-term outcomes of such therapy are still undetermined.

The patient exhibited signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. Comprehensive assessment eliminated other frequently encountered etiologies, and whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible gene. A thorough literature review deepened insight into this unusual clinical manifestation. Supportive care was implemented, and her relatives received genetic counseling. The patient comprehended the characteristics of her disorder and recognized that definitive treatments were unavailable. Two weeks post-discharge, her hematologic parameters were unchanged, with no further evidence of clinical decline.
