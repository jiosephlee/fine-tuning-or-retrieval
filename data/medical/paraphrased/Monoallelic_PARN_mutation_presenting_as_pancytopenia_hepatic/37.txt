Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties was evaluated for a five-year history of increasing fatigue and diffuse weakness. Physical assessment showed evidence of premature aging, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory and imaging studies revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficits were addressed, the combination of cytopenias, premature greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs prompted consideration of a genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing detected a monoallelic PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), designated as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN are linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. In this instance, clinical and genetic findings indicated a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underscores the need to suspect TBDs in young adults with unexplained, multisystem involvement, even if typical mucocutaneous manifestations are lacking. Early detection is essential for informing genetic counselling, ongoing monitoring, and consideration of bone marrow transplant in cases with disease progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes of accelerated aging that arise from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, resulting in excessively short or impaired telomeres. The conditions mainly impact tissues with high cellular turnover, such as the bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver cells. The spectrum of TBD encompasses a range of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the archetypal example. Additional forms include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of conditions, PARN mutations are associated with unique clinical features; individuals with mutations affecting both alleles typically present with autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single-allele mutations are more frequently linked to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a woman in her late twenties presenting with chronic fatigue and signs of involvement across multiple organ systems, who was eventually found to have a telomere biology disorder.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties arrived at our tertiary care facility, describing a 5-year progression of worsening generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. Her ability to carry out everyday tasks had diminished over time, and she found herself less capable compared to others her age. In the preceding three months, her symptoms intensified, leading to difficulties with self-care and a dependence on others for assistance. Additionally, she experienced shortness of breath with exertion, occasional chest pain, unintentional weight loss, and a reduced appetite.

Upon evaluation, her vital signs were within normal limits. Significant observations were pallor, icterus, early greying of the hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, bald tongue accompanied by lingual papillae atrophy.

Assessment of the musculoskeletal system revealed thoracic scoliosis as well as atrophy of both thenar eminences. On abdominal examination, hepatomegaly was detected, extending 5 cm below the right costal margin with a hepatic span of 18 cm. Respiratory evaluation identified diminished breath sounds in the infra-scapular areas bilaterally. The cardiovascular assessment yielded no abnormal findings.

Investigations:

The initial lab workup identified pancytopenia along with a reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). The peripheral blood smear showed a mixed population of erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, all of which are indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum assays demonstrated pronounced deficiencies in vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results revealed increased serum iron and ferritin levels, aligning with a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Examination of the bone marrow showed haematopoiesis in all three cell lines, marked erythroid hyperplasia, and augmented iron reserves, but no signs of malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative processes; cytogenetic assessment returned normal findings.

Assessment of haemolysis showed elevated lactate dehydrogenase and raised indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) yielded a positive result for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nonetheless, the depressed reticulocyte count and marrow characteristics more strongly indicated ineffective erythropoiesis as opposed to destruction mediated by immune mechanisms in the periphery.

To investigate potential gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted, which showed no gross abnormalities. Histopathological evaluation of the duodenal biopsy revealed mild chronic gastritis along with a reduction in parietal cell population, which could interfere with intrinsic factor synthesis and subsequently contribute to vitamin B12 malabsorption. Nonetheless, the extent of the patient’s clinical manifestations seemed excessive relative to what would be expected from a nutritional deficiency alone.

Given evidence of liver dysfunction, additional diagnostic imaging and evaluation were pursued. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated enlargement of both liver and spleen. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) and findings consistent with cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Inflammatory markers were elevated. Conversely, autoimmune screening for markers such as antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies returned negative results. Infectious etiologies, including viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus, were mostly excluded, with the exception of a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG serology (see table 2).

A positron emission tomography–CT scan was conducted to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease. The results demonstrated irregular ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake throughout the bone marrow of both the axial and appendicular skeleton. Bilateral lung fields exhibited widespread ground-glass opacities accompanied by multiple fibrotic regions and subpleural atelectasis. Hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were also present, although there was no indication of definitive malignancy or granulomatous pathology (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results were secondary hyperparathyroidism, abnormalities in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, as well as osteoporosis demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, accounting for her repeated bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyography together with nerve conduction studies identified an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, with chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The combination of symptoms—cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—raised suspicion for a syndromic or genetic cause.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This missense alteration affects a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to have a detrimental impact on protein function. Nevertheless, its association with the disease cannot be confirmed; as a result, it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite ambiguity regarding the pathogenic nature of this particular variant, both PARN involvement and the patient’s clinical presentation are aligned with the features observed in TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pancytopenia, weight reduction, early onset of hair greying, and fatigue, prompting evaluation for hematological, nutritional, and autoimmune conditions. The significantly decreased reticulocyte count indicated impaired erythropoiesis, which led to preliminary assessment for nutrient depletion and marrow failure disorders.

Profound vitamin B12 deficiency was promptly identified and explained many aspects of the presentation, further corroborated by bone marrow findings of ineffective erythropoiesis. Despite replenishing deficient nutrients, the severity and persistence of multisystem dysfunction remained greater than expected.

Given a positive DAT and elevated inflammatory parameters, an immunological etiology was briefly contemplated. Nonetheless, the lack of evidence for haemolysis, negative autoimmune testing, and absence of multisystem involvement refuted autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue diseases.

The cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was unclear, as autoimmune and infectious origins had been ruled out. The combination of premature ageing signs, sustained cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissue in a young adult prompted consideration of an unidentified inherited condition. Despite the lack of familial occurrence making a hereditary syndrome less likely, the chance of a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of resembling acquired diseases and displaying differing degrees of penetrance. Although uncommon, such disorders may not display the classic mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and might instead present insidiously with bone marrow insufficiency, fibrotic changes in organs, and signs of premature aging.

This suspicion was confirmed through genetic testing, which revealed a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, thus accounting for the range of her clinical findings.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, and subsequently continued with monthly maintenance therapy to address her severe deficiency.

Supportive care encompassed whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, and vitamin D at 60,000 IU weekly alongside elemental calcium at 1000 mg daily for treatment of osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed using conventional supplementation protocols.

Additionally, physiotherapy was initiated for the patient, and her family was provided genetic counselling following the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's symptoms improved after deficiencies were addressed and supportive measures were provided. At her 2-week follow-up visit, blood counts remained stable, her fatigue lessened, and there were no additional clinical issues noted.

She maintains routine outpatient follow-up to assess hepatic and pulmonary status and to observe for any changes in bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are stretches of repetitive DNA located at chromosome termini that function to protect chromosomes against degradation, thereby maintaining genomic stability. During each round of cellular replication, telomere length is progressively reduced, and once a threshold is reached, cells enter senescence or undergo programmed cell death. Normally, telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein complex comprised of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), counteracts telomere shortening by extending telomeres. Mutations in genes coding for telomerase components diminish telomerase activity, accelerating telomere shortening and causing a spectrum of inherited disorders known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 on the X chromosome, inherited in a recessive manner, were the initial evidence connecting dyskeratosis congenita (DC) with significantly shortened telomeres, thereby establishing DC as the representative telomere biology disorder (TBD). DC is typically characterized by the mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, dystrophy of the nails, and reticulated pigmentation of the skin, or alternatively by two of these findings along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and an elevated risk of both hematologic and solid tumors. Rare TBD subtypes with early onset in childhood—such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—also arise due to mutations in genes associated with DC.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can manifest as isolated mucocutaneous, organ-specific, or hematological involvement, and may appear in adulthood without the classic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad. Frequently, the underlying inherited abnormality is concealed by diagnoses such as aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic failure (HF), as well as gastrointestinal disorders. The absence of the hallmark triad and delayed onset of symptoms often leads to incorrect diagnoses, particularly in non-specialist settings. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly associated with TBDs, typically presenting as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is part of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family and functions to trim the poly(A) tail of mRNA via deadenylation, thereby modulating gene expression. Advanced sequencing of the 3’ terminus of TERC RNA revealed that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation. This activity is necessary for the maturation of the 3’ end of TERC.

PARN ranks among the 20% of genes least tolerant to rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its vital biological function and supporting haploinsufficiency in affected individuals. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have been established as causative in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader spectrum of DC/TBD. Cases of HH syndrome have been linked to biallelic PARN mutations. Telomere-related PFBMFT4 has been associated with monoallelic germline pathogenic variants. The connection between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was first suggested with the identification of novel monoallelic PARN variants, followed by additional reports identifying rare monoallelic PARN variants in five of 262 patients with sporadic IPF subjected to whole-exome sequencing. This relationship has continued to gain support, with more than 65 documented instances in published literature.1517 Nevertheless, the full clinical phenotype related to PARN mutations is still not well characterized, given the small number of documented cases.

Importantly, this patient possessed a monoallelic missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), that alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). According to four out of five computational missense prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), the variant is anticipated to negatively impact protein function. Applying ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant classification, this variant fulfills several supporting criteria: PM1—the alteration is situated in a crucial, well-characterized functional domain (R3H domain) with no benign variation documented; PM2—the variant is either absent or extremely rare in population databases; PP3—multiple computational assessments suggest a damaging effect on the gene or its product; and PP2—PARN has a low frequency of benign missense variation, and missense variants are a well-recognized cause of disease for this gene. While its clinical significance has not been established, the gene’s recognized role in TBDs and similarity to other PARN missense variants—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which are designated as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar—lend credence to its possible involvement.

TBDs may present with variable involvement of organ systems due to contributions from multiple genes, differing inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue. Environmental exposures and personal habits can also impact the proliferative ability of certain organs. In the case of our patient, these factors likely account for the clinical presentation, even though only a single PARN allele variant was detected.

Monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis rather than primary hematological abnormalities, yet the presence of cytopenia in our patient suggests a potential underlying TBD that could increase bone marrow vulnerability to hematopoietic challenges, such as marked vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres diminish the regenerative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, promoting early cellular aging and heightening the risk for bone marrow failure syndromes. Although the cytopenia in this case was most likely the result of profound vitamin B12 deficiency, identification of the PARN variant highlights the need for continuous clinical and hematological surveillance to monitor for any potential development of bone marrow failure features in this patient with a genetic predisposition.

Cirrhosis associated with TBD has historically been underdiagnosed, though recent data indicate its occurrence may be higher than once thought. In a retrospective review of the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, transient elastography revealed that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT had at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), while 33.3% met criteria for cirrhosis. Similarly, a published report described an individual with pancytopenia and HF who possessed monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). In contrast, our case involved a patient with a single heterozygous PARN mutation who nevertheless developed both liver and lung fibrosis. While heterozygous PARN mutations are most often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their impact on telomerase activity may help explain the HF usually attributed to TERT or TERC variants. Collectively, these observations highlight a widening clinical phenotype of PARN-related TBDs and emphasize the need to recognize HF as a feature of this group of disorders.

The definitive method for diagnosing suspected TBD involves genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing as well as flow cytometry-driven fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays for telomere length assessment. Management at present is mainly supportive, focusing on symptom control, addressing the need for organ transplantation, and reducing risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option for individuals presenting with haematologic abnormalities. Although certain preclinical investigations and limited clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, the sustained impact over the long term has not yet been established.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a comprehensive assessment ruled out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible genetic mutation. Further review of the literature deepened insight into this uncommon clinical scenario. Supportive measures were implemented, and genetic counseling was offered to her relatives. She comprehended the prognosis and acknowledged that definitive treatment options were unavailable. At her follow-up visit two weeks later, hematologic parameters had not changed, and she showed no additional evidence of clinical decline.
