Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 20s presented with a 5-year history of increasing fatigue and widespread weakness. Physical assessment demonstrated indicators of accelerated ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both liver and spleen. Laboratory findings revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, marked deficiency of vitamin B12, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although her nutritional deficiencies were corrected, the combination of cytopenias, early-onset greying, osteoporosis, along with liver and lung fibrosis suggested the possibility of an underlying genetic disorder. Clinical exome analysis detected a heterozygous PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN are known to be linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. In this patient, clinical and genetic findings supported a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs in young adults who present with multisystem unexplained disease, even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous signs. Early identification is essential to facilitate genetic counselling, initiate appropriate surveillance, and evaluate the need for bone marrow transplantation in cases with disease progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated aging due to inherited mutations in genes regulating telomere function, resulting in telomeres that are either excessively short or impaired. The primary cells impacted are those with high proliferative capacity, such as those found in the bone marrow, epidermis, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and liver. TBDs encompass a range of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional forms include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this disorder spectrum, mutations in PARN are associated with unique clinical features: individuals harboring biallelic variants typically develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single allele (monoallelic) mutations are more frequently linked to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We describe a woman in her late 20s with prolonged fatigue and multisystem involvement who was ultimately diagnosed with a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary center after experiencing gradually intensifying generalized weakness and early onset fatigue for five years. During this time, her ability to carry out routine tasks diminished, and she became less capable compared to individuals her age. In the preceding three months, her muscle weakness further progressed, to the extent that she needed help with personal care tasks. She additionally described breathlessness on exertion, episodes of chest discomfort, decreased appetite, and unintentional weight loss.

On examination, she was haemodynamically stable. Notable findings included pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae.

On musculoskeletal assessment, thoracic scoliosis was identified along with wasting of both thenar eminences. Evaluation of the abdomen disclosed an enlarged liver, palpated 5 cm beneath the right costal margin with a total span of 18 cm. Pulmonary examination demonstrated diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. Findings from the cardiovascular exam were normal.

Investigations:

The patient's initial lab workup revealed pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified dimorphic red blood cells, the presence of basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation, all indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum analysis substantiated marked deficiencies of both vitamin B12 and folate. Assessment of iron parameters showed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, findings compatible with ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Examination of the bone marrow demonstrated active production of all three blood cell lines, with a predominance of erythroid precursors and increased iron accumulation, but showed no signs of cancer, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic studies returned within normal limits.

Laboratory markers of haemolysis, including raised lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia, were present. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG at a strength of 2+, whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nonetheless, the diminished reticulocyte count and bone marrow results pointed towards ineffective erythropoiesis rather than destruction of red blood cells in the periphery mediated by immune mechanisms.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted to assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption and revealed no abnormal macroscopic findings. Histological analysis of duodenal tissue demonstrated mild chronic gastritis and a decrease in parietal cells, which may lead to vitamin B12 malabsorption due to reduced intrinsic factor synthesis. Nevertheless, the extent of the clinical manifestations seemed excessive relative to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency by itself.

Liver dysfunction led to additional imaging studies and evaluation. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II fatty liver (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) as well as evidence of cirrhosis, with a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. There was an elevation in inflammatory markers. Nonetheless, autoimmune markers, such as antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies, were negative. Screening for infections, including viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus, yielded no significant findings apart from a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG result (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was conducted to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disorders. The scan demonstrated uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within both the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Additionally, there was widespread ground-glass opacity with numerous fibrotic alterations and subpleural atelectasis observed throughout both lungs. Hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were also identified. No clear signs of malignancy or granulomatous disease were detected (figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation identified secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, deficiency of vitamin D, and osteoporosis, as verified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry; these factors accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyographic assessment along with nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, exhibiting chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Given the constellation of findings—cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic aetiology was suspected.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This amino acid substitution affects a highly conserved site and is predicted to impair protein function. Nevertheless, the contribution of this variant to the disease has not been established, and thus it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenic potential of this particular variant is undetermined, PARN involvement and the patient’s clinical presentation are in alignment with the range of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, early onset greying of the hair, and persistent fatigue in the patient prompted evaluation for underlying hematologic, nutritional, or autoimmune conditions. A significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested impaired red blood cell production, which initially led clinicians to focus on possible nutritional deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was identified promptly and explained many clinical findings, as corroborated by bone marrow findings indicating ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite replenishing the deficiencies, the systemic abnormalities persisted and were more pronounced than expected.

A potential immune-related mechanism was initially contemplated in light of the positive DAT and increased inflammatory markers. However, the lack of evidence for haemolysis, negative results from autoimmune serological testing, and no systemic manifestations made autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disorders unlikely.

The underlying cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was not identified, as both autoimmune and infectious origins were ruled out. The unusual combination of early-onset ageing signs, ongoing cytopenias, and fibrosis affecting both the lungs and liver in a young adult heightened concerns for an unidentified genetic condition. While the lack of family history appeared to counter the likelihood of a hereditary disorder, the chance of a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted consideration of a TBD that could resemble acquired disorders and demonstrate variable penetrance. Although uncommon, such cases may not display the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in DC, but rather present more insidiously with bone marrow failure, fibrosis of various organs, and signs of premature ageing.

This was substantiated by genetic analysis, which identified a pathogenic monoallelic variant in the PARN gene, providing a unifying explanation for the constellation of her clinical features.

Treatment:

The patient was treated with intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance to correct severe deficiency.

Supportive management involved administering whole blood transfusions to address symptomatic anemia. The patient also received oral folic acid at a dose of 1 mg per day, vitamin D 60,000 IU weekly, and elemental calcium 1000 mg daily to treat osteoporosis. Standard supplementation was provided to correct any electrolyte disturbances.

She also underwent physiotherapy, and her family received genetic counselling following diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive management, the patient’s symptoms improved. At her follow-up appointment two weeks later, her hematologic values remained stable, her fatigue had lessened, and she reported no additional clinical issues.

She continues to receive regular outpatient follow-up for surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function and to monitor the progression of bone marrow or systemic involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences at the termini of chromosomes that safeguard the genome by preventing chromosomal degradation. With successive rounds of cell replication, telomere length diminishes until a critical threshold is reached, at which point the cell undergoes senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomere shortening is counteracted by telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein enzyme complex composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which elongates telomeres. Pathogenic mutations in genes that code for elements of the telomerase machinery disrupt its function, resulting in accelerated telomere shortening and giving rise to a spectrum of inherited disorders collectively referred to as telomere biology disorders (TBD).

X-linked recessive mutations in the DKC1 gene were the first to establish an association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly reduced telomere length, designating DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). Traditionally, DC is characterized by the presence of a mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or by two of these features together with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional known clinical features encompass idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and an elevated risk for both hematological and solid tumor malignancies. Severe early-onset TBD phenotypes—including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—arise from genetic variants affecting genes also involved in DC.

Telomere biology disorders can exhibit as single mucocutaneous findings, isolated organ involvement, or hematologic manifestations, and may appear in adulthood absent the traditional dyskeratosis congenita triad. Frequently, the hereditary abnormality is concealed by conditions like aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, heart failure, or gastrointestinal disorders. In the absence of the classic triad and because of delayed onset of symptoms, these cases are often incorrectly diagnosed, particularly outside of specialized centers. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most commonly found in TBDs, frequently associated with clinical presentations of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

The PARN gene is part of a well-conserved group of exoribonucleases and functions to reduce the length of mRNA poly(A) tails via deadenylation, playing a role in controlling gene expression. High-throughput sequencing of the 3’ end of TERC RNA demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation and are necessary for proper 3'-end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks among the top 20% of genes most intolerant to rare, damaging variants, highlighting its critical function and supporting the hypothesis of haploinsufficiency in individuals carrying such variants. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader dyskeratosis congenita/telomere biology disorder (DC/TBD) spectrum. Cases of Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome have been linked to biallelic PARN mutations. Single-allele pathogenic PARN variants have been associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and bone marrow failure, type 4 (PFBMFT4). The connection between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was first observed with the discovery of novel heterozygous variants; later research found rare heterozygous PARN variants in five out of 262 sporadic IPF patients who had whole-exome sequencing, solidifying a link that now includes over 65 reported cases in the literature.1517 Nonetheless, the full clinical spectrum of PARN-associated mutations is still not well delineated due to the relatively few cases described to date.

Of particular interest, our patient possessed a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which affects a highly conserved amino acid in the R3H domain (residues 178–245). According to four out of five computational missense prediction programs (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), this variant is anticipated to have a damaging effect on protein function. Using the ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation, this change satisfies several criteria: PM1—it arises in a key, well-characterized functional domain (R3H domain) that lacks benign variants; PM2—it is either absent from or extremely rare in population databases; PP3—multiple in silico algorithms predict a deleterious impact on the gene or its product; and PP2—missense mutations are a frequent disease mechanism in PARN, a gene with little benign missense variability. While the clinical interpretation of this variant remains uncertain, its possible significance for telomere biology disorders (TBDs) is reinforced by the established function of PARN and by other missense variants in PARN—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—that are labeled as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database.

A range of genes, differing patterns of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue may impact various organ systems differently in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Moreover, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices can also differently modulate the replicative potential across organ systems. In the case of our patient, such factors may account for the clinical manifestations observed, even though only a single heterozygous PARN variant was detected.

While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked with IPF rather than primary blood abnormalities, the cytopenia observed in our patient suggests the possibility of an underlying TBD that could increase the marrow's vulnerability to haematopoietic stress, such as that induced by pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency. Telomere attrition limits the proliferative capacity of haematopoietic stem cells, promoting accelerated senescence and heightening the likelihood of BMFS. Although it is most probable that our patient’s cytopenia resulted from severe vitamin B12 deficiency, the identification of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and haematological surveillance to detect any emergence of marrow failure features in this genetically at-risk patient.

Cirrhosis associated with TBD has historically been overlooked, but new data indicate it may be more common than previously thought. In a retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography, 88.8% of adults carrying pathogenic variants in TERC or TERT were found to have at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with cirrhosis present in 33.3% of cases. Similarly, the literature includes a report of a patient with pancytopenia and HF who possessed monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). In comparison, our case involved a patient with only a monoallelic PARN mutation who developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the resulting effect on telomerase activity may also lead to HF as observed in TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations broaden the recognized clinical manifestations of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the need to include HF as a possible feature of the disease.

Definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis utilizing next-generation sequencing and assessment of telomere length via flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation. Management primarily involves supportive measures, addressing symptom burden, considering organ transplantation when indicated, and minimizing contributory risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a potentially curative option for those presenting with hematologic involvement. Although androgen agents such as danazol and nandrolone have demonstrated telomere lengthening in preclinical models and limited clinical trials, the durability and long-term safety of these interventions are not yet established.

The patient exhibited clinical signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough workup that ruled out more frequent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible gene, and a comprehensive literature review provided additional context for this unusual case. Supportive therapy was initiated, and her family received genetic counseling. The patient demonstrated awareness of her diagnosis and acknowledged that definitive treatment options were unavailable. At her follow-up visit two weeks later, her hematologic parameters had not changed, and she displayed no further evidence of clinical decline.
