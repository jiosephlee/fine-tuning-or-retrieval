Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a 5-year history characterized by worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. On examination, she demonstrated signs of premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, severe vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Correction of nutritional deficiencies did not resolve the findings; the combination of cytopenia, early greying, osteoporosis, as well as hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis led to suspicion of an underlying genetic disorder. Clinical exome sequencing detected a heterozygous PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN are known to be associated with dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. In this case, the clinical and genetic findings were consistent with a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underscores the need to consider TBDs in young adults who exhibit unexplained, multisystem involvement—even in the absence of classic mucocutaneous signs. Prompt diagnosis is essential to guide genetic counseling, clinical monitoring, and to evaluate the need for bone marrow transplantation in cases with disease progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) constitute a group of uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated aging, resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere function, which cause telomeres to be excessively short or impaired. These disorders mainly impact tissues with rapidly proliferating cells such as bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and hepatic cells. TBDs encompass a variety of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Other recognized forms include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of diseases, mutations in PARN are associated with a unique clinical presentation. Biallelic mutations result in autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single allele variants are predominantly implicated in telomere-mediated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a female patient in her late twenties who presented with chronic fatigue and symptoms affecting multiple organ systems, leading to a final diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties arrived at our tertiary care facility, reporting a five-year progression of generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. Throughout this period, her functional abilities diminished, hindering her capacity for routine activities relative to others her age. Over the preceding three months, her weakness further intensified, to the extent that it interfered with self-care and necessitated help. Additional symptoms included shortness of breath with exertion, occasional chest pain, unintended weight loss, and loss of appetite.

On physical assessment, her haemodynamic parameters were within normal limits. Significant observations comprised pallor, icterus, early onset grey hair, hyperpigmentation of the knuckles, and a smooth, bald tongue indicative of lingual papillae atrophy.

Evaluation of the musculoskeletal system identified thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of the thenar muscles bilaterally. The abdominal assessment revealed hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring a span of 18 cm. Examination of the respiratory system indicated diminished breath sounds over both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular evaluation showed no abnormal findings.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessment identified pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed the presence of dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils—findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum evaluations revealed profound deficiencies in vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panels showed elevated serum iron and ferritin levels, aligning with the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow analysis displayed trilineage haematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron reserves, with no evidence to suggest malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic evaluation yielded normal results.

Indicators of haemolysis included heightened lactate dehydrogenase levels and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) returned positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect test was negative. Nevertheless, the diminished reticulocyte count and bone marrow findings supported a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis, rather than peripheral immune-mediated destruction.

To assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was conducted and demonstrated grossly normal findings. Histology from the duodenum identified mild chronic gastritis with decreased parietal cell numbers, a change that may contribute to vitamin B12 malabsorption due to diminished intrinsic factor secretion. Despite this, the extent of the patient’s clinical manifestations seemed excessive in relation to nutritional deficiency as the sole explanation.

Given evidence of hepatic impairment, additional imaging and diagnostic workup were undertaken. Abdominal ultrasound detected both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography demonstrated evidence of grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) as well as cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Elevated inflammatory markers were present. Nonetheless, testing for autoimmune diseases—including antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies—yielded negative results. Infectious etiologies, including serologic studies for viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus, were mostly negative except for positive IgG for Epstein-Barr virus (table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to investigate potential hidden infiltrative or inflammatory conditions. This imaging revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose absorption within the bone marrow of both the axial and appendicular skeleton. The lungs exhibited widespread ground-glass opacity accompanied by several fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectasis in both fields, together with enlarged liver and spleen and accumulation of ascitic fluid; however, there were no signs of clear malignancy or granulomatous process (figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation showed secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes such as low calcium and potassium levels, deficiency in vitamin D, and osteoporosis, as demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, accounting for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography together with nerve conduction assessments demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy featuring chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Considering the combination of symptoms—cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic origin was postulated.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This variant affects a residue that is highly conserved and is considered likely to impact protein function detrimentally. Nonetheless, its contribution to the disease has not been established, and thus it is classified as a 'Variant of Uncertain Significance'. While the pathogenic nature of this particular variant is unclear, the involvement of PARN and the patient's presentation align with the features typically seen in TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, weight loss, premature greying, and fatigue prompted evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. A significantly reduced reticulocyte count indicated impaired erythropoiesis, resulting in early suspicion of nutrient deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

A pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency was identified early in the workup, explaining several clinical findings and corroborated by marrow findings of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite correction of the deficiencies, widespread systemic dysfunction persisted and remained more pronounced than anticipated.

The possibility of an immune-driven mechanism was entertained in light of a positive direct antiglobulin test and increased inflammatory markers. However, the lack of evidence for hemolysis, negative autoimmune serologies, and absence of other systemic manifestations made autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disorders unlikely.

The origin of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was uncertain, as evaluations ruled out both autoimmune and infectious etiologies. The presence of premature aging signs, ongoing cytopenias, and fibrotic changes in both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young adult raised consideration of an undiagnosed genetic condition. While the lack of familial background seemed inconsistent with an inherited disorder, the potential for a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This raised the possibility of an underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of resembling acquired diseases and exhibiting incomplete penetrance. While uncommon, such disorders may not display the characteristic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and could instead present with more discreet symptoms such as bone marrow failure, fibrosis of organs, and signs indicative of premature aging.

Genetic testing supported this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, which accounted for her diverse clinical presentation.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg each day for 5 days, then weekly injections for 4 weeks, and transitioned to monthly maintenance in order to address severe deficiency.

Supportive measures consisted of whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg per day, and vitamin D at 60 000 IU weekly alongside elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) to treat osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with standard supplementation protocols.

Additionally, physiotherapy was initiated, and the patient’s family received genetic counselling after a TBD diagnosis was established.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive treatment, the patient experienced relief from her symptoms. At her 2-week follow-up appointment, her hematological parameters remained consistent, her fatigue lessened, and she did not exhibit any new clinical issues.

She is maintained on routine outpatient follow-up to assess hepatic and pulmonary status and to observe for changes in bone marrow or systemic involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences located at the termini of chromosomes, functioning to safeguard chromosomal integrity by preventing degradation. Each time a cell divides, these telomeres become progressively shorter, eventually reaching a threshold that induces cell senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein enzyme complex composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts this shortening by extending telomeres. However, deleterious mutations in genes coding for elements of the telomerase machinery compromise enzymatic activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and culminating in a spectrum of inherited disorders referred to as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 inherited in an X-linked recessive manner were the first to establish the connection between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, positioning DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). Traditionally, DC is characterized by the mucocutaneous triad comprising oral leukoplakia, dystrophic nails, and reticular skin pigmentation, or the presence of two of these clinical signs alongside bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional manifestations include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), as well as a heightened susceptibility to both hematological and solid organ malignancies. More severe forms of childhood-onset TBD—such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are also caused by mutations in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can exhibit symptoms limited to mucocutaneous tissues, individual organs, or hematologic abnormalities and may emerge in adulthood absent the characteristic DC triad. Usually, the inherited abnormality is concealed by disorders like aplastic anaemia, BMFS, IPF, HF, or gastrointestinal conditions. The lack of the classical triad, combined with a later onset of symptoms, leads to frequent misdiagnosis, particularly outside of specialized institutions. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly detected in TBD cases, frequently appearing as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is a member of a well-conserved group of exoribonucleases that reduces the length of mRNA poly(A) tails via deadenylation, thereby modulating gene expression. High-throughput sequencing of the 3’ end of TERC RNA revealed that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added post-transcriptionally, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation and are necessary for proper 3’ end maturation of TERC.

PARN ranks among the bottom 20% of genes in terms of tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, reinforcing its critical function and the principle of haploinsufficiency in individuals carrying such variants. Pathogenic germline alterations in PARN are acknowledged as causative in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the wider DC/TBD spectrum. Homozygous PARN mutations have been described in patients with HH syndrome. Single-copy germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have also been linked to telomere-related PFBMFT4. The connection between PARN and IPF was first identified by discovering new monoallelic variants, and further investigations have observed monoallelic rare PARN variants in five out of 262 sporadic IPF patients who had undergone whole-exome sequencing, thereby highlighting an emerging association now reported in over 65 cases in published studies.1517 Nevertheless, the comprehensive array of clinical manifestations related to PARN mutations remains largely undefined given the small number of documented instances.

Of particular interest, this patient possessed a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), impacting a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). Four out of five computational missense prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) suggest this variant is likely to impair function. Assessment under the ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines shows several supporting criteria: PM1—the variant is located in a critical, well-characterized functional region (R3H domain) that does not have documented benign variation; PM2—the allele is absent or exceptionally rare in population databases; PP3—multiple prediction tools indicate a deleterious impact on the gene or protein; PP2—PARN has few benign missense variants, and such variants are a frequent cause of disease for this gene. The clinical significance of this variant is unclear; however, its possible association with TBDs is substantiated by PARN’s recognized role and similarities with other PARN missense mutations—including p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which are labeled as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database.

A variety of genes, inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue mechanisms may influence organ involvement in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Furthermore, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices can affect the proliferative capacity of different organs to varying degrees. These factors may account for the patient’s clinical presentation, even though only a single PARN allele variant was detected.

While monoallelic PARN mutations tend to be more frequently linked with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than primary hematological issues, the occurrence of cytopenia in this patient suggests the possibility of an underlying TBD that could make the bone marrow more vulnerable to hematopoietic stressors, such as pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency. Telomere attrition impairs the proliferation potential of hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in accelerated cell aging and a heightened risk for bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the cytopenia seen in this case was most probably due to significant vitamin B12 deficiency, the identification of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematological surveillance to monitor for signs of developing bone marrow failure in a patient with this genetic predisposition.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has historically been overlooked, but new data indicate it may occur more frequently than previously believed. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography identified that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic TERC or TERT mutations exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), and 33.3% demonstrated cirrhosis. The literature also describes a similar case featuring a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis, who carried monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our patient possessed only a monoallelic PARN mutation but experienced both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their downstream effects on telomerase activity may be responsible for the hepatic fibrosis commonly observed in TERT or TERC mutations. These observations broaden the clinical presentation associated with PARN-related TBDs and emphasize the need to recognize hepatic fibrosis within its phenotype.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing and assessment of telomere length using flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assays. Management is primarily supportive, individualized according to clinical presentation, the necessity for organ transplantation, and efforts to reduce risk factors. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers the possibility of cure for those exhibiting hematologic involvement. Although early preclinical and limited clinical trials have reported telomere extension with androgen therapy such as danazol and nandrolone, the long-term impact of these interventions is still unclear.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough assessment that ruled out other frequently encountered etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the pathogenic gene, and a comprehensive review of the literature further deepened insight into this uncommon manifestation. Supportive care was administered, and her family received genetic counseling. The patient comprehended the prognosis and acknowledged the absence of curative treatments. During her follow-up visit two weeks later, her hematologic parameters were unchanged, and she did not display any additional clinical worsening.
