Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year history marked by worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination demonstrated signs of accelerated ageing, anemia, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory tests revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. After nutritional deficits were addressed, the combination of persistent cytopenias, early hair greying, osteoporosis, as well as hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, raised suspicion for an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing detected a heterozygous PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was interpreted as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline alterations in PARN have previously been linked with dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. Correlation between the clinical features and genetic finding in this case was supportive of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) diagnosis. This report underscores the need to evaluate for TBDs in young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem involvement, even when hallmark mucocutaneous signs are lacking. Timely diagnosis is essential to facilitate genetic counseling, structured surveillance, and to inform potential candidacy for bone marrow transplantation in cases of disease progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated ageing, resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance. These defects lead to telomeres that are unusually short or malfunctioning. The primary tissues involved are those with high proliferative activity, including bone marrow, epidermal tissue, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and hepatocytes. TBDs encompass a variety of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the archetypal condition. Additional TBDs comprise Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this group of conditions, PARN mutations are associated with a unique clinical presentation; individuals harboring biallelic variants present with autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas heterozygous variants are more frequently linked to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We present the case of a woman in her late 20s who experienced persistent fatigue and involvement of multiple organ systems, ultimately leading to a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was evaluated at our tertiary care facility due to a five-year progression of generalized weakness and increasing early fatigue. During this period, her functional status gradually deteriorated, limiting her ability to carry out daily routines relative to individuals her age. In the preceding three months, her muscle weakness had further escalated, to the extent that she struggled with personal care and needed help. Additional symptoms included exertional shortness of breath, occasional chest discomfort, unintended weight loss, and reduced appetite.

Upon assessment, her haemodynamic parameters were within normal limits. Significant physical signs included pallor, icterus, early greying of the hair, hyperpigmentation of the knuckles, and a bald tongue accompanied by atrophy of the lingual papillae.

Evaluation of the musculoskeletal system demonstrated thoracic scoliosis as well as atrophy of the thenar eminences bilaterally. On abdominal examination, there was evidence of hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and a span of 18 cm. The respiratory examination identified diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. Cardiovascular assessment yielded no abnormal findings.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory workup identified pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed a mixed population of erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, presence of Cabot rings, and neutrophils with increased segmentation—findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Analysis of serum parameters revealed pronounced deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Assessment of iron status demonstrated high levels of serum iron and ferritin, further supporting ineffective erythropoiesis (refer to table 1).

Bone marrow assessment showed trilineage hematopoiesis with prominent erythroid expansion and increased iron deposition, in the absence of malignancy, dysplastic features, or infiltrative processes. Additionally, cytogenetic evaluation produced normal results.

Evidence of hemolysis included elevated levels of lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin. Direct antiglobulin testing (DAT) was positive for IgG at a 2+ intensity, whereas the indirect test yielded a negative result. Nonetheless, the suboptimal reticulocyte response and the bone marrow findings favored ineffective erythropoiesis as opposed to peripheral immune-mediated cell destruction.

In order to assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was carried out and revealed no gross abnormalities. Duodenal histology demonstrated mild chronic gastritis and a decrease in parietal cell numbers, potentially leading to compromised intrinsic factor production and subsequent vitamin B12 malabsorption. Nonetheless, the extent of clinical manifestations appeared excessive relative to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

Ongoing evaluation for liver dysfunction included advanced imaging. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography detected grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) along with findings consistent with cirrhosis (liver stiffness measured at 18.1 kPa). There was evidence of elevated inflammatory markers. Autoimmune panels, specifically antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis serologies, returned negative results. Screening for infectious etiologies such as viral hepatitis, HIV, cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was predominantly unremarkable, except for positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG serology (table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to investigate possible hidden infiltrative or inflammatory processes. The imaging revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake within both axial and appendicular bone marrow. Additionally, widespread ground-glass opacity was observed across both lungs, accompanied by several fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic alterations. Hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were present, but no clear signs of malignancy or granulomatous disease were detected (see figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation demonstrated secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes including hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, as well as vitamin D deficiency and osteoporosis as verified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These abnormalities accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyography combined with nerve conduction studies identified asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, with chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The combination of cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy led to the suspicion of a syndromic or genetic origin.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This mutation affects an amino acid that is highly conserved and is expected to impair protein function. Nevertheless, its contribution to the patient’s condition could not be definitively determined and has thus been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite uncertainty regarding the pathogenicity of this particular variant, both PARN involvement and the clinical manifestations observed in the patient align with features of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient's symptoms—pancytopenia, weight loss, premature greying, and fatigue—prompted evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The significantly reduced reticulocyte count indicated impaired erythropoiesis, which guided the initial differential toward potential nutritional insufficiencies or bone marrow failure syndromes.

Early investigations established profound vitamin B12 deficiency, explaining multiple clinical findings and corroborated by bone marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite correction of the nutrient deficits, the widespread dysfunction persisted and remained out of proportion to what would be expected.

An immune-mediated etiology was briefly entertained because of a positive direct antiglobulin test and increased inflammatory markers. However, lack of hemolysis, negative autoimmune panel results, and absence of broader systemic signs did not support autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease.

There was no definitive cause identified for the hepatic fibrosis (HF), as both autoimmune and infectious origins were ruled out. The unusual combination of early-onset ageing signs, sustained cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic systems in a young patient prompted consideration of a hidden genetic disorder. Despite the lack of familial antecedents, the possibility of a de novo genetic mutation could not be dismissed.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of imitating acquired diseases and displaying inconsistent penetrance. While uncommon, such a condition may be absent the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), instead presenting more discreetly with bone marrow insufficiency, organ fibrosis, and signs indicative of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this by revealing a disease-causing single-allele mutation in the PARN gene, offering a cohesive rationale for the array of her symptoms.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for five days, subsequently administered weekly for four weeks, and then continued on monthly maintenance therapy to address the severe deficiency.

Additional supportive management consisted of whole blood transfusions for symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, vitamin D 60,000 IU weekly, and elemental calcium at 1000 mg daily for osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with conventional supplementation protocols.

The patient participated in physiotherapy, and her family was provided genetic counselling after a TBD diagnosis was established.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive therapy, the patient experienced alleviation of symptoms. At the two-week follow-up, hematologic parameters remained steady, fatigue had lessened, and there were no emergent clinical issues.

She is maintained on routine outpatient monitoring to assess liver and lung function and to evaluate for changes related to bone marrow or systemic disease progression.

Discussion:

Telomeres consist of repetitive DNA sequences located at chromosome termini and function to protect chromosomes from deterioration, thereby maintaining genomic stability. With successive cell divisions, telomere length progressively decreases until a threshold is reached that initiates cellular senescence or programmed cell death. In normal physiological circumstances, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein comprising telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts this shortening by extending telomeres. Mutations in the genes responsible for telomerase complex components disrupt its activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and subsequently giving rise to a group of inherited disorders known as TBD.

Pathogenic X-linked recessive mutations in DKC1 first demonstrated the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and profoundly shortened telomeres, establishing DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). DC is traditionally characterized by the mucocutaneous triad of nail dystrophy, oral leukoplakia, and reticular skin hyperpigmentation, or the presence of any two of these symptoms along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and a heightened risk of developing both hematologic and solid malignancies. Severe childhood-onset forms of TBD—such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—also stem from mutations in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can appear as singular mucocutaneous symptoms, specific organ involvement, or hematologic manifestations, and may emerge in adult patients without displaying the classic DC triad. Frequently, underlying genetic defects are concealed by concurrent conditions like aplastic anemia, BMFS, IPF, heart failure, or gastrointestinal disorders. In the absence of characteristic triad features and due to delayed symptom onset, these cases are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly outside of specialized clinics. Single allele mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are generally found in TBDs and usually manifest clinically as IPF or BMFS.

PARN, a member of a well-conserved group of exoribonucleases, modulates gene expression by deadenylating mRNAs, resulting in the reduction of poly(A) tail length. Comprehensive sequencing of the 3' terminus of TERC RNA demonstrated that PARN facilitates the elimination of oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which marks nuclear RNAs for degradation, and this activity is essential for proper maturation of the TERC’s 3' end.

PARN ranks among the 20% of genes that show the least tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring both its critical function and the principle of haploinsufficiency in heterozygous carriers. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have been established as causative factors in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the wider DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been linked to biallelic variants in PARN. Monoallelic germline pathogenic alterations in PARN are also associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was first suggested after novel monoallelic variants were identified, and follow-up investigations detected rare monoallelic PARN variants in five out of 262 individuals with sporadic IPF who underwent whole-exome sequencing, suggesting a new relationship, which has now been described in more than 65 cases documented in the literature.1517 Nevertheless, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations related to PARN mutations remains largely unknown, owing to the relatively small number of reported cases.

Of particular interest, the patient possessed a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid located within the R3H domain (spanning residues 178–245). Four out of five computational prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) anticipate that this variant is functionally deleterious. Application of the ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation reveals that this alteration fulfills several supporting criteria: PM1—the change occurs in an important, well-characterized functional region (R3H domain) with no documented benign variants; PM2—the variant is either absent from or extremely infrequent in population databases; PP3—multiple in silico methods predict that this variant negatively impacts the gene or its protein product; PP2—missense variants in PARN are a recognized cause of disease, and benign missense variants are uncommon in this gene. Although the clinical impact of this particular variant is still unknown, its potential association with TBDs is underscored by the established biological function of PARN and the resemblance to other missense variants such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which are classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ within the ClinVar database.

The effects of multiple genes, varying modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can result in diverse manifestations across organ systems in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Moreover, environmental exposures and lifestyle factors can differentially alter the replicative capacity of various organs. In relation to our patient, such complexities might account for the clinical presentation seen, despite identification of only a single pathogenic allele in PARN.

While single-allele PARN mutations are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than primary hematological abnormalities, the patient's cytopenia suggests a possible underlying TBD that could make the bone marrow more vulnerable to hematopoietic stressors, including profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres diminish the proliferative ability of hematopoietic stem cells, promoting premature cellular aging and increasing susceptibility to bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the cytopenia in this case was most likely secondary to marked vitamin B12 deficiency, the presence of a PARN variant highlights the need for continuous clinical and hematologic surveillance to detect any emergence of marrow failure phenotype in this genetically at-risk individual.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has historically been under-recognized, but new studies indicate that its prevalence might be higher than previously thought. A retrospective study conducted by the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, utilizing transient elastography, reported that 88.8% of adult individuals with pathogenic variants in TERC or TERT genes exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with 33.3% meeting criteria for cirrhosis. In the literature, there is a reported case involving an individual with pancytopenia and HF who possessed monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Our patient, in contrast, presented with only a monoallelic PARN mutation but developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Monoallelic PARN alterations are frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF); nonetheless, their negative effects on telomerase activity may explain the hepatic fibrosis more commonly associated with TERT or TERC mutations. These observations point to a broader clinical range of PARN-associated TBDs and emphasize the need to recognize HF as part of the disease's manifestations.

The definitive method for diagnosing presumed TBD involves genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing, alongside assessment of telomere length using flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assays. Management remains primarily supportive, individualized according to symptom intensity, requirements for organ transplantation, and reduction of risk factors, although haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a possible cure for those exhibiting haematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical and clinical data indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere extension, their prolonged effects have yet to be fully determined.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After thorough assessment ruled out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the responsible gene, while comprehensive review of the literature expanded insight into this uncommon manifestation. Supportive care was administered, alongside genetic counselling offered to her relatives. She comprehended her diagnosis and acknowledged that curative interventions were unavailable. During her 2-week post-discharge visit, laboratory values were unchanged and there was no evidence of new clinical decline.
