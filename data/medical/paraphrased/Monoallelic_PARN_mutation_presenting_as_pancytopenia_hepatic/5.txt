Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported a five-year progression of fatigue and generalized weakness. On clinical evaluation, she demonstrated features consistent with premature ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory workup revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although correction of her nutritional deficits was attempted, the presence of cytopenia, premature hair greying, osteoporosis, and both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis suggested a possible underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome analysis uncovered a heterozygous missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which has been classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN have previously been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and to disorders such as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The clinical and genetic findings in this patient were consistent with a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underscores the necessity of including TBDs in the differential diagnosis for young adults presenting with unexplained multisystem pathology, regardless of the absence of classic mucocutaneous findings. Prompt diagnosis plays an essential role in enabling appropriate genetic counseling, surveillance strategies, and timely consideration of bone marrow transplantation for those with disease progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) constitute a group of uncommon, early-onset aging syndromes resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, ultimately giving rise to excessively short or impaired telomeres. Tissues characterized by high cellular turnover, including the bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and liver cells, are particularly vulnerable. The spectrum of TBDs encompasses a range of clinical manifestations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the prototypical example. Additional disorders within this group comprise Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of disorders, PARN mutations exhibit a unique clinical presentation: individuals harboring biallelic variants develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with monoallelic variants are more commonly associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report the case of a woman in her late twenties who experienced chronic fatigue and signs of multisystem disease, eventually leading to a diagnosis of a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care center due to a five-year progression of generalized weakness and increasing early fatigability. During this period, she experienced a noticeable decline in her functional abilities, limiting her daily activities relative to those of her peers. In the preceding three months, her weakness had significantly deteriorated, affecting her capacity for self-care and necessitating help. She further described symptoms of exertional dyspnoea, occasional chest discomfort, involuntary weight loss, and reduced appetite.

Upon assessment, the patient maintained stable haemodynamics. Key observations were pallor, presence of icterus, early onset greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth tongue characterized by loss of lingual papillae.

The musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic scoliosis as well as atrophy of the thenar eminence on both sides. Abdominal assessment demonstrated hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and a total span of 18 cm. Respiratory system examination indicated diminished breath sounds in the infra-scapular areas bilaterally. No significant abnormalities were noted on cardiovascular examination.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment identified pancytopenia accompanied by a reduced reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed the presence of dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with increased nuclear segmentation, findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Biochemical analysis of the serum established pronounced deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Further iron panel testing showed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, supporting the presence of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Evaluation of the bone marrow demonstrated active production across all three cell lineages, marked by erythroid hyperplasia and abundant iron deposition, without signs of malignancy, abnormal maturation, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic results were likewise unremarkable.

Evidence of haemolysis was found in elevated levels of lactate dehydrogenase and unconjugated bilirubin. A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) detected IgG positivity (2+), whereas the indirect test was negative. Nonetheless, the reduced reticulocyte count and bone marrow characteristics pointed toward a predominant process of ineffective erythropoiesis, rather than destruction of red cells by an immune mechanism.

In order to assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, the patient underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, which demonstrated no overt abnormalities on gross inspection. Histological examination of a duodenal biopsy identified mild chronic gastritis with a decrease in parietal cell population, suggesting possible compromise of intrinsic factor synthesis and subsequent vitamin B12 absorption. Nevertheless, the extent of the clinical presentation seemed excessive when compared to what would be anticipated from nutritional deficits alone.

The identification of hepatic dysfunction led to additional diagnostic imaging and evaluations. Abdominal ultrasonography detected both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography quantified hepatic steatosis at grade II, with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, and confirmed the presence of cirrhosis, reflected by a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. Levels of inflammatory markers were elevated. Despite this, testing for autoimmune diseases, including antinuclear antibodies and markers for autoimmune hepatitis, returned negative results. Screening for infectious etiologies such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus produced no notable findings, except for positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (table 2).

In order to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory processes, a positron emission tomography–CT scan was conducted. The results demonstrated uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within both the axial and appendicular regions of the bone marrow. Additionally, there was widespread ground-glass opacity, as well as multiple areas of fibrosis and subpleural atelectasis seen throughout both lungs. Hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were also present, while no clear signs of malignancy or granulomatous pathology were observed (figures 1 and 2).

Other observations comprised secondary hyperparathyroidism, various electrolyte imbalances (specifically hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), vitamin D deficiency, as well as osteoporosis which was verified through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry; these factors accounted for her persistent bone pain and episodes of fractures (see table 3). Electromyography and nerve conduction assessments demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, characterized by chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The combination of cytopenia, liver dysfunction, premature greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy raised the possibility of an underlying syndromic or genetic origin.

Through clinical exome sequencing, a heterozygous missense mutation was found in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This missense mutation affects a residue that is highly conserved across species and computational analyses suggest it likely disrupts protein function. Nonetheless, its exact contribution to disease pathology is unclear, resulting in its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite the indeterminate pathogenicity of this variant, both the association of PARN and the clinical manifestations observed in the patient align with the known spectrum of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, premature greying, and fatigue in this patient suggested potential underlying haematologic, nutritional, or autoimmune etiologies. The observation of a profoundly reduced reticulocyte count indicated impaired erythropoiesis, initially prompting differential diagnoses including nutrient deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Subsequent investigations established a diagnosis of severe vitamin B12 deficiency, which was consistent with several observed clinical and bone marrow findings, particularly the evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite correction of the vitamin deficiencies, the patient’s systemic abnormalities persisted to a degree that was not fully explained by nutritional factors alone.

Given the presence of a positive DAT and raised inflammatory markers, an immune-mediated mechanism was considered. However, the absence of laboratory or clinical indicators of haemolysis, along with negative results for autoimmune serologies and a lack of typical systemic manifestations, made autoimmune cytopenias and connective tissue diseases less likely.

The etiology of Hepatic fibrosis (HF) was unclear, as both infectious and autoimmune origins had been ruled out. The presence of early-onset aging signs, sustained cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young adult led clinicians to consider the possibility of a rare, underlying genetic condition. While no familial cases had been documented—which at first seemed to discount an inherited syndrome—the potential for a de novo genetic mutation could not be dismissed.

This prompted the proposition of a TBD capable of resembling acquired disorders and exhibiting incomplete or inconsistent penetrance. While uncommon, such presentations may not display the characteristic mucocutaneous triad observed in DC, but instead may manifest in a more insidious manner with hematopoietic failure, fibrotic involvement of organs, and signs indicative of premature ageing.

Genetic investigation confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, thus offering a cohesive molecular basis for the array of clinical findings observed in the patient.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dosage of 1000 µg per day for 5 days, then transitioned to weekly administration for the next 4 weeks, and was subsequently maintained on monthly injections to address the marked deficiency.

Additional management consisted of whole blood transfusions to alleviate symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg per day, and vitamin D at 60 000 IU per week combined with elemental calcium at 1000 mg per day for osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed using standard replacement protocols.

She participated in physiotherapy sessions, and her family was provided with genetic counselling after the diagnosis of a TBD was established.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive treatment, the patient experienced relief from her symptoms. At her 2-week follow-up visit, laboratory results indicated stable blood counts, her fatigue had lessened, and there were no emerging clinical issues.

She remains under regular outpatient observation to track hepatic and pulmonary status, as well as to assess for any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the termini of chromosomes, serving to protect the genome from instability and deterioration. During successive cellular divisions, these telomeric regions progressively erode until reaching a threshold length, at which point cells undergo senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomere length is sustained by the activity of telomerase, an enzyme complex comprising the telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC). Mutations in the genes that code for elements of the telomerase complex diminish telomerase activity, leading to accelerated telomere shortening and manifesting clinically as telomere biology disorders (TBD).

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 inherited in an X-linked recessive manner were the first to demonstrate the association between DC and significantly reduced telomere length, thereby designating DC as the archetypal TBD. Traditionally, DC is characterized by the mucocutaneous triad comprising oral leukoplakia, dystrophy of the nails, and reticular skin pigmentation; alternatively, diagnosis can be based on the presence of two of these features along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional clinical findings encompass idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), and an elevated incidence of both haematological and solid tumors. Moreover, severe TBD subtypes presenting in childhood—such as HH syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—also stem from pathogenic alterations in genes associated with DC.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can manifest with singular mucocutaneous, organ-related, or hematological symptoms and may emerge later in life absent the characteristic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad. In many cases, the underlying genetic anomaly remains concealed by other clinical entities, including aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), or gastrointestinal pathologies. The lack of hallmark features and the tendency for symptoms to arise late frequently lead to misdiagnosis, particularly in non-specialist settings. Monoallelic mutations in genes such as TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most commonly detected in TBDs, with initial presentations often resembling IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family, modulates gene expression by trimming the poly(A) tails of mRNA via deadenylation. Deep sequencing analyses of the 3-prime end of TERC RNA have revealed that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails, which are added post-transcriptionally and signal nuclear RNAs for degradation; this removal is crucial for the proper 3-prime-end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks among the bottom 20% of genes in terms of tolerance to rare, deleterious variants, underscoring its critical biological function and supporting the model of haploinsufficiency in heterozygous individuals. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have been established as causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as a wider DC/TBD disease continuum. Homozygous (biallelic) PARN mutations have been described in patients with HH syndrome, while heterozygous (monoallelic) germline pathogenic variants are associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was first demonstrated through the discovery of new monoallelic mutations, followed by reports identifying rare monoallelic PARN variants in five out of 262 individuals with sporadic IPF analyzed via whole-exome sequencing. This finding has been reinforced as additional cases have accumulated, with more than 65 instances documented in published reports to date.1517 Despite these findings, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations linked to PARN mutations remains incompletely characterized, given the relatively small cohort of cases described in the literature.

Of particular interest, our patient was found to carry a heterozygous missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (spanning residues 178–245). According to four out of five computational algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), this variant is likely to be deleterious. Applying the ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant assessment, this change satisfies multiple supportive evidence categories: PM1—the amino acid substitution takes place in a functionally critical and well-defined domain (R3H domain) that lacks documented benign variants; PM2—the variant is either absent from, or exceedingly rare in, population genetic databases; PP3—various computational predictions indicate a detrimental effect on the gene or its product; PP2—PARN has a low incidence of benign missense changes, and disease-associated missense mutations are a recognized mechanism in this gene. While the clinical significance of this variant has not been definitively established, its possible connection to TBDs is underscored by PARN's known biological function and by similarity to other PARN missense alterations, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which are both classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ according to ClinVar entries.

A variety of factors—including multiple genetic loci, diverse patterns of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue—can result in heterogeneous organ involvement in TBDs. Moreover, environmental influences and personal lifestyle choices may have differing effects on the regenerative capabilities of various tissues. Within the context of our patient, these intricate interactions could account for the clinical features seen, even though only a monoallelic PARN variant was detected.

While monoallelic mutations in PARN are most frequently linked to IPF rather than primary haematological symptoms, the finding of cytopenia in our patient suggests the possibility of an underlying TBD that may increase the vulnerability of the bone marrow to haematopoietic challenges, such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Reduced telomere length diminishes the replicative ability of haematopoietic stem cells, promoting premature cell aging and a heightened likelihood of developing BMFS. Although the patient’s cytopenia was likely caused by a marked deficiency in vitamin B12, the identification of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for sustained clinical and haematological surveillance in order to monitor for the possible emergence of a marrow failure syndrome in this genetically at-risk individual.

Cirrhosis within the context of TBD has frequently gone unrecognized, but accumulating data now indicates that its true incidence may have been underestimated. For instance, a retrospective study utilizing transient elastography from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry revealed that 88.8\% of adults harboring pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT presented with at least moderate HF, and 33.3\% manifested cirrhosis. Similarly, literature reports include a patient exhibiting pancytopenia and HF who possessed monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C$>$G) and TERT (c.2476G$>$A). In contrast, the patient described here carried only a monoallelic mutation in PARN yet exhibited both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although monoallelic PARN mutations are most frequently linked to IPF, the potential secondary impairment of telomerase activity may underlie the occurrence of HF typically attributed to TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations highlight a broadening range of clinical manifestations in PARN-related TBDs and emphasize the necessity of recognizing HF as part of the disease phenotype.

The definitive approach for confirming a diagnosis of suspected TBD involves genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing, alongside flow cytometry-coupled fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays to assess telomere length. Current management focuses on supportive measures, individualized according to the degree of symptomatology, requirements for organ transplantation, and reduction of contributory risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation presents a potentially curative option for patients exhibiting haematologic involvement. Preliminary evidence from both preclinical investigations and limited clinical trials indicates that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere extension; however, their prolonged impact has yet to be fully elucidated.

The patient exhibited clinical manifestations consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a comprehensive assessment ruled out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible gene, and a thorough review of the literature provided further insight into this uncommon case. Supportive management was implemented, alongside genetic counselling for her relatives. She demonstrated comprehension of her diagnosis and recognized that curative treatments were not available. Two weeks post-discharge, her hematological parameters were unchanged, and no additional clinical decline was observed.
