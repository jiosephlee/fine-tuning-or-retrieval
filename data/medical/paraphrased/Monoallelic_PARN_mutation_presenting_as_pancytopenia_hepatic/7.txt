Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported experiencing increasing fatigue and generalized weakness over a period of five years. Physical examination demonstrated indications of accelerated aging, anemia, neuropathy, and hepatosplenomegaly. Laboratory evaluations revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Even after addressing her nutritional deficiencies, the simultaneous occurrence of cytopenia, premature greying, osteoporosis, as well as liver and lung fibrosis suggested an underlying genetic condition. Clinical exome sequencing detected a heterozygous PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which is currently classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN have been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and also with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The correlation between clinical features and genetic findings in this individual was consistent with a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to include TBDs in the differential diagnosis of unexplained multisystem involvement in young adults, even in the absence of typical mucocutaneous manifestations. Prompt identification is vital for appropriate genetic counselling, disease monitoring, and consideration of bone marrow transplantation if the disorder advances.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a group of uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated ageing, resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance. These genetic defects result in telomeres that are excessively short or functionally impaired. The primary tissues impacted are those comprised of highly proliferative cells, notably bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and liver cells. The spectrum of TBD manifests as various clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classical example. Additional related conditions include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of disorders, PARN mutations are associated with a unique clinical presentation: individuals harboring biallelic variants develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with monoallelic variants tend to present with telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a woman in her late twenties exhibiting persistent fatigue and signs of multisystem involvement, who was eventually diagnosed with a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care facility due to a 5-year progression of generalized weakness and increasing fatigability. Throughout this period, her physical functioning steadily deteriorated, leading to significant difficulty in completing routine tasks relative to individuals of similar age. In the most recent 3 months, her weakness had advanced further, severely limiting her ability to care for herself independently and necessitating support from others. She additionally described experiencing shortness of breath with exertion, occasional chest discomfort, unintended weight loss, and a decreased appetite.

Upon assessment, her haemodynamic status was stable. Significant clinical features observed were pallor, icterus, early onset of hair greying, hyperpigmentation of the knuckles, and a bald tongue accompanied by atrophic lingual papillae.

The musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of the thenar muscles bilaterally. Abdominal examination demonstrated hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. On respiratory examination, breath sounds were diminished in the bilateral infra-scapular areas. Cardiovascular evaluation did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory workup identified pancytopenia accompanied by a markedly decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, all indicative of defective erythropoiesis. Subsequent serum assays demonstrated profound deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Additionally, iron profiles showed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, supporting the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (table 1).

Assessment of the bone marrow uncovered trilineage haematopoiesis with marked erythroid hyperplasia and augmented iron reserves; importantly, there were no features suggesting malignancy, dysplastic processes, or infiltrative pathology, and cytogenetic testing returned normal findings.

Evaluation for haemolysis disclosed raised lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) yielded a positive result for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nevertheless, the persistently diminished reticulocyte output and bone marrow observations pointed more strongly to a process of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than destruction of red cells through immune-mediated mechanisms.

In order to investigate potential gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was carried out and demonstrated no apparent abnormalities on gross examination. Histological analysis of the duodenal tissue revealed mild chronic gastritis along with a reduction in parietal cell count, which could potentially impair intrinsic factor synthesis and subsequently hinder vitamin B12 absorption. Nevertheless, the extent of the patient’s clinical manifestations was not fully accounted for by nutritional insufficiency alone.

Given evidence of hepatic impairment, further diagnostic imaging and evaluations were undertaken. Abdominal ultrasonography identified both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography detected grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) and established cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Elevated levels of inflammatory markers were also observed. Despite this, autoimmune serological assays—including those for antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis—were negative. Screening for infectious etiologies such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus yielded negative results with the exception of a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG, as detailed in table 2.

To assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disorders, a positron emission tomography–CT scan was conducted. This revealed heterogeneous uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose within both axial and appendicular bone marrow regions. In both lungs, diffuse ground-glass opacities accompanied by numerous fibrotic alterations and subpleural atelectatic areas were identified. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were present. No clear indications of malignancy or granulomatous disease were observed (see figures 1 and 2).

Further assessment revealed the presence of secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, a deficiency in vitamin D, and osteoporosis as demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, collectively accounting for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography combined with nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy accompanied by chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

In light of this range of abnormalities—namely cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early-onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic cause was considered likely.

Through clinical exome sequencing, a heterozygous missense variant was detected in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This substitution affects an evolutionarily conserved amino acid and is anticipated to negatively impact protein function. Nevertheless, its contribution to the disease phenotype remains unconfirmed, and consequently, it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite the unresolved pathogenicity of this particular variant, both the implication of PARN and the patient’s observed clinical characteristics align with the broader spectrum of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient’s constellation of pancytopenia, involuntary weight loss, early onset greying, and pronounced fatigue prompted evaluation for possible haematological, nutritional, or autoimmune etiologies. The discovery of a significantly reduced reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, steering the initial differential towards nutritional insufficiencies or syndromes indicative of marrow failure.

Profound vitamin B12 deficiency was rapidly identified and explained numerous clinical findings, corroborated by marrow histology consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite the prompt correction of these deficiencies, the patient’s widespread dysfunction persisted to a degree greater than could be attributed solely to B12 depletion.

The differential diagnosis briefly included an immune-mediated etiology, suggested by a positive DAT and increased inflammatory markers. Nevertheless, the absence of haemolysis, negative results for autoimmune antibodies, and lack of broader systemic symptoms effectively excluded autoimmune cytopenias or connective tissue disorders.

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) presented without a definitive underlying cause, as both autoimmune and infectious origins had been ruled out. The distinctive combination of early-onset ageing characteristics, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both the lungs and liver in a young adult prompted consideration of an obscure genetic disorder. While the lack of familial occurrence initially seemed to oppose a hereditary syndrome, the prospect of a de novo mutation could not be discounted.

This prompted the proposition of a TBD capable of resembling acquired disorders and exhibiting incomplete or variable penetrance. Although uncommon, such a disorder might not display the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in DC, and instead, may present in a more insidious manner through bone marrow failure, fibrotic changes in organs, and signs indicative of premature ageing.

Genetic investigations confirmed this theory, revealing a pathogenic monoallelic mutation in the PARN gene, thereby offering a cohesive explanation for the spectrum of her clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for 5 days, transitioned to weekly injections for the subsequent 4 weeks, and was then maintained on a monthly regimen to address the pronounced deficiency.

Additional management consisted of whole blood transfusions to treat symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, and vitamin D at 60 000 IU weekly in conjunction with elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) for the treatment of osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with conventional supplementation protocols.

Physiotherapy was also initiated, and after the diagnosis of a TBD, her family was provided with genetic counselling.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive management, the patient experienced relief of symptoms. Over a 2-week follow-up period, her hematologic parameters remained stable, her fatigue lessened, and there were no newly emerging clinical issues.

She remains under routine outpatient observation to assess hepatic and pulmonary status and to track any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres, consisting of repetitive DNA motifs at the termini of chromosomes, function to maintain genomic stability by protecting chromosomal ends from degradation. During successive rounds of cellular replication, telomeres progressively erode until a threshold length is reached, instigating either cell cycle arrest (senescence) or programmed cell death (apoptosis). Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein enzyme complex composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts telomere shortening by extending telomeric sequences. Mutations affecting genes responsible for the telomerase complex disrupt its activity, leading to accelerated telomere loss and, consequently, the onset of a spectrum of hereditary disorders collectively termed TBD.

The initial connection between DC and significantly shortened telomeres was demonstrated through identification of X-linked recessive pathogenic variants in DKC1, thereby designating DC as the archetypal TBD. Traditionally, DC is characterized by a mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation of the skin, or alternatively, by the presence of two of these features in conjunction with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional well-recognized clinical manifestations comprise idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), HF, and a heightened susceptibility to both haematological and solid tumors. Moreover, severe childhood-onset TBD variants—including HH syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are also attributed to mutations in the same genes associated with DC.

TBDs can manifest through isolated mucocutaneous symptoms, involvement of specific organs, or hematologic abnormalities, and may appear during adulthood without exhibiting the classic DC triad. Commonly, the inherited abnormality is concealed by disorders like aplastic anaemia, BMFS, IPF, HF, or gastrointestinal conditions. The absence of the characteristic triad and the tendency for late-onset symptoms lead to frequent misdiagnosis, particularly outside of specialized medical centers. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most often found in TBDs and are frequently associated with clinical presentations such as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of a widely conserved exoribonuclease family, modulates gene expression by deadenylating mRNA, thereby reducing poly(A) tail length. High-throughput sequencing of the TERC RNA’s 3-prime end has demonstrated that PARN is essential for trimming oligo(A) tails added after transcription; these tails otherwise direct nuclear RNAs toward degradation. This removal process is crucial for proper 3-prime-end maturation of TERC.

PARN ranks within the bottom 20% of genes in terms of tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, highlighting both its vital biological function and the phenomenon of haploinsufficiency observed in carriers. Germline pathogenic variants in PARN are well-established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the wider DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been linked to biallelic mutations in PARN. Pathogenic germline changes affecting a single allele have also been found in individuals with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was first suggested by the discovery of new monoallelic variants, and later investigations have identified rare, monoallelic PARN variants in five of 262 sporadic IPF cases subjected to whole-exome sequencing. This has led to an expanding body of evidence, with over 65 such cases now reported in published studies.1517 Nevertheless, due to the scarcity of documented cases, the full clinical phenotype associated with PARN mutations is yet to be comprehensively characterized.

Of particular interest, our patient possessed a heterozygous missense alteration in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), impacting a residue within the R3H domain (residues 178–245) that is highly conserved across species. According to four out of five computational prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), this variant is likely to have a damaging effect on protein function. When assessed according to the ACMG/AMP variant interpretation guidelines, this variant satisfies multiple criteria: PM1—it affects a key functional domain (the R3H domain) with no reported benign variation; PM2—it is either absent or present at very low frequency in population reference databases; PP3—several computational models indicate a deleterious impact on gene function; PP2—PARN exhibits a low rate of benign missense variants and missense mutations in this gene are a recognized mechanism of pathogenicity. Although definitive evidence regarding its clinical impact is lacking, its relevance to TBDs is plausible, given the known biological role of PARN and the existence of analogous PARN missense mutations, including p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which ClinVar classifies as ‘likely pathogenic.’

A range of genetic mechanisms—including involvement of multiple genes, differing modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and phenomena such as somatic genetic rescue—can produce diverse effects across organ systems in TBDs. Moreover, factors related to the environment and lifestyle may also modulate the proliferative abilities of these organs. These layers of complexity could account for the clinical presentation in our patient, even though only a single PARN allele variant was found.

While monoallelic PARN mutations tend to be linked more frequently with IPF than with primary haematological disorders, the cytopenia observed in this case suggests that an underlying TBD may exist, potentially heightening the vulnerability of the bone marrow to haematopoietic stresses like marked vitamin B12 deficiency. Since telomere attrition impairs the proliferative reserve of haematopoietic stem cells, it may hasten cellular aging and raise the probability of BMFS. Although severe vitamin B12 deficiency is likely the principal cause of our patient’s cytopenia, the discovery of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for sustained clinical and haematological surveillance, given the possible risk for future development of bone marrow failure in this genetically at-risk patient.

Cirrhosis associated with TBD has historically been under-diagnosed, yet recent data indicate its prevalence may be higher than once believed. In a retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography, it was observed that 88.8\% of adult individuals harboring pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate HF, with 33.3\% presenting with cirrhosis. Another report in the literature detailed a patient with both pancytopenia and HF who possessed monoallelic variants in PARN (c.1613C$>$G) as well as TERT (c.2476G$>$A). In contrast, our case involved a patient carrying only a monoallelic mutation in PARN, yet who manifested both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most often linked to IPF, the resulting impairment in telomerase function may explain the occurrence of HF commonly seen in the context of TERT or TERC mutations. These observations broaden the recognized clinical spectrum of PARN-associated TBDs and highlight the necessity of including HF within its phenotypic considerations.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing and the assessment of telomere length using flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays. Management strategies are primarily supportive, individualized according to the severity of symptoms, requirements for organ transplantation, and mitigation of risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a potential cure for individuals presenting with haematological involvement. Although limited preclinical and small-scale clinical research indicates that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere lengthening, the prolonged impact of such therapies is still uncertain.

The patient exhibited clinical manifestations consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough assessment ruling out other frequent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the underlying genetic defect, while a comprehensive review of the literature enhanced our comprehension of this atypical case. Management focused on supportive measures, and genetic counselling was offered to her relatives. She demonstrated insight into her diagnosis and acknowledged that curative interventions were not available. Upon review at 2 weeks, her hematological parameters were unchanged, and no additional clinical decline was observed.
