Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported experiencing worsening fatigue and generalized weakness over a period of five years. Physical examination demonstrated features of premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both liver and spleen. Laboratory testing revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Correction of her nutritional deficits did not explain her cytopenias, premature greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs, raising concern for an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing detected a single-copy missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline alterations in PARN have previously been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and also to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The clinical and genetic findings in this patient were consistent with a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report emphasizes the need to consider TBDs in younger adults presenting with unexplained, multisystem involvement, even when classic mucocutaneous signs are not present. Prompt identification is essential for appropriate genetic counseling, monitoring, and evaluation for bone marrow transplantation in progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated aging, resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance and resulting in telomeres that are unusually short or impaired in function. Tissues with high cellular turnover—such as bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and hepatocytes—are especially susceptible. TBDs encompass a wide spectrum of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the prototypical example. Additional conditions in this group include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of disorders, mutations in PARN are associated with a unique clinical presentation: individuals with pathogenic variants on both alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with a single affected allele are more frequently observed to have telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We describe a woman in her late 20s with prolonged fatigue and multisystem involvement who was ultimately diagnosed with a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care facility, reporting a 5-year duration of gradually increasing generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. During this period, her physical capabilities diminished, limiting her capacity for everyday tasks relative to others her age. In the preceding three months, her symptoms intensified to the extent that she was unable to manage self-care independently and needed help. Additional complaints included shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest pain, weight loss, and decreased appetite.

On examination, she was haemodynamically stable. Notable findings included pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae.

On musculoskeletal assessment, thoracic scoliosis was noted along with wasting of the thenar muscles on both hands. Abdominal evaluation identified hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. Pulmonary examination demonstrated decreased breath sounds over both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular exam did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment revealed pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified the presence of dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation, findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Additional serum analyses demonstrated profound deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results showed increased serum iron and ferritin levels, findings that support a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (refer to table 1).

Bone marrow assessment demonstrated active production across all three cell lineages, notable erythroid hyperplasia, and increased iron reserves, with no indications of neoplastic processes, dysplasia, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic testing yielded normal results.

Laboratory indicators of haemolysis showed raised lactate dehydrogenase and increased indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG at 2+, whereas the indirect test was negative. Nonetheless, the reticulocyte count remained inappropriately low and, together with the marrow findings, suggested a primary defect in erythropoiesis rather than destruction of erythrocytes in the periphery through immune mechanisms.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted to assess for malabsorption, and the findings were grossly unremarkable. Histological examination of a duodenal biopsy revealed mild chronic gastritis and a decreased number of parietal cells, suggesting a potential mechanism for vitamin B12 malabsorption due to inadequate intrinsic factor synthesis. Nevertheless, the extent of clinical manifestations seemed excessive compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency in isolation.

Liver dysfunction led to additional diagnostic imaging and evaluation. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis, with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, as well as cirrhosis, with liver stiffness measured at 18.1 kPa. Inflammatory markers were elevated. Nevertheless, autoimmune studies—including antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis-related antibodies—were negative. Screening for infectious causes, including viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus, was unremarkable except for Epstein-Barr virus IgG positivity (refer to table 2).

To investigate possible hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease, a positron emission tomography–CT scan was obtained. The imaging revealed patchy ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake throughout both axial and appendicular bone marrow regions. Both lungs demonstrated widespread ground-glass opacity accompanied by several areas of fibrosis and subpleural atelectasis. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were present; however, there were no findings indicative of clear malignancy or granulomatous disease (see figures 1 and 2).

Other notable findings were secondary hyperparathyroidism, electrolyte abnormalities (specifically hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis as verified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These findings accounted for her ongoing bone pain and repeated fractures (see table 3). Electromyographic assessment along with nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Given the constellation of findings—cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic aetiology was suspected.

Clinical exome analysis revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This missense alteration affects a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to be detrimental to protein function. Nonetheless, a definitive association between this variant and the disease has not been established; as a result, it is classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenic potential of this exact variant is unclear, both the participation of PARN and the clinical presentation in this case align with characteristics seen in the spectrum of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, early onset greying of hair, and persistent fatigue prompted evaluation for hematological, nutritional, and autoimmune conditions. The discovery of a significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested impaired red blood cell production, directing initial diagnostic efforts toward possible nutrient deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Severe deficiency of vitamin B12 was promptly identified and explained multiple clinical findings, as indicated by bone marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite correction of the deficiencies, the overall systemic impairment persisted and was more pronounced than anticipated.

An immune-driven mechanism was momentarily contemplated given the positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and increased inflammatory parameters. However, no evidence of haemolysis, negative results for autoimmune serologic tests, and the absence of systemic manifestations made autoimmune cytopenia or a connective tissue disorder unlikely.

The underlying cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was not identified, as both autoimmune and infectious origins were ruled out. The distinct combination of early-onset ageing symptoms, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrotic changes affecting both the liver and lungs in a young adult led clinicians to consider a concealed genetic disorder. While the initial lack of familial occurrence seemed to argue against a hereditary syndrome, the possibility of a de novo genetic mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) that can resemble acquired diseases and display differing degrees of penetrance. Although uncommon, it might not exhibit the traditional mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), but instead present more insidiously with bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs of premature aging.

This was substantiated by genetic analysis, which identified a pathogenic monoallelic variant in the PARN gene, providing a unifying explanation for the constellation of her clinical features.

Treatment:

The patient was treated with intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance to correct severe deficiency.

Supportive care involved administering whole blood transfusions to address symptomatic anemia, along with daily oral folic acid at a dose of 1 mg. For osteoporosis, the patient received vitamin D supplementation at 60,000 IU per week and 1,000 mg of elemental calcium each day. Standard electrolyte replacement protocols were used to correct any imbalances.

She also underwent physiotherapy, and her family received genetic counselling following diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After her deficiencies were addressed and supportive measures were provided, the patient experienced a reduction in symptoms. At her 2-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters remained stable, she reported less fatigue, and there were no additional clinical issues noted.

She continues to receive regular outpatient follow-up for surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function and to monitor the progression of bone marrow or systemic involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences that form protective caps at the ends of chromosomes, playing a crucial role in maintaining genome stability by preventing chromosomal degradation. With every round of cellular replication, telomeres progressively become shorter until they reach a threshold that induces either cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, the ribonucleoprotein enzyme telomerase, comprising telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), sustains telomere length by adding telomeric repeats. Mutations in the genes responsible for telomerase complex components can disrupt the function of this enzyme, causing accelerated telomere shortening and giving rise to a spectrum of hereditary disorders collectively known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 with X-linked recessive inheritance were the first to demonstrate the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly reduced telomere length, thus establishing DC as the archetype of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). The classical clinical diagnosis of DC involves the mucocutaneous triad—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation—or the presence of two of these findings in combination with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized features encompass idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and a higher likelihood of both hematological and solid tumor malignancies. Severe early-onset TBD subtypes such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome are also caused by mutations in genes associated with DC.

Telomere biology disorders can appear as isolated mucocutaneous findings, organ-specific manifestations, or hematologic involvement, and may become evident in adulthood even in the absence of the characteristic dyskeratosis congenita triad. Commonly, the underlying hereditary defect is obscured by presentations resembling aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatic failure, or gastrointestinal disorders. When the classic triad is absent and clinical symptoms develop later in life, misdiagnosis is frequent, particularly outside specialized care centers. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most frequently detected in TBDs, often initially presenting as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

The PARN gene encodes an exoribonuclease that is part of a well-conserved enzyme family, functioning to control gene expression by catalyzing the deadenylation process, specifically shortening the poly(A) tail of mRNA molecules. Advanced sequencing of the TERC RNA 3' terminus has demonstrated that PARN activity is essential for excising oligo(A) tails that are added post-transcriptionally and mark nuclear RNAs for degradation, a step necessary for the maturation of the TERC’s 3’ end.

PARN ranks among the top 20% of genes exhibiting the least tolerance to rare and disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical biological function and the idea of haploinsufficiency among carriers. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN have been established as causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the wider spectrum of DC/TBD phenotypes. Biallelic mutations in PARN have been described in patients with HH syndrome. In addition, monoallelic germline pathogenic variants are associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was first suggested by the discovery of previously unreported monoallelic variants, and additional research has identified rare monoallelic PARN variants in five of 262 sporadic IPF cases who underwent whole-exome sequencing, contributing to a growing body of evidence that now includes more than 65 cases referenced in the literature.1517 Nevertheless, due to the relatively small number of reported cases, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations linked to PARN mutations is not yet fully characterized.

Importantly, our patient possessed a heterozygous missense change in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), impacting a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). This alteration is predicted to be deleterious by four out of five computational tools for missense variant assessment (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT). According to the ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation, this variant satisfies several supporting criteria: PM1—it arises within a key, well-characterized functional region (R3H domain) that lacks known benign variants; PM2—it is either not present or found at an extremely low frequency in public population datasets; PP3—multiple in silico algorithms suggest it exerts a damaging impact on the gene or gene product; PP2—PARN typically shows few benign missense changes, and pathogenic missense mutations are a known disease mechanism for this gene. While the clinical impact of this particular variant remains ambiguous, its potential association with TBDs is underscored by PARN's recognized biological function and by analogy to other PARN missense substitutions—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—that are labeled as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar repository.

A range of genes, inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue mechanisms can impact organ involvement differently in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Moreover, environmental exposures and personal habits may alter the replication potential of various organs. Regarding our patient, such factors could account for the clinical manifestations seen, even though only a single PARN variant in one allele was found.

While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF rather than primary hematologic features, the cytopenia observed in our case suggests the potential for an underlying TBD, potentially increasing the bone marrow’s vulnerability to hematopoietic stressors like severe vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres impair the proliferative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in premature cellular aging and a heightened likelihood of developing BMFS. Although our patient’s cytopenia was most probably due to a pronounced deficiency in vitamin B12, the detection of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematologic surveillance to monitor for the emergence of marrow failure characteristics in this genetically at-risk individual.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has frequently gone unrecognized, though recent data indicate its occurrence may be higher than once believed. In a retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography, 88.8% of adults with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), while 33.3% showed cirrhosis. Another published case report detailed an individual with pancytopenia and HF who possessed monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Conversely, our case involved a patient with only a single PARN mutation who nonetheless developed hepatic as well as pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their effect on telomerase activity may explain the hepatic fibrosis commonly found with TERT or TERC variants. This evidence suggests a broader clinical manifestation of PARN-related TBDs, emphasizing that HF should be recognized as a potential feature within this group of disorders.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis utilizing next-generation sequencing, as well as assessment of telomere length using flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays. Management is primarily supportive, individualized according to symptom burden, organ transplant requirements, and efforts to address modifiable risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option for individuals presenting with haematologic complications. Although preliminary laboratory and limited clinical investigations indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may increase telomere length, the long-term consequences of these therapies have yet to be established.

The patient exhibited signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a comprehensive workup ruled out more common etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the responsible genetic mutation, and an in-depth review of the literature broadened insight into this uncommon clinical picture. Supportive care was instituted, and genetic counseling was offered to her relatives. She demonstrated understanding regarding her diagnosis and recognized that definitive therapies were not available. Two weeks later, during follow-up, her hematological parameters were unchanged, and there were no emerging clinical concerns.
