Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her late twenties reported a five-year progression of fatigue and diffuse muscle weakness. Physical assessment revealed evidence of accelerated ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory workup uncovered pancytopenia, a dimorphic pattern of anaemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, along with indications of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Correction of nutritional deficits did not resolve the broader findings; the coexistence of cytopenias, early greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in liver and lungs prompted suspicion of a hereditary condition. Subsequent clinical exome sequencing uncovered a heterozygous missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN have previously been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The alignment of clinical features and genetic findings in this patient favored a diagnosis within the spectrum of telomere biology disorders (TBD). This report underscores the necessity of including TBDs in the differential diagnosis for young adults with unexplained multisystem manifestations, irrespective of the absence of classic mucocutaneous stigmata. Timely identification has significant implications for genetic counselling, ongoing monitoring, and evaluation for bone marrow transplantation in individuals with progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a group of uncommon syndromes characterized by early onset of aging features, resulting from inherited mutations in genes responsible for telomere maintenance. These mutations cause telomeres to become excessively short or impaired in function. Cell populations that undergo frequent division—such as those in the bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver—are especially vulnerable to the consequences of these defects. TBDs manifest in a broad spectrum of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the primary example. Additional related disorders encompass Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of disorders, PARN mutations are notable for their unique clinical presentations: individuals with biallelic variants develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those carrying monoallelic variants are more commonly associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we present the case of a woman in her late twenties experiencing longstanding fatigue and manifestations across multiple organ systems, who was eventually found to have a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties arrived at our tertiary care facility reporting a five-year progression of generalized weakness and increased susceptibility to fatigue. During this period, her physical abilities gradually diminished, limiting her daily functioning relative to individuals of similar age. Notably, in the past three months, her symptoms had escalated, significantly hindering her capacity for self-care and necessitating help from others. She further described experiencing shortness of breath upon exertion, occasional chest pain, unintentional weight loss, and reduced appetite.

Upon assessment, the patient demonstrated stable haemodynamic parameters. Significant physical findings encompassed pallor, icterus, premature greying of the hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, depapillated tongue. 

Musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic scoliosis alongside wasting of the thenar muscles on both hands. The abdominal assessment indicated hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. Respiratory examination revealed diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas, while the cardiovascular system exhibited no abnormalities.

Investigations:

The patient’s initial laboratory results indicated pancytopenia accompanied by a markedly reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified dimorphic erythrocytes, presence of basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with excessive nuclear segmentation, all features pointing toward ineffective erythropoiesis. Further serological assessment confirmed profound deficiencies of both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron parameters showed increased serum iron and ferritin concentrations, findings that are in keeping with impaired erythropoietic activity (see table 1).

Assessment of the bone marrow demonstrated active trilineage haematopoiesis, prominent erythroid hyperplasia, and augmented iron stores, with no signs of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltration; additionally, cytogenetic evaluation yielded normal results.

Laboratory indicators of haemolysis included increased levels of lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) showed a positive result for IgG at a strength of 2+, whereas the indirect test was negative. Nonetheless, given the poor reticulocyte output and bone marrow findings, the underlying pathology was considered to reflect ineffective erythropoiesis, as opposed to peripheral immune-mediated haemolysis.

In order to investigate potential causes of gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was undertaken and found to be unremarkable on gross examination. Histopathological evaluation of the duodenal tissue revealed mild chronic gastritis and a decreased number of parietal cells, which may underlie diminished intrinsic factor synthesis and thus contribute to vitamin B12 malabsorption. Nevertheless, the pronounced clinical symptoms seemed excessive when compared to what would be anticipated from nutritional deficiency by itself.

Given evidence of hepatic dysfunction, further diagnostic workup was pursued. Abdominal ultrasonography detected hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated the presence of grade II hepatic steatosis, as demonstrated by a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, and cirrhosis, evidenced by a liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa. Laboratory assessment revealed elevated inflammatory markers. Despite these findings, autoimmune markers, such as antinuclear antibodies and those associated with autoimmune hepatitis, were not detected. Additional testing for infectious etiologies, including viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus, did not yield significant results aside from a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

In order to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease, positron emission tomography–CT was utilized. This imaging revealed uneven uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose within both the axial and appendicular regions of the bone marrow. Additionally, the scan demonstrated diffuse areas of ground-glass opacity and multiple zones of fibrosis and subpleural atelectasis across both lungs. Hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were also present, although there were no signs of clear malignancy or granulomatous processes (see figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluations identified secondary hyperparathyroidism, imbalances in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, a deficiency of vitamin D, and osteoporosis verified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, all of which account for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography along with nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, accompanied by chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle regions.

The combination of clinical features—including cytopenia, liver impairment, premature hair greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—raised suspicion for an underlying syndromic or genetic origin.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This substitution affects a residue that is highly conserved across species and is predicted to negatively impact protein function. Nonetheless, its contribution to the disease phenotype has not been established, resulting in its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite the ambiguity regarding the pathogenic potential of this variant, both the association of PARN and the patient’s clinical presentation align with the phenotypic spectrum of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintended weight loss, premature hair greying, and ongoing fatigue prompted evaluation for possible hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The discovery of a profoundly decreased reticulocyte count was indicative of impaired erythropoiesis, which initially directed attention to potential nutritional insufficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

A diagnosis of severe vitamin B12 deficiency was promptly established, offering an explanation for several clinical findings and corroborated by bone marrow signs consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite successful correction of the vitamin deficiency, the patient continued to exhibit systemic abnormalities that were both persistent and excessive relative to what would be anticipated.

Transient consideration was given to immune-mediated mechanisms owing to a positive DAT and raised inflammatory markers. However, the lack of evidence for haemolysis, negative autoimmune serologic testing, and the absence of systemic manifestations weakened the likelihood of autoimmune cytopenias or an underlying connective tissue disorder.

The cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) remained unidentified, as both autoimmune and infectious origins were ruled out. The unusual combination of early-onset ageing characteristics, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young adult led clinicians to suspect an underlying, obscure genetic disorder. Despite the initial lack of familial cases making a hereditary syndrome seem unlikely, the potential for a de novo mutation could not be dismissed.

This prompted consideration of a TBD capable of emulating acquired disorders and exhibiting incomplete penetrance. While uncommon, such cases may not display the traditional mucocutaneous triad associated with DC, but instead reveal more nuanced signs such as bone marrow failure, fibrotic changes in organs, and manifestations suggestive of premature ageing.

Genetic testing supported this hypothesis by detecting a deleterious heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, thereby offering a cohesive account for the observed clinical phenotype.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for 5 days, followed by weekly administrations over the next 4 weeks, and subsequently transitioned to monthly maintenance therapy to address the profound deficiency.

Additional supportive measures consisted of whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anaemia, daily oral folic acid (1 mg), weekly vitamin D (60 000 IU), and elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) to treat osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with conventional supplementation regimens.

Furthermore, the patient participated in physiotherapy, and, upon confirmation of a TBD diagnosis, her family was provided with genetic counselling.

Outcome and follow-up:

After her deficiencies were addressed and supportive measures provided, the patient experienced a reduction in symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters remained steady, she reported less fatigue, and there were no additional clinical issues observed.

She remains under regular outpatient observation to assess hepatic and pulmonary function and to detect any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres consist of repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the termini of chromosomes and play a crucial role in safeguarding genomic stability by preventing chromosomal degradation. During successive rounds of cellular replication, these telomeric sequences progressively erode, ultimately reaching a threshold length that activates pathways leading to cellular senescence or programmed cell death. The maintenance of telomere length is mediated by telomerase, an enzyme complex composed primarily of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which adds telomeric repeats to chromosome ends under normal physiological circumstances. Mutations in genes encoding elements of the telomerase machinery compromise enzymatic activity, accelerate telomere shortening, and are responsible for a spectrum of inherited disorders collectively known as TBD.

The identification of X-linked recessive pathogenic variants in DKC1 was pivotal in demonstrating the association between DC and extremely shortened telomeres, establishing DC as the prototype for telomere biology disorders (TBDs). The classic diagnostic criteria for DC consist of the mucocutaneous triad—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation—or the presence of two of these clinical features accompanied by bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical presentations include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), HF, and an elevated risk for both hematologic and solid tumors. Notably, severe early-onset TBD forms, such as HH syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, can also arise from genetic variants in the same genes implicated in DC.

TBDs can manifest solely with mucocutaneous signs, involvement of specific organs, or haematological abnormalities, and may appear for the first time in adulthood in the absence of the classical DC triad. In many instances, the underlying hereditary defect remains unrecognised, instead presenting as conditions such as aplastic anaemia, BMFS, IPF, HF, or gastrointestinal disorders. Because of the atypical or late onset of symptoms and the absence of the characteristic triad, misdiagnosis is common, particularly outside of specialised care centres. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most commonly detected in TBDs, which frequently present as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of an evolutionarily conserved exoribonuclease family, modulates gene expression by deadenylating and thereby reducing the length of the mRNA poly(A) tail. High-throughput sequencing of the 3-prime end of TERC RNA has demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails that are added after transcription, which typically mark nuclear RNAs for degradation; this process is critical for proper 3-prime-end maturation of TERC.

PARN ranks among the top 20% of genes exhibiting the greatest intolerance to rare, disruptive genetic variants, underscoring its critical biological function and highlighting the phenomenon of haploinsufficiency in heterozygous individuals. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN have been established as causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and a wider spectrum of DC/TBD disorders. Cases of HH syndrome have been linked to the presence of biallelic PARN mutations. Additionally, monoallelic pathogenic variants inherited through the germline have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was initially identified following the discovery of novel monoallelic PARN variants; further research has detected rare monoallelic variants in five out of 262 sporadic IPF patients analyzed through whole-exome sequencing, providing growing evidence for this connection—now described in more than 65 documented cases in the published literature.1517 Despite these findings, the spectrum of clinical manifestations attributed to PARN mutations has not been fully characterized, primarily owing to the small number of affected individuals reported to date.

Our patient carries a monoallelic missense variant in PARN, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), that alters a highly conserved cysteine within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). According to four out of five in silico missense prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), this alteration is likely to have a detrimental effect on protein function. The application of the ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation provides supporting evidence for pathogenicity: PM1—this missense change resides within the functionally important and evolutionarily conserved R3H domain, where benign variants have not been documented; PM2—the allele is either absent from or exceptionally rare in population datasets; PP3—various computational analyses consistently predict a harmful impact; PP2—PARN generally exhibits few benign missense variations, and pathogenic missense variants represent a known disease mechanism for this gene. While the clinical implications remain ambiguous, the possibility of involvement in TBDs is bolstered by the established disease association of PARN and by analogy with other missense variants in PARN (e.g., p.Lys59Arg [ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2] and p.Ala383Val [ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1]) that are classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database.

The manifestation of TBDs can vary widely due to the involvement of multiple genetic factors, diverse inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and phenomena such as somatic genetic rescue, all of which can impact different organs in distinct ways. Furthermore, environmental exposures and personal lifestyle choices can also modulate the proliferative abilities of various organ systems. These layers of complexity are relevant to our patient's presentation, where a phenotype emerged despite the identification of only a single mutated PARN allele.

While heterozygous PARN mutations are most frequently linked to IPF rather than primarily blood-related symptoms, the development of cytopenia in our patient suggests a latent TBD, potentially making the bone marrow more vulnerable to hematopoietic challenges, including profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres are known to erode the proliferative lifespan of hematopoietic stem cells, accelerating cellular aging and elevating the likelihood of BMFS. Although severe vitamin B12 deficiency is the most probable explanation for the patient's cytopenia, the detection of a PARN variant highlights the necessity for sustained clinical and hematological follow-up in order to identify any progressive marrow failure that might arise in this genetically susceptible individual.

Cirrhosis in the context of TBD has historically been under-recognized, but recent data indicate it may occur more frequently than previously thought. For example, a retrospective study conducted by the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, utilizing transient elastography, demonstrated that 88.8% of adults possessing pathogenic variants in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate HF, with 33.3% showing evidence of cirrhosis. Another reported case in the literature involved an individual with pancytopenia and HF who carried monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Distinct from these cases, our patient exhibited both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis despite only having a monoallelic PARN variant. While monoallelic PARN is typically linked to IPF, its negative downstream effects on telomerase function could explain the occurrence of HF, similar to that seen in patients with TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations broaden the recognized clinical manifestations of PARN-associated TBDs and highlight the need to include HF as a component of its clinical presentation.

The definitive method for diagnosing suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing as well as the assessment of telomere length using flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays. Current therapeutic strategies are primarily supportive, adapted according to the severity of symptoms, the requirement for organ transplantation, and reduction of risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a curative possibility for individuals presenting with haematologic involvement. Although there is evidence from preclinical experiments and limited clinical trials that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may induce telomere extension, the prolonged outcomes of such interventions have not been established.

The patient exhibited clinical manifestations consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. A thorough diagnostic workup was performed to rule out other frequent etiologies, after which whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the pathogenic gene involved. A comprehensive review of existing literature further informed our knowledge regarding this atypical case. Supportive care was administered, and genetic counselling was extended to her relatives. The patient demonstrated clear comprehension of her diagnosis and acknowledged that curative interventions were not available. At her follow-up appointment two weeks later, hematological parameters were stable, and no additional clinical decline was observed.
