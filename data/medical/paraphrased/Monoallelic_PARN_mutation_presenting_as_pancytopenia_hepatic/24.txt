Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her late twenties reported a 5-year duration of worsening fatigue and diffuse weakness. Physical assessment noted features consistent with premature ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory work-up demonstrated pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, severe vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Correction of her nutritional deficits did not resolve the broader picture of cytopenias, premature hair greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in liver and lungs, which suggested a possible genetic etiology. Subsequent clinical exome sequencing detected a heterozygous missense alteration in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), classified as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline alterations in PARN have been previously linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), along with telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The clinical findings and genetic results in this case were compatible with a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report emphasizes the need to consider TBDs in young adults manifesting unexplained multisystem involvement, even if classic mucocutaneous signs are missing. Early identification is essential to facilitate genetic counselling, routine monitoring, and timely evaluation for bone marrow transplantation when disease is progressing.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated aging, arising from inherited mutations in genes responsible for telomere maintenance, which result in telomeres that are unusually short or impaired. These disorders chiefly impact rapidly proliferating cell populations such as those found in bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver cells. TBDs encompass various clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the model condition. Additional disorders in this group include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of diseases, PARN mutations are notable for their unique clinical presentation; individuals carrying biallelic variants develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single allelic mutations are more frequently associated with telomere-mediated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report a female patient in her late twenties experiencing extended fatigue along with involvement of multiple organ systems, who was eventually diagnosed with a telomere biology disorder.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties arrived at our tertiary care facility, complaining of a five-year progression of increasing generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. Throughout this period, her ability to carry out routine activities gradually diminished relative to individuals of similar age. In the past three months, her muscle weakness intensified further, resulting in difficulties with self-care and necessitating help from others. Additionally, she described shortness of breath with exertion, occasional chest pain, unintentional weight loss, and decreased appetite.

Upon evaluation, the patient was found to be haemodynamically stable. Examination revealed pallor, jaundice, early onset greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a depapillated, smooth tongue indicative of lingual papillae atrophy.

Assessment of the musculoskeletal system identified thoracic scoliosis and wasting of the thenar muscles bilaterally. The abdomen was notable for hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. Respiratory assessment demonstrated diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. Cardiovascular findings were within normal limits.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment identified pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). The peripheral blood smear exhibited dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with excessive segmentation, all indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum testing verified marked deficiencies in vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results showed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, aligning with features of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow analysis demonstrated three lineage hematopoiesis, erythroid proliferation, and heightened iron accumulation, with no indications of malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic findings were normal.

Indicators of hemolysis comprised elevated lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) yielded a positive result for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nonetheless, the suppressed reticulocyte response and marrow observations were more indicative of inefficient erythropoiesis than peripheral immune-mediated red cell destruction.

To assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was carried out, which was unremarkable on gross examination. Histology from a duodenal biopsy revealed mild chronic gastritis and a reduction in parietal cells, which might have contributed to B12 malabsorption due to decreased intrinsic factor synthesis. Nevertheless, the degree of clinical manifestations seemed excessive compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

In light of hepatic abnormalities, further investigations were performed. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) and evidence of cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). There was an elevation in inflammatory markers. Autoimmune panel, including tests for antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis, returned negative. Screening for infectious causes such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was notable only for Epstein-Barr virus IgG positivity (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory conditions. The scan revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within the bone marrow of both axial and appendicular regions. Both lungs exhibited widespread ground-glass opacity as well as numerous fibrotic alterations and subpleural areas of atelectasis. Additional findings included enlargement of the liver and spleen and presence of ascites, although there were no signs indicating frank malignancy or granulomatous pathology (figures 1 and 2).

Other observations comprised secondary hyperparathyroidism, imbalances in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, a deficiency in vitamin D, and osteoporosis, which was verified using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These findings accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography and nerve conduction tests demonstrated asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Considering the combination of clinical manifestations—cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early onset of greying hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a syndromic or hereditary cause was deemed likely.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This missense change affects a residue that is highly conserved and is anticipated to negatively impact protein function. Nevertheless, the contribution of this variant to the disease has not been determined, so it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenic nature of this particular variant is unresolved, the association of PARN with disease and the patient's clinical manifestations align with the characteristics observed in TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unexplained weight loss, early onset greying, and persistent fatigue in this patient prompted evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. A substantially decreased reticulocyte count suggested inadequate erythropoiesis, which initially guided clinicians toward possible nutritional deficits or bone marrow failure syndromes.

Early testing identified profound vitamin B12 deficiency, providing an explanation for several of the patient’s clinical and bone marrow findings of ineffective erythropoiesis. Despite appropriate replacement of the nutritional deficiency, the widespread organ dysfunction continued and was notably out of proportion, remaining unresolved.

Given the presence of a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and raised inflammatory markers, an immune-driven mechanism was considered. However, the lack of evidence for hemolysis, negative autoimmune panels, and absence of systemic symptoms effectively ruled out autoimmune cytopenias and connective tissue disorders.

There was no identifiable cause for the hepatic fibrosis (HF), as both autoimmune and infectious origins had been ruled out. The combination of signs including early-onset aging, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrotic changes in both the liver and lungs observed in this young adult led clinicians to consider a concealed genetic condition. Despite a negative family history, the potential for a de novo genetic mutation persisted as a plausible explanation.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of simulating acquired diseases and displaying incomplete penetrance. Although uncommon, such a disorder might not exhibit the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), and may instead present more insidiously with bone marrow failure, fibrotic changes in organs, and signs of premature aging.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, which accounted for her array of clinical symptoms.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg each day for 5 days, subsequently administered weekly over 4 weeks, and thereafter maintained monthly to address her profound deficiency.

Adjunctive management consisted of whole blood transfusions to alleviate symptomatic anaemia, daily oral folic acid (1 mg), vitamin D (60 000 IU weekly) combined with elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) for osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed using routine supplementation.

Additionally, the patient participated in physiotherapy, and her family was provided with genetic counselling after the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After deficiencies were addressed and supportive measures implemented, the patient’s symptoms improved. At a 2-week follow-up visit, her blood counts remained within stable ranges, her fatigue was alleviated, and no additional clinical issues emerged.

She is currently undergoing routine outpatient evaluations to assess liver and lung function, as well as to observe any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres, composed of repetitive nucleotide sequences at the termini of chromosomes, function to maintain genomic stability by protecting chromosomal ends from deterioration. Telomere length decreases with successive cell divisions until a threshold is reached, which prompts either cell senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein complex containing telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts telomere shortening by promoting elongation. Mutations affecting the genes that encode telomerase components reduce enzyme activity, causing early telomere shortening and giving rise to a spectrum of hereditary disorders known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 inherited in an X-linked recessive manner initially demonstrated the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, establishing DC as the representative telomere biology disorder (TBD). The classic clinical criteria for DC consist of the mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or the presence of any two of these findings alongside bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and a heightened risk for both hematological and solid tumors. Severe TBD subtypes presenting in childhood, including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, also arise from mutations in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can appear with singular involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or hematological systems, and may emerge in adults without the characteristic DC triad. Commonly, the underlying inherited abnormality is obscured by conditions including aplastic anaemia, BMFS, IPF, HF, and gastrointestinal disorders. In the absence of the classic triad and because of late onset symptoms, these individuals are frequently misdiagnosed, particularly outside specialised facilities. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly detected in TBD cases, usually presenting as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is part of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family and functions to reduce the length of mRNA poly(A) tails via deadenylation, thereby controlling gene expression. High-throughput sequencing of the 3′ terminus of TERC RNA demonstrated that PARN is necessary for eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation and are essential for proper 3′ end maturation of TERC.

PARN ranks among the 20% of genes with the lowest tolerance for rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its vital biological function and highlighting haploinsufficiency in individuals who carry such variants. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are acknowledged as causative for dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as for the broader group of DC/TBD. Cases of HH syndrome have been described with biallelic PARN mutations. In addition, monoallelic germline pathogenic variants are involved in telomere-related PFBMFT4. The first association between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was based on the identification of new monoallelic variants, with later investigations reporting rare monoallelic PARN mutations in five out of 262 sporadic IPF patients examined via whole-exome sequencing. This has led to a growing recognition of an association, now documented in more than 65 cases in published reports.1517 Nevertheless, because of the relatively small number of cases described, the full clinical spectrum linked to PARN mutations remains insufficiently characterised.

Our patient was found to carry a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which disrupts a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). Four out of five computational missense prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT) suggest this variant is likely to have a detrimental functional impact. According to ACMG/AMP guidelines for classifying genetic variants, this mutation fulfills several supporting criteria: PM1—the change is localized in an essential and well-recognized functional domain (R3H domain) without any documented benign variants; PM2—the mutation is either absent from or extremely infrequent in large population databases; PP3—various bioinformatic tools indicate a harmful effect on the gene or protein; PP2—PARN demonstrates a low rate of harmless missense alterations and is known to have missense mutations as a typical disease-causing mechanism. While the clinical relevance of this variant is yet to be established, its potential association with TBDs is underscored by PARN's known function and comparisons with other missense variants in PARN, like p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), both classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar repository.

A variety of genes, inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can each uniquely impact organ involvement in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Moreover, environmental influences and lifestyle choices may contribute to differences in the replication potential among distinct organ systems. These factors may account for the clinical presentation observed in our patient, who harbors only a monoallelic PARN mutation.

Monoallelic PARN variants are frequently associated with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), rather than primary hematological abnormalities. However, the occurrence of cytopenia in this patient suggests the possibility of an underlying TBD, potentially increasing bone marrow vulnerability to stresses such as marked vitamin B12 deficiency. Telomere attrition diminishes the capacity of hematopoietic stem cells for proliferation, promoting premature senescence and elevating the risk of bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). While the patient’s cytopenia was most likely caused by severe vitamin B12 deficiency, the detection of a PARN variant highlights the need for sustained clinical and hematologic evaluation to monitor for the development of marrow failure features in this individual with genetic susceptibility.

Cirrhosis related to TBD has frequently gone unrecognized; recent data, however, indicate that its prevalence may be higher than previously thought. In a retrospective study utilizing transient elastography from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, 88.8% of adults with pathogenic variants in TERC or TERT demonstrated at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with 33.3% meeting criteria for cirrhosis. Another report in the literature documented a patient who had pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis and was a carrier of monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, the patient described in our report possessed a single monoallelic PARN variant, but nonetheless manifested both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their subsequent effect on telomerase activity may underlie the hepatic fibrosis more typically ascribed to TERT or TERC defects. Taken together, these observations point to a widening clinical phenotype associated with PARN-related TBDs, and highlight the significance of evaluating hepatic fibrosis in affected individuals.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing and assessment of telomere length using flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assays. Current management is largely supportive, focusing on addressing individual symptom burden, organ transplant requirements, and minimizing risk factors, while haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option for patients with hematologic involvement. Though preliminary animal and limited clinical studies indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone can promote telomere elongation, their long-term outcomes are yet to be clarified.

The patient exhibited signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After thorough assessment ruling out more frequent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the pathogenic gene responsible, and a comprehensive review of existing literature provided further insight into this uncommon manifestation. Supportive care was administered, and her family received genetic counseling. She comprehended the diagnosis and acknowledged that definitive treatment was not available. At her follow-up appointment two weeks later, her hematological parameters were unchanged and there were no additional clinical concerns.
