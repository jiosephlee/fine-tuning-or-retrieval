Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year history of worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination demonstrated features suggestive of premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluations revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the coexistence of cytopenias, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, as well as hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis suggested an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing uncovered a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which is designated as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are known to cause dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and have also been implicated in telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. In this instance, the clinical findings in conjunction with the genetic result were consistent with a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underscores the need to consider TBDs in young adults exhibiting unexplained, multisystem involvement, even in the absence of typical mucocutaneous signs. Timely identification is essential for appropriate genetic counseling, monitoring, and evaluating the need for bone marrow transplantation in cases that show progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated ageing due to inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, resulting in telomeres that are unusually short or defective. The primary cells impacted are those that proliferate frequently, such as those found in the bone marrow, epidermis, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and liver. TBDs encompass a wide array of clinical manifestations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the model disorder. Additional conditions within this group include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this group of diseases, PARN mutations display unique clinical characteristics; individuals with two mutated alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with a single mutated allele typically present with telomere-mediated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report on a female patient in her late 20s presenting with persistent fatigue and multiple organ involvement, who was eventually found to have a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary center after experiencing a five-year progression of generalized weakness and increased susceptibility to fatigue. During this period, her physical capabilities diminished, and she became less able to carry out everyday tasks relative to individuals of her age. In the most recent three months, her muscle weakness intensified significantly, hindering her ability to manage self-care and necessitating help. She additionally described experiencing shortness of breath with exertion, occasional chest pain, unintended weight loss, and decreased appetite.

Upon assessment, the patient was haemodynamically stable. Key physical signs comprised pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, bald tongue demonstrating atrophy of the lingual papillae.

The musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of the thenar muscles on both sides. On abdominal examination, hepatomegaly was detected, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. Pulmonary examination noted diminished breath sounds over both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular evaluation revealed no abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment demonstrated pancytopenia accompanied by a reduced reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed a mix of erythrocyte populations, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum assays identified marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panels revealed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, further supporting the presence of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow analysis showed three-lineage hematopoiesis with pronounced erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron reserves, and no signs of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic studies were likewise unremarkable.

Indicators of hemolysis included elevated lactate dehydrogenase and increased indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) returned positive for IgG (2+), while the indirect test was negative. Nonetheless, the diminished reticulocyte output and bone marrow findings were more compatible with ineffective erythropoiesis than with immune-mediated peripheral destruction.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted to investigate gastrointestinal malabsorption and demonstrated a grossly normal appearance. Biopsy of the duodenum revealed mild chronic gastritis with a reduction in parietal cell number, which may have played a role in vitamin B12 malabsorption due to decreased intrinsic factor production. Nevertheless, the intensity of the patient’s clinical manifestations seemed greater than would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

Due to evidence of hepatic impairment, additional imaging and evaluation were undertaken. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography identified grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) along with findings consistent with cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Inflammatory markers were elevated. Autoimmune serologies—including tests for antinuclear antibodies and markers of autoimmune hepatitis—were negative. Infectious etiologies, including hepatitis viruses, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus, were predominantly negative with the exception of positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for potential hidden infiltrative or inflammatory processes. The scan demonstrated uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within both the axial and appendicular regions of the bone marrow. Both lungs exhibited widespread ground-glass opacity accompanied by numerous fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectasis. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were identified. There was no clear indication of malignancy or granulomatous disease (see figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation demonstrated secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, as well as vitamin D deficiency and osteoporosis, the latter verified through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These findings accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyography and nerve conduction assessments identified an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, characterized by chronic neurogenic alterations involving both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The combination of symptoms—including cytopenia, hepatic impairment, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—raised suspicion for an underlying syndromic or genetic cause.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This variant affects a highly conserved amino acid and is anticipated to negatively impact protein function. Despite these findings, the contribution of this variant to the disease is unclear, resulting in its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenic nature of this particular mutation is not established, the participation of PARN and the patient's presenting symptoms align with the typical features seen in TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintended weight loss, early onset greying, and fatigue in the patient prompted evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The pronounced reduction in reticulocyte numbers suggested diminished erythropoietic activity, initially directing attention toward possible nutritional insufficiencies or bone marrow failure syndromes.

Laboratory confirmation of profound vitamin B12 deficiency accounted for several clinical manifestations and was corroborated by marrow findings of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite replacement therapy for the deficiencies, the degree and persistence of multisystem dysfunction remained unexpectedly high.

Consideration was given to an immune-mediated mechanism owing to a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and raised markers of inflammation. Nonetheless, the absence of hemolysis, negative results for autoimmune panels, and lack of typical systemic symptoms reduced the likelihood of autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease.

The origin of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was undetermined, as autoimmune and infectious etiologies had been ruled out. The distinctive combination of signs—premature ageing, ongoing cytopenias, and fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young adult—led clinicians to consider the possibility of a concealed inherited disease. While the lack of familial history was initially a counterpoint to a genetic syndrome, the potential for a de novo mutation could not be dismissed.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of imitating acquired diseases and exhibiting heterogeneous penetrance. Such conditions are uncommon and may not display the characteristic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (DC), instead presenting insidiously with bone marrow insufficiency, visceral fibrosis, and signs of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing monoallelic mutation in the PARN gene, accounting for her diverse clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for five days, transitioning to weekly administrations for four weeks, and then continued on monthly doses for ongoing management of the pronounced deficiency.

Additional supportive care involved whole blood transfusions to address symptomatic anemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, and vitamin D at a dose of 60,000 IU weekly together with elemental calcium at 1,000 mg daily for the management of osteoporosis. Any disturbances in electrolytes were addressed using conventional supplementation methods.

Physiotherapy was initiated, and genetic counseling was provided to her family upon confirmation of a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive treatment, the patient experienced relief of symptoms. At a follow-up visit two weeks later, her blood counts remained stable, her fatigue had lessened, and there were no new clinical issues identified.

She remains under ongoing outpatient monitoring to assess hepatic and pulmonary function, as well as to observe for any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA segments located at chromosome termini that serve to maintain genomic stability by protecting chromosomes from erosion. During successive cell divisions, telomeres progressively shorten until they reach a threshold length, at which point cellular senescence or programmed cell death is induced. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein consisting of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts telomere shortening by lengthening telomeres. Mutations in genes encoding telomerase components disrupt the enzyme’s activity, causing accelerated telomere loss and giving rise to a spectrum of inherited disorders known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1, inherited in an X-linked recessive manner, were the first to demonstrate the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, positioning DC as the representative disorder among telomere biology disorders (TBDs). DC is traditionally characterized by the mucocutaneous triad comprising oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or by the presence of two of these signs in conjunction with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional documented features encompass idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), and an increased susceptibility to both hematological and solid tumors. Certain severe, early-onset TBD subtypes—such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—also originate from mutations in genes involved in DC.

TBDs can appear as isolated involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or the hematological system, and may occur in adults in the absence of the classical DC triad. The hereditary abnormality is commonly concealed by disorders such as aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, heart failure, or gastrointestinal pathologies. When the classical triad is absent and clinical symptoms develop late, patients are often misdiagnosed, particularly if they are not assessed at specialty centres. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly detected in TBDs, frequently presenting clinically as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

The PARN gene is a member of a well-conserved group of exoribonucleases and functions in gene expression control by catalyzing the shortening of mRNA poly(A) tails via deadenylation. Deep sequencing analyses of the 3’ end of TERC RNA revealed that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation, a necessary step in TERC 3’ end processing.

PARN belongs to the top 20% of genes with the lowest tolerance for rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical function and the principle of haploinsufficiency among carriers. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have been identified as causal factors in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as in the broader DC/TBD spectrum. Biallelic alterations in PARN have also been observed in individuals with HH syndrome. Additionally, monoallelic germline pathogenic variants have been linked to telomere-associated PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was first described following the detection of new monoallelic variants, and later studies identified monoallelic rare variants in PARN in five out of 262 sporadic IPF cases subjected to whole-exome sequencing, reinforcing an evolving relationship now reported in more than 65 cases in the literature.1517 Nevertheless, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations related to PARN mutations remains inadequately characterized, owing to the small number of cases available.

Importantly, this patient carried a monoallelic missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). This alteration is predicted to be functionally deleterious by four out of five in silico missense prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT). Based on ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant classification, the variant fulfills several supporting criteria: PM1—the missense change occurs within a crucial and well-characterized functional domain (R3H), which lacks benign variation; PM2—the variant is either absent or extremely rare in population databases; PP3—several computational algorithms predict it will adversely affect the gene or its product; PP2—PARN is a gene with infrequent benign missense changes, and missense variants often cause disease in this gene. Despite the clinical significance of this variant being uncertain, its potential association with TBDs is suggested by the known function of PARN and similarities to other missense variants in PARN, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which are classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ according to the ClinVar database.

The manifestation of TBDs can be influenced by multiple genetic factors, differing inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue, all of which may impact individual organ systems uniquely. Furthermore, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices can alter the proliferative abilities of various tissues. In our patient, such multifactorial influences might account for the clinical presentation, given the detection of only a single PARN variant.

While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF rather than primary hematological issues, our patient’s cytopenia suggests the potential presence of a TBD that could make bone marrow more vulnerable to hematopoietic challenges, such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres reduce the replicative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, prompting premature cellular aging and elevating the risk for BMFS. Although severe vitamin B12 deficiency likely caused the cytopenia in this case, identifying a PARN variant highlights the necessity for continual clinical and hematological surveillance to monitor for the possible development of bone marrow failure in this genetically susceptible individual.

Cirrhosis associated with TBD has frequently been overlooked; nevertheless, new data indicates its occurrence may be higher than formerly recognized. In a retrospective review from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography, at least moderate HF was identified in 88.8% of adult individuals harboring pathogenic variants in TERC or TERT, and cirrhosis was detected in 33.3% of these cases. Another case report detailed a patient presenting with pancytopenia and HF who carried monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our case involved a patient with a monoallelic mutation solely in PARN, yet who developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN alterations are most often linked to IPF, their downstream effect on telomerase activity may explain the presence of HF more commonly associated with TERT or TERC mutations. These observations broaden the recognized clinical manifestations of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the necessity of recognizing HF within its clinical phenotype.

The definitive diagnostic approach for suspected TBD involves genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, as well as flow cytometry fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays for telomere length assessment. Management primarily consists of supportive care, which is individualized according to symptom burden, indications for organ transplantation, and reduction of risk factors; however, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a cure for those exhibiting haematological complications. Though limited preclinical and early-stage clinical research indicates that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, the durability and long-term safety of these interventions are still uncertain.

This patient exhibited signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After comprehensive assessment to rule out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the pathogenic gene. A thorough review of published reports expanded our knowledge regarding this uncommon clinical scenario. Supportive therapy was implemented, and her relatives received genetic counseling. She was fully informed about the disease and acknowledged that definitive treatment was not attainable. At her follow-up visit two weeks later, her hematological parameters were unchanged, and no additional symptoms indicative of worsening were observed.
