Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year history marked by worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. On examination, she displayed indicators of premature ageing, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, severe vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Although her nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the presence of cytopenia, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis prompted consideration of an underlying genetic syndrome. Clinical exome sequencing uncovered a heterozygous missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN have previously been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The alignment between clinical findings and genetic results in this individual supported the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs in younger adults presenting with unexplained multisystem involvement, even in the absence of classical mucocutaneous manifestations. Prompt identification is key for informing genetic counseling, monitoring, and considering hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon premature aging syndromes resulting from Mendelian mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, which produce excessively short or impaired telomeres. This condition principally impacts tissues with high cell turnover, such as the bone marrow, skin, mucosal surfaces, hair follicles, and hepatocytes. The spectrum of TBD encompasses a variety of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional related syndromes include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of disorders, PARN mutations are associated with a unique clinical presentation: individuals harboring two mutated alleles experience autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single mutated alleles predominantly contribute to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report the case of a female in her late twenties presenting with persistent fatigue and signs of involvement across multiple organ systems, who was eventually found to have a TBD.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary center after experiencing a gradual onset of generalized weakness and early onset fatigue over the preceding five years. This led to a steady deterioration in her functional status, limiting her capacity for routine tasks when compared to individuals of similar age. In the past three months, her symptoms had progressed, resulting in such marked weakness that she now needed help with personal care. Additionally, she described breathlessness on exertion, occasional episodes of chest discomfort, unintended weight loss, and a decrease in appetite.

Upon evaluation, the patient exhibited stable haemodynamic parameters. Significant observations were pallor, icterus, early onset greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a bald tongue marked by lingual papillae atrophy.

Musculoskeletal assessment identified thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of the thenar muscles bilaterally. Abdominal assessment detected hepatomegaly, measuring 5 cm beneath the right costal margin with a span of 18 cm. Respiratory examination indicated diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular regions. The cardiovascular examination did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory assessment demonstrated pancytopenia accompanied by a reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, findings indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum assays identified marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel showed increased serum iron and ferritin, correlating with the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow analysis displayed trilineage hematopoiesis with an expansion of the erythroid series and increased iron reserves; there was no evidence supporting malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltration, and cytogenetic results were within normal limits.

Laboratory indices of hemolysis included raised lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nonetheless, the diminished reticulocyte count and marrow findings were indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis, rather than a process of peripheral immune-mediated red cell destruction.

To assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was undertaken and revealed no gross abnormalities. Histological evaluation of the duodenal biopsy identified mild chronic gastritis accompanied by a reduction in parietal cell population, which may contribute to vitamin B12 deficiency as a result of impaired intrinsic factor synthesis. Nevertheless, the extent of the patient’s clinical presentation seemed excessive for nutritional deficiency alone.

Ongoing hepatic dysfunction necessitated additional imaging and evaluation. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography indicated the presence of grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) and cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Laboratory tests revealed elevated inflammatory markers. Despite this, tests for autoimmune disorders—including antinuclear antibody and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies—were negative. Investigation for infectious etiologies such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus were generally negative, with the exception of a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for underlying infiltrative or inflammatory processes. The scan revealed uneven uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose within both axial and appendicular bone marrow. Additionally, there was widespread ground-glass opacity accompanied by several fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic alterations observed in both pulmonary fields. Hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were also identified, yet there were no signs indicative of obvious malignancy or granulomatous disease (figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation demonstrated secondary hyperparathyroidism, abnormalities in electrolyte levels (including hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis validated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which accounted for her repeated bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography alongside nerve conduction studies identified an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy exhibiting chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscles.

Considering the combination of clinical features—cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a genetic or syndromic cause was suspected.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This missense change affects a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to negatively impact protein function. Nevertheless, its contribution to the patient's disease has not been definitively established, so it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the precise pathogenicity of this variant is still unclear, both the genetic findings involving PARN and the clinical presentation of the patient align with features observed in the TBD spectrum.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unexplained weight loss, premature greying, and persistent fatigue prompted consideration of hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The notably decreased reticulocyte count indicated defective red blood cell production, narrowing the differential initially to conditions such as nutrient deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

A pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency was identified early in the evaluation, explaining many clinical findings, corroborated by bone marrow results consistent with impaired erythropoiesis. Despite successful correction of vitamin deficiencies, the widespread systemic abnormalities persisted and appeared more severe than could be explained by nutrient depletion alone.

A possible immune-mediated pathology was briefly explored based on a positive DAT and raised inflammatory markers. Nonetheless, the absence of hemolysis, negative serologic markers for autoimmunity, and lack of systemic manifestations did not support a diagnosis of autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disorder.

The cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was undetermined, as both autoimmune and infectious origins had been ruled out. The unusual combination of signs, including premature ageing, chronic cytopenias, and fibrotic changes in both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young adult, led clinicians to consider an underlying inherited condition. Despite the lack of familial history, the possibility of a de novo genetic mutation was still entertained.

This suggested the possibility of a telomere biology disorder that can resemble acquired diseases and exhibit a spectrum of penetrance. While uncommon, such conditions may not present with the traditional mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita and might instead demonstrate more nuanced symptoms such as bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs of premature aging.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, thus offering a cohesive rationale for her varied clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for 5 days, transitioned to weekly dosing for 4 weeks, and thereafter continued with monthly maintenance therapy to address significant deficiency.

Supportive management involved transfusions of whole blood to alleviate symptomatic anaemia, along with oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, vitamin D at 60 000 IU weekly, and elemental calcium at 1000 mg daily to treat osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed using conventional supplementation protocols.

She participated in physiotherapy, and her family was provided genetic counselling after the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive measures, the patient experienced alleviation of symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters remained stable, she reported reduced fatigue, and there were no additional clinical issues identified.

She remains under routine outpatient monitoring to assess hepatic and pulmonary status and to observe for any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences found at the ends of chromosomes, functioning to maintain genomic stability by protecting chromosomes from damage. During cell division, telomeres progressively shorten, and once they become sufficiently short, cells enter senescence or undergo apoptosis. Normally, telomere length is sustained by telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein comprised of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), which promotes telomere extension. Mutations affecting genes that encode elements of the telomerase complex disrupt telomerase activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and resulting in a spectrum of inherited disorders known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 inherited in an X-linked recessive manner were the first to demonstrate the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and pronounced telomere shortening, positioning DC as the prototype of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). DC is traditionally characterized by a mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or by the presence of two of these findings combined with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and a heightened risk of both hematologic and solid tumors. Severe subtypes of TBD that arise in childhood—including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are likewise caused by genetic alterations in DC-associated genes.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can manifest as isolated involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or haematological abnormalities, and may appear during adulthood in the absence of the characteristic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad. In many cases, the underlying inherited mutation is obscured by diagnoses such as aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), or gastrointestinal pathologies. The absence of the classic triad combined with delayed onset of symptoms often leads to misdiagnosis, particularly outside of specialised care settings. TBDs are commonly associated with monoallelic pathogenic variants in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1, which frequently manifest as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene encodes a member of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family that modulates gene expression by trimming the poly(A) tail of mRNAs during deadenylation. Analysis by deep sequencing of the 3′ terminal region of TERC RNA has revealed that PARN facilitates the elimination of oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which normally label nuclear RNAs for decay; this action is essential for proper 3′-end processing of TERC.

PARN is ranked among the top 20% of genes most intolerant to rare, loss-of-function mutations, underscoring its vital biological function and lending support to the concept of haploinsufficiency in individuals carrying such variants. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and extend to the wider DC/TBD disease spectrum. Homozygous PARN mutations have been documented in patients presenting with HH syndrome. Heterozygous germline pathogenic variants in PARN are also associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The earliest association between PARN and IPF was noted through the discovery of new heterozygous variants, and follow-up investigations identified rare heterozygous PARN variants in five out of 262 individuals with sporadic IPF analyzed by whole-exome sequencing, highlighting a developing link which has now been described in more than 65 published cases.1517 Nonetheless, a comprehensive understanding of the phenotypic spectrum related to PARN mutations is still lacking, primarily because of the small number of cases documented to date.

Importantly, this patient possessed a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which impacts a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). According to four out of five in silico prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, SIFT), this variant is expected to exert a detrimental functional consequence. Assessment using ACMG/AMP variant classification guidelines demonstrates that this variant fulfills several supporting criteria: PM1—the amino acid change is located within a key, established functional domain (R3H) that has no benign variations reported; PM2—the variant is either absent or exceedingly rare in population databases; PP3—multiple computational approaches predict a damaging effect; and PP2—benign missense changes are uncommon in PARN, and pathogenic missense variants represent a frequent disease mechanism. Although the clinical significance remains unclear, its possible association with TBDs is underpinned by PARN’s documented involvement and the presence of analogous missense variants (e.g., p.Lys59Arg, ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2; p.Ala383Val, ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1) described in the ClinVar database as ‘likely pathogenic.’

Various genes, differing inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can influence the involvement of organ systems in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Moreover, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices can differently impact the regenerative capacity across organ systems. In our patient's case, these intricate factors may account for the clinical presentation, even though only a single PARN allele variant was detected.

While heterozygous mutations in PARN are more typically linked with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and less with primary hematological symptoms, the cytopenia identified in our patient suggests a potential underlying TBD. This could predispose the bone marrow to increased vulnerability under hematopoietic stress, such as severe vitamin B12 deficiency. Reduction in telomere length impairs hematopoietic stem cell replication, promoting early cellular aging and elevating the likelihood of bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although it is most probable that the patient's cytopenia was caused by marked vitamin B12 deficiency, the detection of a PARN variant highlights the need for regular clinical and hematologic evaluation to monitor for any emerging features of marrow failure in an individual with this genetic risk factor.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has historically been overlooked, but recent studies indicate it may occur more frequently than previously thought. A retrospective review from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilized transient elastography and determined that 88.8% of adult individuals with disease-causing TERC or TERT variants exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with 33.3% presenting with cirrhosis. Similarly, another published case involved a patient with pancytopenia and HF, found to have monoallelic mutations in PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, the patient described herein carried a single monoallelic PARN mutation but developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN variants are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the consequent effect on telomerase activity may explain the HF commonly associated with TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations suggest a broader range of clinical presentations for PARN-related TBDs and highlight the need to recognize hepatic fibrosis as part of their clinical manifestations.

The definitive approach for confirming suspected TBD involves genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing, along with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays for telomere length determination. Management is largely supportive, individualized according to the degree of symptoms, requirements for organ transplantation, and addressing modifiable risks. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option for those exhibiting haematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical data and smaller clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may extend telomere length, the long-term consequences are not yet understood.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough workup ruled out more frequent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the pathogenic gene, and a comprehensive review of the literature contributed additional insight into this uncommon manifestation. Supportive care was administered, alongside genetic counseling for her relatives. She was informed about her diagnosis and acknowledged the absence of curative therapies. At her two-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters were unchanged and no further clinical decline was observed.
