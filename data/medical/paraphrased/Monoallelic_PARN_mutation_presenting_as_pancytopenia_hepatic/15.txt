Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year history of increasing fatigue and diffuse weakness. Physical assessment demonstrated features of premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficits were corrected, the persistence of cytopenias, premature hair graying, osteoporosis, along with hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis, suggested a possible underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome analysis identified a heterozygous PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN have been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. In this case, the clinical and genetic findings were consistent with a diagnosis of telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs in younger adults who present with unexplained multisystem involvement, even if the typical mucocutaneous signs are absent. Timely diagnosis is vital to inform genetic counseling, guide surveillance, and assess suitability for bone marrow transplantation in cases showing progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a group of uncommon premature aging syndromes that result from Mendelian mutations affecting genes involved in telomere maintenance, causing excessively short or impaired telomeres. The disorders predominantly impact tissues characterized by rapid cell turnover, such as bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and hepatocytes. TBD encompasses a wide spectrum of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classical example. Additional conditions within this group include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of disorders, PARN mutations are associated with a unique clinical presentation: individuals with two mutated alleles typically exhibit autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas carriers of a single mutated allele are more frequently observed to develop telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We describe a woman in her late 20s with prolonged fatigue and multisystem involvement who was ultimately diagnosed with a TBD.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties arrived at our tertiary facility with a 5-year progression of increasing generalized weakness and early-onset fatigue. Her physical functioning deteriorated over time, resulting in decreased performance of everyday tasks relative to others her age. In the most recent 3 months, her weakness became severe enough to hinder her ability to manage personal care, necessitating support. She further described experiencing shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest pain, unintentional weight loss, and loss of appetite.

On examination, she was haemodynamically stable. Notable findings included pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae.

On musculoskeletal assessment, thoracic scoliosis and wasting of both thenar eminences were observed. Abdominal evaluation identified hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in length. Respiratory examination demonstrated diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. Findings from the cardiovascular examination were within normal limits.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment revealed pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified dimorphic red blood cells, the presence of basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmented nuclei, all indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum testing confirmed pronounced deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results showed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, findings in keeping with ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow analysis demonstrated active formation of all three cell lineages, with notable erythroid hyperplasia and increased iron reserves, and no signs of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative processes; cytogenetic studies showed normal results.

Indicators of haemolysis included raised lactate dehydrogenase levels and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) yielded a 2+ positive result for IgG, whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nevertheless, the diminished reticulocyte production in conjunction with marrow findings pointed more toward ineffective erythropoiesis than peripheral destruction mediated by the immune system.

To investigate the possibility of gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was carried out and revealed no gross abnormalities. Histological examination of a duodenal sample indicated mild chronic gastritis along with a decreased number of parietal cells, which might be a factor in B12 deficiency through diminished intrinsic factor synthesis. Nevertheless, the extent of the patient's clinical manifestations seemed excessive relative to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency by itself.

The presence of liver dysfunction led to additional imaging and evaluation. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis, with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, as well as evidence of cirrhosis, with liver stiffness measured at 18.1 kPa. Inflammatory markers were elevated. Nevertheless, testing for autoimmune markers, including antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies, yielded negative results. Screening for infectious causes such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was predominantly negative, with the exception of positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease. The scan demonstrated uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation throughout the bone marrow of both the axial and appendicular skeleton. Bilateral lung fields revealed widespread ground-glass opacity with several fibrotic alterations and subpleural areas of atelectasis. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were present, while there were no signs indicating frank malignancy or granulomatous disorders (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable observations were secondary hyperparathyroidism, abnormalities in electrolyte balance such as low calcium and potassium levels, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis as demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyographic assessment combined with nerve conduction testing indicated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, with evidence of chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Given the constellation of findings—cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic aetiology was suspected.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This alteration affects a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to be deleterious to protein function. Nevertheless, its contribution to the patient’s disease cannot be confirmed at this time; consequently, it is classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance.’ Despite the uncertainty regarding the pathogenicity of this specific variant, both the involvement of PARN and the observed clinical presentation align with the known range of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintended weight loss, early onset greying, and persistent fatigue in this patient suggested potential haematological, nutritional, or autoimmune etiologies. A significantly reduced reticulocyte count indicated impaired red blood cell production, which prompted initial evaluation for possible nutritional deficiencies or bone marrow failure syndromes.

A significant vitamin B12 deficiency was identified promptly and explained many clinical manifestations, corroborated by bone marrow findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite addressing these deficiencies, the ongoing systemic disturbances were unexpectedly extensive and sustained.

An immune-mediated etiology was initially contemplated owing to the positive direct antiglobulin test and raised inflammatory markers. However, the lack of evidence for haemolysis, negative results on autoimmune serologic testing, and no systemic manifestations made diagnoses such as autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease unlikely.

The etiology of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was unidentified, with both autoimmune and infectious origins having been ruled out. The presence of premature aging characteristics, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrotic changes affecting both the liver and lungs in a young adult raised the possibility of an underlying genetic disorder. Despite the lack of any known familial cases, the chance of a de novo genetic mutation could not be excluded.

This gave rise to the consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of imitating acquired diseases and displaying varying degrees of penetrance. While uncommon, it may not exhibit the typical mucocutaneous triad characteristic of dyskeratosis congenita (DC), but rather present insidiously with bone marrow insufficiency, fibrotic changes in organs, and signs indicative of premature aging.

This was substantiated by genetic analysis, which identified a pathogenic monoallelic variant in the PARN gene, providing a unifying explanation for the constellation of her clinical features.

Treatment:

The patient was treated with intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance to correct severe deficiency.

Supportive care involved administering whole blood transfusions to address symptomatic anemia, along with oral folic acid at a dose of 1 mg per day and vitamin D at 60 000 IU weekly, combined with 1000 mg daily of elemental calcium for the management of osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed using standard supplementation protocols.

She also underwent physiotherapy, and her family received genetic counselling following diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After correction of her deficiencies and provision of supportive measures, the patient’s symptoms improved. At her 2-week follow-up visit, hematologic parameters remained stable, fatigue had lessened, and there were no additional clinical issues identified.

She continues to receive regular outpatient follow-up for surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function and to monitor the progression of bone marrow or systemic involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the ends of chromosomes that function to protect chromosomes from deterioration, thereby maintaining genome stability. During cellular replication, telomeres progressively become shorter, and once a threshold length is reached, this initiates cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, the enzyme telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein made up of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts this shortening by adding telomeric repeats. Mutations in genes encoding telomerase subunits disrupt this activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and giving rise to a set of inherited disorders known as telomere biology disorders (TBD).

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 located on the X chromosome recessively were the first to connect dyskeratosis congenita (DC) with significantly abbreviated telomeres, establishing DC as the primary example of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). DC is traditionally diagnosed based on the presence of the classic mucocutaneous triad—oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticulated skin pigmentation—or when two of these findings occur in conjunction with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized features of DC include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and elevated susceptibility to both haematological and solid organ cancers. More severe forms of childhood-onset TBDs, including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, are likewise attributed to pathogenic variants in the same genes implicated in DC.

Telomere biology disorders can manifest with single-system mucocutaneous, organ-based, or hematological symptoms and sometimes become apparent in adulthood without the hallmark dyskeratosis congenita triad. Commonly, the underlying genetic abnormality is obscured by clinical presentations resembling aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, hepatofibrosis, or various gastrointestinal disorders. In the absence of the typical triad and because symptoms may arise later, patients are often misdiagnosed, particularly when evaluated outside of specialized institutions. Heterozygous pathogenic variants in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are frequently detected in TBDs, commonly presenting clinically as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

The PARN gene is part of a well-conserved group of exoribonucleases and functions to reduce the length of mRNA poly(A) tails via deadenylation, playing a role in the control of gene expression. High-throughput sequencing analysis of the 3’ end of TERC RNA revealed that PARN is necessary to eliminate oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which otherwise mark nuclear RNAs for degradation; this removal step is essential for proper 3’-end maturation of TERC.

PARN ranks within the lowest 20% of genes for tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, reinforcing its critical function and the idea of haploinsufficiency among individuals with such variants. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are established as causative for dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader clinical manifestations of DC/TBD. Individuals with biallelic PARN alterations have been documented with HH syndrome, while heterozygous germline pathogenic variants have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was first proposed following discovery of novel heterozygous variants, with later investigations identifying rare heterozygous PARN variants in 5 of 262 subjects with sporadic IPF subjected to whole-exome sequencing. This has solidified a growing correlation, which has now been described in more than 65 reported cases in the literature.1517 Despite these findings, the full clinical spectrum attributed to PARN mutations is not well-characterized because of the relatively small number of published cases.

Of particular interest, the patient carried a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), located at a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). This variant was predicted to be deleterious by four out of five in silico missense prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT). According to ACMG/AMP variant classification criteria, the variant satisfies several lines of supporting evidence: PM1—the alteration lies within a well-characterized and essential functional domain (R3H domain) that shows no benign variation; PM2—the variant is either absent or extremely infrequent in control population datasets; PP3—several computational models suggest a harmful effect on the gene or its product; and PP2—PARN is characterized by a scarcity of benign missense variants and disease causation frequently involves missense alterations. While the clinical relevance of this variant is currently uncertain, its possible association with TBDs is reinforced by the established function of PARN as well as comparisons to other PARN missense changes, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), both classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

A variety of genes, inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and the phenomenon of somatic genetic rescue can impact organ systems in telomere biology disorders (TBDs) in different ways. Moreover, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices may distinctively affect the replicative potential of specific organs. Regarding our patient, such multifactorial influences might account for the clinical features seen, even though only a single heterozygous PARN variant was found.

While heterozygous PARN mutations are typically linked to IPF rather than primary hematologic abnormalities, the development of cytopenia in this case suggests the potential for an underlying TBD, which could increase the bone marrow's vulnerability to hematopoietic stressors like profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Reduced telomere length impairs the proliferative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in premature cellular aging and heightened susceptibility to BMFS. Although the observed cytopenia in this patient was most likely a consequence of marked vitamin B12 deficiency, the identification of a PARN variant highlights the need for continued clinical and hematological surveillance to monitor for any progression toward marrow failure in this individual with a genetic predisposition.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has frequently been overlooked, but recent data indicate it may occur more commonly than once thought. A retrospective study conducted by the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography revealed that 88.8% of adult individuals carrying pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with 33.3% presenting with cirrhosis. Another published case described a patient presenting with both pancytopenia and HF who possessed single allelic variants in PARN (c.1613C>G) as well as TERT (c.2476G>A). In comparison, our case involved a patient with only a heterozygous PARN mutation who nonetheless manifested both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their downstream effects on telomerase activity might explain the development of HF, typically seen in association with TERT or TERC mutations. These observations broaden the clinical profile of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the need to recognize HF as a relevant feature of this disease spectrum.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing along with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays to assess telomere length. Management focuses on supportive care, individualized according to symptom burden, requirements for organ transplantation, and efforts to reduce risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option for individuals presenting with haematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical and early clinical investigations indicate possible telomere extension with androgenic agents such as danazol and nandrolone, the durability of these effects is not yet established.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a comprehensive assessment ruled out other typical etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the underlying genetic mutation, and thorough review of existing literature helped deepen insight into this uncommon manifestation. Supportive care was administered, and genetic counseling was offered to her relatives. She demonstrated comprehension of her diagnosis and recognized that curative treatment was not an option. On reassessment two weeks later, her hematological parameters were unchanged, and no additional clinical decline was noted.
