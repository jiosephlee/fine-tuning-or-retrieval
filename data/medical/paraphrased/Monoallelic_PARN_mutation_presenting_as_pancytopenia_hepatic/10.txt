Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year history of increasing fatigue and widespread weakness. On examination, findings included indicators of premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both liver and spleen. Laboratory assessments demonstrated pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Although her nutritional deficiencies were corrected, the combination of cytopenias, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis suggested an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome analysis revealed a heterozygous missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline changes in PARN are known to cause dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The concordance between clinical presentation and genetic findings in this patient was supportive of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs in young adults presenting with unexplained involvement of multiple organ systems, regardless of the presence of typical mucocutaneous features. Prompt diagnosis is essential to inform genetic counseling, ongoing monitoring, and timely consideration of bone marrow transplantation in advancing disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated aging due to inherited mutations in genes responsible for telomere maintenance, resulting in telomeres that are unusually short or not functioning properly. These disorders primarily impact rapidly proliferating tissues such as those found in the bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver cells. The clinical spectrum of TBDs is broad, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Other recognized forms are Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Of the mutations found within this class of diseases, PARN mutations exhibit a unique clinical presentation. When both alleles are affected, they result in autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6). In contrast, single allele mutations are more frequently associated with pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure linked to telomere abnormalities (PFBMFT4).

We describe a woman in her late 20s with prolonged fatigue and multisystem involvement who was ultimately diagnosed with a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care center with a five-year duration of gradually increasing generalized weakness and early onset of fatigue. During this period, her overall physical capabilities diminished, limiting her participation in daily routines relative to others her age. In the most recent three months, her muscle weakness had deteriorated further, significantly affecting her self-care and necessitating help from others. She also described experiencing shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest pain, unintentional weight loss, and decreased appetite.

On examination, she was haemodynamically stable. Notable findings included pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae.

On musculoskeletal assessment, thoracic scoliosis and wasting of the thenar muscles on both sides were noted. On abdominal evaluation, the liver was palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin with a span of 18 cm, indicating hepatomegaly. Respiratory system examination demonstrated diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular assessment yielded normal findings.

Investigations:

The initial labs revealed pancytopenia along with a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with increased nuclear segmentation, findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Further biochemical assessment showed marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results demonstrated increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, findings also supportive of ineffective erythropoiesis (table 1).

Examination of the bone marrow demonstrated trilineage hematopoiesis, erythroid hyperplasia, and increased iron deposition, with no signs of malignant infiltration, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic studies yielded normal results.

Laboratory indicators of hemolysis consisted of raised lactate dehydrogenase and increased indirect bilirubin levels. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) showed a 2+ positivity for IgG, whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nonetheless, the diminished reticulocyte count and marrow findings were more indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis, rather than destruction mediated by peripheral immune mechanisms.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted to investigate possible malabsorption in the gastrointestinal tract, revealing no gross abnormalities. Histological examination of a duodenal biopsy indicated mild chronic gastritis and a decreased number of parietal cells, suggesting impaired intrinsic factor secretion as a potential cause for B12 deficiency. Nevertheless, the pronounced clinical manifestations seemed excessive compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

Liver dysfunction led to additional imaging studies and evaluations. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m and evidence of cirrhosis, as shown by a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. There was an elevation in inflammatory markers. Autoimmune screening, including tests for antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies, returned negative results. The infectious disease evaluation—including viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus—was generally negative, with the exception of a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG result (table 2).

To assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease, a positron emission tomography–CT scan was conducted. This revealed uneven uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose within both the axial and appendicular bone marrow. In addition, both lungs demonstrated widespread ground-glass opacities accompanied by multiple areas of fibrosis and subpleural atelectasis. Hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were present, but there were no signs of clear malignancy or granulomatous pathology (see figures 1 and 2).

Other observations revealed secondary hyperparathyroidism, imbalances in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D insufficiency, as well as osteoporosis identified through dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (table 3). Electromyography alongside nerve conduction evaluations demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with longstanding neurogenic alterations involving both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Given the constellation of findings—cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic aetiology was suspected.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This missense mutation affects a highly conserved amino acid and computational predictions indicate it is likely detrimental to protein function. Nevertheless, a definitive connection between this variant and the disease could not be established, leading to its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite the unclear pathogenic status of this particular variant, both PARN involvement and the patient's clinical presentation align with the range of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintended weight loss, early onset of hair greying, and persistent fatigue prompted evaluation for underlying haematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune conditions. The presence of a significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, directing the initial differential diagnosis toward nutritional deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Profound vitamin B12 deficiency was identified promptly and explained multiple clinical manifestations, with bone marrow findings indicating ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite addressing the deficiencies, significant systemic disturbances persisted and appeared out of proportion to the corrected deficits.

An immune-driven mechanism was initially contemplated owing to a positive direct antiglobulin test and raised inflammatory markers. However, the lack of haemolysis, negative results on autoimmune serological testing, and no evidence of systemic symptoms made autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disorders unlikely.

The origin of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was unclear, as evaluations ruled out both autoimmune and infectious etiologies. The distinctive combination of early-onset ageing signs, chronic cytopenia, and fibrotic changes affecting both the liver and lungs in a young adult prompted consideration of an unrecognized genetic disorder. While the lack of relevant family history suggested a hereditary syndrome was less likely, the potential for a de novo genetic mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of resembling acquired diseases and exhibiting variable degrees of expression. Although uncommon, it might present without the classic mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), and instead manifest more insidiously through bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs of premature ageing.

This was substantiated by genetic analysis, which identified a pathogenic monoallelic variant in the PARN gene, providing a unifying explanation for the constellation of her clinical features.

Treatment:

The patient was treated with intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance to correct severe deficiency.

Management included transfusion of whole blood to address symptomatic anemia, along with administration of oral folic acid at a dose of 1 mg daily. Osteoporosis was treated using vitamin D at 60,000 IU weekly in combination with 1,000 mg daily of elemental calcium. Standard electrolyte supplementation was provided to rectify any detected imbalances.

She also underwent physiotherapy, and her family received genetic counselling following diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After deficiencies were corrected and supportive measures provided, the patient experienced relief of symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters remained stable, she reported decreased fatigue, and there were no additional clinical issues noted.

She continues to receive regular outpatient follow-up for surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function and to monitor the progression of bone marrow or systemic involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the termini of chromosomes and function to maintain chromosomal stability by preventing DNA degradation. During normal cell proliferation, these telomeric regions progressively become shorter, ultimately reaching a threshold that induces either cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Telomere length is physiologically preserved by telomerase, an enzyme complex composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC), which extends telomeres. Pathogenic mutations affecting genes that encode telomerase components compromise the activity of this enzyme, resulting in accelerated shortening of telomeres and giving rise to a spectrum of hereditary disorders known as telomere biology disorders (TBD).

X-linked recessive mutations in DKC1 were the first to demonstrate the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, thus identifying DC as the classic example of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). The traditional clinical criteria for DC involve the mucocutaneous triad of oral leukoplakia, dystrophic nails, and reticular hyperpigmentation of the skin, or the presence of two of these findings along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and an elevated risk for both hematologic and solid tumors. Certain severe telomere biology disorders manifesting in childhood—such as Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are also attributable to variants in genes associated with DC.

Telomere biology disorders can appear with isolated involvement of the mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or the hematologic system, and may occur in adults in the absence of the characteristic dyskeratosis congenita triad. The underlying genetic abnormality is often concealed by diagnoses such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, heart failure, or gastrointestinal disorders. Due to the absence of the typical triad and a later onset of symptoms, these individuals are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly when not evaluated at specialized institutions. Heterozygous mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 genes are frequently observed in telomere biology disorders, commonly manifesting as idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure syndromes.

The PARN gene encodes a member of an evolutionarily conserved exoribonuclease family and functions to regulate gene expression by trimming the poly(A) tail of mRNA via deadenylation. Extensive sequencing of the 3'-terminal region of TERC RNA revealed that PARN is necessary for eliminating oligo(A) tails added post-transcriptionally to nuclear RNAs destined for degradation, a process essential for proper maturation of the 3' end of TERC.

PARN ranks among the 20% of genes most intolerant to rare, disruptive mutations, emphasizing its critical biological function and supporting haploinsufficiency in heterozygous individuals. Pathogenic germline alterations in PARN are established causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and are implicated in the broader clinical range of DC/TBD. Biallelic pathogenic variants in PARN have been described in patients with HH syndrome. Germline heterozygous pathogenic variants have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association of PARN with IPF was initially proposed following the discovery of novel heterozygous variants, with additional research identifying monoallelic rare PARN mutations in five of 262 sporadic IPF patients who underwent whole-exome sequencing. This connection is now supported by reports of more than 65 cases in the literature.1517 Nonetheless, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations linked to PARN mutations is not well characterized because of the scarcity of reported cases.

Our patient was found to carry a heterozygous missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). This variant is predicted to be deleterious by four out of five computational missense prediction programs, including LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT. According to ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant classification, this variant satisfies several supportive criteria: PM1—the alteration is located in a critical, well-established functional domain (the R3H domain) without any known benign variations; PM2—the variant is absent or extremely rare in population data; PP3—multiple computational algorithms support a damaging effect on the gene or its product; PP2—PARN has a low frequency of benign missense changes, and missense variants are a common disease mechanism for this gene. Although its clinical importance is not yet established, its possible involvement in telomere biology disorders (TBDs) is underscored by the recognized function of PARN and similarities to other PARN missense changes such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), both of which are annotated as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

Numerous genes, diverse patterns of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue may impact different organ systems in distinct ways in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Furthermore, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices can modulate the proliferative potential across various tissues. In the case of our patient, such multifactorial influences could account for the clinical presentation, even though only a single PARN allele variant was detected.

While heterozygous PARN mutations are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis rather than primary hematological presentations, our patient's cytopenia suggests the potential for an underlying telomere biology disorder that could heighten the bone marrow's vulnerability to hematopoietic stressors, such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres impair the proliferative ability of hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in premature cellular aging and a greater likelihood of bone marrow failure syndromes. Although the cytopenia in this case was most plausibly due to marked vitamin B12 deficiency, identification of a PARN variant highlights the need for regular clinical and hematological follow-up to monitor for the development of bone marrow failure features in this genetically at-risk patient.

Cirrhosis has frequently gone unrecognized in TBD, but new data indicate it may occur more commonly than previously thought. A retrospective study utilizing transient elastography from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry reported that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), and 33.3% progressed to cirrhosis. A similar case in published literature detailed an individual with pancytopenia and HF, who possessed monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Unlike this, our case involved a patient with a single monoallelic PARN mutation, yet who developed hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are more typically linked with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), the resulting telomerase dysfunction could explain HF, a feature more commonly linked to TERT or TERC mutations. These observations highlight a broadened clinical range for PARN-related TBDs and emphasize the need to recognize HF within its phenotypic presentation.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis utilizing next-generation sequencing in combination with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assays for telomere length assessment. Management is primarily supportive and individualized, depending on symptom intensity, the requirement for organ transplantation, and efforts to minimize risk factors. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative approach for patients presenting with hematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical and early-phase clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may induce telomere lengthening, their long-term efficacy and safety have yet to be established.

The patient exhibited clinical signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough assessment ruled out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible genetic abnormality, and a comprehensive review of the literature enhanced insight into this uncommon case. Supportive care was administered, along with genetic counseling offered to family members. The patient comprehended the characteristics of her illness and acknowledged that definitive curative treatments were unavailable. During her follow-up visit two weeks later, her blood counts had not changed, and no new clinical worsening was observed.
