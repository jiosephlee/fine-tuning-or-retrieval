Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year duration of worsening fatigue and diffuse weakness. On physical examination, findings included signs of premature ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory tests revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, marked deficiency of vitamin B12, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were corrected, the combination of cytopenia, early hair greying, osteoporosis, hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis suggested the possibility of an underlying genetic condition. Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), categorized as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline PARN mutations have previously been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. In this instance, the clinical and genetic findings together supported the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to evaluate TBDs in young adults with unexplained multisystem involvement, even in the absence of the classic mucocutaneous findings. Prompt identification is important for directing genetic counseling, clinical monitoring, and timely consideration of bone marrow transplantation if disease progression occurs.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) are uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated aging, resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance and function, which cause telomeres to become excessively short or impaired. Tissues with high cellular turnover, such as those in the bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver, are principally involved. TBDs encompass a range of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) representing the primary example. Additional conditions include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Of the numerous mutations recognized within this category of conditions, PARN mutations are associated with a unique clinical presentation. Individuals with mutations affecting both alleles exhibit autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with single-allele mutations are typically predisposed to telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a female patient in her late twenties presenting with persistent fatigue and multiple organ system manifestations who was subsequently found to have a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary institution with a 5-year duration of gradually increasing generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. Her physical functioning progressively deteriorated over this period, limiting her participation in daily tasks relative to her age group. During the preceding three months, her muscle weakness significantly intensified, leading to compromised self-care and the need for help with routine activities. Additionally, she described shortness of breath upon exertion, occasional chest pain, unintentional weight loss, and decreased appetite.

Upon assessment, her haemodynamic status was stable. Key observations consisted of pallor, jaundice, early greying of scalp hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and an atrophic bald tongue with loss of lingual papillae.

The musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of the thenar muscles bilaterally. Abdominal assessment found the liver to be enlarged, palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin with a total span of 18 cm. Respiratory examination detected diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular assessment yielded no abnormal findings.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory tests indicated pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte count (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation—findings pointing toward ineffective erythropoiesis. Serum assays identified marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results showed increased serum iron and ferritin levels, correlating with ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow evaluation demonstrated trilineage hematopoiesis with erythroid hyperplasia and augmented iron stores, with no evidence to suggest malignancy, dysplasia, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic assessment yielded normal results.

Haemolysis markers were characterized by heightened lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) returned a positive result for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect test was negative. Nonetheless, the inadequate reticulocyte production and bone marrow findings were indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than destruction mediated by peripheral immune mechanisms.

In order to investigate potential gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was carried out and appeared normal upon gross inspection. Histological examination of duodenal tissue demonstrated mild chronic gastritis with decreased numbers of parietal cells, which may have led to vitamin B12 deficiency by reducing intrinsic factor production. Nevertheless, the clinical manifestations seemed too severe to be fully explained by nutritional deficiency alone.

The presence of liver dysfunction warranted additional imaging and evaluation. Abdominal ultrasonography identified hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography revealed grade II hepatic steatosis, indicated by a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, and evidence of cirrhosis, with a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. Inflammatory markers were elevated. Despite this, serologic tests for autoimmune conditions—including antinuclear antibodies and markers for autoimmune hepatitis—returned negative. The infectious disease panel for viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus yielded unremarkable results, except for a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG result (refer to table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was conducted to investigate potential hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disorders. The imaging revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose activity within both the axial and appendicular regions of the bone marrow. There was widespread ground-glass opacity accompanied by numerous fibrotic alterations and subpleural areas of atelectasis affecting both lungs. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were observed; however, there were no indications of clear malignancy or granulomatous conditions (figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation demonstrated secondary hyperparathyroidism, electrolyte imbalances including hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis, as verified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, accounting for her ongoing bone pain and frequent fractures (see table 3). Electromyography along with nerve conduction studies indicated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy accompanied by chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

The aggregation of clinical features—cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early onset greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—raised suspicion for an underlying syndromic or genetic cause.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single allele missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This variant affects a highly conserved amino acid and is anticipated to impair protein function. Despite these findings, its contribution to the disease cannot be definitively determined, so it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the exact pathogenic nature of this variant is unresolved, both the presence of the PARN gene alteration and the patient’s clinical presentation align with the features seen in TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient’s symptoms—including pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, early onset greying of hair, and persistent fatigue—suggested potential involvement of hematologic, nutritional, or autoimmune conditions. The significantly decreased reticulocyte count indicated inadequate red blood cell production, initially prompting evaluation for deficiencies in essential nutrients as well as bone marrow failure syndromes.

Early confirmation of profound vitamin B12 deficiency explained many of the clinical and laboratory findings, as bone marrow analysis showed evidence of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite correction of the vitamin deficiencies, the patient’s multisystem abnormalities persisted and were notably disproportionate to the resolved nutritional deficits.

The possibility of an immune-mediated mechanism was entertained due to the presence of a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and increased inflammatory markers. However, the lack of hemolysis, negative autoimmune laboratory tests, and absence of other systemic manifestations made diagnoses such as autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disorders unlikely.

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) presented without an identifiable cause, as both autoimmune and infectious origins were ruled out. The unusual combination of signs indicating premature aging, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young adult raised concerns regarding a hidden genetic condition. Despite the lack of familial occurrence, suggesting that a hereditary syndrome was unlikely, the possibility of a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD), known to imitate acquired diseases and show variable expression among individuals. Although uncommon, such disorders may not display the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), but instead present more insidiously with bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs of premature aging.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a disease-causing heterozygous mutation in the PARN gene, offering a comprehensive account for her observed clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg daily for five days, then transitioned to weekly injections over four weeks, and subsequently moved to monthly maintenance therapy to address the marked deficiency.

Supportive measures encompassed transfusion of whole blood for management of symptomatic anemia, administration of oral folic acid (1 mg daily), vitamin D (60,000 IU weekly), and elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) for osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with conventional supplementation.

In addition, the patient participated in physiotherapy, and her family was provided with genetic counselling after the TBD diagnosis was established.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive management, the patient experienced relief of symptoms. At the two-week follow-up, her hematologic parameters remained steady, she reported reduced fatigue, and there were no additional clinical issues identified.

She remains under routine outpatient observation to assess liver and lung function, as well as to track any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the ends of chromosomes, functioning to protect chromosomal integrity and prevent degradation. During normal cell division, telomeres progressively shorten, and when they reach a threshold length, this initiates cellular senescence or programmed cell death. The telomerase enzyme, consisting of a ribonucleoprotein complex with telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), serves to counteract telomere shortening by elongating telomeric regions. Nevertheless, pathogenic mutations in genes responsible for telomerase complex constituents disrupt enzymatic activity, causing early telomere shortening and giving rise to a spectrum of genetic disorders classified as TBD.

The association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres was first identified through X-linked recessive mutations in the DKC1 gene, establishing DC as the prototype among telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Traditionally, DC is characterized by a triad of mucocutaneous findings: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticulated skin pigmentation; alternatively, a diagnosis may be made when two of these clinical signs occur alongside bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional commonly observed features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and a higher incidence of both hematologic and solid tumors. Moreover, certain severe, early-onset TBD variants—including Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are caused by mutations in genes also associated with DC.

TBDs can exhibit only mucocutaneous, organ-specific, or hematologic manifestations and may arise in adulthood absent the characteristic DC triad. Frequently, the underlying genetic abnormality is concealed by disorders like aplastic anaemia, BMFS, IPF, HF, or gastrointestinal conditions. In the absence of the classic triad and because of late clinical onset, such cases are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly outside specialized facilities. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly detected in TBDs and often manifest as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-preserved group of exoribonucleases, functions to reduce mRNA poly(A) tail length via deadenylation, thereby controlling gene expression. High-throughput sequencing of the TERC RNA 3’ terminal revealed that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added post-transcriptionally, which designate nuclear RNAs for degradation and are necessary for proper 3’-end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks within the bottom 20% of genes regarding tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical function and the notion of haploinsufficiency among carriers. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and the broader group of DC/TBD. Cases of HH syndrome have involved biallelic PARN mutations, while monoallelic germline pathogenic variants are linked with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was first suggested after discovery of novel monoallelic mutations, with subsequent studies finding monoallelic, rare PARN variants in five out of 262 sporadic IPF cases subjected to whole-exome sequencing. This connection has now been reported in more than 65 cases in the literature.1517 Despite these findings, the full clinical spectrum tied to PARN mutations remains largely unclear, given the small number of cases described.

Importantly, this patient carried a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which impacts a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). According to four out of five in silico missense prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), the variant is expected to exert a deleterious effect on function. Assessment using ACMG/AMP guidelines reveals that this variant fulfills several supporting criteria: PM1—the alteration is located within a critical, well-established functional domain (R3H domain) where no benign variants have been documented; PM2—it is either absent or extremely infrequent in population databases; PP3—multiple computational methods forecast a damaging impact on the gene or its product; and PP2—the PARN gene exhibits a low incidence of benign missense variation and missense mutations are a typical disease-causing mechanism in this gene. While the clinical impact of this variant remains unclear, its possible significance for telomere biology disorders is reinforced by PARN’s known function and by similarities with other missense variants in PARN, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), both of which are classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can display variability in clinical presentation due to multiple genes involved, differing modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and the phenomenon of somatic genetic rescue, each potentially impacting organ systems differently. Furthermore, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices may alter the regenerative abilities of these systems in distinct ways. These factors may account for the phenotype seen in our patient, despite the identification of only a single pathogenic allele in PARN.

Monoallelic mutations in PARN are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than to initial haematological findings; however, the detection of cytopenia in this case raises concern for a possible underlying TBD, which could render the bone marrow more vulnerable to haematopoietic insults such as severe vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres impair the self-renewal capacity of haematopoietic stem cells, increasing cellular aging and the risk for bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although our patient’s cytopenia is most likely a result of significant vitamin B12 deficiency, the identification of a PARN variant highlights the need for continued clinical and haematological follow-up to monitor for potential progression to marrow failure in this at-risk individual.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has historically been under-diagnosed, but recent data indicates its incidence may be higher than previously assumed. In a retrospective study conducted by the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, transient elastography revealed that 88.8% of adults with disease-causing mutations in TERC or TERT demonstrated at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with 33.3% showing evidence of cirrhosis. Another report in the literature detailed a patient exhibiting pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who carried monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our patient possessed only a monoallelic PARN variant and nevertheless developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are most frequently linked with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their effect on telomerase activity may result in hepatic fibrosis that is more commonly associated with TERT or TERC mutations. These observations contribute to a broader understanding of the clinical range of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the need to recognize hepatic fibrosis as a component of this phenotype.

Definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, along with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation to assess telomere length. Management primarily focuses on supportive care, addressing symptom burden, consideration of organ transplantation, and modification of risk factors; however, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a potential cure for individuals presenting with haematological involvement. Although androgens such as danazol and nandrolone have shown some evidence of promoting telomere elongation in preclinical and limited clinical investigations, the durability of these effects over time has yet to be established.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough assessment ruling out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the responsible genetic mutation, and comprehensive review of the literature shed light on the unusual clinical scenario. Supportive care was initiated, and her family received genetic counselling. The patient comprehended her diagnosis and acknowledged the absence of curative treatments. At her follow-up visit two weeks later, her hematologic parameters were unchanged, and there was no evidence of worsening clinical status.
