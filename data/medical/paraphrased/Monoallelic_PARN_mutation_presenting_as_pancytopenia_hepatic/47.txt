Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties was evaluated for a five-year duration of worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination uncovered evidence of early-onset ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory workup demonstrated pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although her nutritional deficits were corrected, the combination of cytopenia, premature hair greying, osteoporosis, liver and lung fibrosis suggested a possible inherited disorder. Clinical exome analysis revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was considered a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN are implicated in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and in telomere-related syndromes causing pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The clinical and genetic findings in this instance supported a diagnosis of telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the necessity of considering TBDs in young individuals presenting with unexplained multisystem involvement, irrespective of the presence of classic mucocutaneous manifestations. Timely identification is essential for facilitating genetic counselling, ongoing surveillance, and evaluating bone marrow transplantation in progressive cases.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes characterized by early-onset ageing, arising from inherited mutations in telomere-associated genes that result in unusually short or impaired telomeres. Cells with high proliferative activity, such as those in the bone marrow, epidermis, mucosal tissues, hair follicles, and liver, are primarily impacted. TBDs encompass a variety of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional disorders in this group include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of diseases, PARN mutations are characterized by specific clinical features; two mutated alleles result in autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single mutated alleles are typically associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report a case of a female in her late twenties who presented with chronic fatigue and symptoms affecting multiple organ systems, eventually receiving a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A female in her late 20s arrived at our tertiary facility with a 5-year progression of generalized weakness and increased susceptibility to fatigue. Throughout this period, her ability to carry out routine tasks diminished, making daily activities more difficult relative to others her age. During the past 3 months, her weakness intensified, hindering self-care and necessitating support. Additionally, she described symptoms including shortness of breath with exertion, occasional chest discomfort, loss of appetite, and weight reduction.

On physical assessment, the patient demonstrated stable haemodynamic parameters. Significant findings were pallor, presence of icterus, early greying of the hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth tongue with loss of lingual papillae.

Evaluation of the musculoskeletal system identified thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of the thenar muscles bilaterally. The abdominal exam detected hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and a span of 18 cm. Pulmonary assessment showed diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular examination revealed no abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment identified pancytopenia accompanied by a reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). The peripheral blood smear displayed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, which are indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Further serum analyses confirmed marked deficiencies of vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel showed increased serum iron and ferritin concentrations, findings aligned with ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow analysis demonstrated active trilineage hematopoiesis with prominent erythroid hyperplasia and augmented iron reserves, with no signs of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltration; cytogenetic testing yielded normal results.

Haemolytic markers were positive for elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. The direct antiglobulin (DAT) test was reactive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nevertheless, the diminished reticulocyte production and bone marrow observations favored a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than destruction secondary to immune mechanisms.

To investigate the possibility of gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was conducted, which did not reveal any gross abnormalities. Histological analysis of the duodenal sample demonstrated mild chronic gastritis with a decrease in parietal cells, a finding that could have contributed to vitamin B12 malabsorption by affecting intrinsic factor secretion. Despite this, the clinical manifestations were more severe than would be expected from nutritional deficiency as the sole cause.

Given the evidence of hepatic dysfunction, additional imaging and evaluation were performed. Abdominal ultrasound showed enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography identified grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) as well as cirrhosis, indicated by liver stiffness of 18.1 kPa. Inflammatory markers were elevated. Testing for autoimmune liver disease, including antinuclear antibody and autoimmune hepatitis antibody panels, was negative. Assessment for infectious etiologies such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus yielded negative results with the exception of Epstein-Barr virus IgG positivity (table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was conducted to investigate potential hidden infiltrative or inflammatory conditions. The scan revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation throughout the bone marrow of both the axial and appendicular skeleton. Bilateral pulmonary findings included widespread ground-glass opacities accompanied by numerous fibrotic alterations and areas of subpleural atelectasis. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were observed, yet there was no indication of obvious malignancy or granulomatous disorder (see figures 1 and 2).

Further evaluation revealed secondary hyperparathyroidism, abnormalities in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, deficiency in vitamin D, and osteoporosis demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, accounting for her ongoing bone pain and repeated fractures (table 3). Electromyographic assessment and nerve conduction tests identified asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, with chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscles.

Considering this combination of manifestations—including cytopenia, impaired liver function, early greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic cause was considered likely.

Clinical exome analysis revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This variant affects a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to have a detrimental effect on protein function. Nonetheless, its exact contribution to the disease remains unclear, and as a result, it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite the indeterminate pathogenicity of this particular variant, the association of PARN and the patient's clinical presentation are compatible with the spectrum of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited pancytopenia, unintended weight loss, early greying of hair, and fatigue, prompting consideration of hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The markedly reduced reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis and prompted initial evaluation for possible nutrient deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Severe deficiency of vitamin B12 was promptly identified, which explained several clinical manifestations and was corroborated by bone marrow findings consistent with ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, the overall systemic impairment persisted and appeared disproportionate, even following correction of the identified deficiencies.

Given a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and increased inflammatory markers, an immune-mediated disorder was briefly contemplated. However, lack of evidence for haemolysis, negative results from autoimmune serologic studies, and absence of systemic symptoms did not support a diagnosis of autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease.

The cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was indeterminate, as both autoimmune and infectious origins had been ruled out. The unusual combination of early-onset aging manifestations, sustained cytopenia, and fibrotic changes in the lungs and liver observed in a young adult prompted clinicians to consider an obscure inherited disorder. Despite no reported familial occurrence, the possibility of a de novo genetic mutation could not be dismissed.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of resembling acquired diseases and exhibiting diverse penetrance. Although infrequently encountered, such a disorder may not display the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), but instead may present with milder signs including bone marrow dysfunction, fibrosis of organs, and indications of premature ageing.

Genetic testing supported this by detecting a pathogenic single-allele variant in the PARN gene, thereby offering a cohesive rationale for the patient’s range of clinical symptoms.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for five consecutive days, subsequently on a weekly basis for four weeks, and thereafter monthly for ongoing maintenance to address profound deficiency.

Additional supportive measures comprised whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anemia, oral folic acid at 1 mg daily, and vitamin D at 60,000 IU weekly in combination with elemental calcium 1000 mg per day for treatment of osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with routine supplementation.

Physiotherapy was instituted, and genetic counseling was provided to her family after a diagnosis of a TBD was established.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient experienced relief of symptoms after addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive treatment. At her two-week follow-up appointment, her hematologic parameters remained stable, her fatigue was reduced, and no additional clinical issues had arisen.

She remains under ongoing outpatient care for monitoring liver and lung function, as well as assessment for any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the ends of chromosomes, functioning to protect chromosomes from deterioration and thereby maintaining genomic stability. During normal cellular replication, telomeres progressively become shorter, eventually reaching a threshold at which cells enter senescence or undergo apoptosis. Typically, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein enzyme consisting of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts telomere shortening by extending telomere length. Mutations in genes encoding telomerase complex constituents disrupt this enzyme's activity, causing accelerated telomere loss and manifesting as a spectrum of inherited disorders known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 exhibiting X-linked recessive inheritance were the first to demonstrate the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and profoundly short telomeres, establishing DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). DC is traditionally characterized by a mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation of the skin, or by the presence of any two of these features together with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical manifestations of DC include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), as well as an elevated risk for both hematological and solid tumor malignancies. Severe TBD subtypes presenting in childhood, such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, are likewise associated with mutations in genes related to DC.

TBDs can appear as isolated mucocutaneous, organ-specific, or hematologic manifestations, and may develop in adult patients even without the classic DC triad. The underlying genetic abnormality is commonly concealed by disorders such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic failure (HF), as well as gastrointestinal conditions. In the absence of the typical triad and due to a delayed onset of symptoms, affected individuals are frequently misdiagnosed, particularly outside of specialized healthcare facilities. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most often associated with TBDs, commonly presenting clinically as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-preserved group of exoribonucleases, functions in gene expression control by reducing the length of mRNA poly(A) tails through deadenylation. Extensive sequencing of the 3' end of TERC RNA demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which direct nuclear RNAs toward degradation; this removal is necessary for the proper maturation of the TERC 3' end.

PARN belongs to the top 20% of genes exhibiting the lowest tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical biological function and the relevance of haploinsufficiency among those with mutations. Germline pathogenic alterations in PARN have been acknowledged as etiological factors in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the larger DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been described with biallelic PARN mutations. Monoallelic germline pathogenic changes have been associated with telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis-bone marrow failure syndrome (PFBMFT4). The first association between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) arose when new monoallelic variants were identified, with later research reporting monoallelic rare PARN variants in five out of 262 individuals with sporadic IPF who underwent whole-exome sequencing. This link has been further supported, now noted in more than 65 cases within published reports.1517 Nonetheless, the complete clinical presentation related to PARN mutation carriers remains inadequately characterized owing to the relatively few cases described.

Of particular interest, the patient possessed a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which disrupts a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). According to four out of five computational missense prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), this variant is anticipated to have a harmful functional impact. Assessment per ACMG/AMP guidelines yields several supporting criteria: PM1—the amino acid change is situated in a vital, well-recognized functional domain (R3H domain), with no documented benign variants at this location. PM2—the variant is either absent or very uncommon in population datasets. PP3—numerous in silico tools predict a deleterious effect on the gene or its product. PP2—PARN exhibits a low frequency of benign missense changes and missense mutations are a typical cause of disease for this gene. While the clinical implications of this variant remain indeterminate, its relevance to TBDs is underscored by PARN's established biological role and similarities to other PARN missense mutations—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which are classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database.

The involvement of multiple genes, diverse inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can lead to distinct effects on various organ systems in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Moreover, environmental and lifestyle influences may impact the replicative abilities of different organs to varying degrees. These factors, in relation to our patient, may account for the clinical presentation seen, even though only a single PARN allele variant was detected.

While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than primary hematological manifestations, the presence of cytopenia in this patient suggests a possible underlying TBD, which could increase the susceptibility of bone marrow to hematopoietic stressors such as pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency. Telomere attrition impairs the proliferation capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, contributing to premature cellular aging and elevating the risk for bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the cytopenia in this case was most likely due to a significant vitamin B12 deficit, identification of the PARN variant highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematologic observation to monitor for any emerging marrow failure features in this genetically at-risk patient.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has frequently been overlooked, but recent studies indicate its incidence may be higher than previously thought. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography reported that 88.8% of adult individuals with pathogenic variants in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), while 33.3% were diagnosed with cirrhosis. A similar report in the literature described a patient presenting with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who had monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Conversely, our case involved a patient with only a monoallelic PARN mutation who developed both liver and lung fibrosis. Although monoallelic PARN variants are usually linked with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their impact on telomerase activity might explain the occurrence of hepatic fibrosis often observed in TERT or TERC mutations. These observations highlight a broadening clinical range of PARN-associated TBDs and emphasize the need to recognize hepatic fibrosis as part of its clinical manifestations.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD involves genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, along with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays to assess telomere length. Management is largely supportive, adapted to the severity of symptoms, requirements for organ transplantation, and reduction of risk factors. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may provide a cure for individuals presenting with hematologic abnormalities. Although limited preclinical and small clinical studies indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere extension, the consequences over the long term have not yet been determined.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After methodically ruling out other frequent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the responsible gene. A thorough literature review offered additional insights into this uncommon clinical scenario. Her management focused on supportive care, including genetic counseling for her relatives. The patient comprehended the specifics of her disease and acknowledged that curative treatments were not available. Two weeks later, her follow-up showed persistent stability in blood counts, with no evidence of new or worsening clinical findings.
