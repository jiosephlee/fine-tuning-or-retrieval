Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a 5-year course of worsening fatigue and generalized weakness. Physical examination demonstrated premature signs of aging, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory investigations revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, along with evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficits were corrected, the combination of cytopenias, early onset hair greying, osteoporosis, and both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis suggested an underlying genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing identified a heterozygous missense variant in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), described as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have been implicated in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. Correlation between clinical findings and genetic results in this patient pointed toward a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the necessity of considering TBDs in young adults with unexplained multisystem manifestations, even in the absence of typical mucocutaneous signs. Prompt diagnosis is key for genetic counseling, ongoing monitoring, and timely assessment for bone marrow transplantation in advancing cases.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a group of uncommon syndromes characterized by accelerated aging, resulting from inherited mutations in telomere-associated genes that produce unusually short or impaired telomeres. The most impacted tissues are those with high cellular turnover, including bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver cells. TBDs encompass a range of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional forms include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this group of diseases, PARN mutations are associated with a unique clinical presentation: individuals with mutations in both alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with a single affected allele are more commonly found to have telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a woman in her late twenties who experienced persistent fatigue and involvement of multiple organ systems, leading to a final diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was evaluated at our tertiary care facility due to a 5-year progression of increasing generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. During this period, her ability to carry out routine activities steadily diminished compared to individuals of similar age. In the most recent 3 months, her weakness had intensified sufficiently to interfere with personal care and necessitate help from others. Additional symptoms included shortness of breath with exertion, episodic chest pain, loss of weight, and diminished appetite.

On assessment, her haemodynamic parameters were within normal limits. Significant observations comprised pallor, icterus, early greying of the hair, hyperpigmentation of the knuckles, and a smooth, depapillated tongue indicative of lingual papillae atrophy.

Evaluation of the musculoskeletal system demonstrated thoracic scoliosis along with atrophy of the thenar eminences bilaterally. Abdominal inspection indicated hepatomegaly, palpated 5 cm beneath the right costal margin with a span of 18 cm. Respiratory findings included diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular regions. The cardiovascular examination did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment revealed pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed dimorphic red cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, all indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum assays established profound deficiencies in vitamin B12 and folate. Iron profile indicated raised serum iron and ferritin levels, aligning with features of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Evaluation of the bone marrow displayed trilineage hematopoiesis with pronounced erythroid proliferation and increased iron accumulation, in the absence of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative pathology; cytogenetic results were normal.

Evidence of hemolysis was observed, including increased lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinemia. The direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test was positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect test yielded negative results. Nonetheless, the reduced reticulocyte count and bone marrow findings favored ineffective erythropoiesis as the primary mechanism, rather than peripheral immune-mediated destruction.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted to investigate possible gastrointestinal malabsorption; the findings were macroscopically unremarkable. Histological analysis of a duodenal biopsy identified mild chronic gastritis and a decrease in parietal cell number, a change that could result in B12 malabsorption owing to compromised intrinsic factor secretion. Nonetheless, the extent of the patient’s clinical presentation seemed excessive compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

Due to evidence of liver dysfunction, further imaging and evaluation were undertaken. Abdominal ultrasound identified both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography demonstrated grade II steatosis of the liver, reflected by a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, and cirrhosis with a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. Inflammatory marker levels were elevated. Serological testing for autoimmunity—including antinuclear antibodies and those related to autoimmune hepatitis—yielded negative results. Screening for infectious etiologies, such as viral hepatitis, HIV, cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus, did not reveal abnormalities with the exception of Epstein-Barr virus IgG, which was positive (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was carried out to investigate potential hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease. The scan revealed uneven uptake of ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose within both axial and appendicular bone marrow. Both lungs demonstrated widespread ground-glass opacity accompanied by various fibrotic alterations and subpleural atelectatic areas. Additional findings included enlargement of the liver and spleen as well as ascites. No definite signs of malignancy or granulomatous disease were observed (figures 1 and 2).

Further assessment demonstrated secondary hyperparathyroidism, disturbances in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, deficiency of vitamin D, and osteoporosis, which was verified using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. These findings provided an explanation for her repeated episodes of bone pain and multiple fractures (table 3). Electromyography combined with nerve conduction studies identified an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, with evidence of chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

In light of the array of clinical manifestations—including cytopenia, impaired liver function, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic cause was considered.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This variant affects a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to adversely impact protein function. Nevertheless, its contribution to the disease could not be determined with certainty and thus it has been classified as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenicity of this particular mutation is unclear, both PARN involvement and the patient's clinical presentation align with the telomere biology disorder (TBD) spectrum.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, early greying of hair, and fatigue in this patient prompted differential considerations including hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The significantly reduced reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, which led to prioritization of nutritional deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes in the initial diagnostic approach.

Profound vitamin B12 deficiency was identified promptly and explained several clinical manifestations, with bone marrow findings further corroborating the presence of ineffective erythropoiesis. Despite replacement of deficiencies, the patient’s persistent and disproportionate multisystem involvement raised additional concern.

A possible immune-mediated mechanism was contemplated due to a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and increased inflammatory markers. However, given the lack of hemolysis, negative autoimmune serologies, and absence of typical systemic symptoms, diagnoses such as autoimmune cytopenias or connective tissue diseases were considered unlikely.

The etiology of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was indeterminate, as investigations ruled out both autoimmune and infectious origins. The combination of signs suggesting accelerated ageing, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting the lungs and liver in a young adult led clinicians to consider an underlying genetic condition. Despite the lack of familial cases, the possibility of a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of imitating acquired diseases, exhibiting heterogeneous penetrance. While uncommon, such cases may not display the classic mucocutaneous triad of dyskeratosis congenita (DC), instead presenting more discreetly with bone marrow failure, organ fibrosis, and signs indicative of premature aging.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a pathogenic heterozygous variant in the PARN gene, thereby accounting for her array of clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg each day for 5 days, subsequently administered weekly for 4 weeks, and thereafter continued on monthly maintenance to address the marked deficiency.

Supportive management consisted of whole blood transfusions to alleviate symptomatic anaemia, oral folic acid (1 mg daily), vitamin D (60,000 IU weekly), and elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) for the treatment of osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed using standard supplementation protocols.

Physiotherapy was provided, and after the diagnosis of a TBD, genetic counselling was offered to her family.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive treatment, the patient experienced relief from symptoms. At the two-week follow-up, her hematologic values remained consistent, her fatigue subsided, and no additional clinical issues were observed.

She is still attending routine outpatient appointments to track hepatic and pulmonary function, as well as to observe for further bone marrow or systemic disease progression.

Discussion:

Telomeres, consisting of repetitive nucleotide sequences at chromosome termini, function to maintain chromosome stability by protecting against degradation. During normal cell division, telomeres undergo progressive shortening until a critical threshold is reached, inducing either cellular senescence or apoptosis. Typically, telomere length is preserved by telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC), which extends telomeres. Nonetheless, deleterious mutations in genes encoding telomerase components disrupt this maintenance mechanism, causing accelerated telomere shortening and giving rise to a spectrum of inherited disorders collectively termed telomere biology disorders (TBD).

Pathogenic variants in DKC1, inherited in an X-linked recessive manner, were the first to establish the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, positioning DC as the prototypical telomere biology disorder (TBD). The classical diagnostic criteria for DC consist of the mucocutaneous triad: oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation of the skin. Alternatively, DC may be identified by two of these features in conjunction with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical manifestations include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and an elevated risk for both haematological and solid tumors. Variants in genes associated with DC also give rise to severe childhood-onset TBD subtypes, including Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can display isolated mucocutaneous, organ-specific, or hematological symptoms and may appear in adult patients absent the characteristic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad. Often, the inherited anomaly is concealed by other diagnoses such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic failure (HF) and gastrointestinal pathologies. The absence of the classic triad combined with late onset frequently leads to incorrect diagnoses, particularly when patients are evaluated outside specialized clinics. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly found in TBDs and typically manifest as IPF or BMFS.

PARN, a member of a well-preserved group of exoribonucleases, reduces mRNA poly(A) tail length via deadenylation and, in doing so, modulates gene expression. Analysis using high-throughput sequencing of the 3′ end of TERC RNA revealed that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which direct nuclear RNAs toward degradation. This removal is crucial for proper maturation of the TERC’s 3′ end.

PARN is one of the genes least tolerant to rare, disruptive mutations, indicating its critical function and supporting haploinsufficiency in individuals carrying such variants. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN are established causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader DC/TBD spectrum. Cases of HH syndrome have been linked to biallelic PARN mutations, while monoallelic germline pathogenic variants are connected to telomere-related PFBMFT4. The connection between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was first observed when novel monoallelic variants were identified, and subsequent investigations found rare monoallelic PARN mutations in 5 of 262 patients with sporadic IPF subjected to whole-exome sequencing, highlighting a growing association that is now described in over 65 published cases.1517 Nevertheless, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations resulting from PARN mutations remains unclear, owing to the limited number of reported instances.

Importantly, the patient possessed a monoallelic missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which affects a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). Four out of five in silico missense prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, SIFT) predict a detrimental impact on function for this variant. Assessment according to ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation reveals that this variant satisfies multiple supporting criteria: PM1—the change is situated in a critical, well-characterized functional domain (R3H domain) that lacks documented benign variants; PM2—the variant is absent or extremely infrequent in population databases; PP3—various computational models predict a damaging consequence for the gene or gene product; and PP2—PARN is associated with low rates of benign missense variation, with pathogenic missense changes frequently contributing to disease in this gene. Though the clinical significance is presently unclear, its potential implication for telomere biology disorders (TBDs) is reinforced by the gene's recognized role, and similarities to other PARN missense variants—namely p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which are classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

Numerous genes, diverse modes of inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue can influence individual organ systems in telomere biology disorders (TBDs) in different ways. Moreover, environmental and lifestyle factors may alter the replicative capacity across various organs. These intricate factors may account for our patient’s presentation, considering that only a single allele PARN variant was detected.

While monoallelic mutations in PARN are more often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) than primary hematologic symptoms, the finding of cytopenia in our patient suggests the possibility of an underlying TBD that could increase the susceptibility of the bone marrow to hematopoietic stressors such as significant vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres impair the replicative potential of hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in early cellular aging and a higher likelihood of bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the cytopenia noted in our case likely resulted from severe vitamin B12 deficiency, the presence of a PARN variant emphasizes the need for continuous clinical and hematological evaluation to monitor for the development of marrow failure manifestations in this genetically vulnerable individual.

Cirrhosis has traditionally been under-recognized in TBD, though recent data indicate it may occur more frequently than previously thought. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry utilizing transient elastography reported that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic TERC or TERT mutations exhibited at least moderate HF, and 33.3% met criteria for cirrhosis. A similar case documented in the literature involved a patient presenting with pancytopenia and HF who carried monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By contrast, the patient described here possessed only a monoallelic PARN mutation and still developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN is typically linked to IPF, its downstream effects on telomerase activity may explain the HF usually attributed to TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations highlight a broadened clinical spectrum of PARN-related TBDs and emphasize the relevance of including HF in its clinical phenotype.

The definitive approach to confirming a suspected case of TBD involves genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing in addition to telomere length assessment by flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation. Management primarily focuses on supportive measures, addressing the severity of symptoms, evaluating requirements for organ transplantation, and reducing risk factors; however, for patients presenting with hematologic involvement, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a possible cure. Limited preclinical and early clinical trials have indicated that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, though the prolonged consequences of such therapy are still uncertain.

The patient exhibited symptoms characteristic of TBD, HF, and IPF. After thorough assessment ruled out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible gene, and a comprehensive review of the literature further clarified this uncommon manifestation. Supportive care was instituted, alongside genetic counseling for her relatives. She comprehended the limitations of current treatment and agreed that definitive therapies were not available. At the two-week review, her hematologic parameters were unchanged and there were no fresh indications of clinical decline.
