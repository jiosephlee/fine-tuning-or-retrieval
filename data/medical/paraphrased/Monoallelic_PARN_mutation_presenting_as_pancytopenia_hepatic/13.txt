Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year duration of worsening fatigue and diffuse weakness. Physical examination revealed findings consistent with premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory workup demonstrated pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, as well as evidence of hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the combination of cytopenia, early hair greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs prompted consideration of a genetic etiology. Clinical exome sequencing identified a heterozygous PARN missense mutation (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), which was interpreted as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and to telomere-related bone marrow failure and/or pulmonary fibrosis 4. The clinical and genetic findings in this case supported a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to suspect TBDs in younger adults presenting with otherwise unexplained involvement of multiple organ systems, even if characteristic mucocutaneous manifestations are lacking. Prompt diagnosis is important to facilitate genetic counseling, implement surveillance, and evaluate eligibility for bone marrow transplantation in patients with progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent a group of uncommon premature aging conditions resulting from inherited mutations in genes responsible for telomere maintenance, producing excessively short or defective telomeres. The primary tissues impacted are those with high cellular turnover, such as the bone marrow, epidermis, mucosal linings, hair follicles, and hepatocytes. TBDs encompass a variety of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional related syndromes are Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this category of conditions, PARN mutations present with a unique clinical profile: individuals with biallelic alterations develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single-allele mutations are more typically associated with telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We describe a woman in her late 20s with prolonged fatigue and multisystem involvement who was ultimately diagnosed with a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care facility due to a five-year history of gradually increasing generalized weakness and early onset fatigue. During this period, she experienced a noticeable reduction in her ability to carry out routine daily tasks compared to individuals of similar age. In the preceding three months, her weakness intensified significantly, resulting in difficulties with personal care and necessitating support. Additionally, she described experiencing shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest discomfort, unintended weight loss, and a diminished appetite.

On examination, she was haemodynamically stable. Notable findings included pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae.

On musculoskeletal assessment, thoracic scoliosis was observed along with atrophy of the thenar muscles on both sides. The abdominal exam identified hepatomegaly, with the liver edge palpable 5 cm beneath the right costal margin and a span of 18 cm. Pulmonary evaluation demonstrated diminished breath sounds over the infra-scapular areas bilaterally. The cardiovascular examination yielded no abnormal findings.

Investigations:

The first set of laboratory tests revealed pancytopenia alongside a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified dimorphic red blood cells, presence of basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and neutrophils with hypersegmentation, all indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Subsequent serum analyses demonstrated profound deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results showed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, supporting the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Examination of the bone marrow demonstrated active formation of all three blood cell lines, notable expansion of the erythroid lineage, and increased iron accumulation; there were no signs of malignant processes, abnormal cell development, or infiltration. Additionally, cytogenetic testing yielded normal results.

Laboratory indicators of haemolysis included raised lactate dehydrogenase levels and an increase in indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test was positive for IgG at a strength of 2+, whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nonetheless, the combination of a reduced reticulocyte count and marrow findings favored a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis over destruction of erythrocytes through immune mechanisms in the periphery.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was carried out to assess for malabsorption, revealing no notable macroscopic abnormalities. Histological examination of a duodenal biopsy identified mild chronic gastritis accompanied by a decrease in parietal cell numbers, which may have resulted in compromised intrinsic factor synthesis and subsequent vitamin B12 deficiency. Nonetheless, the extent of the patient's symptoms seemed greater than would be expected from nutritional deficiency by itself.

Due to evidence of hepatic dysfunction, additional imaging and evaluations were conducted. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis, as shown by a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m, and cirrhosis, with a liver stiffness measurement of 18.1 kPa. Inflammatory markers were elevated. Despite this, testing for autoimmune markers—including antinuclear antibodies and antibodies associated with autoimmune hepatitis—returned negative results. Screening for infectious causes such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was generally negative, apart from a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was conducted to investigate for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease. The imaging demonstrated heterogeneous ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation in both the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Both lungs exhibited diffuse areas of ground-glass opacity accompanied by multiple fibrotic changes and subpleural atelectasis. Additionally, hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were present. No clear signs of malignancy or granulomatous disorder were detected (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable observations were secondary hyperparathyroidism, abnormalities in electrolyte levels such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, deficiency of vitamin D, and osteoporosis as demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry; these findings accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography and nerve conduction assessments identified an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy along with chronic neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Given the constellation of findings—cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic aetiology was suspected.

Clinical exome analysis revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This missense change affects a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to have a deleterious effect on protein function. Nonetheless, the clinical impact of this variant on the patient’s condition could not be definitively determined, resulting in its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the precise pathogenic role of this variant is not established, PARN involvement and the observed clinical manifestations in the patient align with the typical features of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, early onset greying of the hair, and persistent fatigue prompted evaluation for possible hematologic, nutritional, or autoimmune conditions. The patient’s substantially decreased reticulocyte count suggested impaired red blood cell production, guiding the initial differential towards nutritional insufficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

A significant vitamin B12 deficiency was promptly identified, explaining multiple clinical manifestations and corroborated by bone marrow findings indicative of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, the generalized dysfunction persisted and remained out of proportion despite correction of the deficiencies.

An immune-related etiology was initially contemplated in light of the positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and raised inflammatory markers. However, the lack of hemolysis, negative results on autoimmune serologic tests, and absence of systemic manifestations did not support a diagnosis of autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease.

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) did not have an identifiable cause, as both autoimmune and infectious origins were ruled out. The combination of early-onset ageing characteristics, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrosis affecting both the lungs and liver in a young adult led clinicians to consider the possibility of a previously unrecognized genetic disorder. While the lack of affected family members seemed to counter the likelihood of an inherited syndrome, the potential for a de novo mutation could not be excluded.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) that is capable of imitating acquired disorders and displaying variable penetrance. While uncommon, such disorders may not exhibit the characteristic mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC), instead presenting more subtly with bone marrow insufficiency, organ fibrosis, and signs consistent with premature ageing.

This was substantiated by genetic analysis, which identified a pathogenic monoallelic variant in the PARN gene, providing a unifying explanation for the constellation of her clinical features.

Treatment:

The patient was treated with intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance to correct severe deficiency.

Management consisted of administering whole blood transfusions to address symptomatic anemia, in addition to providing oral folic acid at a dose of 1 mg per day. The patient received vitamin D supplementation (60,000 IU weekly) alongside elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) to treat osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with conventional supplementation protocols.

She also underwent physiotherapy, and her family received genetic counselling following diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive management, the patient experienced a reduction in symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters remained stable, her fatigue had lessened, and there were no additional clinical issues identified.

She continues to receive regular outpatient follow-up for surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function and to monitor the progression of bone marrow or systemic involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at the ends of chromosomes that function to safeguard chromosomal DNA from deterioration. During successive cycles of cell division, telomeres become progressively shorter, and once a critical threshold is surpassed, cells are directed towards senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein enzyme comprised of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—counteracts this process by extending telomeric sequences. Pathogenic mutations affecting genes that encode the telomerase complex compromise enzyme activity, leading to accelerated telomere loss and giving rise to a spectrum of inherited disorders collectively known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1 with an X-linked recessive inheritance pattern were the first to associate dyskeratosis congenita (DC) with significantly shortened telomeres, establishing DC as the prototype of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). DC is traditionally characterized by a mucocutaneous triad composed of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular hyperpigmentation of the skin, or by the presence of two of these findings in conjunction with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized clinical features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and a heightened risk of both hematological and solid tumors. More severe TBD variants with childhood onset—such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are likewise caused by mutations in genes involved in the pathogenesis of DC.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can manifest as isolated mucocutaneous, organ-specific, or hematologic abnormalities and may appear in adulthood without the classic triad of dyskeratosis congenita (DC). Frequently, the underlying inherited anomaly is obscured by diagnoses such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or heart failure (HF), as well as gastrointestinal disorders. In the absence of the typical triad and because of a delayed onset of symptoms, affected individuals are often incorrectly diagnosed, particularly outside of specialized centers. Single-allele mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most commonly found in TBDs and frequently present with clinical features resembling IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is part of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family and functions to reduce mRNA poly(A) tail length via deadenylation, playing a role in gene expression control. High-throughput sequencing of the TERC RNA 3' terminus revealed that PARN is necessary for eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation; this removal is essential for proper 3'-end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks among the 20% of genes most intolerant to rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical function and supporting the notion of haploinsufficiency in affected individuals. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN are established causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader category of DC/TBD disorders. Cases of HH syndrome have been described in patients with biallelic PARN mutations. Monoallelic pathogenic germline variants have also been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The initial association between PARN and IPF was based on the identification of novel heterozygous variants, and further studies documented monoallelic rare PARN variants in five of 262 sporadic IPF patients subjected to whole-exome sequencing. This growing connection is now reflected in over 65 published cases.1517 Nevertheless, a comprehensive characterization of the clinical manifestations linked to PARN mutations is lacking due to the relatively small number of documented instances.

Importantly, our patient carried a heterozygous missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), located at a highly conserved site within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). This alteration is considered functionally harmful according to four out of five in silico missense prediction platforms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT). According to ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation, several criteria are satisfied by this variant: PM1, due to the change occurring in a crucial and well-established functional region (the R3H domain) lacking recognized benign variants; PM2, as the variant is absent or exceptionally rare in population databases; PP3, since multiple computational predictors suggest a deleterious effect on the gene or protein product; and PP2, given that PARN is characterized by a low frequency of benign missense variants and that missense changes represent a frequent pathogenic mechanism for this gene. While the exact clinical significance of this variant remains uncertain, its potential implication in TBDs is highlighted both by PARN’s known biological functions and by similarities to other missense variants in the gene—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which are catalogued as 'likely pathogenic' in ClinVar.

A range of factors—including involvement of multiple genes, different inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue—can each influence various organ systems in TBDs. Moreover, environmental exposures and personal lifestyle can differently impact how well distinct organs maintain replicative function. With regard to our patient, these complexities could account for the clinical presentation observed, even though only a single PARN allele variant was found.

While heterozygous PARN mutations are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis rather than primary blood abnormalities, the cytopenia observed in our patient suggests the potential presence of an underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD) that may increase vulnerability of the bone marrow to haematopoietic stressors, such as pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency. Telomere attrition impairs the proliferative capacity of haematopoietic stem cells, resulting in premature cellular aging and heightened risk for bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the patient’s cytopenia was most plausibly due to severe vitamin B12 deficiency, identification of a PARN variant highlights the need for continuous clinical and haematological follow-up to monitor for development of any signs of bone marrow failure in this individual with a genetic predisposition.

Cirrhosis related to TBD has frequently gone unrecognized, though recent data indicate it may be more common than previously thought. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry employing transient elastography demonstrated that 88.8% of adult individuals with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), while 33.3% had progressed to cirrhosis. In the published literature, a similar case was reported involving a patient presenting with pancytopenia and HF, who had monoallelic mutations in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our case featured a patient carrying only a monoallelic PARN variant but who nevertheless developed hepatic as well as pulmonary fibrosis. Though monoallelic PARN mutations are most often linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their downstream effects on telomerase activity could explain the HF generally associated with TERT or TERC mutations. Collectively, these observations highlight a broader clinical range for PARN-related TBDs and emphasize the necessity of recognizing HF as a potential component of the associated phenotype.

Definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing in combination with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assays to assess telomere length. Management is primarily supportive, customized according to symptom burden, necessity for organ transplantation, and risk reduction strategies. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a cure in cases presenting with haematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical and early-phase clinical trials indicate that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere extension, their prolonged impact is yet to be determined.

The patient exhibited clinical signs consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After a thorough assessment ruled out more common etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible gene, and an in-depth review of existing literature further clarified this unusual manifestation. Supportive care was given, along with genetic counseling for her relatives. The patient comprehended the diagnosis and recognized that curative treatments were not an option. During her follow-up visit two weeks later, her hematologic parameters were unchanged, and no additional clinical decline was observed.
