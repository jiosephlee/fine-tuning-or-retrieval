Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported a 5-year progression of fatigue and widespread weakness. Physical assessment revealed indicators of accelerated ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory findings included pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were corrected, the presence of cytopenia, premature hair greying, osteoporosis, as well as fibrosis of both liver and lungs, pointed toward a potential genetic cause. Clinical exome sequencing uncovered a heterozygous missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), designated as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Germline pathogenic variants in PARN have previously been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The alignment of clinical features and genetic findings in this patient was consistent with a diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underscores the need to consider TBDs in young adults exhibiting unexplained, multisystem involvement, even when hallmark mucocutaneous symptoms are not observed. Prompt identification is essential for appropriate genetic counselling, monitoring, and evaluation for bone marrow transplantation in cases with disease progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) comprise a group of uncommon, early-onset aging syndromes that result from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere function, culminating in excessively short or malfunctioning telomeres. Tissues with high cell turnover—such as bone marrow, epidermis, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver cells—are primarily impacted. The spectrum of TBDs encompasses a range of clinical manifestations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the archetypal disorder. Additional related syndromes include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of disorders, PARN mutations are associated with a characteristic clinical presentation: individuals with mutations in both alleles typically develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with a single mutant allele are more frequently affected by telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report the case of a female patient in her late twenties who experienced persistent fatigue and involvement of multiple organ systems, and was eventually found to have a TBD diagnosis.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties arrived at our tertiary care facility, reporting a five-year progression of increasing generalized weakness and early onset of fatigue. During this time, she experienced a notable decrease in her physical abilities, limiting her performance of routine activities relative to individuals of her age group. In the past three months, her symptoms had intensified significantly, resulting in difficulty with self-care and necessitating help from others. Additionally, she described symptoms of breathlessness during exertion, occasional chest pain, unintentional weight loss, and reduced appetite.

Upon evaluation, her haemodynamic parameters were within normal limits. Key physical signs observed were pallor, icterus, premature greying of the hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, bald tongue with evidence of lingual papillae atrophy.

Assessment of the musculoskeletal system demonstrated thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of the thenar eminences bilaterally. Abdominal examination identified hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. On respiratory examination, diminished breath sounds were detected in both infra-scapular regions. The cardiovascular assessment did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory workup indicated pancytopenia accompanied by a markedly reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed a dimorphic red blood cell population, basophilic stippling, the presence of Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, all pointing toward ineffective erythropoiesis. Biochemical assessment confirmed pronounced deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Evaluation of iron metabolism demonstrated increased serum iron and ferritin levels, findings that further support the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy showed active trilineage haematopoiesis, notable erythroid hyperplasia, and increased iron accumulation, while no evidence of malignancy, dysplasia, or marrow infiltration was present; cytogenetic studies were unremarkable.

Assessment of haemolytic parameters revealed elevated levels of lactate dehydrogenase and indirect hyperbilirubinaemia. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) returned positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nevertheless, the observed reticulocytopenia and the bone marrow features predominantly indicated ineffective erythropoiesis over peripheral immune-mediated haemolysis.

To investigate potential malabsorption within the gastrointestinal tract, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was carried out, revealing no gross abnormalities. Histological examination of a duodenal biopsy identified mild chronic gastritis and a reduction in parietal cell numbers, which may be associated with compromised intrinsic factor synthesis and thus vitamin B12 malabsorption. Nonetheless, the pronounced clinical manifestations seemed excessive in relation to the degree of nutritional insufficiency detected.

Due to indications of hepatic dysfunction, additional imaging and diagnostics were undertaken. Abdominal ultrasonography identified both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography measurements demonstrated grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) as well as evidence of cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Elevated inflammatory markers were present. Conversely, tests for autoimmunity—including antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibody panels—were negative. Broad infectious screening for viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus did not reveal active infections, apart from a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG, as summarized in table 2.

In order to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory disease, positron emission tomography–CT was conducted. The scan revealed irregular ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose accumulation within both the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Additionally, diffuse ground-glass opacity, numerous fibrotic alterations, and subpleural atelectatic changes were identified throughout both lung fields. The examination also detected hepatosplenomegaly and ascites; however, there were no signs suggestive of clear malignancy or granulomatous processes (see figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results were secondary hyperparathyroidism, imbalances in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis as demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, accounting for her repeated bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography along with nerve conduction studies identified asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, with chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Given this combination of clinical features—cytopenia, liver impairment, early onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic cause was considered likely.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single-copy missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This missense alteration affects an evolutionarily conserved amino acid and is anticipated to impair protein function. Nonetheless, its contribution to the observed disease phenotype is unclear, leading to its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. Despite the indeterminate pathogenicity of this particular variant, both the association of PARN and the patient’s presentation align with the typical features observed in TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, early onset of greying hair, and persistent fatigue prompted evaluation for hematological, nutritional, and autoimmune pathologies. The discovery of a significantly diminished reticulocyte count suggested defective erythropoiesis, thereby directing initial diagnostic efforts toward identifying possible nutritional deficits or bone marrow failure syndromes.

Subsequent investigations confirmed profound vitamin B12 deficiency, which explained several clinical findings and was corroborated by marrow findings indicating ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, despite correction of these deficiencies, the degree and persistence of the patient’s multisystem dysfunction appeared excessive relative to the identified deficits.

A possible immune-mediated etiology was entertained, particularly given the positive DAT and increased inflammatory markers. Nevertheless, the diagnosis of autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease was not supported due to the absence of haemolysis, negative autoimmune serology results, and the lack of additional systemic manifestations.

The origin of hepatic fibrosis (HF) remained unidentified, as both autoimmune and infectious origins were ruled out. The combination of early-onset ageing signs, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrotic changes affecting both the lungs and liver in a young patient led clinicians to consider an underlying, previously unrecognized genetic condition. While the lack of familial cases seemed to contradict the presence of an inherited syndrome, the potential for a de novo mutation was still considered.

As a result, it was hypothesized that a TBD might resemble acquired disorders, displaying incomplete penetrance and an atypical presentation. Such cases, although uncommon, could be distinguished by the absence of the traditional mucocutaneous triad seen in DC, instead showing mild symptoms such as bone marrow insufficiency, fibrotic organ changes, and signs suggestive of premature ageing.

Genetic testing supported this hypothesis by detecting a disease-causing monoallelic mutation in the PARN gene, thereby offering a coherent account for the observed clinical findings.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg daily over a 5-day period, subsequently administered weekly for 4 weeks, and thereafter maintained on monthly injections to address the pronounced deficiency.

Supportive management encompassed whole blood transfusions to alleviate symptomatic anaemia, daily oral folic acid (1 mg), and weekly vitamin D (60 000 IU) alongside elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) for the treatment of osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed using standard supplementation protocols.

Physiotherapy was incorporated into her care regimen, and following the identification of a TBD, genetic counselling was provided to her family.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing the deficiencies and providing supportive management, the patient experienced alleviation of symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up, her hematologic parameters remained stable, her fatigue lessened, and there were no emergent clinical issues.

She remains under ongoing outpatient surveillance, with regular assessments focused on hepatic and pulmonary function, as well as monitoring for potential advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive nucleotide sequences located at chromosome termini, functioning to protect chromosomes from degradation and thus safeguarding genomic stability. During successive rounds of cell division, telomeres progressively erode, and once they reach a critically short length, they induce cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Under normal physiological circumstances, telomere maintenance is mediated by telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein enzyme composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC)—which counteracts telomere shortening by adding repeat sequences. Nevertheless, disease-causing mutations in genes encoding components of the telomerase complex compromise its activity, leading to accelerated telomere shortening and the development of a group of hereditary disorders referred to as TBD.

The initial association between DC and significantly shortened telomeres was identified through X-linked recessive pathogenic variants in DKC1, positioning DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). Traditionally, DC is characterized by the presence of the mucocutaneous triad, which includes oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular skin pigmentation, or by a combination of any two of these symptoms along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized features encompass idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), as well as an elevated susceptibility to both hematological and solid malignancies. Notably, severe, early-onset forms of TBD—such as HH syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are likewise attributable to mutations in genes associated with DC.

TBDs can appear as isolated involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or hematological abnormalities, and may emerge in adult patients who do not exhibit the traditional DC triad. The underlying genetic defect is often obscured by presentations such as aplastic anaemia, BMFS, IPF, HF, or various gastrointestinal disorders. In the absence of the classic triad and given the typically late onset of symptoms, affected individuals are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly outside of specialized medical facilities. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are frequently detected in TBD cases, with clinical presentations often resembling IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is a member of a broadly conserved exoribonuclease family and functions to modulate gene expression by catalyzing the shortening of mRNA poly(A) tails via deadenylation. High-throughput sequencing of the 3-prime terminus of TERC RNA has revealed that PARN is essential for excising post-transcriptionally appended oligo(A) tails, which would otherwise mark nuclear RNAs for degradation. This removal process is necessary for proper 3-prime-end maturation of TERC.

PARN ranks within the bottom 20% of genes in terms of tolerance to rare, deleterious variants, underscoring both its vital biological function and the likelihood of haploinsufficiency among heterozygous individuals. Germline pathogenic mutations in PARN have been identified as established causes of dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), and a range of disorders encompassed by DC/TBD. Cases of HH syndrome have been associated with biallelic mutations in PARN, while heterozygous pathogenic variants in this gene are linked to telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was first recognized through the discovery of new monoallelic variants, and later investigations found rare heterozygous PARN variants in five of 262 individuals with sporadic IPF through whole-exome sequencing, further substantiating this relationship, which is now reported in more than 65 instances according to the literature.1517 Nevertheless, comprehensive characterization of the clinical spectrum related to PARN mutations remains elusive, given the scarcity of documented cases.

Importantly, our patient was found to carry a monoallelic missense mutation in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which impacts a highly conserved amino acid located within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). According to four out of five in silico prediction algorithms (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT), this variant is likely to have a detrimental functional impact. Assessment in line with ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation reveals that this variant fulfills several criteria: PM1—this amino acid change is situated in a crucial and well-characterized functional domain (R3H domain) without documented benign variants at this position; PM2—the variant is either absent or present at extremely low frequency in large-scale population datasets; PP3—a range of computational prediction tools indicate a deleterious consequence for the gene or gene product; PP2—the PARN gene exhibits a low frequency of benign missense changes, and pathogenicity due to missense variation is recognized for this gene. Although the clinical implications of this variant have not yet been established, its possible association with TBDs is reinforced by PARN’s known biological function and by comparison to other PARN missense changes—such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—that are classified as ‘likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database.

The impact of multiple genes, varying inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue may distinctly influence different organ systems in telomere biology disorders (TBDs). Furthermore, environmental exposures and lifestyle choices can also differently affect the proliferative abilities of organ systems. In our patient’s case, these intricate factors could account for the clinical features observed, even though only a single monoallelic PARN variant was detected.

While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) rather than to primary hematological disorders, the finding of cytopenia in this patient suggests a possible underlying TBD, which could predispose the bone marrow to increased vulnerability under hematopoietic stressors, such as marked vitamin B12 deficiency. Telomere attrition impairs the replicative lifespan of hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in accelerated senescence and a heightened probability of bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the cytopenia in our patient is most plausibly a consequence of pronounced vitamin B12 deficiency, identification of the PARN variant highlights the necessity for sustained clinical and hematologic surveillance to detect any emerging signs of marrow failure in this genetically at-risk individual.

Cirrhosis associated with TBD has historically been underdiagnosed, yet recent data indicate that its incidence may be higher than once assumed. For example, a retrospective study utilizing transient elastography from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry revealed that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate HF, and cirrhosis was observed in 33.3% of these individuals. Similarly, literature reports exist of a patient presenting with both pancytopenia and HF, who possessed monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By contrast, the patient we describe carried only a monoallelic PARN mutation, yet still manifested hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although monoallelic PARN mutations are most often linked to IPF, the subsequent effects on telomerase activity might explain the development of HF typically associated with TERT or TERC alterations. Collectively, these observations broaden the recognized phenotypic spectrum of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the need to include HF as a consideration within this group of disorders.

The definitive approach to diagnosing suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing in combination with flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays for telomere length quantification. Management strategies are presently supportive and individualized according to the patient’s symptom severity, requirements for organ transplantation, and efforts to address modifiable risk factors. Haematopoietic stem cell transplantation represents a potentially curative intervention for individuals exhibiting haematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical and early-phase clinical studies have indicated that androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, the long-term outcomes of such therapies are not yet established.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. A thorough assessment ruled out more prevalent etiologies, after which whole-exome sequencing revealed the pathogenic gene involved. A comprehensive review of the literature further clarified the rarity and specifics of this clinical scenario. Management consisted of supportive care alongside the provision of genetic counselling for her relatives. The patient demonstrated an understanding of her diagnosis and acknowledged the lack of curative treatments. At her two-week review, her hematologic parameters were unchanged, and no additional clinical decline was observed.
