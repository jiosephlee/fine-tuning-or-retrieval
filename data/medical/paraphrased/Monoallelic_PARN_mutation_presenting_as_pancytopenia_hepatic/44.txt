Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties presented with a five-year course of worsening fatigue and widespread weakness. Physical examination demonstrated features consistent with premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, and evidence of hepatic as well as pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the combination of cytopenia, early-onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs suggested the possibility of an underlying genetic condition. Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense variant in PARN (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), designated as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Germline pathogenic PARN variants have previously been implicated in dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), in addition to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The alignment of clinical findings and genetic data in this patient pointed towards a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This case underscores the need to consider TBDs in younger adults presenting with unexplained, multisystem involvement, even in the absence of typical mucocutaneous manifestations. Prompt recognition is vital for informing genetic counseling, ongoing monitoring, and timely referral for bone marrow transplantation in progressive disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon premature aging syndromes stemming from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, resulting in telomeres that are unusually short or defective. The most commonly impacted tissues are those with high proliferative activity, including bone marrow, skin, mucous membranes, hair, and liver cells. TBDs encompass a variety of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the archetypal condition. Additional disorders within this group include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of conditions, PARN mutations are associated with unique clinical features: biallelic alterations are responsible for autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single allelic mutations tend to be linked with telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We report the case of a female in her late twenties presenting with persistent fatigue and evidence of involvement across multiple organ systems, who was subsequently found to have a telomere biology disorder (TBD).

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties arrived at our tertiary care facility, experiencing a 5-year progression of generalized weakness and increasing fatigue with minimal activity. This gradual deterioration has resulted in decreased functional status, limiting her capability to carry out routine tasks relative to others her age. Within the past three months, her weakness intensified enough to hinder self-care, necessitating help from others. Additionally, she described shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest pain, unintended weight loss, and reduced appetite.

During physical assessment, the patient exhibited stable haemodynamics. Significant observations were pallor, icterus, early greying of the hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, atrophic tongue with loss of lingual papillae.

On musculoskeletal evaluation, thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of both thenar muscles were observed. Abdominal assessment identified hepatomegaly, with the liver palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin and measuring 18 cm in span. The respiratory exam noted diminished breath sounds in both infra-scapular areas. The cardiovascular evaluation was within normal limits.

Investigations:

The initial laboratory assessment identified pancytopenia accompanied by a reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear revealed dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, presence of Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, all indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum assays demonstrated marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results showed increased serum iron and ferritin levels, which further supported the diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (refer to table 1).

Evaluation of the bone marrow showed active trilineage haematopoiesis, prominent erythroid hyperplasia, and excess iron stores, with no signs of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltration; cytogenetic analysis was unremarkable.

Haemolysis markers included raised lactate dehydrogenase and indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) returned positive for IgG (2+), but the indirect antiglobulin test was negative. Nevertheless, the diminished reticulocyte response and bone marrow findings suggested ineffective erythropoiesis as the primary process rather than destruction by peripheral immune mechanisms.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was carried out to investigate possible gastrointestinal malabsorption, revealing a macroscopically normal appearance. Histological analysis of the duodenal biopsy demonstrated mild chronic gastritis accompanied by a reduction in parietal cells, which could play a role in B12 malabsorption through decreased intrinsic factor secretion. Nevertheless, the extent of clinical manifestations seemed too severe to be explained solely by nutritional deficiencies.

Due to signs of liver impairment, additional imaging and diagnostics were performed. Abdominal ultrasonography demonstrated hepatosplenomegaly. Transient elastography indicated grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) as well as findings consistent with cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). Elevated inflammatory markers were observed. Autoimmune testing, including antinuclear antibodies and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies, yielded negative results. Screening for infectious etiologies such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was predominantly negative, with the exception of a positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (refer to table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was utilized to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory conditions. The imaging demonstrated uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake throughout the bone marrow of both the axial and appendicular skeleton. Additionally, widespread ground-glass opacity accompanied by numerous fibrotic and subpleural atelectatic alterations were observed in both lungs, as well as enlargement of the liver and spleen and the presence of ascites. However, no clear signs of malignancy or granulomatous disease were detected (see figures 1 and 2).

Further examination revealed secondary hyperparathyroidism, various electrolyte abnormalities including hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, as well as vitamin D deficiency. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry confirmed osteoporosis, which accounted for her repeated bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography alongside nerve conduction assessments identified an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with evidence of chronic neurogenic alterations in both proximal and distal muscles.

Taken together—cytopenia, liver dysfunction, early hair greying, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic origin was considered likely.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single-allele missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This variant causes a change at a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to adversely affect protein function. Nevertheless, its significance in relation to the disease has not been established, leading to its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenicity of this particular variant has not been determined, both PARN involvement and the patient's clinical presentation align with the typical manifestations of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, early onset greying of hair, and fatigue prompted evaluation for hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. The patient’s significantly decreased reticulocyte count suggested impaired erythropoiesis, leading clinicians to initially focus on possibilities such as nutritional deficiencies and bone marrow failure syndromes.

Severe vitamin B12 deficiency was promptly identified and explained multiple clinical manifestations, further corroborated by bone marrow findings of impaired erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, even with correction of these deficiencies, the patient's multi-system abnormalities remained excessive and sustained.

Consideration was briefly given to an immune-driven mechanism, as indicated by a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and elevated inflammatory markers. However, in light of absent evidence of hemolysis, negative results for autoimmune antibodies, and no other systemic signs, autoimmune cytopenias or connective tissue disorders were considered unlikely.

The origin of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was not identifiable, as both autoimmune and infectious etiologies had been ruled out. The unusual combination of signs including features of premature ageing, ongoing cytopenia, and fibrotic changes affecting both lung and hepatic tissues in a young adult directed attention toward a hidden genetic disorder. Despite the lack of familial occurrence, the possibility of a de novo mutation could not be dismissed.

This raised the possibility of a telomere biology disorder capable of resembling acquired diseases, with a spectrum of expression ranging from mild to severe. Although uncommon, such cases may not exhibit the typical mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita and may instead show more insidious signs such as bone marrow failure, fibrotic changes in organs, and indications of premature ageing.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by detecting a disease-causing heterozygous variant in the PARN gene, thereby offering a cohesive account for her diverse clinical manifestations.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dose of 1000 µg per day for 5 days, then once weekly for four weeks, transitioning to monthly maintenance dosing in order to address the marked deficiency.

Supportive measures consisted of whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anaemia, along with oral folic acid at 1 mg daily and vitamin D at 60 000 IU weekly accompanied by 1000 mg daily elemental calcium for the treatment of osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were managed with routine supplementation.

Additionally, physiotherapy was provided, and genetic counselling was offered to her family subsequent to the diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

The patient's symptoms improved after addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive management. At her 2-week follow-up, hematologic parameters remained steady, fatigue had lessened, and there were no additional clinical issues identified.

She remains under consistent outpatient observation to assess hepatic and pulmonary function, as well as to track potential progression involving the bone marrow or systemic manifestations.

Discussion:

Telomeres consist of repetitive DNA elements located at the termini of chromosomes and serve to maintain genomic stability by protecting chromosomes from deterioration. During successive cellular replications, telomere length progressively declines, and upon reaching a threshold, this reduction induces cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Normally, telomerase—a ribonucleoprotein consisting of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC)—preserves telomere length by facilitating their extension. Mutations in genes coding for telomerase complex constituents compromise telomerase activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and thereby giving rise to a spectrum of inherited disorders known as TBD.

DKC1 mutations with X-linked recessive inheritance were the initial evidence associating dyskeratosis congenita (DC) with significantly shortened telomeres, thereby identifying DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). Traditionally, DC is diagnosed by the presence of the mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, dystrophic nails, and reticular hyperpigmentation of the skin, or alternatively, the presence of any two of these clinical signs together with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional commonly observed features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and an elevated risk for developing both hematologic and solid tumors. Severe TBD variants emerging during childhood—including Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are likewise attributed to mutations in genes associated with DC.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can manifest as isolated involvement of mucocutaneous tissues, specific organs, or hematological abnormalities, and may occur in adults lacking the characteristic dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad. Frequently, the underlying hereditary defect is concealed by overlapping conditions such as aplastic anemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), or gastrointestinal disorders. Due to absence of the classical triad and later onset of symptoms, patients are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly outside of specialized institutions. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are often detected in TBDs, with clinical presentations usually including IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene is part of a well-preserved group of exoribonucleases that reduce the poly(A) tail length on mRNA by deadenylation, thereby controlling gene expression. Advanced sequencing of the 3-prime end of TERC RNA demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation and are necessary for proper 3-prime-end processing of TERC.

PARN ranks among the 20% of genes with the lowest tolerance for rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its critical function and the principle of haploinsufficiency in individuals who carry these variants. Pathogenic germline mutations in PARN have been recognized as causative for dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as for the broader DC/TBD phenotypic spectrum. Homozygous PARN mutations have been documented in patients with HH syndrome. Additionally, single-copy germline pathogenic PARN variants have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was initially established through the detection of new monoallelic PARN variants, with subsequent studies identifying monoallelic PARN rare variants in five out of 262 patients with sporadic IPF who underwent whole-exome sequencing. This has solidified a developing connection, which has now been reported in more than 65 cases according to the literature.1517 Nonetheless, the full extent of clinical manifestations linked to PARN mutations is not yet well-characterized, largely owing to the limited number of published case reports.

Of particular interest, our patient carried a heterozygous missense alteration in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which impacts a highly conserved amino acid located within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). Analysis with five in silico tools indicated a potentially damaging effect by four (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT). Applying the ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant assessment, several supporting criteria were met: PM1—the amino acid change occurs within a key, functionally significant domain (R3H domain), and there is no record of benign variation in this region. PM2—the variant is either absent or extremely infrequent in population databases. PP3—multiple computational predictions suggest a deleterious impact on the gene or protein function. PP2—benign missense variants are uncommon in PARN, and disease-related missense changes are frequently observed. Despite the clinical significance being unclear, its potential connection to TBDs is suggested by the established involvement of PARN and similarities with other reported missense variants in this gene, including p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which are classified as 'likely pathogenic' in ClinVar.

TBDs can exhibit diverse effects across organ systems due to multiple genes, varying inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue. Furthermore, environmental influences and lifestyle factors may alter the replicative potential in specific organs. Regarding our patient, these complexities potentially account for the phenotype observed, notwithstanding the detection of only a single-allele PARN variant.

Monoallelic mutations in PARN are typically linked with IPF rather than with primary hematological abnormalities. However, the patient’s cytopenia suggests the possibility of an underlying TBD that may increase bone marrow susceptibility to hematopoietic stressors such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres impair the replication capability of hematopoietic stem cells, resulting in accelerated cellular aging and heightened risk for BMFS. Although the cytopenia in this case was likely secondary to significant vitamin B12 deficiency, the presence of a PARN variant highlights the need for continued clinical and hematological surveillance to monitor for the potential development of marrow failure features in a genetically predisposed individual.

Cirrhosis associated with TBD has historically been overlooked, but recent data indicates its incidence may be higher than previously thought. In a retrospective review utilizing transient elastography from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, 88.8% of adult subjects carrying pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), and 33.3% demonstrated cirrhosis. Another report in the literature described an individual with pancytopenia and HF, who possessed monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Differing from this case, our patient had a single monoallelic PARN mutation but presented with both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are typically linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their influence on telomerase function may also explain the hepatic fibrosis usually associated with TERT or TERC alterations. Collectively, these observations highlight a broader range of clinical presentations in PARN-related TBDs and emphasize the need to recognize hepatic fibrosis as part of its clinical phenotype.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis via next-generation sequencing as well as flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridization assays for telomere length evaluation. Management is primarily supportive, customized according to symptom burden, necessity for organ transplantation, and efforts to reduce risk factors; however, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers a potential cure for individuals presenting with haematologic involvement. Although limited preclinical and preliminary clinical trials have reported telomere extension with androgen therapies such as danazol and nandrolone, the durability of these effects is not yet established.

The patient exhibited symptoms characteristic of TBD, HF, and IPF. After conducting a thorough assessment to rule out prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing pinpointed the responsible gene. A comprehensive review of the literature further illuminated this uncommon clinical picture. She received supportive care and her family was offered genetic counseling. The patient comprehended the specifics of her diagnosis and recognized that curative therapies were not available. At the 2-week post-discharge visit, her hematologic parameters were unchanged, and no additional evidence of clinical decline was observed.
