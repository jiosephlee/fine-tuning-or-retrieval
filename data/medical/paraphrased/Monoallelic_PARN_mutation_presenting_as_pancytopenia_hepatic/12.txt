Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her late twenties reported experiencing worsening fatigue and widespread weakness over a period of five years. On physical examination, there were indications of premature aging, anemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluation revealed pancytopenia, dimorphic anemia, marked vitamin B12 deficiency, and evidence of both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. Although nutritional deficiencies were addressed, the combination of cytopenia, early onset greying, osteoporosis, and fibrotic changes in the liver and lungs suggested the presence of an underlying genetic condition. Clinical exome sequencing detected a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), interpreted as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Pathogenic germline PARN variants have been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. In this instance, the clinical and genetic findings were consistent with a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the necessity of evaluating TBDs in younger adults presenting with unexplained disease affecting multiple organ systems, particularly when classic mucocutaneous manifestations are not observed. Prompt identification is essential for appropriate genetic counseling, ongoing surveillance, and consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in those with advancing disease.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes of accelerated aging resulting from inherited mutations in genes associated with telomere maintenance, leading to excessively short or impaired telomeres. Tissues characterized by rapid cell turnover, such as bone marrow, epidermis, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver cells, are primarily involved. TBDs encompass a wide array of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional disorders within this spectrum include Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Within this spectrum of disorders, mutations in PARN present a unique clinical picture: individuals with two pathogenic alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas those with a single mutant allele are more frequently associated with telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

We describe a woman in her late 20s with prolonged fatigue and multisystem involvement who was ultimately diagnosed with a TBD.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care facility, citing a 5-year course of gradually intensifying generalized weakness and early onset of fatigue. During this period, she noted a decline in her functional abilities, rendering her less capable of managing everyday tasks relative to individuals of similar age. In the preceding 3 months, her weakness further deteriorated, significantly limiting her ability to care for herself and resulting in dependency on others for assistance. Additional symptoms included shortness of breath on exertion, occasional episodes of chest discomfort, unintended weight loss, and loss of appetite.

On examination, she was haemodynamically stable. Notable findings included pallor, icterus, premature greying of hair, knuckle hyperpigmentation and a bald tongue with atrophy of lingual papillae.

On musculoskeletal assessment, thoracic scoliosis and atrophy of both thenar eminences were noted. On abdominal evaluation, the liver was enlarged, palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin, with a span of 18 cm. Pulmonary examination identified decreased breath sounds in the infra-scapular areas on both sides. Cardiovascular assessment did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

The first set of laboratory tests revealed pancytopenia accompanied by a decreased reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed the presence of dimorphic red blood cells, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, indicating ineffective erythropoiesis. Subsequent serum analyses demonstrated marked deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron panel results showed raised levels of serum iron and ferritin, findings that also supported a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis (table 1).

Bone marrow assessment demonstrated active production of all three hematopoietic lineages, with a predominance of erythroid precursors and increased iron deposition, but no signs suggestive of cancer, abnormal cell development, or marrow infiltration; cytogenetic studies yielded normal results.

Laboratory evidence of hemolysis consisted of raised lactate dehydrogenase and an increase in indirect bilirubin levels. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG at a strength of 2+, whereas the indirect antiglobulin assay was negative. Nonetheless, both the diminished reticulocyte count and bone marrow observations indicated that the underlying problem was more aligned with ineffective erythropoiesis than with destruction of red blood cells from immune causes.

An upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was conducted to assess for gastrointestinal malabsorption and revealed no visible abnormalities. Histological examination of a duodenal biopsy demonstrated mild chronic gastritis and a decreased number of parietal cells, findings that may impair intrinsic factor synthesis and thereby hinder B12 absorption. Nonetheless, the extent of the clinical manifestations seemed excessive relative to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

Liver dysfunction led to additional imaging and evaluation. An abdominal ultrasound demonstrated enlargement of both the liver and spleen. Transient elastography identified grade II hepatic steatosis with a controlled attenuation parameter of 295 dB/m and evidence of cirrhosis indicated by liver stiffness measuring 18.1 kPa. There was an elevation in inflammatory markers. Despite this, autoimmune serological testing, including antinuclear antibodies and antibodies associated with autoimmune hepatitis, was negative. Screening for infectious etiologies such as viral hepatitis, HIV, Cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus returned essentially normal results, with the exception of Epstein-Barr virus IgG, which was positive (table 2).

A positron emission tomography–CT scan was conducted to investigate potential hidden infiltrative or inflammatory processes. The imaging revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake within both the axial and appendicular bone marrow. Additionally, there were widespread areas of ground-glass opacity, multiple fibrotic regions, and subpleural atelectatic alterations present in both lungs. Hepatosplenomegaly and ascites were also observed, but there were no signs of clear malignancy or granulomatous disease (see figures 1 and 2).

Further assessment identified secondary hyperparathyroidism, imbalances in electrolytes (notably hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia), deficiency of vitamin D, as well as osteoporosis as demonstrated by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry; these findings accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and multiple fractures (refer to table 3). Electromyographic evaluation combined with nerve conduction studies demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy, characterized by chronic neurogenic alterations involving both proximal and distal muscle groups.

Given the constellation of findings—cytopenia, hepatic dysfunction, premature greying, osteoporosis and neuropathy—a syndromic or genetic aetiology was suspected.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, located in exon 8). This mutation affects a residue that is highly conserved and is anticipated to impair protein function. Nonetheless, a definitive association between this variant and the patient’s disease has not been established, leading to its classification as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenic nature of this particular variant is unclear, both the gene implicated and the clinical presentation of the patient align with those observed in the spectrum of TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The combination of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, early greying of hair, and persistent fatigue prompted evaluation for possible hematological, autoimmune, and nutritional etiologies. The patient’s significantly decreased reticulocyte count indicated inadequate erythropoiesis, directing the preliminary differential toward nutrient deficiency states and bone marrow failure syndromes.

A marked deficiency of vitamin B12 was promptly identified and explained multiple clinical findings, as corroborated by bone marrow findings indicating ineffective erythropoiesis. Nevertheless, despite repletion of deficiencies, the extent and persistence of systemic dysfunction exceeded expectations.

An immune-mediated mechanism was initially contemplated in light of the positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and increased inflammatory markers. However, autoimmune cytopenia or connective tissue disease became unlikely given there was no evidence of haemolysis, autoimmune serologies were negative, and no systemic manifestations were present.

Hepatic fibrosis (HF) was of undetermined origin, as autoimmune and infectious etiologies had been ruled out. The unusual combination of early-onset aging manifestations, ongoing cytopenias, and fibrotic changes affecting both the liver and lungs in a young adult prompted consideration of an obscure genetic disorder. While a negative family history initially reduced suspicion for a hereditary syndrome, the potential for a de novo mutation persisted.

This gave rise to the supposition of a telomere biology disorder that can resemble acquired diseases and display variable penetrance. While uncommon, it may not exhibit the classic mucocutaneous triad associated with dyskeratosis congenita, and instead present more discreetly with bone marrow failure, fibrosis of organs, and signs of premature ageing.

This was substantiated by genetic analysis, which identified a pathogenic monoallelic variant in the PARN gene, providing a unifying explanation for the constellation of her clinical features.

Treatment:

The patient was treated with intravenous cyanocobalamin 1000 µg daily for 5 days, then weekly for 4 weeks, followed by monthly maintenance to correct severe deficiency.

Supportive care comprised transfusions of whole blood to address symptomatic anemia, as well as oral folic acid at a dose of 1 mg per day and vitamin D at 60,000 IU weekly combined with 1,000 mg elemental calcium daily to manage osteoporosis. Electrolyte disturbances were treated using standard replacement therapy.

She also underwent physiotherapy, and her family received genetic counselling following diagnosis of a TBD.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive measures, the patient experienced relief of her symptoms. At the 2-week follow-up visit, her hematologic parameters remained steady, her fatigue had lessened, and no additional clinical issues were identified.

She continues to receive regular outpatient follow-up for surveillance of hepatic and pulmonary function and to monitor the progression of bone marrow or systemic involvement.

Discussion:

Telomeres consist of repetitive DNA elements located at the ends of chromosomes, serving to protect the genome from deterioration. Each round of cellular replication results in progressive telomere shortening until a threshold is reached, at which point cells enter senescence or undergo apoptosis. Normally, telomere maintenance is achieved by telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and telomerase RNA component (TERC), which facilitates telomere extension. Mutations in genes coding for telomerase complex components disrupt telomerase activity, causing accelerated telomere shortening and leading to a spectrum of inherited disorders referred to as telomere biology disorders (TBD).

The connection between DC and significantly shortened telomeres was first demonstrated through identification of X-linked recessive pathogenic variants in DKC1, establishing DC as the archetypal telomere biology disorder (TBD). Traditionally, DC is characterized by a mucocutaneous triad consisting of oral leukoplakia, nail dystrophy, and reticular pigmentation of the skin, or by the presence of two of these signs along with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional recognized features include idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), heart failure (HF), and elevated susceptibility to both haematological and solid organ cancers. Certain severe, early-onset TBD variants—such as Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome—are likewise caused by genetic mutations in genes associated with DC.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can exhibit isolated mucocutaneous findings, organ-specific involvement, or haematological manifestations, and may first become apparent in adulthood without the traditional dyskeratosis congenita (DC) triad. Commonly, underlying hereditary abnormalities are concealed by presentations such as aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or hepatic failure (HF) as well as gastrointestinal pathology. In the absence of the characteristic triad and because of late-onset symptoms, these cases are often erroneously diagnosed, particularly outside of specialized care settings. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are most frequently found in TBDs, commonly with clinical features of IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of a well-conserved exoribonuclease family, modulates gene expression by deadenylating mRNA, thereby reducing the length of its poly(A) tail. High-throughput sequencing of the 3' end of TERC RNA has demonstrated that PARN is essential for eliminating oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation and are necessary for the proper 3' end maturation of TERC.

PARN ranks among the top 20% of genes exhibiting the least tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, underscoring its crucial biological function and supporting the idea of haploinsufficiency in individuals with heterozygous variants. Germline pathogenic alterations in PARN are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader disease spectrum of DC/TBD. Cases of HH syndrome have been associated with individuals carrying two pathogenic PARN alleles. Additionally, heterozygous germline pathogenic variants in PARN have been associated with telomere-related PFBMFT4. The association between PARN and IPF was initially noted following the discovery of novel monoallelic mutations, and later studies have reported rare heterozygous PARN variants in five of 262 sporadic IPF patients examined by whole-exome sequencing. This growing link has now been described in more than 65 published cases.1517 Nevertheless, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations resulting from PARN mutations has yet to be delineated due to the relatively small number of documented cases.

Of particular importance, our patient possessed a heterozygous missense mutation in PARN, specifically c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), which alters a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (amino acids 178–245). This variant is assessed as likely damaging by four out of five computational prediction programs (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT). According to ACMG/AMP guidelines for variant interpretation, it satisfies several supporting criteria: PM1—this change lies within a crucial and functionally significant region (the R3H domain) that does not harbor benign variants; PM2—the variant is either absent from, or extremely uncommon in, population reference databases; PP3—several in silico models suggest a deleterious impact on gene function; and PP2—PARN displays a low incidence of benign missense changes, with missense mutations being a recognized pathogenic mechanism. Though its definitive clinical significance is still unresolved, the variant’s association with telomere biology disorders is bolstered by the recognized function of PARN and the existence of other PARN missense mutations—like p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1)—which are catalogued as ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

A range of genes, diverse inheritance patterns, incomplete penetrance, and somatic genetic rescue may each impact different organ systems in telomere biology disorders (TBDs) to varying degrees. Environmental exposures and lifestyle choices can also modulate the replicative potential of distinct organ systems. For our patient, these factors may account for the clinical presentation, even though only a single heterozygous variant in PARN was detected.

While heterozygous mutations in PARN are more frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) than to primary hematological conditions, the cytopenia detected in our patient suggests the potential for an underlying telomere biology disorder (TBD), which could increase bone marrow vulnerability to hematopoietic stressors such as profound vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres impair the replicative ability of hematopoietic stem cells, contributing to premature cellular aging and elevating the risk of bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS). Although the observed cytopenia was most plausibly due to marked vitamin B12 deficiency, the identification of a PARN mutation highlights the necessity for continued clinical and hematologic surveillance to monitor for the possible development of marrow failure features in this at-risk patient.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has frequently been overlooked, but new data indicate it may occur more commonly than once thought. In a retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry, transient elastography revealed that 88.8% of adults carrying pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), and 33.3% met criteria for cirrhosis. Another reported case involved a patient with pancytopenia and hepatic fibrosis who possessed single-copy pathogenic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). By comparison, our case involved a monoallelic PARN mutation only, with subsequent development of both liver and lung fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN variants are most frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), impairment in telomerase activity could explain the development of hepatic fibrosis more typically attributed to TERC or TERT defects. Altogether, these observations point to a broader clinical manifestation of PARN-related TBDs and highlight the necessity of recognizing hepatic fibrosis within its potential presentations.

Definitive diagnosis of suspected telomere biology disorders (TBD) relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing, along with assessment of telomere length via flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation assays. Management remains supportive, focusing on alleviating symptoms, addressing the need for organ transplantation, and minimizing risk factors. Hematopoietic stem cell transplantation may offer a curative option for individuals presenting with hematologic involvement. Although androgen therapies such as danazol and nandrolone have shown potential for telomere lengthening in preclinical and limited clinical studies, the long-term outcomes of these interventions are yet to be established.

The patient exhibited symptoms consistent with TBD, HF, and IPF. After conducting a thorough assessment to rule out more prevalent etiologies, whole-exome sequencing revealed the pathogenic gene responsible, and a comprehensive literature review enhanced insight into this uncommon clinical scenario. Supportive care was implemented, and her family received genetic counseling. She comprehended the characteristics of her disease and recognized that curative treatments were unattainable. At the follow-up appointment two weeks later, her hematologic parameters were unchanged, and there was no evidence of new clinical decline.
