Title: Monoallelic PARN mutation presenting as pancytopenia, hepatic fibrosis and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis

Jinal Soni, Karun Saathveeg Sam, Vinus Taneja, Pooja Khosla

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late twenties reported a five-year history marked by worsening fatigue and diffuse weakness. Physical examination demonstrated features of early-onset ageing, anaemia, neuropathy, and enlargement of the liver and spleen. Laboratory evaluation identified pancytopenia, dimorphic anaemia, profound vitamin B12 deficiency, hepatic fibrosis, and pulmonary fibrosis. After addressing the nutritional deficits, the presence of multiple cytopenias, premature hair greying, osteoporosis, and both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis suggested an underlying genetic condition. Clinical exome analysis detected a heterozygous PARN missense variant (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg), interpreted as a Variant of Uncertain Significance. Germline pathogenic variants in PARN have been linked to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive 6 (DKCB6), as well as telomere-related pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure 4. The observed clinicogenetic findings in this patient supported the diagnosis of a telomere biology disorder (TBD). This report underscores the need to consider TBDs in young adults presenting with unexplained, multisystem involvement—even in the absence of typical mucocutaneous findings. Timely identification is vital for appropriate genetic counselling, ongoing monitoring, and evaluation of bone marrow transplantation in cases with disease progression.

Background:

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) represent uncommon syndromes of accelerated aging resulting from inherited mutations in genes responsible for telomere function, leading to telomeres that are unusually short or impaired. Tissues with high proliferative activity—such as bone marrow, epidermis, mucous membranes, hair follicles, and liver cells—are mainly impacted. TBDs encompass a wide range of clinical presentations, with dyskeratosis congenita (DC) serving as the classic example. Additional disorders within this category are Hoyeraal-Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome.

Of the mutations detected within this spectrum of diseases, alterations in PARN are associated with specific clinical features: individuals with two mutated alleles develop autosomal recessive dyskeratosis congenita (DKCB6), whereas single allele mutations are typically linked to telomere-associated pulmonary fibrosis and/or bone marrow failure (PFBMFT4).

Here, we report a female patient in her late twenties who experienced extended fatigue and manifestations affecting multiple organ systems, eventually receiving a diagnosis of a TBD.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her late twenties was referred to our tertiary care facility due to a 5-year duration of gradually intensifying generalized weakness and early fatigue. Her level of functioning had deteriorated over time, resulting in diminished ability to carry out routine daily tasks compared to individuals her age. In the preceding three months, her symptoms had exacerbated further, significantly hindering her ability to care for herself and making her dependent on others for assistance. Additionally, she described experiencing shortness of breath on exertion, occasional chest discomfort, unintended weight loss, and a decrease in appetite.

On physical assessment, her vital signs were within normal limits. Key observations were pallor, jaundice, early onset greying of hair, hyperpigmentation over the knuckles, and a smooth, depapillated tongue. 

Musculoskeletal evaluation identified thoracic curvature of the spine and wasting of the muscles over both thenar eminences. On abdominal examination, the liver was enlarged, palpable 5 cm below the right costal margin with a span of 18 cm. Respiratory assessment showed diminished breath sounds bilaterally in the infra-scapular areas. Examination of the cardiovascular system did not reveal any abnormalities.

Investigations:

Initial laboratory findings revealed pancytopenia accompanied by a reduced reticulocyte percentage (0.39%). Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified dimorphic erythrocytes, basophilic stippling, Cabot rings, and hypersegmented neutrophils, all indicative of impaired erythropoiesis. Serum assays established profound deficiencies in both vitamin B12 and folate. Iron studies showed increased levels of serum iron and ferritin, aligning with ineffective erythropoiesis (see table 1).

Bone marrow analysis demonstrated trilineage haematopoiesis, erythroid hyperplasia, and augmented iron reserves. There was no evidence of malignancy, dysplastic changes, or infiltrative pathology, and cytogenetic testing returned normal results.

Evidence of haemolysis included elevated lactate dehydrogenase and raised indirect bilirubin. The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) was positive for IgG (2+), whereas the indirect test yielded a negative result. Nonetheless, the diminished reticulocyte count along with bone marrow observations favored a diagnosis of ineffective erythropoiesis rather than destruction attributable to peripheral immune mechanisms.

To investigate possible gastrointestinal malabsorption, an upper GI endoscopy was carried out, which showed no significant abnormalities on gross examination. Histology of the duodenal biopsy indicated mild chronic gastritis with diminished parietal cell population, suggesting a potential mechanism for B12 malabsorption due to decreased intrinsic factor secretion. Despite this, the degree of clinical manifestation seemed excessive when compared to what would be expected from nutritional deficiency alone.

The presence of hepatic dysfunction led to additional diagnostic imaging and evaluation. Abdominal ultrasonography identified both hepatomegaly and splenomegaly. Transient elastography demonstrated grade II hepatic steatosis (controlled attenuation parameter: 295 dB/m) along with evidence of cirrhosis (liver stiffness: 18.1 kPa). There was an elevation in inflammatory markers. Nevertheless, results for autoimmune markers—including antinuclear and autoimmune hepatitis antibodies—were negative. An infectious disease panel assessing for viral hepatitis, HIV, cytomegalovirus, and scrub typhus was largely negative, except for positive Epstein-Barr virus IgG (see table 2).

Positron emission tomography–CT was conducted to assess for hidden infiltrative or inflammatory conditions. The scan revealed uneven ¹⁸F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake throughout both the axial and appendicular bone marrow. There were widespread ground-glass opacities accompanied by multiple fibrotic areas and subpleural atelectasis in both lungs, in addition to hepatosplenomegaly and ascites. No definitive signs of malignancy or granulomatous disease were present (figures 1 and 2).

Other notable results were secondary hyperparathyroidism, abnormalities in electrolytes such as hypocalcaemia and hypokalaemia, vitamin D deficiency, and osteoporosis verified by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which accounted for her repeated episodes of bone pain and fractures (see table 3). Electromyography and nerve conduction examinations demonstrated an asymmetric sensorimotor axonal polyneuropathy with persistent neurogenic alterations affecting both proximal and distal muscles.

The combination of findings—including cytopenia, liver impairment, early-onset greying of hair, osteoporosis, and neuropathy—raised suspicion for an underlying syndromic or genetic cause.

Clinical exome sequencing revealed a single allele missense mutation in the PARN gene (c.613T>C; p.Cys205Arg, exon 8). This missense mutation affects a highly conserved amino acid and is predicted to impair protein functionality. Despite this, its direct contribution to the disease has not been established; thus, it is categorized as a ‘Variant of Uncertain Significance’. While the pathogenic potential of this particular variant has yet to be confirmed, the association of PARN and the patient's phenotype align with the spectrum observed in TBD.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient's symptoms of pancytopenia, unintentional weight loss, early onset greying of hair, and persistent fatigue prompted consideration of hematologic, nutritional, and autoimmune etiologies. A significantly reduced reticulocyte count suggested impaired red blood cell production, which directed the differential towards possible nutritional deficiencies and bone marrow failure disorders.

A diagnosis of profound vitamin B12 deficiency was established early and explained several clinical findings, with bone marrow studies confirming the presence of ineffective erythropoiesis. Nonetheless, even after addressing the deficiencies, the extent and persistence of systemic disturbances remained unexplained.

An immune-mediated etiology was entertained due to the detection of a positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) and elevated inflammatory markers. However, the absence of hemolysis, negative results from autoimmune serological testing, and a lack of systemic manifestations made autoimmune cytopenias and connective tissue disorders unlikely.

The cause of hepatic fibrosis (HF) was not identifiable, as both infectious and autoimmune origins had been ruled out. The combination of early-onset aging signs, chronic cytopenias, and fibrotic changes in both the pulmonary and hepatic tissues in a young patient prompted consideration of an underlying, rare genetic disorder. Despite the lack of familial history, a hereditary syndrome could not be definitively dismissed, since a de novo genetic alteration could still be responsible.

This prompted consideration of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) capable of resembling acquired diseases and exhibiting variable penetrance. While uncommon, such disorders may not present with the classic mucocutaneous triad seen in dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and instead show mild clinical signs such as bone marrow failure, fibrosis of internal organs, and indications of premature aging.

Genetic testing confirmed this suspicion by revealing a pathogenic single-allele variant in the PARN gene, thereby accounting for the range of her observed clinical findings.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous cyanocobalamin at a dosage of 1000 µg per day for five days, then once weekly over four weeks, and thereafter transitioned to monthly dosing as maintenance therapy to address the marked deficiency.

Additional supportive measures comprised whole blood transfusions to manage symptomatic anemia, daily oral folic acid (1 mg), and vitamin D (60,000 IU each week) in combination with elemental calcium (1000 mg daily) for osteoporosis management. Standard electrolyte replacement protocols were utilized to address and correct any imbalances.

Physiotherapy was implemented as part of her rehabilitation, and genetic counseling was provided to her family subsequent to the confirmation of a telomere biology disorder (TBD) diagnosis.

Outcome and follow-up:

After addressing her deficiencies and providing supportive therapy, the patient experienced a resolution of symptoms. At her two-week follow-up appointment, blood counts remained within normal limits, her fatigue had lessened, and there were no additional clinical issues noted.

She is maintained on regular outpatient monitoring to assess hepatic and pulmonary function and to observe for any advancement of bone marrow or systemic disease.

Discussion:

Telomeres are repetitive DNA sequences located at the termini of chromosomes and function to maintain genomic stability by protecting chromosomes from degradation. During normal cell proliferation, telomeres progressively shorten and, upon reaching a critical threshold, induce cellular senescence or programmed cell death. Telomerase, a ribonucleoprotein enzyme composed of telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) and the telomerase RNA component (TERC), is responsible for extending telomeres and preserving their length under normal conditions. When pathogenic mutations occur in genes coding for elements of the telomerase complex, the enzyme's activity is compromised, leading to accelerated telomere shortening and ultimately giving rise to a spectrum of inherited disorders collectively known as TBD.

Pathogenic variants in DKC1, inherited in an X-linked recessive manner, first established the association between dyskeratosis congenita (DC) and significantly shortened telomeres, positioning DC as the archetype of telomere biology disorders (TBDs). DC is typically diagnosed by the presence of the mucocutaneous triad, which includes oral leukoplakia, dystrophic nails, and reticulated skin pigmentation, or by at least two of these features in combination with bone marrow failure syndrome (BMFS). Additional well-documented complications encompass idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), hepatic fibrosis (HF), and an increased propensity for developing both hematologic and solid tumors. Severe subtypes of TBD that present in childhood, such as Hoyeraal–Hreidarsson (HH) syndrome, Revesz syndrome, and Coats plus syndrome, also arise from mutations in genes involved in DC.

Telomere biology disorders (TBDs) can appear with isolated mucocutaneous, organ-specific, or hematological symptoms and may first become apparent during adulthood without the classic triad associated with dyskeratosis congenita (DC). Often, the underlying hereditary abnormality is concealed by concurrent conditions such as aplastic anaemia, bone marrow failure syndromes (BMFS), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), or heart failure (HF) and various gastrointestinal disorders. In the absence of the traditional triad and because of delayed clinical onset, these cases are commonly misdiagnosed, particularly when not managed in specialty clinics. Monoallelic mutations in TERT, TERC, and RTEL1 are commonly found in TBDs, typically presenting as IPF or BMFS.

The PARN gene, a member of a widely conserved group of exoribonucleases, functions to reduce the length of mRNA poly(A) tails via deadenylation, playing a role in controlling gene expression. High-throughput sequencing of the TERC RNA 3' end revealed that PARN is necessary to eliminate oligo(A) tails added after transcription, which mark nuclear RNAs for degradation and are essential for proper maturation of TERC’s 3’ terminus.

PARN belongs to the group of genes with the lowest tolerance to rare, disruptive mutations, indicating its critical biological function and supporting haploinsufficiency among carriers. Pathogenic germline variants in PARN are established contributors to dyskeratosis congenita, autosomal recessive type 6 (DKCB6), as well as the broader classification of DC/TBD. Homozygous PARN mutations have been identified in individuals with HH syndrome, while heterozygous germline pathogenic changes have been associated with telomere-linked PFBMFT4. The initial association between PARN and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) was reported following detection of new monoallelic variants, and later investigations found rare heterozygous PARN variants in five of 262 sporadic IPF patients who underwent whole-exome sequencing. This supports a developing correlation now described in more than 65 published cases.1517 Despite these findings, the full spectrum of clinical manifestations attributable to PARN mutations remains unclear due to the relatively small number of cases documented to date.

Importantly, our patient carried a monoallelic missense variant in PARN, c.613T>C (p.Cys205Arg), impacting a highly conserved amino acid within the R3H domain (residues 178–245). This alteration is considered likely deleterious by four out of five in silico missense prediction tools (LRT, Mutation Assessor, MutationTaster, and SIFT). According to the ACMG/AMP variant classification standards, this variant fulfills multiple supporting criteria: PM1—the change occurs in a crucial and well-established functional region (R3H domain) with no reported benign variants; PM2—the variant is absent or extremely uncommon in population reference databases; PP3—various computational tools anticipate a detrimental effect on the gene or its product; PP2—PARN has a low frequency of benign missense changes, and missense variants frequently cause disease. While its clinical relevance is currently unclear, its possible association with TBDs is backed by the known function of the gene and similarities to other PARN missense variants, such as p.Lys59Arg (ClinVar ID: VCV000424063.2) and p.Ala383Val (ClinVar ID: VCV000180661.1), which are designated ‘likely pathogenic’ in ClinVar.

TBDs may involve numerous genes, exhibit variable patterns of inheritance, demonstrate incomplete penetrance, and include somatic genetic rescue, all of which can influence organ involvement differently. Moreover, environmental and lifestyle factors may impact the replicative ability of distinct organ systems to varying degrees. These factors, in the case of our patient, might account for the clinical presentation observed, given that only a monoallelic PARN variant was detected.

Monoallelic mutations in PARN are typically linked to IPF, rather than primary hematological presentations; however, the presence of cytopenia in this patient suggests the possibility of an underlying TBD, which may increase the bone marrow’s vulnerability to hematopoietic stress, such as severe vitamin B12 deficiency. Shortened telomeres reduce the replicative capacity of hematopoietic stem cells, causing premature cellular aging and a heightened risk for BMFS. Although the cytopenia seen in our patient was most likely the result of severe vitamin B12 deficiency, identifying a PARN variant emphasizes the necessity for continuous clinical and hematological surveillance, to monitor for the emergence of bone marrow failure symptoms in this genetically susceptible individual.

Cirrhosis in telomere biology disorders (TBD) has historically been overlooked, but new research indicates it may be more common than previously understood. A retrospective study from the Aachen Telomere Biology Disease Registry using transient elastography demonstrated that 88.8% of adults with pathogenic mutations in TERC or TERT exhibited at least moderate hepatic fibrosis (HF), with 33.3% presenting with cirrhosis. The literature includes a similar case involving a patient with pancytopenia and HF who carried monoallelic variants in both PARN (c.1613C>G) and TERT (c.2476G>A). Differently, our patient possessed only a monoallelic PARN mutation but developed both hepatic and pulmonary fibrosis. While monoallelic PARN mutations are frequently linked to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), their effects on telomerase function may explain the hepatic fibrosis often observed in TERT or TERC mutation cases. Collectively, these data highlight a broader range of clinical manifestations in PARN-related TBDs and emphasize the need to recognize hepatic fibrosis within its clinical presentation.

The definitive diagnosis of suspected TBD relies on genetic analysis using next-generation sequencing as well as flow cytometry-based fluorescence in situ hybridisation to assess telomere length. Current management focuses on supportive care, addressing symptom burden, considering organ transplantation when indicated, and reducing risk factors; haematopoietic stem cell transplantation offers potential cure for individuals presenting with haematologic complications. Although limited preclinical and clinical data indicate androgens such as danazol and nandrolone may promote telomere elongation, their sustained effects are yet to be determined.

The patient exhibited symptoms of TBD, HF, and IPF. A thorough assessment was performed to rule out more prevalent etiologies, after which whole-exome sequencing revealed the pathogenic gene. A comprehensive review of existing literature further clarified the rarity of this clinical manifestation. Supportive measures were instituted, and her relatives received genetic counseling. She demonstrated awareness regarding her diagnosis and acknowledged the absence of curative therapies. At her follow-up visit two weeks later, laboratory results showed stable blood counts, with no additional evidence of disease progression.
