Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early forties presented with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, as well as nocturnal choking events occurring 3–4 times per week. She also reported persistent occipital headaches for the past 14 years. During self-monitoring at home amid the COVID-19 pandemic, she was found to have episodes of marked oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopic and endoscopic examinations performed to evaluate her choking complaints were unremarkable. Reevaluation of a head CT performed 14 years prior for trauma revealed a crowded foramen magnum that had previously been missed. MRI of the head and cervical spine showed a Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography identified mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, consisting mainly of central events (72%). This case demonstrates that CMI may present with respiratory manifestations resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse) and underscores the importance of evaluating for structural brain lesions in patients with otherwise unexplained respiratory impairment.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils measuring at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI is present in 1% of adults, while symptomatic cases comprise around 0.1% of the overall population. The disorder displays a slight female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1 and most frequently manifests during the third decade of life.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, leading to a disproportion between the volume of neural elements and the confines of the cranial space. Nevertheless, current research questions the classic view of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) as inherently abnormal, suggesting it exists along a continuum from benign anatomical variants to overtly symptomatic malformations. Recent studies employing volumetric analysis have highlighted considerable demographic diversity in posterior fossa dimensions, frequently without a direct association to clinical presentation. As a result, this evolving understanding supports a more tailored approach to management, emphasizing individualized assessment based on symptoms to differentiate incidental anatomical variations from conditions warranting surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the symptomatic expression of CMI and is marked by a recognizable set of neurological signs. The primary symptom is episodic pain in the occipital region, usually triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, and occurs in more than 80% of patients. Adult cases generally exhibit more pronounced clinical features than pediatric cases, particularly in the presence of concomitant syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is observed in 15%–26% of individuals, most often manifesting as oropharyngeal deficits such as weakness of the palate, wasting of the tongue, or dysfunction of the cricopharyngeal muscle.

Pulmonary involvement is an increasingly acknowledged consequence of CMI. In a prospective analysis involving 90 adult patients with CMI, half exhibited sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. A central apnoea index of 5 or greater was identified in 6.7% of individuals, but purely central sleep apnoea was detected in only a single patient, possibly indicative of brainstem compression. Respiratory complications are not confined to sleep disorders; a reported case series described episodes of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia among those with CMI.

Respiratory dysfunction arises from compression of the brainstem, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) egress, and disruption of respiratory control centers, which together produce episodic respiratory symptoms. These manifestations constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—often referred to as 'Ondine’s curse'—that is separate from the congenital type related to PHOX2B gene mutations. Contemporary guidelines highlight this relationship by advocating for routine polysomnography in patients with CMI who have suspected sleep-related breathing disorders. Significantly, decompression of the posterior fossa can normalize CSF dynamics and may reverse respiratory symptoms, demonstrating the necessity of considering CMI when evaluating unexplained respiratory disturbances.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early forties came to our family medicine practice reporting a seven-year history of unpredictable choking episodes during both eating and sleeping, equally involving solids and liquids. She described these episodes as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting meals or waking her at night with forceful coughing and gasping, but never progressing to full airway blockage. She experienced nocturnal episodes three to four times weekly, sometimes twice in a single night. Multiple prior investigations, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, had been performed, with all results noted as normal. Despite extensive evaluation, her symptoms remained unchanged. The effects on her daily life were profound; she refrained from going to restaurants or social events because of concern about experiencing a choking episode in public. The ongoing fear and embarrassment caused her to become increasingly socially isolated and emotionally distressed.

A more comprehensive assessment of her clinical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, following a low-impact car accident in which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after, she began experiencing ongoing headaches located in the occipital and vertex regions. The headaches were described as continuous, felt as a pressure sensation, and frequently restricted to a small, coin-sized area at the vertex. Notably, these headaches did not worsen with coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. She received a diagnosis of nummular headache from multiple clinicians and underwent repeated local injections into the scalp, but these interventions only provided short-term improvement. The most prominent symptom was severe allodynia at the vertex, where even the weight of her own hair provoked discomfort. She eventually found that keeping her hair short lessened her usual pain from 6/10 down to 3/10, although this conflicted with cultural norms and led to significant emotional distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started using a pulse oximeter at home to check her oxygen saturation. Over a period of months, she consistently recorded episodes where her oxygen saturation fell to between 70% and 80%, despite remaining asymptomatic. She typically only became aware of these events after reviewing her readings. Repeated assessments in the emergency department attributed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she developed sudden onset of dizziness and nausea. Medical staff present at the scene measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from boarding the aircraft. Her symptoms promptly resolved within minutes after receiving supplemental oxygen. Later, pulmonary assessment, which encompassed chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal results.

Considering her persistent occipital and vertex headaches, increasing episodes of choking, and unexplained episodes of hypoxia, efforts were made to identify a single neurological etiology. Upon examination of her past medical history, it was noted that a non-contrast cranial CT had been performed 14 years prior, after her accident, with an initial interpretation of normal results. A thorough reassessment of that imaging, however, revealed evidence of a crowded foramen magnum and diminished cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction, findings indicative of CMI that had been overlooked at first. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was suspected at first due to the presence of choking episodes. However, normal findings on pH monitoring and endoscopy, as well as no improvement despite maximal acid suppression therapy, ruled out this condition.

Myasthenia gravis was considered as a potential diagnosis in light of bulbar symptoms. However, negative results on antibody assays, together with no evidence of fatigability or diurnal symptom fluctuation, argued against this condition.

An in-depth assessment for primary pulmonary disorders was undertaken in response to episodes of hypoxia. Intrinsic lung disease was ruled out based on unremarkable chest imaging and normal pulmonary function test findings.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly indicated by the presence of intact voluntary respiration while automatic respiratory drive was compromised during periods of distraction or sleep. Its development after head injury differentiated it from the congenital variants.

All symptoms were ultimately attributed to brainstem involvement resulting from CMI, which compressed the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei, offering a single comprehensive diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of our patient’s symptom development following a motor vehicle accident requires thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is recognized in the literature as a congenital disorder, but it can also arise following head trauma or other neurological insults. Modern etiological frameworks2022 differentiate primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that are linked to traumatic injury or elevations in intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan taken right after the injury showed a congested foramen magnum. Since no imaging was available prior to the trauma, it is unclear if the trauma was responsible for this finding or simply uncovered it. The sudden occurrence of occipital headache immediately after the accident, with subsequent gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory symptoms spanning 14 years, may indicate either: (1) trauma-triggered tonsillar herniation, or (2) an injury that intensified or disclosed an asymptomatic congenital abnormality.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory features associated with CMI, contributing to our knowledge of acquired central hypoventilation patterns arising from structural lesions in the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is fundamental in unraveling the respiratory pathophysiology of CMI. While voluntary breathing—which depends on cortical input—is generally maintained in individuals with CMI, automatic breathing, governed by brainstem centers and critical during sleep, can be impaired as a result of compression of medullary regions by the cerebellar tonsils. In this patient, voluntary ventilatory effort was retained, whereas the capacity for automatic respiratory drive was lost, closely resembling the clinical picture seen in congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, but with a distinct underlying mechanism. The airport incident, in which a brief lapse of attention led to marked hypoxemia without any sensation of breathlessness, starkly demonstrates this selective impairment of automatic respiratory control: the patient, in essence, ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea during marked episodes of oxygen desaturation results from an underlying dysfunction in the brainstem. This suspected brainstem abnormality likely interferes with essential ascending afferent pathways that must reach the cerebral cortex to elicit conscious perception. The sense of breathlessness represents a type of interoceptive awareness, relying on the transmission of afferent input to central regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If these signals are disrupted at the level of the brainstem, the cortex would be deprived of the information required to perceive air hunger, which could account for the patient’s perilous unawareness of severe hypoxic states.

The pronounced mismatch between the intensity of this patient’s clinical symptoms and the modest polysomnographic findings (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. For logistical reasons, we employed type III polysomnography, as opposed to type I, which does not include EEG monitoring. As a result, the assessment of total sleep time was imprecise, probably causing a considerable underestimation of respiratory events. With EEG data absent, AHI is determined based on total recording duration, encompassing wakeful periods as well as sleep, rather than actual sleep time. Therefore, this technical constraint likely results in an AHI of 6.0 that does not reflect the full extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Although this limitation exists, there are multiple factors that indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central events constitute the majority (72%) even though the total AHI is low; (2) recorded episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% in the absence of both dyspnea and patient awareness, observed both at the airport and in routine clinical settings; and (3) anatomical findings of notable brainstem compression, with tonsillar herniation measuring 11 mm.

A Type I polysomnography, including comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation, has been scheduled at an outside sleep center. The study has not yet been performed due to the need for synchronization between patient availability, the patient’s consent to undergo the procedure, and the scheduling constraints at the offsite location. Following completion, the results are expected to yield a more precise assessment of sleep architecture, patterns of respiration, and clarify both the presence and extent of central hypoventilation.

It should be noted that, based on current findings, central hypoventilation remains a strongly supported clinical consideration but has not yet been conclusively diagnosed.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, presenting as equal difficulty with both solid and liquid foods and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, represented the primary source of discomfort for our patient. Prospective assessment using standardized swallowing tests demonstrated dysfunction in 36% of cases, with neurogenic etiologies being the most common. The patient’s comparable challenge with all food textures serves to distinguish neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical types. Despite thorough initial and repeated investigations by both gastroenterology and otolaryngology, which yielded unremarkable results, the patient’s diagnosis was postponed for years. This prolonged interval before diagnosis underscores a crucial clinical point: in instances where dysphagia or choking occurs absent any identifiable structural abnormality on endoscopic evaluation, clinicians should maintain suspicion for neurologic causes. Retrospective analysis revealed that the rate of CMI among children with persistent dysphagia was 3.3%, considerably exceeding the prevalence reported in the general pediatric population (0.1%–0.77%). These findings imply that ongoing dysphagia may serve as a significant marker for underlying CMI.

The patient's description of discomfort localized to the vertex, alleviated by hair trimming, illustrates a notable yet insufficiently investigated clinical occurrence. Occipital neuralgia may be observed in conjunction with CMI and often leads to increased sensitivity of the scalp; diagnostic guidelines stipulate that 'dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair' must be present. Nevertheless, reports documenting the relationship between occipital neuralgia and CMI are infrequent, and definitive evidence connecting the two has not been established.

Another possible explanation is the newly identified 'burning vertex syndrome', which is characterized by scalp pain, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align precisely with recognized primary headache disorders like migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The proposed underlying mechanism may involve small nerve fibers in the scalp, and patient responses to greater occipital nerve blockade are inconsistent. For our patient, the symptoms at the vertex might signify: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) an incidental co-occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a completely distinct primary headache disorder. The literature lacks comprehensive records of cutaneous allodynia presentation in CMI populations, as shown by the absence of validated tools specifically designed to evaluate these symptoms before the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Due to this limited data, we offer this finding as a noteworthy clinical observation rather than an established diagnostic criterion.

This case illustrates the considerable difficulty in diagnosing Chiari malformation type I (CMI) when its presentation includes non-classic manifestations like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, which resulted in a delayed diagnosis spanning 14 years. The diagnostic process was marked by initial misinterpretation and an eventual progressive realization of the correct neurological cause, a pattern commonly noted in published reports.
