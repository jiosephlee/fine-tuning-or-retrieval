Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early 40s reported a 7-year duration of recurrent choking events affecting both solids and liquids, nocturnal choking episodes occurring three to four times per week, and longstanding occipital headaches for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home monitoring unexpectedly showed episodes of significant oxygen desaturation (ranging from 70% to 80%) despite the absence of respiratory discomfort. Multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies conducted to evaluate the choking complaints did not reveal any abnormalities. A retrospective review of a head CT scan performed 14 years earlier following unrelated trauma identified an overlooked crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck confirmed Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with cerebellar tonsils herniated 11 mm below the foramen magnum. Polysomnography indicated mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0), characterized by predominantly central events (72%). This case demonstrates that CMI can manifest as respiratory symptoms that closely resemble acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to consider structural abnormalities of the brain in patients who present with unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils measuring 5 mm or more below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate CMI affects 1% of adults, but only around 0.1% of individuals experience symptoms. There is a slight predominance among females, with a ratio of 1.3:1, and it most often manifests in the third decade of life.

The underlying mechanism involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, leading to a disproportion between the size of neural tissue and the space it occupies. Nevertheless, recent findings question the longstanding belief that CMI always represents a pathological condition, suggesting it encompasses a range from benign anatomical differences to symptomatic malformations. Contemporary volumetric analyses reveal marked variability in posterior fossa dimensions across demographic groups, with symptoms not uniformly linked to these differences. This evolving perspective underscores the importance of tailored clinical approaches, focusing on patient symptoms to differentiate incidental anatomical features from those that warrant surgical management.

Chiari syndrome refers to the symptomatic presentation associated with CMI, which is marked by a specific set of neurological signs and symptoms. The most prominent feature is episodic occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, observed in more than 80% of affected individuals. Symptom severity tends to be greater in adults than in pediatric patients, particularly in cases complicated by syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in 15%–26% of cases, commonly manifesting as oropharyngeal abnormalities such as palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary complications are now acknowledged as a growing concern in individuals with CMI. Among 90 adult patients enrolled in a prospective investigation, half exhibited sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. An elevated central apnoea index (≥5) was identified in 6.7% of these subjects, while isolated central sleep apnoea was detected in only one participant, possibly indicating compression at the brainstem. Such respiratory issues are not limited to disturbances during sleep; a series of cases has described episodes of acute respiratory insufficiency and severe hypoxaemia in persons with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in this context arises due to compression of the brainstem, obstruction to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) egress, and disturbance of centers governing respiratory regulation, resulting in episodic respiratory manifestations. These manifestations constitute a secondary central hypoventilation syndrome—sometimes referred to as 'Ondine’s curse'—which differs from the congenital form related to PHOX2B gene mutations. Current recommendations highlight the significance of this relationship by advising routine polysomnographic evaluation for individuals with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who exhibit signs suggestive of sleep-related breathing disorders. Importantly, surgical decompression of the posterior fossa can normalize CSF flow and potentially alleviate respiratory symptoms, emphasizing the critical need to consider CMI in patients presenting with unexplained respiratory disturbances.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s attended our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year history of recurrent episodes of choking that occurred unpredictably both during meals and while sleeping, with equal involvement of solids and liquids. She described these events as abrupt and disturbing, often disrupting her meals or awakening her from sleep with intense coughing and gasping. Despite their severity, the episodes had never caused total airway obstruction. The nocturnal events happened three to four times per week, sometimes occurring twice in a single night. Throughout these years, she had been evaluated extensively, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Although no abnormalities were found, her symptoms remained. The consequences for her social life were substantial; she avoided eating out and attending social gatherings due to anxiety about experiencing a choking episode in public. This persistent fear and embarrassment contributed to her growing social isolation and emotional suffering.

A thorough review of her medical history indicated that her symptoms initially appeared 14 years prior, following a minor car accident in which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after the incident, she began experiencing ongoing headaches located at the occipital region and the vertex. She characterized these headaches as constant and pressure-like, frequently confined to a small, coin-shaped spot at the vertex. Notably, the headaches were unaffected by coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple clinicians diagnosed her with nummular headache, leading to repeated scalp injections at the affected site, which provided only temporary relief. The most prominent symptom was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, where even the sensation of her hair caused discomfort. Ultimately, she found that keeping her hair short lessened her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10; however, this strategy conflicted with cultural norms and led to significant psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started tracking her oxygen levels at home with a personal pulse oximeter. For several months, she frequently recorded asymptomatic decreases in oxygen saturation falling within the 70%–80% range. She was typically unaware of these occurrences until measuring with her device. Numerous visits to the emergency department resulted in her symptoms being explained as anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her departure, she developed sudden onset of dizziness and nausea. Medical staff present noted her oxygen saturation was 78%, resulting in her being prevented from boarding the flight. Symptoms abated entirely within several minutes after receiving supplemental oxygen. Later assessments of her pulmonary status, consisting of chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed no abnormalities.

Considering the constellation of persistent headaches in the occipital and vertex regions, recurrent episodes of choking, and unexplained low oxygen levels, efforts were made to identify a singular neurological cause. Review of her prior medical documentation indicated that a non-contrast head CT was obtained 14 years ago after her accident, with an initial interpretation describing no abnormalities. Nevertheless, upon careful reassessment, the scan demonstrated foramen magnum crowding and diminished cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastroesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected due to the presence of choking episodes. However, this was ruled out based on normal findings from pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluation, as well as the absence of improvement despite maximum acid suppression therapy.

Assessment for myasthenia gravis was prompted by involvement of bulbar musculature. However, the diagnosis was considered improbable given negative serological markers, no evidence of muscle fatigability, and stability of symptoms throughout the day.

An in-depth workup for primary pulmonary disease was conducted following episodes of hypoxemia. Intrinsic pulmonary disorders were ruled out as both chest imaging and pulmonary function assessments yielded normal results.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine's curse) was indicated by the patient's maintained voluntary respiration, while automatic breathing was compromised during periods of distraction or sleep. The onset after head trauma differentiated this condition from its congenital variants.

The symptoms were ultimately accounted for by brainstem involvement resulting from CMI, as this led to compression of the respiratory centers and the nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thus serving as the unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of our patient's symptoms relative to the motor vehicle collision merits close examination. Chiari malformation type I is known to occur congenitally, but has also been reported following head injuries and other neurological traumas. Recent classifications in the literature2022 reflect this variety, differentiating congenital (primary) Chiari malformations from those that develop secondary to trauma or heightened intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan conducted directly after the incident showed a narrow foramen magnum. Since prior imaging was unavailable, it is unclear if this feature resulted from the injury or if it simply became apparent following trauma. The sudden appearance of occipital headaches after the event, with gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory manifestations across 14 years, might indicate either (1) tonsillar herniation resulting from the injury or (2) the trauma aggravating or exposing a previously silent congenital anomaly.

This report illustrates the intricate respiratory involvement seen in CMI, contributing to a better comprehension of the acquired central hypoventilation patterns observed in structural abnormalities of the brainstem. Differentiating voluntary from automatic ventilation is fundamental to deciphering respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. While voluntary respiration, directed by cortical circuits, is usually maintained in individuals with CMI, automatic respiration—governed by brainstem centers and necessary for breathing during sleep—is impaired due to compression of medullary regions by the tonsils. In the presented case, voluntary breathing was retained; however, there was a loss of the automatic respiratory drive, mirroring the clinical profile of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, but originating from a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, where a brief lapse in attention triggered severe oxygen desaturation in the absence of dyspnoea, offers a striking example of this selective disruption in automatic respiratory regulation, in which the patient quite literally “forgets to breathe.”

We propose that the underlying cause of the patient’s remarkable lack of dyspnoea despite considerable oxygen desaturation is a primary dysfunction within the brainstem. This suspected brainstem pathology is believed to impede the essential afferent neural pathways responsible for transmitting signals to the cortex and producing conscious sensation. The perception of breathlessness represents an aspect of interoception, relying on the integration of these afferent inputs by specific brain regions such as the anterior insular cortex. When these signals are disrupted at the brainstem level, the cortex fails to receive the requisite information to elicit the sensation of air hunger, accounting for the patient’s perilous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The pronounced difference between the patient’s intense symptoms and the modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was utilized instead of type I, resulting in no EEG monitoring. The lack of EEG compromised accurate assessment of actual sleep duration, probably causing notable underreporting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG, AHI is determined using total recording time, which includes intervals of wakefulness as well as sleep. As a consequence, this technical limitation suggests that the observed AHI of 6.0 does not accurately reflect the actual severity of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Although this limitation exists, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central events comprised the majority (72%) of apneas, even though the total AHI was low; (2) observed drops in oxygen saturation to 70%–80% occurred without accompanying dyspnoea or the patient's awareness (noted at the airport and during standard evaluations); and (3) imaging revealed notable brainstem compression, demonstrated by 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils.

A Type I polysomnography, including comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal CO2 measurement, and video documentation, has been scheduled at an offsite sleep center. The study has not yet been conducted due to ongoing arrangements involving patient scheduling, the patient's consent to proceed, and securing an available appointment at the external location. Upon completion, the study is expected to yield detailed information on sleep structure, breathing patterns, and will help to assess and quantify the extent of central hypoventilation.

It is recognized that, based on the current body of evidence, central hypoventilation continues to be a strongly considered clinical suspicion, though it cannot yet be conclusively established as a diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, presenting with comparable impairment in swallowing solids and liquids and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, was the primary concern for our patient. A prospective study utilizing validated tools for swallowing assessment revealed that 36% of patients exhibited swallowing dysfunction, most often manifesting as neurogenic in origin. Difficulty with all food textures differentiates neurogenic dysphagia from the mechanical type. Despite the prominence of this symptom, initial and repeated evaluations from both gastroenterology and otolaryngology yielded unremarkable results, resulting in a delayed diagnosis that persisted over several years. This case underscores the clinical importance of considering neurological causes when dysphagia or choking occurs without evidence of structural abnormalities on endoscopic examination. In a retrospective review, the rate of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) among children with chronic dysphagia was 3.3%, notably greater than the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence seen in the broader paediatric population. These findings indicate that persistent dysphagia may serve as an important clue to underlying CMI.

The patient described experiencing pain at the vertex of the scalp, which was alleviated following haircuts—a noteworthy occurrence that has not been extensively investigated. In cases of Chiari malformation type I (CMI), occipital neuralgia may develop and lead to heightened sensitivity of the scalp. The diagnostic guidelines stipulate that dysesthesia or allodynia must be evident when the scalp or hair is subject to otherwise non-painful stimulation. Despite this, there are only infrequent reports documenting the connection between occipital neuralgia and CMI, and current evidence does not definitively support a causal relationship.

Another possible explanation is the recently reported ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp pain, cutaneous allodynia, and symptoms that do not align with the established primary headache types, including migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology may involve small fiber nerve pathways within the scalp, and patient responses to greater occipital nerve blockade have been variable. In this particular case, the symptoms affecting the vertex could signify: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) a coincident occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache disorder. There is a notable deficiency in systematic reporting of cutaneous allodynia patterns among individuals with CMI, highlighted by the absence of validated assessment tools for specific CMI-related symptoms prior to the creation of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In view of the limited supporting evidence, we share this finding as an intriguing clinical observation rather than a conclusive diagnostic characteristic.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when the condition manifests through unusual or ambiguous clinical features, such as central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a prolonged delay of 14 years before a definitive diagnosis was established. The process, which began with uncertainty and slowly culminated in an accurate neurological diagnosis, is repeatedly documented in the literature.
