Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, nocturnal choking events occurring 3–4 times per week, and persistent occipital headaches lasting 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home monitoring unexpectedly identified severe oxygen desaturation episodes (ranging from 70% to 80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies conducted to evaluate her choking did not reveal any abnormalities. On review, a CT scan of the head performed 14 years prior for unrelated trauma showed a crowded foramen magnum that had previously gone unrecognized. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck established a diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography detected mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, consisting primarily of central events (72%). This report demonstrates that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms mimicking acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the need to consider underlying structural brain lesions in patients with unexplained respiratory symptoms.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils by at least 5 mm beyond the foramen magnum, as visualized on sagittal MRI scans. Recent imaging data show a prevalence of CMI in about 1% of adults, while symptomatic cases represent roughly 0.1% of the population. There is a slight female predominance with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases are diagnosed in individuals in their third decade of life.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between the neural contents and the bony compartment. Nonetheless, recent research questions the classic view that Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is inherently abnormal in all cases, suggesting it exists along a continuum from benign anatomical differences to malformations that cause clinical symptoms. New volumetric analyses reveal marked demographic differences in posterior fossa size, often without a clear association with symptoms. This evolving perspective calls for personalized, symptom-focused management that differentiates incidental, asymptomatic structural findings from those cases that constitute pathological conditions warranting surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the symptomatic presentation of Chiari malformation type I (CMI), marked by a characteristic set of neurological signs. The primary clinical feature is an episodic occipital headache that is triggered by activities such as performing a Valsalva maneuver, observed in more than 80% of individuals. In general, adults demonstrate more pronounced symptoms than pediatric patients, particularly in the context of associated syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in approximately 15%–26% of cases and usually manifests as oropharyngeal impairments, which may include weakness of the palate, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now being acknowledged more frequently as a complication associated with CMI. In a prospective cohort of 90 adult patients with CMI, half demonstrated sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. An elevated central apnoea index (≥5) was detected in 6.7% of subjects, though only a single individual exhibited isolated central sleep apnoea, which may be attributable to compression of the brainstem. These respiratory issues are not limited to sleep dysfunction; one case series reported occurrences of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia in individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment arises due to factors such as brainstem compression, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, and interference with neural respiratory regulatory centers, which result in episodic breathing disturbances. These respiratory manifestations constitute an acquired variety of central hypoventilation syndrome—often referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which differs from the hereditary version linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Contemporary recommendations highlight the relevance of this association, advising regular polysomnographic evaluation in patients with CMI who are suspected of having sleep-related breathing disorders. Importantly, surgical decompression of the posterior fossa can reestablish normal CSF dynamics and may alleviate respiratory symptoms, highlighting the necessity of considering CMI in the differential for unexplained respiratory problems.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early 40s attended our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year course of repeated choking incidents that occurred unpredictably with both solids and liquids, during meals as well as while sleeping. She characterized these episodes as abrupt and distressing, frequently interrupting her eating or waking her from sleep with intense bouts of coughing and gasping, though she never experienced total airway blockage. Nighttime incidents happened three to four times weekly, and sometimes twice within a single night. Throughout these years, she had been extensively assessed—including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies—all of which yielded normal results. Nonetheless, her symptoms persisted. The impact on her social life was considerable; she deliberately avoided dining out or attending social events due to apprehension about choking in public. The enduring fear and embarrassment resulted in progressive social isolation and increased emotional strain.

A more comprehensive review of her medical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, after she was involved in a minor car accident that resulted in a brief loss of consciousness. Not long afterwards, she began to experience ongoing headaches in the occipital and vertex regions. She characterized these as continuous, with a pressure-like quality, often concentrated within a coin-sized area at the vertex. The headaches did not worsen with coughing, straining, or Valsalva maneuvers. Several healthcare providers diagnosed her with nummular headache, leading to multiple local scalp injection treatments; these, however, provided only short-lived relief. The most remarkable aspect was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, such that even the presence of her own hair provoked discomfort. She eventually found that wearing her hair short lessened her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, but this choice conflicted with her cultural norms and led to additional emotional strain.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started using a home pulse oximeter to monitor her own oxygen saturation levels. Across several months, she consistently recorded episodes where her oxygen saturation decreased to the 70%–80% range, despite remaining asymptomatic. These episodes were typically unnoticed by her until she reviewed her readings. On several occasions, emergency assessments concluded that her symptoms were due to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she developed sudden onset of dizziness accompanied by nausea. Medical staff at the scene recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prevented from boarding the plane. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms fully resolved within a few minutes. Further pulmonary assessment—including chest radiography and pulmonary function tests—revealed no abnormalities.

In light of her persistent headaches involving the occipital and vertex regions, ongoing episodes of choking, and unexplained episodes of hypoxia, clinicians sought a single neurological explanation for her symptoms. Examination of her prior medical documentation indicated that a non-contrast CT head scan had been obtained 14 years prior, post-accident, and was originally interpreted as unremarkable. Nonetheless, a thorough retrospective review of that imaging demonstrated evidence of crowding at the foramen magnum and diminished cerebrospinal fluid around the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Due to the presence of choking episodes, gastroesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was suspected at first. However, the diagnosis was ruled out after pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations returned normal results and the patient failed to improve with maximum acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was evaluated due to symptoms affecting the bulbar region. Negative antibody testing, lack of fatigability and absence of diurnal variation made this unlikely.

Primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated following hypoxic episodes. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests excluded intrinsic lung disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly suspected due to intact voluntary respiration contrasted with deficient automatic respiratory drive observed during sleep or periods of distraction. The post-traumatic onset differentiated this presentation from congenital variants.

Brainstem pathology secondary to CMI ultimately explained all symptoms through compression of the respiratory centres and lower cranial nerve nuclei, providing the unifying diagnosis.

In light of the patient’s choice to delay surgical intervention, a systematic follow-up protocol was established. Discussion:

The timing of our patient's symptom development following the motor vehicle accident necessitates thoughtful evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is recognized in the literature as a condition that may be present from birth or can arise secondary to cranial trauma and other neurological insults. Current etiological frameworks (2022) differentiate between primary (congenital) Chiari malformations and those considered secondary or acquired, which develop after trauma or episodes of raised intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained directly following the injury revealed a crowded foramen magnum. Since imaging from before the trauma is unavailable, it is unclear whether this anatomical finding resulted from the trauma itself or if it was pre-existing and simply became apparent after the injury. The patient experienced occipital headaches immediately after the accident and subsequently developed bulbar and respiratory symptoms over a span of 14 years. This clinical course could suggest either (1) the occurrence of trauma-induced tonsillar herniation or (2) trauma acting as a trigger to worsen or disclose a previously silent congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory presentations associated with CMI and contributes to our knowledge of how structural lesions in the brainstem can lead to acquired central hypoventilation. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is key to grasping the underlying respiratory disturbances in CMI. While voluntary respiration, reliant on cortical input, is generally preserved in individuals with CMI, automatic breathing—which depends on brainstem mechanisms and is critical during sleep—may be disrupted owing to compression of the medullary region by herniated tonsils. In this case, the patient retained the ability to breathe voluntarily but exhibited a loss of involuntary respiratory drive, a clinical picture similar to that seen in congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit with a distinct underlying mechanism. The episode at the airport, where transient inattention led to marked oxygen desaturation in the absence of dyspnoea, clearly demonstrates the isolated impairment of automatic respiratory control: the patient, quite literally, 'forgets to breathe'.

We propose that a fundamental dysfunction within the brainstem underlies the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea despite marked episodes of oxygen desaturation. This suggested abnormality in the brainstem likely impairs essential afferent pathways responsible for conveying critical sensory information to the cerebral cortex, which is required for conscious perception. Breathlessness is understood as an interoceptive phenomenon that depends on the integration of these ascending neural signals by principal regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If these pathways are disrupted while traversing the brainstem, the cortex fails to receive adequate input to create the subjective sensation of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s perilous unawareness of their compromised physiological state.

The pronounced mismatch between the patient's pronounced clinical symptoms and the relatively low AHI (6.0) identified on polysomnography calls for clarification. For practical reasons, type III polysomnography was performed instead of type I, resulting in the omission of EEG monitoring. The lack of EEG data made it impossible to determine actual total sleep time, which probably caused a notable undercount of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG, AHI is computed based on the entire recording period—including times when the patient may have been awake—rather than using only sleep epochs. As a result, this methodological constraint suggests that the AHI of 6.0 likely understates the real extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Despite this constraint, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) a majority of events were central in nature (72%), even though the total AHI was low; (2) recorded oxygen saturations dropped to 70%–80% without the patient experiencing dyspnoea or being cognizant of the events (observed both at the airport and during standard assessments); and (3) imaging revealed marked brainstem compression, evidenced by an 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A Type I polysomnographic evaluation, including comprehensive EEG tracking, end-tidal CO2 monitoring, and continuous video surveillance, has been scheduled at an offsite sleep facility. The test has not yet taken place due to the need to align the patient’s schedule and willingness to undergo the procedure with the availability of the facility. When finalized, this assessment is expected to yield conclusive information regarding sleep structure, respiratory dynamics, and the existence and extent of central hypoventilation.

We acknowledge that, given the current evidence, central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis, rather than a definitively confirmed diagnosis.

Our patient’s most troublesome symptom was oropharyngeal dysphagia, impacting both solids and liquids to a similar extent and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. In a prospective study using standardized swallowing evaluations, dysfunction was identified in 36% of cases, with neurogenic patterns being the most common. The uniform difficulty across all food consistencies helps differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical forms. Despite numerous investigations from both gastroenterological and otolaryngological approaches—each yielding normal results—the underlying cause of the patient’s dysphagia was not diagnosed for several years. This prolonged period without diagnosis underscores an important clinical teaching point: if a patient presents with dysphagia or choking and no obvious anatomical abnormality is detected on endoscopy, a neurological etiology should be considered. Notably, a retrospective review found that 3.3% of children with ongoing dysphagia were diagnosed with CMI, compared to a prevalence of 0.1%–0.77% in the broader pediatric population. This suggests that enduring dysphagia may serve as an important clinical clue to possible underlying CMI.

The patient described vertex pain that was alleviated by cutting hair, a noteworthy yet insufficiently investigated occurrence. Occipital neuralgia, which has been identified in connection with CMI, may produce hypersensitivity of the scalp; its diagnostic criteria include ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Nevertheless, reports documenting occipital neuralgia alongside CMI are infrequent, and current evidence does not conclusively demonstrate a causal relationship.

Another possible explanation is the newly identified ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp pain, cutaneous allodynia, and symptoms that do not align clearly with existing primary headache categories such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. It is postulated that this condition may involve small fiber pathways originating in the scalp, and reported patient responses to greater occipital nerve blocks have been inconsistent. For our patient, symptoms located at the vertex could be indicative of: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) a coincident case of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a separate primary headache disorder entirely. Comprehensive documentation regarding patterns of cutaneous allodynia in populations with CMI remains insufficient, highlighted by the previous lack of published tools for assessing these specific symptoms in CMI prior to the creation of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Due to this significant lack of evidence, we present this as a noteworthy clinical observation rather than establishing it as a definitive diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI when it manifests with unusual symptoms like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, resulting in a 14-year diagnostic delay. The progression from initial misinterpretation to eventual identification of the underlying neurological condition reflects a pattern frequently noted in existing reports.
