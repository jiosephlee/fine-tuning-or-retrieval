Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early forties reported a 7-year pattern of recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, as well as nocturnal choking events that occurred 3–4 times per week, alongside chronic occipital headaches persisting for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home monitoring incidentally detected severe hypoxemia (oxygen saturation between 70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopic evaluations aimed at identifying a cause for the choking episodes yielded normal findings. Retrospective analysis of a cranial CT scan obtained 14 years prior due to unrelated trauma identified a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck confirmed a diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with cerebellar tonsillar herniation measuring 11 mm. Polysomnography indicated mild sleep apnea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, and central events accounting for 72% of apneic episodes. This case demonstrates that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms closely resembling those seen in acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the necessity of considering underlying structural brain abnormalities in patients presenting with otherwise unexplained respiratory issues.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending a minimum of 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI scans. Recent imaging research indicates that CMI is present in 1% of adults, yet symptomatic cases are seen in only about 0.1% of the overall population. There is a slight predominance in females with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases are diagnosed in individuals during their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa, resulting in disproportion between the volume of neural tissues and the limited space. Nonetheless, recent research contests the longstanding view of CMI as invariably pathological, suggesting it exists along a continuum ranging from benign anatomical differences to meaningful malformations that produce symptoms. Volumetric analyses reveal considerable demographic differences in posterior fossa size, often without a reliable link to symptomatology. This evolving perspective calls for tailored, symptom-driven management strategies that differentiate asymptomatic anatomical variations from conditions necessitating surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinically apparent form of CMI, marked by a specific pattern of neurological symptoms. The most prominent feature is intermittent occipital headache brought on by Valsalva maneuvers, which occurs in more than 80% of patients. Adult patients often experience more pronounced symptoms than pediatric patients, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is observed in approximately 15%–26% of individuals and usually manifests as oropharyngeal disturbances, including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now being acknowledged more frequently as complications associated with CMI. In a prospective cohort of 90 adults diagnosed with CMI, half exhibited sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. Among these, 6.7% demonstrated a central apnoea index of at least 5, although isolated central sleep apnoea was reported in just a single patient, which may suggest the involvement of brainstem compression. The respiratory issues seen in CMI are not limited to disturbances during sleep; a series of cases has shown occurrences of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia posing significant risk to patients.

Respiratory impairment in these cases stems from factors such as compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation, and disturbance of the neural centers controlling respiration, which culminate in episodic respiratory manifestations. These manifestations represent a secondary, acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, often referred to as 'Ondine’s curse,' and are clinically separate from the congenital form associated with PHOX2B gene mutations. Contemporary recommendations stress the relationship by advising that patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) and suspected sleep-related breathing disorders undergo routine polysomnography. Importantly, surgical decompression of the posterior fossa can normalize CSF flow and may alleviate respiratory disturbances, highlighting the critical need to consider CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory abnormalities.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s attended our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year history of recurrent choking incidents, which arose unpredictably during eating and sleep and involved both solid foods and liquids. She characterized these episodes as abrupt and disturbing, frequently disrupting meals or waking her from sleep with intense coughing and difficulty breathing, although she had never experienced complete airway blockage. Nighttime events happened three to four times per week, and on occasion, occurred twice in a single night. Throughout the years, she had received thorough evaluations, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, none of which revealed any abnormalities. Despite the absence of identifiable causes, her symptoms continued unabated. These issues had considerable social repercussions; she abstained from dining out or attending gatherings due to apprehension about having a choking episode in public. This persistent fear and embarrassment resulted in progressive social isolation and emotional hardship.

A more comprehensive assessment of her medical history disclosed that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, following a minor car accident during which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after the event, she experienced ongoing headaches localized to the occipital region and the vertex. She characterized these headaches as continuous, with a pressure-like quality, frequently confined to a small, coin-sized spot at the vertex. The headaches did not worsen with coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple clinicians diagnosed her with nummular headache and she underwent numerous local scalp injections, resulting only in temporary improvement. The most striking aspect was pronounced allodynia at the vertex; even the sensation of her hair’s weight provoked discomfort. Eventually, she realized that trimming her hair short decreased her usual pain intensity from 6 out of 10 to 3 out of 10, although this choice conflicted with cultural norms and led to psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started to regularly check her oxygen saturation at home with a pulse oximeter. Over a period of several months, she frequently recorded episodes of decreased oxygen saturation, reaching values between 70% and 80%, without experiencing any symptoms. These low readings were typically unnoticed by her until she reviewed her pulse oximeter. Several visits to the emergency department ultimately concluded that her symptoms were due to anxiety.

A significant event transpired at an international airport. As she awaited her departure, she suddenly developed severe dizziness accompanied by nausea. Medical staff present at the scene recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in refusal of boarding. After supplemental oxygen was administered, all symptoms resolved entirely within a few minutes. Follow-up assessment by pulmonology, including chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal findings.

In light of her persistent occipital and vertex headaches, increasing episodes of choking, and unexplained episodes of hypoxia, clinicians sought to identify a neurological diagnosis that could account for all symptoms. Examination of her medical history showed that she underwent a non-contrast head CT scan 14 years prior, immediately after her accident, which was originally interpreted as normal. Upon detailed retrospective review, the CT demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and diminished cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction, findings consistent with CMI that were overlooked at the initial assessment. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastroesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected due to the patient's episodes of choking. However, this diagnosis was ruled out following normal pH monitoring, unremarkable endoscopic findings, and no improvement despite maximal acid suppression therapy.

An assessment for myasthenia gravis was conducted because of bulbar-related symptoms. However, the condition was considered improbable given negative antibody results, absence of fatigue, and no diurnal symptom fluctuation.

Comprehensive evaluation for primary pulmonary disease was performed after episodes of hypoxia. Imaging of the chest and pulmonary function measurements were within normal limits, ruling out intrinsic pulmonary disorders.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by the maintenance of voluntary respiration while automatic breathing was compromised during periods of inattention or sleep. The onset after head injury set it apart from the congenital variants.

The patient's symptoms were fully accounted for by brainstem abnormalities resulting from Chiari malformation type I (CMI), which caused pressure on the respiratory centers and the nuclei of the lower cranial nerves. This offered a cohesive diagnostic explanation.

Discussion:

The timing of our patient's motor vehicle collision in relation to the development of symptoms requires thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I has been reported to occur congenitally or to develop following head injury and other neurological insults. Current etiological frameworks2022 acknowledge this range by differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants arising from trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In the present case, a CT scan obtained directly after the injury showed a crowded foramen magnum. Due to the absence of imaging prior to the trauma, it is unclear if this observation was a consequence of the traumatic event or a pre-existing condition uncovered by the trauma. The patient experienced sudden occipital headaches following the accident, with a subsequent gradual appearance of bulbar and respiratory symptoms over a fourteen-year period. These clinical developments may indicate either (1) tonsillar herniation resulting from trauma or (2) trauma aggravating or exposing an asymptomatic congenital abnormality.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory complications associated with CMI and contributes to our knowledge of acquired central hypoventilation arising from structural pathology in the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is essential for interpreting the respiratory physiology in CMI. Voluntary respiration, governed by cortical networks, usually remains unimpaired in individuals with CMI. Conversely, automatic respiration, which is regulated by brainstem centers and becomes particularly critical during sleep, is impaired owing to compression of the medullary regions by the tonsils. In this patient, voluntary ventilatory capability was maintained, while there was a loss of innate respiratory drive—a pattern similar to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome but resulting from a different underlying process. The episode at the airport, where a fleeting lapse in attention led to marked oxygen desaturation in the absence of dyspnea, provides a striking demonstration of selective impairment in automatic control: the patient effectively ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that a primary dysfunction within the brainstem is responsible for the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea despite marked episodes of oxygen desaturation. This potential pathology in the brainstem may interfere with essential afferent neural pathways required to transmit signals to the cortex for conscious perception. Breathlessness constitutes an interoceptive sensation, relying on the cortical processing of these afferent inputs, particularly within regions such as the anterior insular cortex. When these signals are disrupted at the brainstem level, the cortical areas are deprived of the necessary information to create the sensation of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The significant gap between the pronounced clinical symptoms in our patient and the modest findings from polysomnography (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was employed instead of type I, resulting in the omission of EEG recordings. This missing data prevented us from determining the actual total sleep time, most likely causing substantial underreporting of respiratory disturbances. When EEG is unavailable, the AHI is based on the total duration of the recording—including wakeful periods—not just the time spent asleep. As a result, this methodological issue suggests that the AHI value of 6.0 does not accurately reflect the real extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

In spite of this constraint, multiple findings suggest central hypoventilation: (1) central events comprise 72% of occurrences, despite a modest total AHI; (2) there were recorded episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% in the absence of dyspnoea or patient-reported symptoms, both in the airport and during routine evaluations; and (3) imaging revealed marked brainstem compression, indicated by 11 mm of tonsillar herniation.

A Type I polysomnography incorporating comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation has been scheduled at an off-site sleep center. The study has not yet been performed, as it awaits coordination of the patient’s schedule, consent to undergo the procedure, and appointment availability at the outside institution. The results, once available, are expected to yield more precise information on sleep structure, respiratory activity, and to assess both the presence and degree of central hypoventilation.

It is recognized that, based on presently available data, central hypoventilation persists as a strong clinical consideration, but has not yet been conclusively diagnosed.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia affecting both solids and liquids equally, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, was the patient’s most troubling complaint. Prospective assessment with standardized swallowing tests showed impairment in 36% of individuals, with neurogenic dysfunction being most common. Difficulty swallowing all types of food textures, without distinction, is characteristic of neurogenic dysphagia rather than mechanical obstruction. Although the patient's symptom was evaluated repeatedly from both gastroenterology and otolaryngology viewpoints and yielded normal results, her correct diagnosis was postponed for several years. This delay illustrates an important clinical principle: in cases of dysphagia or choking where endoscopic examination reveals no structural abnormality, a neurological etiology should be considered. In a retrospective review of children experiencing persistent dysphagia, CMI was identified in 3.3% of cases, a markedly higher rate than the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence observed in the general pediatric population. This finding supports the notion that ongoing dysphagia may signify an underlying CMI.

The patient's description of vertex pain that improves following hair cutting highlights a notable, yet insufficiently researched, occurrence. In cases of Chiari malformation type I (CMI), occipital neuralgia may develop and contribute to heightened sensitivity of the scalp; the diagnostic standards necessitate the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia when the scalp and/or hair is stimulated in a non-painful manner’. Despite this, reports connecting occipital neuralgia to CMI are uncommon, and definitive evidence for a causal relationship remains lacking.

Another possible explanation is the newly recognized 'burning vertex syndrome,' characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align precisely with established primary headache types such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headaches. The proposed pathophysiology may involve involvement of small fiber pathways in the scalp, and patients have demonstrated inconsistent responses to greater occipital nerve blocks. In this case, the patient’s vertex symptoms could reflect: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) a coincidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache condition. Comprehensive characterization of cutaneous allodynia in CMI populations remains insufficient, as highlighted by the lack of specialized assessment tools for CMI symptoms prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Due to this limited evidence, we report this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than assigning it diagnostic significance.

This case demonstrates the considerable difficulties encountered in diagnosing CMI when it manifests with unusual or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, which contributed to a delay in diagnosis of 14 years. The process of reaching an accurate neurological diagnosis—marked by early uncertainty and a slow progression toward clarity—is often reported in the literature.
