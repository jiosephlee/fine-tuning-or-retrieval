Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year duration of recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, as well as episodes of nocturnal choking occurring three to four times per week, and persistent occipital headaches lasting 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home monitoring fortuitously identified significant oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) occurring without signs of respiratory distress. Comprehensive evaluations with repeated laryngoscopies and endoscopies aimed at determining the cause of choking were unremarkable. Reassessment of a 14-year-old CT head scan, originally obtained following unrelated trauma, revealed an overlooked crowded foramen magnum. Further imaging with MRI of the head and neck identified Chiari malformation type I (CMI), showing 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0), with 72% central events. This report highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underlining the need to evaluate for structural abnormalities of the brain in individuals presenting with unexplained respiratory issues.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum as visualized on sagittal MRI. Current imaging data indicate that CMI can be found in about 1% of adults, whereas only about 0.1% of individuals experience clinical symptoms. There is a slight female predominance (ratio 1.3:1), and onset most often occurs in the third decade of life.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, which results in a discrepancy between the size of neural elements and the compartment they occupy. Nevertheless, recent data dispute the long-held belief that CMI is inherently pathological, instead suggesting a continuum that ranges from benign anatomical differences to malformations with clinical relevance. Volumetric analyses now reveal marked demographic differences in posterior fossa size, but these do not uniformly align with symptom presentation. This evolving perspective emphasizes the importance of tailoring clinical management to the patient's symptoms, carefully differentiating incidental anatomical variants from clinically significant forms that may warrant operative management.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical presentation of CMI, marked by a characteristic group of neurological symptoms. The primary complaint is intermittent occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, observed in more than 80% of cases. Adult patients generally display more pronounced symptoms than pediatric cases, particularly in the presence of associated syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves has been documented in 15%–26% of individuals, most often manifesting as oropharyngeal abnormalities, including palatal weakness, tongue wasting, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory involvement is now acknowledged as a complication seen more frequently in patients with CMI. One prospective investigation involving 90 adult subjects with CMI revealed that half exhibited sleep-associated breathing disturbances. Among these, a central apnoea index of at least 5 was present in 6.7% of the cohort, though only a single individual developed isolated central sleep apnoea, possibly attributable to compression of the brainstem. The spectrum of respiratory dysfunction in CMI is not limited to sleep-related phenomena; acute respiratory insufficiency and severe hypoxaemia have also been reported in patients according to a case series.

Respiratory impairment arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and disruption of neural centres responsible for respiratory regulation, resulting in episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—often referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which differs from the congenital central hypoventilation syndrome linked to PHOX2B genetic mutations. Contemporary guidelines highlight this association by advising routine polysomnography in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who exhibit potential sleep-disordered breathing. Notably, surgical decompression of the posterior fossa can re-establish normal CSF circulation and may resolve respiratory symptoms, reinforcing the critical need to consider CMI in patients presenting with unexplained respiratory disturbances.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early 40s attended our family medicine clinic, reporting a 7-year history of recurrent choking incidents that occurred unpredictably during both meals and sleep. These episodes affected her ability to swallow solids and liquids alike. She described them as abrupt and alarming, frequently forcing her to stop eating or waking her from sleep with intense coughing and gasping, though she never experienced complete airway blockage. Nocturnal occurrences happened three to four times per week, and sometimes even twice in a single night. Throughout these years, she underwent extensive workup, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopic examinations, all yielding normal results. Despite the absence of any diagnostic findings, her symptoms continued unabated. The consequences were considerable for her social life, as she avoided dining out and social events due to the apprehension of choking in public. The persistent anxiety and embarrassment contributed to growing social isolation and emotional hardship.

A comprehensive assessment of her clinical background revealed that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, following a minor automobile accident in which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after this event, she experienced ongoing headaches located in the occipital and vertex regions. She characterized the headaches as continuous, resembling a pressure sensation, and frequently confined to a coin-sized area on the vertex. The pain was not intensified by actions such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple clinicians diagnosed nummular headache, and she received several targeted scalp injections; however, these interventions provided only short-lived relief. The most notable characteristic was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, to the extent that even the sensation of her hair provoked pain. Eventually, she found that trimming her hair short diminished her baseline pain intensity from 6/10 to 3/10. Nevertheless, this choice conflicted with cultural norms and caused additional psychological discomfort.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started tracking her oxygen levels at home with a personal pulse oximeter. Throughout several months, she frequently recorded episodes of reduced oxygen saturation, ranging from 70% to 80%, without any accompanying symptoms. These events were frequently unnoticed until she used her device to measure them. On several occasions, emergency assessments concluded that her symptoms were due to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she was awaiting her flight, she suddenly developed severe dizziness and nausea. Medical staff at the scene measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her not being permitted to board the aircraft. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms resolved entirely within minutes. Later, comprehensive pulmonary assessment—including chest imaging and pulmonary function tests—revealed no abnormalities.

Considering her persistent occipital and vertex headaches alongside increasing episodes of choking and unaccounted-for hypoxia, a comprehensive neurological explanation was sought. Review of past medical documentation identified a non-contrast head CT performed 14 years prior, after her accident, which had originally been interpreted as normal. Nevertheless, meticulous reassessment of this scan demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and absent cerebrospinal fluid spaces at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was suspected at first due to the presence of choking episodes. However, this was ruled out after pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations yielded normal findings, and the patient did not improve with maximum doses of acid-suppressing therapy.

Assessment for myasthenia gravis was prompted by bulbar symptomatology. However, the diagnosis was considered improbable given negative antibody results, no evidence of fatigability, and no diurnal fluctuation of symptoms.

The possibility of primary pulmonary disease was thoroughly explored in response to episodes of hypoxia. Unremarkable chest imaging and normal results from pulmonary function testing ruled out inherent pulmonary pathology.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (also known as acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by intact voluntary respiration coupled with deficient involuntary regulation during periods of sleep or distraction. Its onset after head trauma differentiated it from congenital variants.

The patient’s clinical presentation was attributable to brainstem abnormalities resulting from CMI, which led to compression of both the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei and thus accounted for all symptoms under a single diagnosis.

Discussion:

The close timing between the patient’s motor vehicle collision and the emergence of symptoms necessitates thoughtful evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is known to occur congenitally, but it may also develop as a consequence of head injury or other neurologic events. Modern etiological frameworks2022 acknowledge this range, differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that arise due to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained right after the incident revealed a constricted foramen magnum. Due to the absence of imaging prior to the injury, it is unclear whether this feature was a consequence of the trauma or pre-existing but uncovered by the event. The acute appearance of occipital headache directly after the accident, with subsequent gradual onset of bulbar and respiratory manifestations across 14 years, may indicate either: (1) trauma resulting in tonsillar herniation or (2) trauma that triggered or exposed an otherwise silent congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory presentations of CMI and contributes to the knowledge regarding acquired central hypoventilation patterns resulting from brainstem structural abnormalities. Recognizing the differentiation between voluntary and automatic respiration is fundamental to comprehending the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. Voluntary respiration, which is governed by cortical neural circuits, generally remains unaffected in individuals with CMI. Conversely, automatic respiration—regulated by brainstem centers and critical during sleep—is impaired due to compression of the medullary regions by herniated tonsils. In the presented patient, voluntary breathing capacity was maintained, whereas automatic ventilatory drive was absent, closely mimicking the clinical features of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome but resulting from a distinct underlying mechanism. The event at the airport, where an episode of inattention led to severe desaturation without any sensation of dyspnea, starkly demonstrates the selective disruption of automatic respiration: the patient literally “forgets to breathe.”

We propose that underlying dysfunction within the brainstem is responsible for the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea despite considerable episodes of hypoxaemia. This suspected brainstem abnormality likely interrupts essential ascending neural pathways, preventing their transmission to the cerebral cortex and thereby blocking conscious perception. The sensation of breathlessness constitutes an aspect of interoception and relies on afferent input being processed by regions such as the anterior insular cortex. When these signals are disrupted as they pass through the brainstem, the cortex fails to receive adequate information to elicit the experience of air hunger, which may account for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The significant divergence observed between the patient’s pronounced clinical manifestations and the relatively modest polysomnographic result (AHI 6.0) necessitates clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was utilized instead of type I, resulting in the lack of EEG monitoring. This omission prevented the precise measurement of total sleep duration, probably causing an appreciable underrepresentation of the frequency of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG, the AHI is derived from the entire recorded period, which encompasses episodes of wakefulness in addition to sleep, rather than reflecting actual sleep time exclusively. Consequently, this methodological shortcoming suggests the reported AHI of 6.0 is likely an underestimate of the true extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Nevertheless, multiple findings are indicative of central hypoventilation: (1) central respiratory events comprise the majority (72%), even though the total AHI remains low; (2) episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% have been observed, in the absence of dyspnea or patient recognition (noted both at the airport and during standard evaluations); and (3) imaging reveals marked brainstem compression, demonstrated by 11 mm of tonsillar herniation.

Comprehensive overnight polysomnography (Type I) including full electroencephalogram monitoring, end-tidal CO2 measurement, and video documentation is scheduled at an off-site sleep center. The study's completion is currently on hold due to the need for aligning the patient's schedule, obtaining consent to proceed, and coordinating with the external facility. Following its completion, this assessment is expected to yield more conclusive information regarding the structure of sleep, breathing dynamics, and to clarify the extent and presence of central hypoventilation.

It should be noted that, at present, the diagnosis of central hypoventilation is strongly supported clinically but has yet to be definitively established by objective testing.

Our patient’s most troubling complaint was oropharyngeal dysphagia, which impaired swallowing of both solid foods and liquids equally and was accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. Validated, prospective swallowing evaluations revealed dysfunction in 36% of patients, predominantly showing neurogenic features. The uniform difficulty with all types of consistencies points to neurogenic dysphagia, in contrast to mechanical causes. Despite initial and repeated investigations by gastroenterology and otolaryngology—each yielding normal results—the patient’s diagnosis was not established for several years. This prolonged diagnostic process underscores an important clinical principle: in cases of dysphagia or choking where endoscopic evaluation does not reveal a structural abnormality, neurological causes should be suspected. A retrospective analysis found the prevalence of CMI in children with persistent dysphagia to be 3.3%, much higher than the 0.1%–0.77% reported in the broader paediatric population. These findings indicate that chronic dysphagia may be a significant marker for underlying CMI.

The patient's experience of vertex pain that improves following hair cutting illustrates a noteworthy but insufficiently researched occurrence. Occipital neuralgia, which can present alongside CMI, frequently leads to heightened scalp sensitivity. Its diagnostic guidelines include 'dysesthesia and/or allodynia elicited by harmless stimulation of the scalp and/or hair.' Nevertheless, connections between occipital neuralgia and CMI are seldom documented, and existing evidence does not firmly support a causal relationship.

Another possibility is the newly characterized ‘burning vertex syndrome’, which is marked by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and symptoms that do not align precisely with recognized primary headache disorders like migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology likely involves small fiber pathways within the scalp, with reported patient responses to greater occipital nerve blockade being inconsistent. In the present case, the vertex-related complaints could indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) coexisting burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache entity. Notably, consistent reporting of cutaneous allodynia patterns in individuals with CMI is minimal, highlighted by the lack of any dedicated tools to assess these specific symptoms before the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Owing to the limited data, we describe this presentation as a notable clinical observation rather than as a conclusive diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when it manifests with unusual or deceptive clinical features, including central hypoventilation and choking, which resulted in a fourteen-year postponement of accurate diagnosis. The progression towards a definitive neurological diagnosis, marked by early uncertainty and incremental clarification, echoes patterns observed in other reports.
