Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported experiencing choking episodes involving both solids and liquids for 7 years, with nighttime choking occurring 3–4 times per week, and had persistent occipital headaches for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home monitoring incidentally identified episodes of significant oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) despite the absence of respiratory distress. Diagnostic evaluations with repeated laryngoscopies and endoscopies aimed at the choking complaints yielded normal results. A re-examination of a head CT scan from 14 years prior, obtained following an unrelated trauma, found a previously unnoticed crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck revealed Chiari malformation type I (CMI), characterized by an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. A sleep study showed mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0), primarily featuring central apnoeic events (72%). This case demonstrates that CMI may manifest with respiratory signs resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to consider structural brain anomalies in patients with unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils measuring 5 mm or more past the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI scans. Recent imaging data estimate a prevalence of CMI in about 1% of adults; however, clinical symptoms are present in roughly 0.1% of the population. There is a slight female predominance with a ratio of 1.3:1, and the condition most commonly manifests in individuals in their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an undersized posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between neural tissues and the surrounding cranial volume. Nevertheless, current research disputes the longstanding view that CMI is inherently abnormal, instead suggesting a continuum ranging from benign anatomical differences to those with clinical importance. Recent investigations utilizing volumetric analysis reveal notable variation in posterior fossa size across demographic groups, often lacking a clear association with clinical manifestations. This evolving perspective underscores the need for tailored, symptom-driven approaches in clinical management, emphasizing the differentiation of asymptomatic structural variants from those malformations that merit operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical expression of CMI, marked by a recognizable pattern of neurological symptoms. The principal complaint is sudden onset occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of patients. Adults often experience more pronounced symptoms than pediatric patients, particularly when syringomyelia is also present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is noted in 15% to 26% of individuals, most commonly manifesting as oropharyngeal disorders including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are being identified more frequently as a complication associated with CMI. In a prospective cohort of 90 adults diagnosed with CMI, half displayed sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. A central apnoea index of 5 or greater was present in 6.7% of the cohort, although isolated central sleep apnoea was reported in only a single patient, possibly indicative of compression of the brainstem. The respiratory involvement is not limited to disruptions during sleep; a case series described episodes of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia among individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in this context arises due to compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and disturbance of respiratory regulatory centres, resulting in episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms reflect an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which differs from the congenital form associated with PHOX2B mutations. Contemporary guidelines underscore this association by advocating for routine polysomnographic evaluation in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) presenting with suspected sleep-related breathing disorders. Importantly, surgical decompression of the posterior fossa can re-establish normal CSF dynamics and potentially ameliorate respiratory manifestations, highlighting the necessity of considering CMI as a cause of unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s came to our family medicine clinic with a 7-year history of recurring choking spells that happened without warning during both eating and sleep, with no difference between solids and liquids. She reported that these incidents were abrupt and highly uncomfortable, frequently interrupting meals or abruptly waking her from sleep due to intense coughing and difficulty breathing; however, her airway was never fully blocked. She experienced nighttime events three to four times per week, with rare instances of two occurrences in a single night. Throughout this period, she had multiple workups, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Her symptoms continued despite the absence of objective findings. The condition had a major social effect—she started to avoid dining out and social gatherings because of anxiety over potential choking in public. Persistent worry and humiliation eventually contributed to progressive social isolation and worsening emotional distress.

A thorough review of her medical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, after she was involved in a minor car accident during which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after, she began experiencing continuous headaches located at the occipital region and vertex. She characterized the headaches as persistent, with a pressing sensation, and often confined to a small, coin-sized area on the vertex. The headaches did not worsen with actions such as coughing, straining, or performing Valsalva maneuvers. She received a diagnosis of nummular headache from several clinicians and underwent numerous local scalp injections; however, these provided only short-term relief. The most prominent symptom was significant allodynia at the vertex, with even her hair’s weight causing discomfort. Eventually, she found that shortening her hair decreased her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, although this choice was at odds with cultural norms and led to emotional hardship.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started to track her oxygen levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Across several months, she consistently recorded asymptomatic declines in oxygen saturation, with values falling into the 70%–80% range. She frequently only discovered these events when consulting her device. Numerous emergency assessments concluded her symptoms were due to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she abruptly developed severe dizziness accompanied by nausea. Medical staff at the scene measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, after which she was prevented from boarding. Her symptoms abated entirely within minutes following supplemental oxygen therapy. Later assessment by a pulmonologist, including imaging studies of the chest and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal results.

Due to the presence of longstanding headaches affecting the occipital and vertex regions, in addition to recurrent choking events and unexplained episodes of hypoxia, clinicians sought an overarching neurological explanation. Review of her prior medical documents identified a non-contrast CT head scan obtained 14 years prior, following her trauma, which was interpreted as normal at that time. Upon thorough reassessment, the scan demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and absence of cerebrospinal fluid at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

An initial diagnosis of gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with accompanying laryngopharyngeal reflux was entertained due to the presence of choking episodes. However, this was ruled out based on unremarkable pH monitoring, normal findings on endoscopy, and absence of clinical improvement despite maximal acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered as a potential diagnosis given the presence of bulbar symptoms. However, the diagnosis was deemed improbable based on negative antibody results, the absence of muscle fatigability, and no observed diurnal fluctuations.

A thorough assessment for primary lung pathology was performed after episodes of hypoxemia. Both chest imaging and pulmonary function testing were unremarkable, effectively ruling out underlying pulmonary disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (also known as acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by the presence of normal voluntary respiration alongside disrupted automatic respiratory function, particularly evident during sleep or periods of distraction. The onset after head trauma set it apart from congenital variants.

Compression of the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei due to brainstem pathology associated with CMI accounted for all the patient’s symptoms, thus serving as the single, comprehensive diagnosis.

Discussion:

It is important to closely examine the timing between the patient’s involvement in a motor vehicle accident and the emergence of symptoms. Chiari malformation type I is known to arise both as a congenital anomaly and as an outcome of traumatic brain injury or other neurological insults. Recent aetiological frameworks2022 have acknowledged this diversity, differentiating between congenital (primary) Chiari malformations and those acquired (secondary) following trauma or elevations in intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained directly after the injury showed a congested foramen magnum. As there are no pre-injury images available, it is unclear if this finding resulted from the trauma itself or was simply disclosed by it. The acute occurrence of occipital headache following the accident, in combination with the gradual appearance of bulbar and respiratory symptoms across 14 years, may indicate either: (1) herniation of the tonsils triggered by the trauma, or (2) trauma aggravating or exposing an otherwise asymptomatic congenital abnormality.

This case illustrates the multifaceted respiratory complications seen in CMI and provides insight into the acquired central hypoventilation patterns associated with brainstem structural abnormalities. Understanding the distinction between voluntary and automatic respiration is key to comprehending the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. Voluntary respiration, which involves cortical pathways, is generally preserved in individuals with CMI, whereas automatic respiration—managed by brainstem centers and critical during sleep—is disrupted due to compression of the medullary region by tonsillar herniation. Our patient maintained the ability for voluntary ventilation but lacked an automatic respiratory drive, closely mirroring the presentation of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, although stemming from a separate underlying mechanism. The event at the airport, in which transient inattention triggered significant oxygen desaturation absent any sensation of breathlessness, sharply demonstrates this distinct impairment of automatic respiratory control, effectively causing the patient to ‘forget to breathe’.

We propose that the underlying reason for the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea in spite of marked oxygen desaturation is a primary dysfunction of the brainstem. Such a disturbance in the brainstem probably interferes with essential ascending afferent neural pathways responsible for transmitting sensory information to the cortex, thereby enabling conscious perception. Breathlessness represents a form of interoception, which relies on afferent signal processing in crucial cerebral areas such as the anterior insular cortex. If these signals are obstructed or impaired during their journey through the brainstem, the cortex would not be sufficiently stimulated to elicit the sensation of air hunger, accounting for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their physical compromise.

The pronounced difference between the patient’s intense symptomatology and the modest polysomnographic result (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was performed instead of the more comprehensive type I study, resulting in a lack of EEG data. The absence of EEG monitoring impeded precise measurement of actual sleep duration and likely led to a substantial underreporting of respiratory disturbances. In this context, the AHI was derived from the entire recorded period, encompassing both sleep and wake intervals, rather than solely periods of sleep. Consequently, this methodological shortcoming suggests that the measured AHI of 6.0 does not reflect the full extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Although this constraint exists, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central events account for 72% of occurrences, even with a modest aggregate AHI; (2) oxygen saturation declines to 70%–80% were observed in the absence of dyspnoea or subjective awareness by the patient, including in settings such as the airport and during everyday interactions; and (3) structural imaging revealed marked compression of the brainstem, evidenced by 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography—including full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video surveillance—has been scheduled at a separate sleep center. The study’s completion awaits alignment of the patient’s availability, the patient’s willingness to undergo the procedure, and the remote facility’s schedule. Upon completion, the study will offer more precise assessment of sleep stages, respiratory activity, and the extent of central hypoventilation.

It is recognized that, based on presently available data, central hypoventilation is a strongly considered clinical possibility but has not yet been conclusively diagnosed.

The most troubling symptom for our patient was oropharyngeal dysphagia impacting both liquids and solids to the same degree, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. In one prospective study utilizing validated tools to assess swallowing, 36% of patients exhibited dysfunction, primarily of a neurogenic nature. The parallel impairment with all textures helps differentiate neurogenic from mechanical causes of dysphagia. Despite this, the patient's predominant symptom was initially—and repeatedly—examined from gastroenterology and otolaryngology perspectives, yielding normal results; her ultimate diagnosis was postponed by several years. This protracted diagnostic process underlines a critical point: when patients present with dysphagia or choking and no obvious structural abnormalities are found during endoscopy, clinicians should consider a neurological etiology. Retrospective data show the prevalence of CMI among children with refractory dysphagia to be 3.3%, much higher than the 0.1%–0.77% documented in the general pediatric population. Therefore, persistent dysphagia may serve as an important clue to undiagnosed CMI.

The patient described pain localized to the vertex region, which subsided following hair cutting—an uncommon phenomenon that has received limited investigation. Occipital neuralgia is sometimes seen alongside CMI and may result in hypersensitivity of the scalp; its diagnosis mandates the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia elicited by non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Nonetheless, occurrences linking occipital neuralgia with CMI are infrequently documented, and evidence supporting a causal connection is lacking.

A recently identified condition, termed ‘burning vertex syndrome’, has also been proposed as a possible explanation. This syndrome is characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not correspond well with established primary headache categories including migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. It is postulated that the pathophysiology may involve small nerve fibers within the scalp, and patients have demonstrated varied responses to greater occipital nerve block interventions. In this case, the vertex symptoms could be attributable to: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) an unrelated manifestation of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache entity. Notably, there is insufficient systematic reporting regarding cutaneous allodynia in individuals with CMI, highlighted by the lack of tools to assess these specific symptoms before the advent of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Owing to the limited available data, we describe this finding as an intriguing clinical observation rather than as a conclusive diagnostic indicator.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI when it manifests through uncommon or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a fourteen-year interval before the condition was identified. The process of diagnosis, marked by early uncertainty and a slow progression toward accurate neurological determination, frequently appears in previously reported cases.
