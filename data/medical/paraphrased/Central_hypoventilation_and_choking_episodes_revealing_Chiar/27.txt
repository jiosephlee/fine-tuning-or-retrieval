Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year duration of repeated choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, as well as nocturnal choking events occurring 3–4 times per week, accompanied by chronic occipital headaches persisting for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home-based monitoring unexpectedly identified significant episodes of oxygen desaturation to levels between 70%–80% in the absence of any signs of respiratory distress. Repeated laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations aimed at elucidating the cause of her choking yielded normal findings. Retrospective assessment of a cranial CT conducted 14 years prior for unrelated traumatic injury revealed a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI imaging of the brain and cervical spine demonstrated a Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with herniation of the cerebellar tonsils measuring 11 mm. Polysomnography indicated mild sleep apnoea with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, predominantly central in nature (72% central events). This case highlights that CMI may present with respiratory manifestations that can closely resemble acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the need to consider structural brain lesions in the diagnostic workup of patients with unexplained disturbances in respiration.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils of 5 mm or more past the foramen magnum as visualized on sagittal MRI scans. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI is present in about 1% of adults, while symptoms manifest in nearly 0.1% of people overall. There is a slight preponderance in females with a ratio of 1.3:1, and onset commonly occurs in individuals in their twenties.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an undersized posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between the neural elements and the surrounding cranial compartment. Nevertheless, recent research has questioned the longstanding belief that CMI invariably represents a pathological condition, suggesting it may range from typical anatomical differences to abnormalities with clinical impact. Modern volumetric analyses have revealed notable variation in posterior fossa dimensions across populations, often without a direct relationship to symptomatology. This evolving perspective underscores the need for tailored, symptom-guided management, differentiating innocuous anatomical variations from those malformations that warrant operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the symptomatic presentation of CMI, marked by a specific pattern of neurological signs. The primary complaint is episodic occipital headache, commonly triggered by Valsalva maneuvers and found in more than 80% of cases. Adult patients often exhibit more pronounced symptoms than pediatric patients, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in 15%–26% of cases and usually manifests as oropharyngeal disturbances, including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory involvement is now acknowledged as an emerging complication associated with CMI. A prospective investigation involving 90 adult subjects with CMI identified that half exhibited sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. In 6.7% of these individuals, a central apnoea index of 5 or greater was reported, but isolated central sleep apnoea was seen in only a single patient, which may indicate the effect of brainstem compression. Respiratory issues related to CMI are not limited to disturbances during sleep; a series of cases reported acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia posing significant risks to affected patients.

Respiratory dysfunction in this context arises due to compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and impairment of respiratory control centers, which manifest as episodic respiratory symptoms. These manifestations correspond to an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome, sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’, differing from the congenital central hypoventilation syndrome associated with PHOX2B mutations. Current guidelines stress the significance of this relationship by advising routine polysomnography in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who exhibit possible sleep-related breathing disturbances. Notably, decompression of the posterior fossa can normalize CSF flow dynamics and may alleviate respiratory symptoms, highlighting the necessity of considering CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A female in her early 40s attended our family medicine clinic, reporting a 7-year history of recurrent choking spells that transpired unpredictably during both eating and sleep. These episodes affected the ingestion of both solid and liquid foods, with equal frequency. She explained that the events were abrupt and caused considerable distress, frequently disrupting meals or waking her from sleep with intense bouts of coughing and gasping, though they never resulted in full blockage of her airway. Nighttime occurrences happened three to four times weekly, and sometimes occurred twice in a single night. Throughout the years, she underwent numerous investigations, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all of which yielded normal findings. In spite of the absence of identifiable causes, her symptoms persisted. The effects on her social life were substantial; she refrained from dining out and attending social events due to concern over choking in public. This persistent anxiety and embarrassment contributed to growing social isolation and worsening emotional distress.

A comprehensive review of her clinical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, following a minor motor vehicle collision accompanied by a brief loss of consciousness. Shortly thereafter, she experienced ongoing headaches located in the occipital region and at the vertex. She described these headaches as unrelenting, pressure-like, and frequently confined to a small, coin-sized area at the vertex. The headache intensity was unaffected by actions such as coughing, straining, or performing Valsalva maneuvers. Several healthcare providers diagnosed nummular headache and administered multiple localized scalp injections, which provided only temporary alleviation. The most notable symptom was significant vertex allodynia, with even the sensation of her hair being enough to provoke discomfort. Eventually, she learned that keeping her hair short diminished her baseline pain score from 6/10 to 3/10; however, this choice conflicted with cultural norms and resulted in emotional upset.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started tracking her oxygen levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Over the course of several months, she frequently recorded asymptomatic drops in oxygen saturation, reaching values between 70% and 80%. These occurrences typically went unnoticed until she measured her saturation with the device. Several emergency department assessments attributed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at a foreign airport. As she awaited her flight, she developed sudden onset vertigo accompanied by nausea. Medical staff at the scene measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from boarding. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms dissipated entirely within a few minutes. Later assessment of her lungs, including chest radiography and spirometry, revealed no abnormalities.

Considering the persistent occipital and vertex headaches, escalating episodes of choking, and unexplained low oxygen levels, clinicians sought a single neurological cause. Review of her prior medical documents indicated a non-contrast head CT scan conducted 14 years previously after her injury, which was interpreted as normal at that time. Nonetheless, meticulous reassessment of this imaging uncovered a crowded foramen magnum with absence of cerebrospinal fluid around the craniocervical junction, consistent with features of CMI that were not recognized originally. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the presence of choking symptoms, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected. However, this was ruled out due to normal pH monitoring results, unremarkable endoscopic findings, and absence of improvement with maximum acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered because of bulbar symptomatology. However, it was deemed improbable given negative antibody results, no evidence of fatigability, and stable symptoms throughout the day.

A thorough assessment for primary pulmonary disease was undertaken in response to hypoxic events. Both chest imaging and pulmonary function testing were unremarkable, ruling out underlying lung pathology.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (also referred to as acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly indicated by the presence of intact voluntary respiration while automatic respiratory control was compromised during periods of distraction or sleep. Its occurrence subsequent to head injury set it apart from congenital variants.

The unifying diagnosis was established as brainstem pathology resulting from CMI, which accounted for all symptoms due to its effect of compressing the respiratory centers and nuclei of the lower cranial nerves.

Discussion:

It is important to closely examine the timing between the patient’s motor vehicle collision and the development of symptoms. Chiari malformation type I is noted in the literature as occurring in congenital cases, as well as arising after head injuries or other neurological trauma. Recent aetiological frameworks (2022) acknowledge this range, differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that develop as a result of trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, post-accident CT imaging revealed a crowded foramen magnum. However, as no pre-injury imaging was available, it remains unclear if this anatomical finding resulted from the trauma or was simply discovered because of it. The sudden appearance of occipital headaches immediately after the event, subsequently progressing to bulbar and respiratory symptoms over the next 14 years, may indicate either: (1) herniation of the cerebellar tonsils as a direct result of trauma, or (2) trauma that worsened or uncovered an underlying, previously silent congenital anomaly.

This case illustrates the intricate respiratory presentations associated with CMI, expanding insight into secondary central hypoventilation patterns stemming from structural abnormalities in the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic ventilation is pivotal for understanding respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. Voluntary respiration, governed by cortical mechanisms, is generally preserved in individuals with CMI, while automatic respiration—managed by brainstem centers and critical for maintaining ventilation during sleep—is impaired due to the medullary structures being compressed by cerebellar tonsils. In this patient, voluntary respiratory function remained intact, whereas the automatic drive was absent, displaying a phenotype similar to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome but with a distinct underlying pathogenesis. The airport incident, during which brief distraction led to marked hypoxemia without awareness of breathlessness, provides a clear demonstration of selective loss of automatic respiratory control: the individual, in effect, “forgets to breathe.”

We propose that a fundamental dysfunction within the brainstem underlies the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea despite marked episodes of oxygen desaturation. Such brainstem involvement is likely impeding essential ascending afferent neural pathways required to elicit conscious perception. Breathlessness represents an interoceptive experience dependent on afferent input being relayed to and processed by regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If these signals are disrupted while passing through the brainstem, the cortex is deprived of the inputs necessary to create the sensation of air hunger, accounting for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their respiratory compromise.

The notable inconsistency between the severity of our patient’s clinical manifestations and the relatively low AHI value of 6.0 observed on polysomnography merits clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was performed instead of type I; type III does not include EEG monitoring. As a result, true total sleep time could not be measured, likely causing a substantial undercount of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG, the AHI is determined by dividing the number of events by the total recording time—which encompasses both sleep and wakefulness—rather than by actual sleep duration alone. Consequently, this methodological shortcoming probably resulted in an AHI of 6.0 that does not accurately reflect the real burden of sleep-disordered breathing in this patient.

Although this constraint exists, multiple aspects are indicative of central hypoventilation: (1) a high proportion of central episodes (72%) even though the total AHI was minimal; (2) observed oxygen saturations falling to 70%–80% in the absence of both dyspnoea and subjective recognition by the patient (recorded at the airport and in standard interactions); and (3) anatomical findings of substantial brainstem impingement, with 11 mm tonsillar herniation.

A Type I polysomnography including comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal carbon dioxide measurement, and video surveillance has been scheduled at an external sleep center. However, the study has not yet been performed due to the need to coordinate patient availability, willingness to undergo the procedure, and scheduling logistics at the remote site. When completed, this investigation will allow for a more precise assessment of sleep architecture, respiratory behavior, and the detection and grading of central hypoventilation.

At present, central hypoventilation is a strongly considered clinical suspicion based on available data, though it has not yet been conclusively diagnosed.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, presenting with equal impairment in swallowing both solids and liquids and accompanied by choking and coughing episodes, was the symptom that caused the most discomfort for our patient. In a prospective study utilizing standardized swallowing tests, dysfunction was observed in 36% of cases, with neurogenic patterns being most frequent. The similar challenges across all food textures serve to differentiate neurogenic from mechanical types of dysphagia. Despite repeated and initial investigations focused on gastrointestinal and otolaryngological causes, which yielded normal results, the patient’s diagnosis was postponed for several years. This prolonged period before diagnosis underscores a significant clinical takeaway: when dysphagia or choking occurs and endoscopy fails to identify a structural abnormality, a neurological cause should be considered. A retrospective review of children presenting with ongoing dysphagia revealed a CMI prevalence of 3.3%, much higher than the 0.1%–0.77% observed in the general pediatric population, indicating that persistent dysphagia may be an important sign of underlying CMI.

The patient's experience of vertex pain alleviated by hair cutting illustrates a noteworthy but insufficiently researched occurrence. Occipital neuralgia is observed in conjunction with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) and may present as heightened sensitivity of the scalp; diagnosis is based on findings such as dysesthesia or allodynia provoked by non-painful stimulation of the scalp or hair. Despite this, reports describing occipital neuralgia together with CMI are uncommon, and definitive evidence for a causal relationship is lacking.

Another potential explanation is the newly recognized ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align precisely with established primary headache disorders like migraine, tension-type headache, cluster headache, or nummular headache. The suggested pathophysiology may include involvement of small fiber neural pathways from the scalp, with patient responses to greater occipital nerve blockade reported to be inconsistent. In this case, the vertex complaints might be attributed to: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) a coincidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct, unrelated primary headache syndrome. There is a notable gap in systematic recording of cutaneous allodynia distribution among CMI patients, highlighted by the lack of validated tools to measure these particular symptoms in this population prior to the advent of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Given the limited available data, we offer this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding, not as a conclusive diagnostic marker.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when it manifests with unusual or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a 14-year interval before the condition was correctly identified. The process of diagnosis, initially marked by uncertainty and ultimately leading to an accurate neurological assessment, is frequently reported in the literature.
