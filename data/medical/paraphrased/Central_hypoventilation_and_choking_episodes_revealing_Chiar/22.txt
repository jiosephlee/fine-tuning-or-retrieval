Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female in her early 40s reported a 7-year history of repeated choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, as well as nightly choking events occurring 3–4 times per week. She also described persistent occipital headaches over the past 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home monitoring detected severe oxygen desaturations (levels of 70%–80%) in the absence of noticeable respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies were conducted to assess the choking complaints, all yielding normal findings. A retrospective review of a head CT performed 14 years prior for unrelated trauma incidentally showed a crowded foramen magnum, which had been missed initially. Subsequent MRI imaging of the head and neck established the diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography indicated mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, and a predominance (72%) of central apnoeic events. This case underscores that CMI may manifest with respiratory features that resemble acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the necessity of evaluating for brain structural abnormalities in individuals with unexplained respiratory symptoms.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending 5 mm or more beyond the foramen magnum, as observed on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate a prevalence of CMI in about 1% of adults, whereas only 0.1% of the population experience symptoms. There is a slight female predilection with a ratio of 1.3:1, and onset most frequently occurs in the third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between the size of neural components and the surrounding cranial compartment. Nonetheless, recent research has questioned the conventional view of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) as invariably pathologic, suggesting it exists along a continuum that includes benign anatomical variants and more severe, clinically relevant deformities. Current volumetric analyses reveal considerable differences in posterior fossa dimensions across populations, but these differences do not consistently correlate with clinical symptoms. This evolving perspective underscores the importance of tailored, symptom-oriented management, allowing clinicians to differentiate harmless anatomical variations from true disease requiring surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical expression of CMI, which is defined by a specific set of neurological symptoms. The principal complaint is episodic pain in the occipital region triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of individuals. Adults often exhibit more pronounced symptoms than pediatric patients, particularly when syringomyelia is also present. Impairment of the lower cranial nerves is observed in 15%–26% of cases, most commonly presenting as oropharyngeal abnormalities including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now acknowledged more frequently as complications associated with CMI. A prospective investigation involving 90 adult patients diagnosed with CMI identified sleep-related breathing disorders in half of the cohort. Within this group, 6.7% demonstrated a central apnoea index of at least 5, though only a single patient exhibited isolated central sleep apnoea, which may be indicative of brainstem involvement. The respiratory issues are not confined to sleep disorders; evidence from a case series has shown that CMI can lead to acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia.

Respiratory impairment in this context is attributed to factors such as compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and disruption of the respiratory regulatory centers, which give rise to episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—that is separate from the congenital form associated with PHOX2B gene mutations. Contemporary guidelines highlight the relevance of this association by advocating for routine polysomnographic evaluation in patients with CMI who are suspected of having sleep-related breathing disorders. Notably, posterior fossa decompression may normalize CSF circulation and alleviate respiratory symptoms, reinforcing the importance of considering CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory compromise.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early forties came to our family medicine clinic reporting a seven-year history of unpredictable choking episodes during both meals and sleep. These events affected the ingestion of solids and liquids equally. She characterized the episodes as abrupt and highly distressing, frequently disrupting her meals or abruptly waking her with intense coughing and gasping, although she never experienced complete blockage of her airway. The nighttime episodes happened three to four times weekly, and occasionally would occur twice in a single night. During this period, she had been extensively investigated, including three flexible laryngoscopy procedures and two upper GI endoscopies, all of which returned normal findings. Nevertheless, her symptoms continued unabated. The consequences for her social life were profound; she refrained from attending restaurants and social events due to apprehension about potential choking incidents in public. The persistent anxiety and shame resulted in progressively greater social isolation and emotional hardship.

A thorough review of her clinical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, following a minor car accident that resulted in a brief period of unconsciousness. Soon after the incident, she began experiencing ongoing headaches located in the occipital and vertex regions. She characterized these headaches as persistent, with a sensation of pressure, typically concentrated in a coin-sized area at the vertex. Coughing, straining, or engaging in other Valsalva maneuvers did not worsen the pain. She received a diagnosis of nummular headache from several clinicians and underwent a series of local scalp injections, which provided only short-lived improvement. The most notable symptom was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, as even the simple act of her hair resting on this spot provoked discomfort. Ultimately, she found that keeping her hair short lowered her usual pain rating from 6/10 to 3/10, though this choice was at odds with her cultural norms and caused her significant emotional strain.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started regularly checking her oxygen levels at home with a personal pulse oximeter. Throughout the following months, she recorded several instances where her oxygen saturation fell into the 70%–80% range without experiencing symptoms. She only became aware of these episodes upon reviewing her device readings. Several visits to the emergency department resulted in her symptoms being ascribed to anxiety.

An important event took place at a foreign airport. As she waited for her departure, she developed sudden vertigo and felt nauseated. Medical staff present at the scene measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in refusal to board the aircraft. Providing supplemental oxygen alleviated all symptoms rapidly. Later, comprehensive assessment of her lungs—including imaging studies and pulmonary function tests—revealed no abnormalities.

In light of her persistent headaches localized to the occipital and vertex regions, recurrent episodes of choking, and unaccounted-for hypoxia, a search for a singular neurological explanation was initiated. Upon examination of her prior medical documentation, it was found that a non-contrast head CT had been obtained 14 years earlier, after her initial trauma, and was interpreted as unremarkable at that time. Nonetheless, a meticulous reassessment of the imaging demonstrated a congested foramen magnum along with obliteration of the cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. These imaging features were indicative of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) and were not identified in the initial interpretation. Comprehensive evaluation for a primary pulmonary disorder was undertaken after occurrences of hypoxia. Chest imaging and pulmonary function assessments were unremarkable, effectively ruling out primary pulmonary pathology.

Discussion:

It is important to evaluate the timing between the motor vehicle accident experienced by our patient and the emergence of his symptoms. Chiari malformation type I is known to occur as a congenital anomaly as well as an outcome of head trauma or other injuries affecting the nervous system. Current etiological frameworks (2022) acknowledge this range, categorizing Chiari malformations as either primary (congenital) or secondary/acquired, the latter arising in response to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan conducted directly after the injury revealed a crowded foramen magnum. As we lack imaging from before the trauma, it remains unclear whether this was a result of the injury or an incidental finding brought to attention by the event. The rapid appearance of occipital headaches immediately post-trauma, with subsequent gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory manifestations spanning 14 years, may represent either: (1) trauma-associated descent of the cerebellar tonsils, or (2) trauma serving to worsen or make symptomatic an underlying congenital anomaly that had been asymptomatic prior.

This report highlights the intricate respiratory complications associated with CMI and contributes to the comprehension of acquired central hypoventilation arising from structural abnormalities in the brainstem. Differentiating voluntary from automatic breathing is pivotal in deciphering the respiratory pathophysiology of CMI. Volitional respiration, relying on cortical circuits, generally remains unaffected in individuals with CMI. However, brainstem-dependent automatic breathing—critical during sleep—becomes impaired due to compression of the medulla by herniated tonsils. In this case, the patient retained the ability for conscious breathing but exhibited an absent automatic ventilatory response, closely resembling the clinical features of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit resulting from a distinct underlying process. The incident in the airport, where a brief lapse in attention led to severe oxygen desaturation without any sensation of breathlessness, powerfully demonstrates this isolated impairment of automatic respiration: the patient, in effect, ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that underlying dysfunction within the brainstem may explain the patient’s striking lack of breathlessness during pronounced episodes of hypoxaemia. Such a brainstem abnormality could interfere with essential afferent neural pathways responsible for transmitting signals to the cortex, which are required for the conscious perception of respiratory discomfort. The awareness of dyspnoea constitutes interoceptive processing, relying on the interpretation of afferent input by structures such as the anterior insular cortex. If these neural messages are disrupted as they travel through the brainstem, the cortical regions may not receive adequate information to trigger the subjective sensation of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient's impaired recognition of serious physiological compromise.

The pronounced difference between the patient’s significant clinical manifestations and the comparatively mild polysomnography results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Because of logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was employed instead of type I; this method does not include EEG measurements. Consequently, precise determination of total sleep time was not possible, which probably caused a considerable underreporting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG monitoring, the AHI is based on the entire recording duration, encompassing both wakefulness and sleep, rather than actual sleep periods. Therefore, this methodological drawback suggests that the recorded AHI of 6.0 is likely an underestimate of the actual burden of sleep-disordered breathing.

Although this limitation exists, multiple aspects indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central events make up 72% of all events even though the total AHI is low; (2) oxygen saturation was observed falling to 70%–80%, occurring without the patient experiencing dyspnoea or being aware of it (both at the airport and during typical daily activities); and (3) substantial anatomical compression of the brainstem was identified, with an 11 mm tonsillar herniation.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography—including full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video surveillance—has been scheduled at an off-site sleep laboratory. At present, completion of this evaluation is awaiting alignment of patient availability, consent to the procedure, and coordination with the external center’s schedule. Upon execution, this study is expected to yield a more conclusive assessment of sleep stages, ventilatory dynamics, and the existence and severity of central hypoventilation.

It is important to note that, based on current data, central hypoventilation continues to be a strongly supported working diagnosis rather than an unequivocally established condition.

The patient’s most troubling complaint was oropharyngeal dysphagia, which impaired the swallowing of both solids and liquids to an equal degree, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. Validated prospective swallow evaluations revealed impairment in 36% of subjects, predominantly exhibiting neurogenic characteristics. Difficulty with all food consistencies is a hallmark differentiating neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical forms. Despite thorough initial and subsequent assessments by gastroenterology and otolaryngology yielding normal results, the patient’s diagnosis was postponed for several years. This extended diagnostic process underscores a critical clinical point: when endoscopic examination fails to identify structural abnormalities, clinicians should consider neurological causes for dysphagia or choking. In a retrospective cohort of children experiencing persistent dysphagia, the prevalence of CMI was found to be 3.3%, a rate much greater than the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence described in the broader paediatric population. Thus, enduring dysphagia may represent a key marker of underlying CMI.

The patient described discomfort localized to the vertex region, which improved following hair cutting—an unusual yet insufficiently explored clinical feature. While occipital neuralgia may develop in the context of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) and manifest as increased scalp sensitivity, its diagnostic standards specify the presence of dysesthesia or allodynia that becomes evident upon non-noxious stimulation of the scalp or hair. Nevertheless, occurrences of occipital neuralgia in connection with CMI are infrequently documented, and definitive evidence supporting a causal relationship is lacking.

A further differential is the recently characterized ‘burning vertex syndrome’, distinguished by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not correspond clearly with recognized primary headache types such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The underlying pathophysiology is proposed to involve small fiber pathways from the scalp, with reported patient responses to greater occipital nerve blocks being inconsistent. In this case, the vertex complaints could indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) a coincidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache disorder. There is a notable deficiency of systematic reporting of cutaneous allodynia manifestations within CMI populations, highlighted by the previous lack of validated tools for symptom assessment in CMI prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Owing to this limited data, we interpret this observation as an intriguing clinical finding rather than a conclusive diagnostic indicator.

This case demonstrates the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when its symptoms are unusual or deceptive, such as episodes of central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a delayed diagnosis spanning 14 years. The process toward an accurate neurological diagnosis was marked by early uncertainty and a slow progression to the correct conclusion, a pattern frequently noted in published accounts.
