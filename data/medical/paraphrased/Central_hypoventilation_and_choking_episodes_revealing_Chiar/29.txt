Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early 40s reported a 7-year history of repeated episodes of choking involving both solid foods and liquids, as well as nighttime choking incidents occurring 3–4 times per week. She also had chronic occipital headaches for the past 14 years. While monitoring at home during the COVID-19 pandemic, she was found to have episodes of marked oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory symptoms. Repeated laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations failed to reveal any abnormalities to explain the choking. On review, a CT scan of the head obtained 14 years earlier after a traumatic injury showed a congested foramen magnum that had previously been missed. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck identified Chiari malformation type I (CMI), demonstrated by an 11 mm downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography showed mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0), mainly composed of central events (72%). This case highlights that CMI can manifest with respiratory symptoms resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the necessity of assessing for structural brain anomalies in individuals with unexplained respiratory impairment.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils measuring at least 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum, as seen on sagittal MRI. Modern imaging research identifies CMI in about 1% of adults, while clinical symptoms manifest in only around 0.1% of people. There is a slight female predominance with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases are diagnosed in the third decade of life.

The underlying mechanism involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between the size of neural components and the confines of the space. Nonetheless, newer research disputes the longstanding view of CMI as inherently abnormal, suggesting a continuum ranging from harmless structural differences to those with clinical consequence. Contemporary volumetric analyses reveal wide demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions, often lacking direct association with clinical symptoms. This evolution in perspective calls for a personalized, symptom-oriented approach to patient care, differentiating incidental anatomical findings from disorders necessitating operative management.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical presentation of CMI, marked by a specific set of neurological symptoms. The primary complaint is episodic occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of affected individuals. Symptom severity tends to be greater in adults than in pediatric patients, particularly when syringomyelia is present. Involvement of lower cranial nerves has been documented in 15%–26% of cases, most commonly manifesting as oropharyngeal abnormalities including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory complications are becoming more widely acknowledged as a consequence of CMI. In a prospective investigation involving 90 adult patients with CMI, half exhibited sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. A central apnoea index of 5 or greater was noted in 6.7% of individuals, but isolated central sleep apnoea was seen in just one patient, possibly indicating compression at the brainstem level. Respiratory dysfunction related to CMI is not limited to sleep disturbances; a series of cases highlighted occurrences of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia among those with CMI.

Respiratory impairment arises from factors such as compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and disturbances in the respiratory control centres, which together contribute to periodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—that is distinct from the congenital form linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Current recommendations highlight this association by advising routine polysomnography in patients with CMI who are suspected of having sleep-related breathing disorders. Notably, posterior fossa decompression surgery can normalize CSF flow and may alleviate respiratory symptoms, illustrating the need to identify CMI when encountering unexplained respiratory issues.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early 40s arrived at our family medicine practice reporting a seven-year duration of intermittent choking episodes that manifested unpredictably both during meals and at night. These episodes involved both solids and liquids without preference. She characterized the events as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting her eating or awakening her from sleep with intense coughing and gasping, although she never experienced total airway blockage. Nocturnal events were reported three to four times weekly, and occasionally occurred twice in a single night. Throughout this period, she underwent multiple investigations—including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies—which consistently yielded normal results. Nonetheless, her clinical symptoms remained unchanged. The repercussions on her social life were profound; she refrained from dining out or attending gatherings, fearing an episode in front of others. This persistent apprehension and embarrassment progressively led to greater social isolation and increased psychological distress.

A comprehensive review of her medical history indicated that her symptoms started 14 years prior, immediately after a low-impact car accident that resulted in a brief period of unconsciousness. Soon after, she began experiencing ongoing headaches situated at the occipital region and the vertex. She characterized these headaches as unremitting, pressure-like, and frequently confined to an area the size of a coin at the vertex. The pain did not worsen with activities such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple clinicians diagnosed her with nummular headache, leading to repeated local scalp injection treatments, which provided only temporary symptom relief. Notably, she suffered pronounced allodynia at the vertex, with even the sensation of her hair causing discomfort. Eventually, she found that keeping her hair short diminished her usual pain level from 6/10 to 3/10, although this choice conflicted with cultural norms and led to emotional hardship.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started tracking her oxygen levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Throughout several months, she consistently recorded episodes where her oxygen saturation fell to between 70% and 80% without accompanying symptoms. She typically became aware of these desaturations only upon measurement. Several emergency department assessments concluded that her symptoms were related to anxiety.

A significant event transpired at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly developed severe dizziness and nausea. Medical staff at the scene measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, which resulted in her being prevented from boarding the plane. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms disappeared entirely within a matter of minutes. Later assessment by a pulmonary specialist, which included chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed no abnormalities.

Due to the presence of persistent headaches localized to the occipital and vertex regions, along with escalating episodes of choking and unexplained episodes of low oxygen saturation, a search for a single neurological cause was initiated. Review of her old medical documents identified a non-contrast head CT conducted 14 years prior, post-accident, which was originally interpreted as unremarkable. On closer reassessment, this scan demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and obliteration of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastroesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected due to the patient’s choking episodes. However, the diagnosis was ruled out following normal results from pH monitoring, unremarkable endoscopic evaluations, and no improvement with maximal acid-suppressive therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered as a diagnosis because of bulbar symptom involvement. However, the absence of specific antibodies, no demonstration of fatigability, and no fluctuations throughout the day argued against this condition.

An in-depth assessment for primary pulmonary disorders was conducted after the patient experienced hypoxic events. Unremarkable findings on both chest imaging and pulmonary function testing ruled out primary pulmonary disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly indicated by the presence of intact voluntary respiration alongside disrupted automatic regulation when the patient was distracted or asleep. Its onset after head injury differentiated this case from congenital variants.

Compression of the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei due to brainstem involvement from CMI ultimately accounted for all symptoms, serving as the single underlying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of the patient’s motor vehicle collision in relation to the emergence of symptoms requires thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is described in the literature both as a congenital abnormality and as a possible consequence of cranial trauma or other neurological damage. Modern aetiological frameworks2022 acknowledge this range, differentiating congenital (primary) Chiari malformations from acquired or secondary variants that may develop after injury or elevated intracranial pressure.

In the present case, a CT scan obtained right after the injury showed a congested foramen magnum. As there were no imaging studies available prior to the trauma, it is unclear if the trauma was responsible for this appearance or simply disclosed an existing condition. The patient experienced occipital headaches immediately after the accident, with gradual onset of bulbar and respiratory complaints over a 14-year period. This clinical progression may suggest either: (1) trauma leading to tonsillar herniation, or (2) trauma aggravating or exposing an otherwise asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the multifaceted respiratory presentations associated with CMI and advances our knowledge regarding acquired central hypoventilation secondary to structural lesions of the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic ventilation is pivotal for interpreting respiratory dysfunction in CMI. While voluntary respiration, governed by cortical networks, is generally preserved in CMI individuals, automatic ventilation—regulated by brainstem centers and crucial during sleep—is disrupted due to compression of the medullary region by herniated tonsils. In this patient, voluntary breathing persisted; however, automatic ventilatory drive was lost, mirroring the clinical profile of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome but arising from a distinct underlying pathogenesis. The episode at the airport, wherein brief inattentiveness led to significant desaturation in the absence of dyspnoea, powerfully underscores the specific impairment of involuntary respiratory control; in such moments, the patient simply ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that an underlying dysfunction within the brainstem may underlie this patient’s notable lack of dyspnoea during episodes of marked oxygen desaturation. Such a brainstem lesion may interfere with essential ascending neural pathways required to convey sensory input to the cerebral cortex and produce conscious perception. The sensation of breathlessness represents a type of interoceptive awareness, reliant on the transmission and interpretation of afferent information by critical regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If these neural signals are disrupted while traversing the brainstem, the cortex would fail to receive the input necessary to elicit the subjective sensation of air hunger, thus providing a rationale for the patient’s dangerous unawareness of their own respiratory compromise.

The significant gap between the pronounced clinical manifestations in our patient and the modest polysomnographic result (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was performed instead of type I, which does not incorporate EEG monitoring. The lack of EEG precludes accurate measurement of total sleep duration, likely resulting in considerable underreporting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG, the AHI is determined by dividing events by total recording time, encompassing periods when the patient was awake as well as asleep, rather than solely during sleep. Consequently, this methodological shortcoming suggests that the recorded AHI of 6.0 likely undervalues the actual degree of sleep-disordered breathing.

Nevertheless, multiple findings are consistent with central hypoventilation: (1) central events comprise the majority (72%) even though the total AHI remains low; (2) there are recorded episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% without accompanying dyspnea or the patient’s recognition (as occurred both in the airport and during routine assessments); and (3) imaging reveals marked compression of the brainstem with 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils.

A Type I polysomnography that includes comprehensive EEG assessment, end-tidal CO₂ monitoring, and video capture has been scheduled at an external sleep laboratory. However, this evaluation has not yet been performed due to the need to coordinate patient availability, ensure patient consent for the procedure, and arrange scheduling at the off-site center. When the study is ultimately completed, it will yield clearer information regarding sleep stage architecture, breathing patterns during sleep, and will help delineate both the presence and the extent of central hypoventilation.

We recognize that, based on the current data, central hypoventilation should be considered a strong clinical suspicion but has not yet been definitively established as a diagnosis.

In our patient, oropharyngeal dysphagia equally involving solids and liquids, accompanied by choking and coughing episodes, proved to be the most bothersome symptom. Prospective evaluation using established swallowing tests revealed dysfunction in 36% of subjects, with a predominance of neurogenic characteristics. The uniformity of difficulty across all food and liquid consistencies serves to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical etiologies. The patient's most troubling complaint was investigated extensively from gastroenterological and otolaryngological viewpoints, each time yielding unremarkable results; however, her definitive diagnosis was postponed for several years. This lag in recognizing the diagnosis underscores a key clinical point: unexplained dysphagia or episodes of choking, with a normal endoscopic examination, should lead clinicians to consider a neurological basis. In a retrospective review, Chiari malformation I (CMI) was found in 3.3% of children with chronic dysphagia, a figure considerably higher than the estimated prevalence in the general pediatric population (0.1%–0.77%). This indicates that chronic dysphagia may be an important marker for underlying CMI.

The patient’s experience of vertex pain alleviated by hair cutting illustrates a noteworthy yet insufficiently explored presentation. Occipital neuralgia is observed alongside CMI and may result in increased sensitivity of the scalp; the diagnostic requirements include ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia evident during harmless stimulation of the scalp and/or hair.’ Nonetheless, the connection between occipital neuralgia and CMI has infrequently been documented, and definitive evidence for a causal relationship is lacking.

Another possibility is the recently identified ‘burning vertex syndrome’, which is characterized by pain at the scalp vertex, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align with well-defined primary headache types such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headaches. This syndrome is hypothesized to result from dysfunction in small nerve fibers supplying the scalp, and therapeutic responses to greater occipital nerve blocks have been inconsistent. For our patient, the vertex manifestations could be due to: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) the incidental coexistence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) another primary headache disorder distinct from the above. There is a lack of systematic data capturing the presentation of cutaneous allodynia in individuals with CMI, demonstrated by the previous absence of assessment tools before the Chiari Symptom Profile was developed. Because of the limited available evidence, we regard this finding as a notable clinical observation rather than a clear diagnostic marker.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI, particularly when symptoms are uncharacteristic or deceptive—such as central hypoventilation and choking—which resulted in a 14-year period before the correct diagnosis was made. The process of arriving at the proper neurological diagnosis after initial uncertainty is a common motif in published reports.
