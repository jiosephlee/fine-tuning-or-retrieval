Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early forties reported a 7-year history of repeated choking episodes involving both solid foods and liquids, along with frequent nocturnal choking occurring 3–4 times per week, and chronic occipital headaches persisting for 14 years. During home monitoring amid the COVID-19 pandemic, she was found to have marked episodes of oxygen desaturation (reaching 70%–80%) despite a lack of respiratory discomfort. Despite undergoing several laryngoscopic and endoscopic examinations aimed at elucidating the cause of her choking, all results were unremarkable. Retrospective analysis of a cranial CT scan conducted 14 years prior for unrelated head trauma incidentally identified a previously unnoted crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI imaging of the head and neck established the diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm herniation of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0), predominantly featuring central apnoeic events (72%). This report demonstrates that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms that imitate acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the necessity of evaluating for anatomical brain abnormalities in individuals with otherwise unexplained respiratory impairment.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils by a minimum of 5 mm past the foramen magnum as observed on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI can be detected in roughly 1% of adults, but clinical symptoms manifest in only about 0.1% of the overall population. The disorder displays a slight female bias with a ratio of 1.3:1 and most frequently becomes apparent in individuals in their twenties.

The underlying pathophysiology primarily involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, leading to a disproportion between the volume of neural elements and the bony compartment. Nevertheless, recent research has contested the classic view of CMI as inherently pathological, instead suggesting it may exist along a continuum ranging from benign anatomical variants to serious malformations with clinical implications. Volumetric analyses reveal marked demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions that do not consistently align with the presence of symptoms. As a result, current approaches emphasize tailoring clinical decisions to the individual's symptoms, carefully differentiating between incidental anatomical variants and true pathological cases that may warrant operative management.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical expression of CMI, distinguished by a characteristic set of neurological symptoms. The most prominent complaint is a sudden onset occipital headache triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, which occurs in more than 80% of patients. Adults are more likely than children to exhibit pronounced symptoms, a pattern that is especially apparent when syringomyelia coexists. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is observed in approximately 15%–26% of individuals, usually manifesting as oropharyngeal disturbances, including palatal weakness, tongue muscle wasting, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary complications are now understood to be a significant aspect of CMI. In a prospective analysis involving 90 adult CMI patients, half were identified as having sleep-related respiratory disorders. Among these, 6.7% exhibited a central apnoea index of at least 5, but only a single individual had purely central sleep apnoea, which may be attributable to brainstem compression. The spectrum of respiratory dysfunction associated with CMI is not limited to abnormalities during sleep; reports in the literature include case series describing episodes of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia among those affected by CMI.

Respiratory impairment in this context arises due to factors such as compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and interference with the neural centres responsible for respiratory regulation, resulting in episodic disturbances in breathing. These manifestations are characteristic of an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which differs from the congenital form linked to mutations in PHOX2B. Contemporary recommendations highlight this association by advising that patients with CMI and clinical suspicion of sleep-related breathing abnormalities undergo standard polysomnographic evaluation. Notably, surgical intervention via posterior fossa decompression may re-establish normal CSF circulation and ameliorate respiratory disturbances, highlighting the critical need to identify CMI in cases of otherwise unexplained respiratory compromise.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s attended our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year duration of recurrent, unpredictable choking episodes during both eating and sleeping, affecting the ingestion of solids and liquids alike. She characterized these events as abrupt and distressing, frequently disrupting her meals or waking her from sleep with intense coughing and gasping, although she had never experienced a total blockage of her airway. These nocturnal incidents typically occurred three to four times per week and sometimes happened twice in a single night. Throughout this period, she underwent a comprehensive workup, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Nevertheless, her symptoms persisted in the absence of any explanatory findings. The repercussions for her social life were profound: she began to shun restaurants and social gatherings out of fear of choking publicly. As a consequence, her mounting anxiety and shame contributed to escalating social isolation and emotional hardship.

A comprehensive review of her medical history indicated that the onset of her symptoms traced back 14 years, subsequent to a minor car accident in which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after the incident, she began experiencing ongoing headaches located in the occipital and vertex regions. These headaches were characterized as unremitting, pressure-like sensations, typically confined to a coin-sized region at the vertex. Importantly, her headaches did not worsen with actions such as coughing, straining, or performing other Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple clinicians concluded she was suffering from nummular headache, leading to several localized scalp injections; however, these interventions provided only temporary improvement. Notably, she exhibited pronounced allodynia at the vertex—so much so that even the sensation of her hair resting on this area provoked pain. Ultimately, she found that keeping her hair short diminished her baseline pain level from 6/10 to 3/10, though this choice conflicted with societal norms and generated psychological discomfort.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient initiated regular self-assessment of her oxygen saturation with a home pulse oximeter. Across a span of several months, she consistently recorded asymptomatic declines in oxygen saturation levels, reaching the 70%–80% range. Frequently, these low readings went unnoticed by her until she measured them with the device. Despite numerous visits to emergency services, clinicians commonly ascribed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly developed severe dizziness and nausea. Medical staff present measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prevented from boarding. The introduction of supplemental oxygen brought about rapid and full alleviation of her symptoms. Further assessment by a pulmonologist, which encompassed both chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed no abnormalities.

Considering the coexistence of persistent occipital and vertex headaches, recurring episodes of choking, and unexplained hypoxia, efforts were made to identify a single neurological disorder that could account for all symptoms. Upon examination of her historical medical documentation, a non-contrast head CT scan—conducted 14 years prior in the aftermath of her accident—was identified. Although the initial interpretation of the scan was normal, a subsequent meticulous reassessment demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum as well as obliteration of the cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the presence of choking symptoms, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was originally suspected. However, this diagnosis was ruled out following normal findings from pH monitoring and endoscopic examinations, as well as the absence of improvement with maximal acid suppression therapy.

Evaluation for myasthenia gravis was prompted by bulbar symptoms. However, the diagnosis was deemed improbable because antibody tests were negative, there was no evidence of fatigability, and symptoms did not fluctuate throughout the day.

After episodes of hypoxia, thorough assessment for primary pulmonary pathology was performed. Imaging of the chest and results from pulmonary function tests were both unremarkable, effectively ruling out intrinsic disease of the lungs.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by the maintenance of voluntary respiratory effort, whereas the automatic regulation of breathing was compromised, especially during periods of inattention or sleep. The onset after head trauma differentiated this acquired variant from congenital presentations.

Compression of the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei caused by brainstem pathology secondary to CMI accounted for all presenting symptoms, thereby establishing a cohesive diagnosis.

Discussion:

It is important to closely examine the timing between the patient’s motor vehicle accident and the emergence of symptoms. Chiari malformation type I may present congenitally, but has also been observed following head trauma or neurological insult. Current aetiological frameworks2022 acknowledge this range by differentiating primary, congenital Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that arise due to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, a CT scan taken right after the accident revealed that the foramen magnum was crowded. Since we lack imaging prior to the trauma, it is unclear whether this feature was a result of the injury or if the trauma simply exposed a pre-existing condition. The acute appearance of occipital headaches directly after the incident, with gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory symptoms spanning 14 years, may indicate either: (1) that the trauma precipitated tonsillar herniation, or (2) that the trauma worsened or brought to light an otherwise asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory complications associated with CMI and contributes to a more nuanced understanding of acquired central hypoventilation resulting from structural lesions in the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiratory processes is essential when considering the pathophysiology of respiratory dysfunction in CMI. Voluntary breathing, which is governed by cortical circuits, is often preserved in individuals with CMI. By contrast, the capacity for automatic respiration, which depends on brainstem centers and is particularly critical during sleep, is often impaired due to compression of medullary regions by herniated tonsils. In the current case, the patient demonstrated intact voluntary respiratory function but an absence of spontaneous automatic breathing, a clinical picture analogous to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome but caused by a distinct pathophysiological basis. The incident at the airport—where a brief lapse in attention led to significant hypoxemia without any perception of dyspnoea—clearly exemplifies this selective disruption of automatic respiratory regulation, wherein the patient quite literally ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the striking lack of dyspnoea observed in the patient during marked episodes of oxygen desaturation can be attributed to an underlying brainstem dysfunction. Such a lesion within the brainstem likely impairs essential ascending afferent neural pathways responsible for transmitting information to the cortex, where conscious awareness arises. The sensation of breathlessness, as an aspect of interoception, relies upon the integration of these afferent signals in critical brain structures, notably the anterior insular cortex. If the transmission of these signals is interrupted at the level of the brainstem, the cortical regions necessary for producing the perception of air hunger would be deprived of vital input. Consequently, this disruption accounts for the patient’s dangerously reduced awareness of their compromised physiological state.

The notable difference between the pronounced clinical symptoms in our patient and the modest polysomnographic findings (AHI 6.0) requires further clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was utilized instead of the gold-standard type I study, resulting in the lack of EEG data. This omission made it impossible to precisely determine total sleep duration, almost certainly contributing to a substantial underreporting of respiratory events. Since EEG was not available, AHI was calculated based on the total duration of monitoring rather than actual periods of sleep, inevitably including time spent awake. As a result, the AHI value of 6.0 probably represents a significant underestimation of the actual degree of sleep-disordered breathing in this case.

Although this limitation exists, multiple findings point toward central hypoventilation: (1) central events constituted the majority (72%) even though the overall AHI was low; (2) oxygen saturation dropped to 70\%–80\% in the absence of dyspnoea or subjective awareness by the patient (both at the airport and in typical clinical settings); and (3) structural imaging revealed marked brainstem compression, evidenced by 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography—including full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation—has been scheduled at an external sleep centre. However, conducting this assessment is contingent upon aligning the patient’s availability, obtaining their consent for the procedure, and organizing a suitable time at the off-site facility. Upon completion, this evaluation will yield more precise insights into the patient's sleep architecture, respiratory activity, and the existence and extent of central hypoventilation.

It is important to note that, based on the data currently available, central hypoventilation is best considered a strongly supported clinical suspicion rather than an unequivocally established diagnosis.

In our patient, oropharyngeal dysphagia—characterized by equivalent difficulty swallowing both solids and liquids and accompanied by choking and coughing—proved to be the most troubling symptom. When systematically assessed with validated tools, swallowing dysfunction was identified in 36% of cases, with neurogenic dysphagia being most prevalent. Unlike mechanical dysphagia, neurogenic types present with similar impairment across all food consistencies. Despite the significance of this symptom, the patient initially underwent thorough investigations by both gastroenterology and otolaryngology, all yielding unremarkable results, which resulted in a diagnostic delay spanning several years. This experience underscores a vital clinical point: in cases of dysphagia or choking where endoscopic evaluation reveals no structural abnormalities, clinicians should consider a neurological cause. In a retrospective study, the frequency of CMI among children suffering from persistent dysphagia was 3.3%, notably exceeding the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence seen in the general pediatric population. These findings indicate that ongoing dysphagia may serve as an important clinical clue for underlying CMI.

The description of pain localized to the vertex and its alleviation following hair cutting highlights a notable yet insufficiently researched clinical observation. While occipital neuralgia is known to be associated with CMI and may lead to hypersensitivity of the scalp—diagnosis hinges on the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia triggered by harmless stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’—there is limited literature documenting this relationship. Furthermore, current evidence does not substantiate a definitive causal connection between occipital neuralgia and CMI.

Another possibility to consider is the newly identified ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not conform precisely to recognized categories of primary headaches such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology is thought to involve small fibre nerve pathways within the scalp, and patients exhibit varying degrees of response to greater occipital nerve blockade. In the case described, the vertex-related symptoms might be explained by: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) an independent occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) an unrelated primary headache disorder. There remains a notable deficit in the systematic recording of cutaneous allodynia patterns among those with CMI, illustrated by the lack of validated tools to measure these particular symptoms before the Chiari Symptom Profile was established. Given the limited available evidence, we report this finding as a noteworthy clinical observation, not as a definitive criterion for diagnosis.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties encountered in diagnosing CMI, particularly when the clinical presentation is dominated by unusual or deceptive manifestations such as central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, resulting in a protracted diagnostic delay of 14 years. The process of diagnosis, marked by early misunderstandings and an eventual slow progression toward accurate neurological identification, frequently emerges in previous reports.
