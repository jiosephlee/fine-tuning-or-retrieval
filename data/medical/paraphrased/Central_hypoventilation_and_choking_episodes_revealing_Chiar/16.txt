Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early 40s reported a 7-year history of recurrent episodes of choking involving both solids and liquids, in addition to nocturnal choking that occurred 3 to 4 times per week and persistent occipital headaches over 14 years. While monitoring at home during the COVID-19 pandemic, she was found to have significant episodes of oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) without any signs of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies were conducted to assess the choking, all yielding normal results. Upon reevaluation, a head CT scan obtained 14 years earlier for unrelated trauma showed a previously missed crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck identified Chiari malformation type I (CMI), with cerebellar tonsillar herniation measuring 11 mm. A sleep study revealed mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0 and mainly central respiratory events (72%). This case highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms similar to those seen in acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underlining the need to assess for structural central nervous system abnormalities in patients with unexplained respiratory compromise.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending 5 mm or more beneath the foramen magnum as visualized on sagittal MRI scans. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI is present in 1% of adults, although only about 0.1% of people develop symptoms. There is a slight female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, and symptoms most often arise in individuals in their twenties.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, which leads to a disproportion between the neural tissue and the confined bony compartment. Nonetheless, current research questions the classical view of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) as inherently pathological, suggesting it exists along a continuum ranging from benign anatomical differences to conditions with clinical implications. Recent studies employing volumetric analysis reveal notable differences in posterior fossa size across various demographics, with no clear link to clinical manifestations. This evolving perspective emphasizes the need for personalized, symptom-oriented management, carefully differentiating incidental anatomical variants from true pathologic forms that warrant surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the symptomatic presentation of CMI and is identified by a unique set of neurological signs. The hallmark complaint is a sudden occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, observed in more than 80% of individuals. Symptom severity tends to be greater in adults compared to pediatric patients, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in 15% to 26% of cases and most commonly manifests as oropharyngeal impairment, including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary involvement is now acknowledged as a growing complication associated with CMI. In a prospective cohort involving 90 adults diagnosed with CMI, half exhibited sleep-disordered breathing. Among these, 6.7% demonstrated a central apnea index of at least 5, but isolated central sleep apnea was identified in just one patient, possibly due to compression at the brainstem. These respiratory abnormalities are not limited to nocturnal symptoms; one case series also reported instances of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia presenting in individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in this context is attributed to brainstem compression, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and interference with respiratory regulatory centers, resulting in episodic breathing abnormalities. These manifestations constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—commonly referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which differs from the congenital form linked to PHOX2B mutations. Current guidelines highlight this association by advising the routine use of polysomnography in patients with CMI who are suspected of having sleep-related breathing disorders. Notably, posterior fossa decompression can reestablish normal CSF circulation and may alleviate respiratory issues, highlighting the necessity for CMI consideration in cases of unexplained respiratory failure.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early forties came to our family medicine clinic reporting a seven-year history of recurrent, unpredictable choking incidents during both eating and sleep, affecting ingestion of solids and liquids to the same extent. She characterized these events as abrupt and distressing, frequently disrupting her meals or awakening her from sleep with intense coughing and gasping, though her airway was never completely blocked. Nocturnal episodes occurred three to four times weekly, with some nights involving two occurrences. Despite multiple assessments over several years—including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all of which yielded normal findings—her symptoms remained unresolved. The repercussions on her social life were substantial; she refrained from dining out or attending social events due to concerns about experiencing a choking episode in public. This ongoing anxiety and embarrassment contributed to progressively worsening social isolation and psychological distress.

A more comprehensive assessment of her history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, immediately following a minor car accident during which she experienced a brief loss of consciousness. Soon thereafter, she began to experience unremitting headaches located in the occipital region and at the vertex. She described these headaches as continuous, with a sensation of pressure, frequently confined to a small, coin-sized spot at the vertex. These headaches were not intensified by actions such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. She received a diagnosis of nummular headache from several clinicians, and was treated with numerous localized scalp injections, each affording only short-lived alleviation. The most notable clinical sign was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, to the extent that even her hair's weight provoked discomfort. Ultimately, she found that cutting her hair short decreased her baseline pain from 6 out of 10 to 3 out of 10, although this choice conflicted with her cultural norms and caused her psychological discomfort.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started checking her oxygen levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Over the course of several months, she consistently recorded asymptomatic drops in oxygen saturation to values between 70% and 80%. She frequently remained unaware of these episodes until using her device. Several emergency assessments concluded that her symptoms were related to anxiety.

A significant event took place at a foreign airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly developed severe dizziness accompanied by nausea. Airport medical staff measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from boarding. After being given supplemental oxygen, her symptoms abated entirely within a few minutes. Follow-up assessment of her lungs, which included imaging studies and pulmonary function tests, revealed normal results.

In light of her longstanding occipital and vertex headaches, along with recurring choking episodes and unexplained hypoxemia, clinicians sought a single neurological explanation for her symptoms. Review of her historical records uncovered a non-contrast head CT performed 14 years prior, post-trauma, which had originally been interpreted as normal. Upon thorough re-examination, however, the scan demonstrated crowding at the foramen magnum and diminished cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. These radiological features pointed towards Chiari malformation type I (CMI), which had not been recognized at the time. The timeline of her clinical course is outlined in Table 1.

Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

An initial suspicion was raised for gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux due to the presence of choking episodes. However, this was ruled out after pH monitoring and endoscopic examinations returned normal results, and there was no improvement despite maximal acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was evaluated due to symptoms affecting the bulbar region. Negative antibody testing, lack of fatigability and absence of diurnal variation made this unlikely.

Primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated following hypoxic episodes. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests excluded intrinsic lung disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome, also known as acquired Ondine’s curse, was indicated by intact voluntary respiration but deficient automatic breathing, particularly noted during sleep or when the patient was distracted. Its onset after head injury differentiated it from the congenital type.

Brainstem pathology secondary to CMI ultimately explained all symptoms through compression of the respiratory centres and lower cranial nerve nuclei, providing the unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The close timing of our patient's motor vehicle collision and the emergence of symptoms requires thoughtful evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is recognized not only as a congenital disorder but also as a possible outcome of head injuries or other neurological insults. Current etiological classifications2022 acknowledge this range, differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that develop following trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained directly after the injury showed narrowing at the foramen magnum. As there is no imaging available from before the trauma, it remains unclear if the accident led to this abnormality or simply exposed a pre-existing condition. The rapid development of occipital headaches immediately following the incident, with bulbar and respiratory symptoms gradually emerging over a 14-year period, may indicate either: (1) herniation of the tonsils precipitated by the injury, or (2) the trauma worsening or disclosing a previously silent congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory complications seen in CMI, contributing to our comprehension of how structural abnormalities in the brainstem can result in acquired central hypoventilation. Differentiating voluntary from automatic respiration is key to understanding the respiratory pathophysiology associated with CMI. Voluntary breathing, which depends on cortical pathways, is generally preserved in individuals with CMI. However, the automatic respiratory control, which relies on brainstem centers and is vital during sleep, is impaired when the tonsils compress medullary regions. In this patient, while the ability to ventilate voluntarily remained, the automatic respiratory drive was lost, mirroring the clinical presentation of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome but stemming from a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, during which a brief period of inattention caused significant oxygen desaturation without subjective dyspnea, starkly demonstrates this selective loss of automatic respiration—where the patient effectively "forgets to breathe."

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea despite marked episodes of hypoxaemia can be attributed to an underlying primary dysfunction of the brainstem. Such brainstem pathology likely disrupts essential afferent neural pathways that ascend toward the cortex, which are needed for the conscious perception of internal sensations. Breathlessness, a form of interoception, relies on these afferent inputs being relayed to and processed by specific brain areas, such as the anterior insular cortex. When these signals are interrupted as they travel through the brainstem, the cortex would be deprived of the necessary sensory information to evoke a sensation of breathlessness, accounting for the patient’s hazardous lack of awareness regarding their physiological compromise.

The pronounced difference between the patient’s significant clinical symptoms and the modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. For logistical reasons, we employed a type III polysomnography instead of a type I study; type III studies do not incorporate EEG monitoring. Consequently, we were unable to accurately determine total sleep time, which likely resulted in a substantial undercount of respiratory events. In the absence of EEG, AHI is calculated based on total recording time rather than actual time spent asleep, since wake periods are included. As a result, this methodological limitation suggests that the AHI of 6.0 almost certainly understates the true degree of sleep-disordered breathing present.

Nevertheless, certain findings are indicative of central hypoventilation: (1) central events accounted for 72% of apneas and hypopneas, even though the total AHI was low; (2) observed episodes of oxygen desaturation to 70%–80% occurred without symptoms such as dyspnoea or the patient's awareness, both at the airport and during standard evaluations; and (3) imaging revealed marked brainstem compression, with the tonsils descended by 11 mm.

A comprehensive sleep study (Type I polysomnography) incorporating full EEG monitoring, end-tidal CO₂ measurement, and video surveillance has been scheduled at an external sleep center. The examination is yet to be performed, as it depends on aligning the patient’s schedule, securing their consent for the procedure, and organizing a suitable appointment at the distant location. Upon completion, this study will yield a more precise assessment of sleep structure, breathing patterns, and help delineate both the existence and the degree of central hypoventilation.

We acknowledge that, given the current evidence, central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis, rather than a definitively confirmed diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, impacting both solid and liquid intake to a similar degree and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, represented the patient’s most troubling complaint. Prospective assessment with standardized swallowing evaluation tools identified swallowing abnormalities in 36% of patients, with neurogenic etiologies being most common. Equal impairment with all types of food consistency is a distinguishing feature of neurogenic as opposed to mechanical dysphagia. The symptom that caused the patient the most discomfort underwent repeated investigation by both gastroenterology and otolaryngology specialists, with results consistently unremarkable; despite this, her diagnosis was not established for several years. This prolonged interval before correct diagnosis emphasizes a key clinical point: in cases where dysphagia or choking occurs without evidence of structural lesions on endoscopy, clinicians should contemplate neurological causes. In a retrospective study of pediatric patients with unremitting dysphagia, the prevalence of CMI was 3.3%, notably greater than the reported prevalence of 0.1%–0.77% in the general pediatric population. This finding implies that longstanding dysphagia may serve as a significant clinical clue for underlying CMI.

The patient’s experience of vertex pain alleviated by hair cutting illustrates a notable, yet insufficiently explored, clinical observation. Occipital neuralgia has been documented in relation to CMI and may lead to increased sensitivity of the scalp; diagnostic guidelines specify the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Despite this, reports describing occipital neuralgia in conjunction with CMI are uncommon, and definitive evidence supporting a causal relationship is lacking.

Another possibility to consider is the recently identified ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp discomfort, skin allodynia, and clinical features that do not align precisely with recognized primary headache categories such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. It is postulated that the underlying mechanism may involve small fiber nerve pathways within the scalp, and affected individuals demonstrate varied responses to greater occipital nerve block procedures. In the case described, the vertex-related symptoms could signify: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) burning vertex syndrome occurring independently; or (3) a distinct primary headache syndrome. There is a notable deficiency in systematic reporting of cutaneous allodynia in CMI cohorts, highlighted by the prior lack of tools for symptom assessment in these patients until the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In light of the limited available data, we report this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than as an established diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties encountered in diagnosing CMI when it manifests through unusual or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a diagnostic delay of 14 years. Such a protracted and uncertain diagnostic course, marked by early misdirection and eventual identification of the neurological etiology, is frequently described in existing literature.
