Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s was evaluated for a 7-year history of recurring episodes of choking that occurred with both solids and liquids, alongside nocturnal choking events that happened three to four times per week. She also had longstanding occipital headaches that had persisted for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, she used home oxygen monitoring and found severe desaturation episodes (oxygen levels dropping to 70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory symptoms. Multiple laryngoscopy and endoscopy procedures conducted to assess her choking spells yielded normal results. Upon re-examination of a head CT scan from 14 years ago—originally taken for unrelated trauma—a crowded foramen magnum was identified. Subsequent MRI imaging of the head and neck demonstrated Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with cerebellar tonsils herniating 11 mm below the foramen magnum. Polysomnography detected mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, predominantly characterized by central events (72%). This case highlights how CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the necessity to consider structural brain abnormalities in patients who present with unexplained respiratory compromise.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward movement of the cerebellar tonsils measuring 5 mm or more beneath the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI scans. Modern imaging data indicate that CMI is identified in 1% of adults, but only around 0.1% of the population manifests clinical symptoms. The disorder shows a slight female predominance with a ratio of 1.3:1 and most frequently arises in individuals during their thirties.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportionate relationship between neural elements and the space available. Yet, current research disputes the conventional view of Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI) as inherently abnormal, suggesting it exists along a continuum that includes both benign anatomical variations and clinically important deformities. Volumetric analyses in recent studies reveal significant differences in posterior fossa size across populations, often without a clear association with symptoms. This evolving perspective underscores the need for a personalized, symptom-oriented approach to clinical management, with a focus on separating asymptomatic structural variants from disease processes that warrant surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome is the clinical expression of CMI, marked by a recognizable pattern of neurological symptoms. Its primary feature is episodic occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of individuals. Symptom severity tends to be greater in adults than in pediatric patients, particularly when syringomyelia coexists. Lower cranial nerve involvement affects 15%–26% of cases, most frequently manifesting as oropharyngeal disturbances including palatal paresis, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary symptoms are now acknowledged as a growing complication in patients with CMI. In a prospective analysis involving 90 adults diagnosed with CMI, sleep-disordered breathing was present in half of the cohort. A central apnoea index of 5 or higher was identified in 6.7% of subjects, though true central sleep apnoea was seen in just one individual, likely attributable to compression of the brainstem. The respiratory impairment is not limited to disturbances during sleep; a case series reported episodes of acute respiratory insufficiency and severe hypoxaemia among individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment arises from factors such as brainstem compression, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and disturbances affecting respiratory regulatory centers, which together produce episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome, occasionally referred to as 'Ondine’s curse', and are differentiated from the congenital form associated with PHOX2B gene mutations. Contemporary guidelines highlight this association by advocating for routine polysomnography in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who exhibit signs of possible sleep-disordered breathing. Notably, decompression of the posterior fossa can re-establish normal CSF circulation and may resolve respiratory symptoms, reinforcing the need to consider CMI in patients presenting with unexplained respiratory issues.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early 40s presented to our family medicine practice reporting a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes that occurred unexpectedly during both meals and sleep, involving both solid foods and liquids without preference. She characterized these events as abrupt and highly distressing, frequently disrupting meal times or causing awakening from sleep accompanied by intense coughing and gasping, though she never experienced total airway blockage. Episodes during the night happened three or four times weekly, with occasional occurrences of two episodes in a single night. Over the years, she underwent thorough evaluation, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopic procedures, all yielding normal results. Nevertheless, her symptoms continued unabated. The impact on her social life was substantial; she avoided eating at restaurants and participating in social gatherings for fear of experiencing a choking episode in public. This persistent apprehension and embarrassment contributed to increasing isolation and emotional difficulty.

A more comprehensive evaluation of her medical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, subsequent to a minor automobile accident during which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after, she started to experience continuous headaches in the occipital region and at the vertex. The headaches were characterized as constant, with a sensation of pressure, frequently confined to a coin-sized area atop the vertex. The pain did not intensify with coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. She received diagnoses of nummular headache from various physicians and was treated with several local injections to the scalp, which provided only short-lived relief. The most notable symptom was prominent allodynia at the vertex; even the weight of her hair caused discomfort. Ultimately, she learned that keeping her hair short lessened her usual pain intensity from 6/10 to 3/10, although this measure conflicted with her cultural norms and resulted in emotional hardship.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started routinely checking her oxygen levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Over a period of months, she recorded several instances where her oxygen saturation decreased to between 70% and 80%, despite being asymptomatic. She generally became aware of these episodes only when reviewing the measurements from her device. Several emergency department visits resulted in her symptoms being attributed to anxiety.

A significant event transpired at an overseas airport. As she awaited her departure, the patient developed sudden-onset vertigo and nausea. Medical staff present at the location recorded an oxygen saturation of 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from boarding her flight. Her symptoms resolved entirely within minutes following the initiation of supplemental oxygen. Later assessment by a pulmonologist, including chest imaging studies and pulmonary function tests, yielded unremarkable results.

Considering her persistent occipital and vertex headaches, the progressive nature of her choking episodes, and episodes of unexplained hypoxia, the medical team sought a neurologic explanation that could account for all these findings. A review of her prior medical history uncovered a non-contrast head CT obtained 14 years prior, soon after her injury, which had originally been interpreted as normal. Upon thorough reassessment, however, this scan demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and absent cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Given the presence of choking episodes, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was initially suspected. However, this diagnosis was ruled out due to normal findings on pH monitoring, unremarkable endoscopic examinations, and absence of symptom improvement with maximum acid suppression therapy.

An assessment for myasthenia gravis was undertaken because the patient exhibited bulbar symptoms. However, negative results on antibody tests, no evidence of fatigability, and no diurnal fluctuation in symptoms argued against this diagnosis.

After experiencing episodes of hypoxia, a thorough workup for primary pulmonary disease was performed. Both chest imaging and pulmonary function testing returned normal findings, thereby ruling out inherent lung disorders.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly suspected due to maintained voluntary respiration alongside deficient involuntary breathing during periods of distraction or sleep. Its onset after head trauma differentiated it from the congenital variant.

The brainstem abnormalities resulting from CMI accounted for the entire symptom constellation by exerting pressure on both the respiratory centers and nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby establishing a comprehensive diagnosis.

Discussion:

It is important to closely examine the timing of our patient’s motor vehicle accident relative to when symptoms first appeared. Chiari malformation type I is recognized in the literature as a disorder that may be present from birth or may develop following head injuries and other neurologic insults. Recent etiological frameworks (2022) reflect this continuum, differentiating between primary Chiari malformations—which are congenital—and secondary, or acquired variants, which may arise due to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained shortly after the injury showed a crowded foramen magnum. As there are no imaging studies predating the trauma, it remains unclear if this appearance is a consequence of the trauma or if the incident uncovered a pre-existing condition. The abrupt emergence of occipital headache following the accident, with gradual progression to bulbar and respiratory complaints across 14 years, may indicate either (1) tonsillar herniation precipitated by trauma, or (2) trauma accentuating or exposing a congenital variant that had been asymptomatic until that point.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory complications associated with CMI, and contributes to knowledge regarding the acquired central hypoventilation observed in individuals with brainstem structural abnormalities. Differentiation between voluntary and automatic respiration is fundamental for elucidating the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. While voluntary breathing—which depends on cortical networks—generally remains unaffected in these patients, automatic respiration—regulated by brainstem centers and critical during sleep—may be impaired owing to compression of medullary regions by the cerebellar tonsils. In this case, the patient retained the capacity for voluntary ventilation but lost the innate drive for automatic respiration, mirroring the presentation seen in congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit with a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, in which the patient’s brief inattention led to severe oxygen desaturation in the absence of dyspnoea, underscores this selective disruption of automatic respiratory control—the patient effectively “forgets to breathe”.

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea in the presence of marked oxygen desaturation is due to an underlying dysfunction within the brainstem. This presumed pathology may interfere with essential afferent neural circuits responsible for transmitting signals to the cortex required for conscious perception. The sensation of breathlessness represents an aspect of interoception and relies on processing these signals in principal brain areas such as the anterior insular cortex. If these pathways are disrupted within the brainstem, the necessary sensory input would fail to reach the cortex, resulting in an absence of the perception of air hunger and thereby accounting for the patient’s failure to recognize their severe physiological compromise.

The significant mismatch observed between the patient’s pronounced clinical symptoms and the relatively low AHI value of 6.0 on polysomnography requires clarification. In this case, practical constraints led us to employ a type III sleep study instead of the gold-standard type I polysomnography, resulting in the absence of EEG measurements. Without EEG data, we were unable to precisely determine actual sleep duration, which most likely caused notable underreporting of respiratory disturbances. The calculation of AHI in this context utilizes total recording time, encompassing both wakefulness and sleep, rather than exclusively the period during which the patient was asleep. As a consequence, this methodological shortcoming suggests that the measured AHI of 6.0 considerably underrepresents the real severity of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Although this limitation exists, multiple findings are indicative of central hypoventilation: (1) central events accounted for 72% of respiratory disturbances, even though the overall AHI remained low; (2) oxygen saturations dropped to 70%–80% in the absence of dyspnea or the patient's recognition (noted at the airport and during standard evaluations); and (3) imaging revealed substantial brainstem compression, demonstrated by 11 mm of tonsillar herniation.

Comprehensive polysomnography (Type I), incorporating full EEG evaluation, end-tidal CO2 monitoring, and video documentation, has been planned at a separate sleep clinic. The study’s completion is awaiting alignment of the patient’s schedule, agreement to undergo the testing, and available appointment times at the offsite centre. Upon successful acquisition, the study is expected to offer definitive insights into sleep structure, ventilatory dynamics, and to elucidate both the presence and extent of central hypoventilation.

We recognize that, based on existing data, central hypoventilation remains a strongly substantiated clinical suspicion rather than an unequivocally established diagnosis.

The patient’s most troublesome symptom was oropharyngeal dysphagia involving both solids and liquids equally, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. Upon prospective assessment using established swallowing evaluation tools, dysfunction was observed in 36% of cases, with neurogenic causes being most common. The presence of comparable difficulty across different textures helps differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical types. Initial and repeated investigations by gastroenterology and otolaryngology failed to reveal abnormalities, which contributed to a diagnosis delayed by several years. This emphasizes a significant clinical point: when dysphagia or choking occurs in the absence of identifiable structural abnormalities on endoscopic examination, clinicians should suspect a neurological etiology. In a retrospective review of children with chronic dysphagia, the frequency of CMI was found to be 3.3%, markedly exceeding the general paediatric prevalence range of 0.1%–0.77%. These findings indicate that ongoing dysphagia may be an important clinical clue for underlying CMI.

The patient described experiencing pain localized to the vertex of the scalp, which improved following haircutting. This represents a unique occurrence that has received limited attention in the literature. Occipital neuralgia has been identified in connection with CMI and may lead to increased scalp sensitivity; diagnostic guidelines specify ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia present during non-noxious stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Nevertheless, reports documenting occipital neuralgia in patients with CMI are infrequent, and evidence does not definitively support a causal relationship.

A further possibility is the newly recognized ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by pain in the scalp, skin sensitivity to touch (cutaneous allodynia), and clinical features that do not align with established primary headache types like migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology may involve small-fiber sensory pathways originating from the scalp, and reported cases have demonstrated variable responses to greater occipital nerve blockade. In this patient, symptoms involving the vertex could reflect: (1) occipital neuralgia in association with CMI; (2) the simultaneous presence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache entity. There remains a notable deficiency in systematic reporting of cutaneous allodynia distributions among individuals with CMI, which is highlighted by the previous lack of tools to document such symptoms prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Owing to the limited available data, we report this manifestation as an intriguing clinical finding rather than a pathognomonic sign.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI when it manifests with unusual or confusing clinical signs like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a prolonged 14-year period before correct identification. The process of reaching the right neurological diagnosis, initially marked by misinterpretation and eventual clarification, is consistently documented in previous reports.
