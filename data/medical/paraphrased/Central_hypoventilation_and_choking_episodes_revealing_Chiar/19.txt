Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s presented with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, accompanied by nocturnal choking episodes 3 to 4 times per week and chronic occipital headaches persisting for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, incidental home monitoring detected episodes of marked oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Despite multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies for her choking symptoms, findings were unremarkable. Retrospective review of a head CT performed 14 years prior for unrelated trauma identified a previously missed crowded foramen magnum. MRI imaging of the head and cervical spine confirmed Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with a cerebellar tonsillar descent measuring 11 mm. Polysomnography indicated mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, predominantly featuring central events (72%). This report highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory disturbances resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the necessity of evaluating for structural brain lesions in patients with unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized on sagittal MRI by the inferior migration of the cerebellar tonsils measuring 5 mm or more beneath the foramen magnum. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI is present in roughly 1% of adults, while symptomatic cases are observed in about 0.1% of the population. There is a slight preponderance among females, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, and most patients present during their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between neural elements and cranial space. Nonetheless, current research contests the longstanding view of CMI as inherently disease-causing, suggesting it may represent a continuum ranging from benign anatomical differences to clinically relevant malformations. Emerging volumetric analyses indicate marked differences in posterior fossa sizes across populations, often without reliable association with symptoms. This paradigm shift demands tailored clinical approaches that prioritize patient symptoms, enabling clinicians to differentiate between asymptomatic variants and those cases necessitating operative management.

Chiari syndrome is the symptomatic presentation of CMI, distinguished by a specific set of neurological signs. The primary symptom is intermittent headache in the occipital region, which is frequently triggered by Valsalva maneuvers and occurs in more than 80% of individuals. Adults generally exhibit more pronounced symptoms than pediatric patients, particularly in cases associated with syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is noted in 15%–26% of cases, often manifesting as dysfunction in the oropharynx, including weakness of the palate, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are being acknowledged with greater frequency as a complication associated with CMI. A prospective investigation involving 90 adult patients with CMI revealed that half experienced sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. Among these, a central apnoea index of 5 or higher was identified in 6.7% of cases, though only a single instance of isolated central sleep apnoea was recorded, which may be indicative of brainstem compression. The scope of respiratory involvement is not limited to sleep disorders; a series of cases has demonstrated episodes of acute respiratory insufficiency and severe hypoxaemia in individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation, and interruption of neural pathways controlling respiration, which in turn produces episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome, often referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’, and are clinically distinct from the congenital central hypoventilation syndrome associated with PHOX2B genetic mutations. Recent guidelines highlight this association by recommending that CMI patients presenting with possible sleep-disordered breathing undergo routine polysomnography. Notably, decompression of the posterior fossa can restore normal CSF dynamics and may alleviate respiratory complaints, accentuating the need to consider CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early 40s came to our family medicine clinic with a 7-year history of recurring choking events that happened unpredictably during both eating and sleep, affecting ingestion of both solids and liquids equally. She explained that the episodes were abrupt and alarming, frequently disturbing meals or awakening her at night with intense coughing and gasping. However, she noted that her airway was never fully obstructed. Nocturnal episodes occurred three to four times weekly, with some nights experiencing two incidents. Throughout these years, she had been thoroughly investigated, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Although no cause had been identified, her symptoms continued. The impact on her social life was considerable; she avoided restaurants and social events for fear of experiencing a choking episode in public. This persistent anxiety and embarrassment contributed to growing social isolation and emotional strain.

A thorough review of her medical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, following a minor car accident during which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon thereafter, she experienced ongoing headaches in the occipital region and at the vertex. She characterized these headaches as continuous, with a pressing sensation, frequently confined to a coin-sized area at the vertex. The headaches were unaffected by coughing, physical straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Several clinicians diagnosed nummular headache, and she received numerous localized injections in the scalp, which provided only short-lived relief. Notably, she exhibited significant allodynia at the vertex, so much so that even the weight of her hair elicited discomfort. Eventually, she found that shortening her hair lessened her baseline pain from a 6/10 to a 3/10; however, this choice conflicted with cultural norms and resulted in psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient used a personal pulse oximeter to track her oxygen levels at home. For several months, she observed frequent, asymptomatic declines in oxygen saturation, reaching the 70%–80% range. These instances were typically not noticed until she measured her saturation. Several emergency assessments concluded that her symptoms were due to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly developed severe dizziness and nausea. Airport medical staff recorded an oxygen saturation of 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from boarding. Her symptoms rapidly resolved following the administration of supplemental oxygen. Later pulmonary assessments, which included chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed normal results.

In light of her persistent occipital and vertex headaches, increasing episodes of choking, and otherwise unexplained hypoxemia, clinicians sought a comprehensive neurological explanation. Review of her past medical history uncovered a non-contrast head CT performed 14 years prior, post-injury, which was initially interpreted as normal. Upon closer reassessment, however, the scan demonstrated crowding at the foramen magnum and obliteration of cerebrospinal fluid spaces at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was suspected at first due to the presence of choking. However, the diagnosis was ruled out because pH monitoring yielded normal results, endoscopic examinations were unremarkable, and the patient did not improve with maximal acid suppression therapy.

An assessment for myasthenia gravis was conducted because of bulbar-related manifestations. The diagnosis was considered improbable given negative antibody results, no evidence of fatigability, and the absence of diurnal symptom fluctuation.

A thorough evaluation for primary lung disorders was undertaken after incidents of hypoxia. Imaging of the chest and pulmonary function measurements were within normal parameters, effectively ruling out intrinsic pulmonary disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (also known as acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by intact voluntary respiration alongside deficient automatic respiratory regulation when the patient was distracted or sleeping. Its development after head trauma separated it from congenital variants.

Ultimately, the patient’s constellation of symptoms was attributed to brainstem pathology resulting from CMI, which produced compression of both the respiratory centers and the nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thus serving as the unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of our patient's motor vehicle accident relative to the emergence of symptoms merits thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is reported to occur both as a congenital disorder and as an outcome of head injury or other neurologic trauma. Current etiological categorizations (2022) reflect this range, separating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants arising from trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, a CT scan obtained right after the incident showed a constricted foramen magnum. As there is no imaging from prior to the injury, it is unclear if this was a consequence of the trauma or simply uncovered by it. The rapid appearance of occipital headaches following the accident, along with the gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory complaints over a 14-year period, may be indicative of either: (1) tonsillar herniation secondary to trauma, or (2) trauma that intensified or brought to light an asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This report highlights the intricate respiratory presentations seen in CMI, contributing to the understanding of acquired central hypoventilation associated with structural lesions of the brainstem. Recognizing the difference between voluntary and automatic ventilation is essential when considering the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. While voluntary respiration, governed by cortical pathways, is generally preserved in individuals with CMI, automatic breathing—regulated by brainstem centers and crucial during sleep—can be impaired due to compression of medullary regions by the cerebellar tonsils. In this case, the patient retained the ability to breathe voluntarily but lacked automatic respiratory drive, a profile that resembles congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though the underlying mechanism is distinct. The incident at the airport, during which a brief lapse in attention led to severe oxygen desaturation in the absence of dyspnea, dramatically demonstrates the selective deficiency of automatic respiratory control: in effect, the patient 'forgets to breathe.'

We propose that the patient’s notable lack of dyspnoea despite marked oxygen desaturation is attributable to a primary dysfunction within the brainstem. This brainstem abnormality likely disrupts essential afferent neural pathways that are required to reach the cortex and elicit conscious awareness. The perception of breathlessness constitutes a form of interoception and relies on the integration of these incoming neural signals by critical cerebral regions, such as the anterior insular cortex. If these pathways are impaired as they traverse the brainstem, the cortical regions may not receive adequate input, preventing the emergence of air hunger and thereby accounting for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The significant gap between the pronounced clinical manifestations observed in our patient and the relatively modest findings from polysomnography (AHI 6.0) necessitates clarification. For logistical reasons, type III polysomnography was employed instead of the type I modality, resulting in the omission of EEG monitoring. The lack of EEG precluded precise determination of total sleep duration, which probably caused considerable underrepresentation of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG data, the AHI is calculated based on the entire period of recording, including times when the patient was awake, rather than restricting it to actual periods of sleep. As a result of this methodological shortcoming, the documented AHI of 6.0 most likely does not reflect the actual burden of sleep-disordered breathing in this case.

In spite of this constraint, multiple factors indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central respiratory events accounted for 72% of occurrences even though the total AHI was low; (2) observed episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% occurred without symptoms of dyspnoea or the patient's recognition, both during routine assessments and at the airport; and (3) imaging demonstrated considerable brainstem compression with 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils.

A Type I polysomnographic study, including comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal carbon dioxide measurement, and video surveillance, has been scheduled at an outside sleep laboratory. The finalization of this assessment is awaiting synchronization of the patient’s schedule, willingness to undergo the test, and the availability of appointments at the remote center. Upon completion, the study will allow for clearer delineation of sleep structure, respiratory activity, and extent and nature of central hypoventilation.

We recognize that, based on present data, central hypoventilation continues to be a strongly considered clinical hypothesis but has not yet been conclusively diagnosed.

In our patient, oropharyngeal dysphagia impacting both solid and liquid foods, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, was the symptom that caused the greatest discomfort. Validated swallowing assessment tools applied prospectively showed impaired function in 36% of cases, with neurogenic patterns being most common. The equivalent difficulty in swallowing substances of various consistencies serves to differentiate neurogenic from mechanical types of dysphagia. The patient's primary concern underwent repeated evaluation by both gastroenterology and otolaryngology, yielding normal results; however, her final diagnosis was postponed for multiple years. This prolonged period before diagnosis underscores a crucial clinical insight: when dysphagia or choking occurs in the absence of an identifiable structural abnormality on endoscopic examination, a neurologic etiology should be considered. In a retrospective analysis, the prevalence of CMI among pediatric patients with ongoing dysphagia was found to be 3.3%, which greatly exceeds the reported general pediatric prevalence of 0.1%–0.77%. These findings imply that chronic dysphagia may serve as an important clue to underlying CMI.

The patient's experience of pain localized to the vertex, alleviated by haircutting, highlights an intriguing yet insufficiently explored clinical observation. Occipital neuralgia may be seen with Chiari malformation type I (CMI), potentially leading to increased scalp sensitivity. The diagnostic standards specify ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia elicited by benign stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Despite this, occipital neuralgia in conjunction with CMI is infrequently documented, and definitive evidence connecting the two conditions causally is lacking.

A recently identified condition, termed ‘burning vertex syndrome’, may also be relevant; it is characterized by pain at the top of the scalp, cutaneous allodynia, and symptomatology that does not align precisely with recognized primary headache disorders such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headaches. The proposed underlying mechanism involves small nerve fibers in the scalp, with reported cases showing inconsistent improvement following greater occipital nerve block administration. For the patient described, possible explanations for the vertex symptoms include: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) burning vertex syndrome occurring independently; or (3) a distinct primary headache entity. There is a notable lack of systematic assessment of cutaneous allodynia patterns among individuals with CMI, as illustrated by the historical unavailability of any validated tool for symptom evaluation before the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In light of the limited data, we offer this observation as a notable clinical point, rather than as a conclusive diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when patients exhibit unusual or deceptive signs like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, resulting in a prolonged 14-year period before a definitive diagnosis was achieved. The process, marked by early uncertainty and a slow progression to accurate identification of the neurological condition, reflects a pattern frequently reported in the literature.
