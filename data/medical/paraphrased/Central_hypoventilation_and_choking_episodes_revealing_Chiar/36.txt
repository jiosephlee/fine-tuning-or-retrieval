Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year history of repeated choking episodes affecting both solids and liquids, as well as nocturnal choking occurring 3 to 4 times per week, and persistent occipital headaches for the past 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home pulse oximetry incidentally detected significant drops in oxygen saturation (70%–80%) without accompanying signs of respiratory distress. Several laryngoscopies and endoscopies were conducted to assess her choking episodes, all of which yielded normal findings. Reevaluation of a 14-year-old cranial CT, originally obtained after trauma, revealed a previously unnoticed crowded foramen magnum. MRI imaging of the head and neck established the presence of Chiari malformation type I (CMI), with 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography demonstrated mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, characterized mainly by central events (72%). This case underscores that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms resembling those seen in acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the necessity of considering anatomical brain abnormalities in patients presenting with unexplained respiratory issues.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils measuring at least 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI scans. Current imaging data indicate that CMI can be identified in 1% of adults, but clinical symptoms are observed in about 0.1% of people overall. There is a slight predominance in females, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases become apparent in individuals during their 30s.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, resulting in disproportion between the neural components and the confined space. Yet, newer research disputes the notion that Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is invariably abnormal, suggesting instead a continuum ranging from benign anatomical differences to overt clinical malformations. Recent volumetric analyses reveal considerable variation in posterior fossa dimensions across populations, often lacking a clear relationship to symptoms. This shift in perspective underscores the importance of personalized, symptom-driven management strategies, emphasizing the distinction between incidental anatomical findings and disease processes warranting operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical presentation associated with CMI, marked by a recognizable set of neurological symptoms. The primary complaint is an episodic headache localized to the occipital region, often triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, and observed in over 80% of cases. Compared to pediatric patients, adults tend to experience more pronounced symptoms, particularly when syringomyelia occurs concurrently. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is seen in 15%–26% of affected individuals, most often manifested as oropharyngeal disorders, including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now acknowledged as a rising complication associated with CMI. Among 90 adult patients prospectively evaluated for CMI, half exhibited sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. Notably, 6.7% had a central apnoea index of 5 or higher, though isolated central sleep apnoea was identified in just one individual, possibly indicating compression of the brainstem. The respiratory impairment is not limited to sleep disorders, as a case series has reported acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia threatening the lives of those with CMI.

Respiratory dysfunction in these cases is attributed to compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and impairment of respiratory regulatory centers, all of which can cause episodic respiratory manifestations. Such symptoms reflect a secondary central hypoventilation syndrome—also referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which differs from the congenital variant linked to PHOX2B mutations. Contemporary guidelines highlight this association by advocating for routine polysomnography in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who are suspected to have sleep-disordered breathing. Significantly, posterior fossa decompression may normalize CSF flow and ameliorate respiratory symptoms, emphasizing the need to consider CMI when evaluating patients with otherwise unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early forties sought care at our family medicine clinic, reporting a seven-year history of recurrent, unpredictable choking events that arose during both eating and sleep, affecting intake of solids and liquids equally. She characterized these episodes as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting her meals or waking her at night with forceful coughing and gasping, but never resulting in complete airway compromise. Nocturnal occurrences were noted three to four times weekly, with rare instances of two episodes within a single night. Throughout the years, she had received extensive workup, including three flexible laryngoscopic examinations and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Despite the absence of abnormal findings, her symptoms continued unabated. The consequences for her social life were considerable; she refrained from dining out or attending social events due to apprehension about choking in public. This persistent fear and embarrassment contributed to progressive social isolation and worsening emotional distress.

A comprehensive review of her clinical background showed that her symptoms first appeared 14 years prior, after she experienced a minor car accident with a brief period of unconsciousness. Soon after, she started having ongoing headaches located in the occipital region and at the vertex. She described these headaches as unremitting, pressure-like, and frequently confined to a small, coin-sized area at the vertex. The pain did not worsen with actions such as coughing, straining, or performing Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple clinicians diagnosed her with nummular headache, for which she received several local scalp injections, although these interventions provided only short-term improvement. A particularly notable characteristic was significant allodynia at the vertex, where even the presence of her hair caused pain. She eventually realized that keeping her hair short decreased her usual pain score from 6 out of 10 to 3 out of 10, but this change conflicted with cultural norms and led to additional emotional discomfort.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started tracking her oxygen levels at home with a personal pulse oximeter. Over a period of months, she consistently recorded episodes in which her oxygen saturation fell to between 70% and 80% without accompanying symptoms. These events typically went unnoticed until detected via her device. Repeated emergency assessments ascribed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, the patient developed sudden-onset dizziness accompanied by nausea. Medical staff at the scene recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prevented from boarding the plane. Following the initiation of supplemental oxygen therapy, her symptoms fully subsided within a few minutes. Further pulmonary assessment, which included both chest imaging and pulmonary function studies, yielded normal findings.

Considering her persistent headaches localized to the occipital region and vertex, in conjunction with escalating episodes of choking and unexplained hypoxemia, clinicians sought a comprehensive neurological explanation. Examination of her past medical records uncovered a non-contrast head CT that had been completed 14 years prior, subsequent to her accident; at that time, it was interpreted as normal. Nonetheless, a thorough reassessment of this imaging demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and an absence of cerebrospinal fluid at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastroesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected due to the presence of choking episodes. However, this diagnosis was ruled out following normal pH monitoring, unremarkable endoscopic findings, and failure to improve with maximal acid-suppressive therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered as a potential diagnosis given the presence of bulbar symptoms. However, the condition was deemed improbable as antibody tests returned negative results, and there was no evidence of fatigability or variation in symptoms throughout the day.

Comprehensive assessment of primary pulmonary causes was conducted in response to episodes of hypoxia. Imaging of the chest and pulmonary function testing demonstrated normal findings, effectively ruling out intrinsic disorders of the lungs.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly suspected due to the maintenance of voluntary respiration while automatic breathing control was lost during periods of sleep or distraction. Its development after head trauma differentiated it from the congenital variant.

Compression of the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei from brainstem involvement secondary to CMI accounted for the full spectrum of symptoms, thereby offering a single, cohesive diagnosis.

Discussion:

Close attention should be paid to the sequence of events linking the patient’s motor vehicle accident to the development of symptoms. Chiari malformation type I is reported in the literature as a congenital disorder but can also arise following head injuries or other neurologic insults. Current etiological frameworks, as of 2022, acknowledge this range, differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired cases, which may stem from trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, a CT scan obtained right after the incident showed that the foramen magnum appeared crowded. Because no imaging was available prior to the trauma, it is unclear whether this finding resulted from the injury or was simply uncovered by it. The patient’s occipital headaches began immediately post-accident, with bulbar and respiratory symptoms emerging gradually over 14 years. This clinical course might represent: (1) tonsillar herniation triggered by trauma, or (2) trauma aggravating or exposing an otherwise silent congenital abnormality.

This report illustrates the intricate respiratory complications associated with CMI and broadens current knowledge of acquired central hypoventilation patterns linked to structural abnormalities in the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic forms of respiration is fundamental to interpreting the respiratory pathophysiology seen in CMI. Cortically mediated voluntary respiration is generally unaffected in individuals with CMI. In contrast, automatic breathing—governed by brainstem regions and especially vital during sleep—can be impaired by compression of medullary areas from herniated tonsils. The patient described retained the ability for voluntary breathing, but experienced loss of involuntary respiratory drive, exhibiting a pattern analogous to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit resulting from a distinct pathological mechanism. The incident at the airport, in which transient inattention led to severe hypoxemia in the absence of dyspnoea, offers a striking demonstration of isolated failure of automatic respiratory control: in essence, the patient 'forgets to breathe'.

We propose that the striking lack of dyspnoea observed in this patient during marked oxygen desaturation results from a primary dysfunction within the brainstem. Such a lesion could interfere with essential afferent neural pathways required for transmitting signals to the cortex that enable the perception of respiratory discomfort. The sensation of breathlessness represents an aspect of interoception that relies on the integration of these afferent impulses within crucial cortical areas, such as the anterior insular cortex. If these signals are disrupted en route through the brainstem, the cortex would be deprived of the information needed to produce the awareness of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s potentially hazardous unawareness of severe hypoxaemia.

The notable contrast between the pronounced clinical manifestations observed in our patient and the comparatively modest polysomnographic result (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Due to practical constraints, type III polysomnography was employed instead of type I; the former does not include EEG monitoring. As a result, total sleep time could not be precisely determined, likely resulting in considerable underestimation of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG, the AHI is computed using the entire recording duration, encompassing wakeful intervals, rather than just actual sleep time. Consequently, this methodological limitation suggests that the recorded AHI of 6.0 may substantially underestimate the extent of sleep-disordered breathing present.

In spite of this limitation, multiple factors indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central events comprised 72% of respiratory episodes, even though the total AHI was low; (2) recorded episodes of oxygen desaturation to 70%–80% occurred without dyspnoea or the patient's awareness, both at the airport and in standard clinical settings; and (3) anatomical findings revealed marked brainstem compression, demonstrated by an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils.

A comprehensive sleep evaluation utilizing Type I polysomnography, which includes full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation, has been scheduled at an offsite sleep clinic. The study has not yet been performed due to the need to coordinate patient logistics, patient consent for the procedure, and appointment availability at the outside center. Upon completion, this assessment will allow for a more precise evaluation of sleep stages, breathing patterns during sleep, and the identification and quantification of central hypoventilation.

We recognize that, at present, central hypoventilation is a strongly considered clinical possibility, but lacks definitive diagnostic confirmation based on current findings.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, manifesting with equal difficulty in swallowing both solids and liquids and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, was the most prominent and bothersome symptom experienced by our patient. In a prospective study utilizing validated tools for swallowing assessment, dysfunction was identified in 36% of cases, with neurogenic etiologies being the most common pattern observed. The uniform impairment with all types of food and liquid textures serves to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical causes. The patient’s primary and most troublesome complaint was thoroughly evaluated through gastroenterological and otolaryngological investigations, all of which yielded normal results; however, her diagnosis was postponed for a number of years. This diagnostic delay underscores a crucial clinical point: in cases of dysphagia or choking where structural abnormalities are not apparent on endoscopic examination, clinicians should consider a neurological etiology. A retrospective review found that the prevalence of CMI among children with persistent dysphagia was 3.3%, substantially greater than the reported prevalence of 0.1%–0.77% in the wider pediatric population. These findings indicate that persistent dysphagia may serve as a significant marker for underlying CMI.

The patient describes experiencing discomfort localized to the vertex region, which is alleviated by hair cutting—an unusual observation that has not been thoroughly investigated. Occipital neuralgia, which is sometimes seen alongside CMI, may lead to increased sensitivity of the scalp. According to diagnostic guidelines, this condition is characterized by 'dysesthesia and/or allodynia triggered by non-painful contact with the scalp or hair.' Despite this, the relationship between occipital neuralgia and CMI is infrequently documented in the literature, and there is a lack of definitive evidence confirming a causal connection.

Another possible explanation is the newly recognized ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp discomfort, skin hypersensitivity, and clinical features that do not align clearly with established primary headache categories such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The proposed underlying mechanism may include involvement of small fiber nerve pathways in the scalp, and clinical response to greater occipital nerve blockade has been inconsistent among affected individuals. In this case, the vertex-related symptoms could indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) burning vertex syndrome occurring independently; or (3) a distinct primary headache disorder. To date, detailed reporting of cutaneous allodynia patterns among patients with CMI is minimal, reflected in the lack of any validated tool for capturing these symptoms before the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Given these limitations in existing literature, we report this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than as a conclusive diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing Chiari malformation type I (CMI), particularly when initial symptoms are unusual or ambiguous, such as central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a 14-year postponement before the condition was correctly identified. The process of diagnosis, marked by early misunderstanding and eventual accurate identification of the neurological disorder, mirrors patterns described in existing literature.
