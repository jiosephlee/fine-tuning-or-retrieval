Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported experiencing recurrent choking incidents involving both solids and liquids over a 7-year period, along with nocturnal choking episodes occurring 3–4 times per week and a 14-year history of persistent occipital headaches. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home oxygen monitoring inadvertently identified marked episodes of hypoxemia (oxygen saturation between 70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Despite several laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations for her choking complaints, no abnormalities were detected. Retrospective evaluation of a head CT scan taken 14 years prior for unrelated head trauma uncovered a previously unrecognized overcrowding at the foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck demonstrated Chiari malformation type I (CMI) characterized by an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. A polysomnographic study identified mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0), predominantly central in nature (72%). This report underscores that CMI may present with respiratory disturbances closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to consider structural abnormalities of the brain in individuals with unexplained respiratory symptoms.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by cerebellar tonsils extending a minimum of 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI scans. Recent imaging research indicates that CMI is present in about 1% of adults, although only around 0.1% of the overall population exhibits symptoms. The disorder shows a slight female preponderance, with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most commonly manifests in individuals during their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa size, leading to a discrepancy between the volume of neural tissue and the surrounding bony confines. Nevertheless, current research questions the long-held view of CMI as inherently abnormal, instead suggesting a continuum ranging from benign anatomical variants to true pathological states. Recent volumetric analyses reveal marked demographic diversity in posterior fossa dimensions, often lacking direct association with clinical symptoms. This paradigm shift underscores the importance of tailoring clinical management to each patient, emphasizing symptom-driven evaluation to differentiate between incidental anatomical findings and those anomalies that necessitate operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome constitutes the symptomatic expression of CMI, marked by a unique cluster of neurological signs. The principal clinical feature is a sudden, episodic headache in the occipital region, commonly triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, and observed in more than 80% of affected individuals. Symptom severity is generally greater in adults compared to pediatric patients, particularly in the context of concomitant syringomyelia. Lower cranial nerve deficits are documented in approximately 15%–26% of cases, often manifesting as disturbances in oropharyngeal function, including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now acknowledged as a notable complication associated with CMI. In a prospective cohort of 90 adults diagnosed with CMI, half exhibited sleep-disordered breathing. A central apnoea index of at least 5 was identified in 6.7% of participants, yet isolated central sleep apnoea was documented in just a single individual, possibly as a result of compression at the brainstem level. The spectrum of respiratory involvement is not limited to disturbances during sleep; one series of cases described instances of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia among individuals with CMI.

Respiratory dysfunction in this context arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow, and impairment of respiratory control centers, which collectively precipitate episodic disturbances in breathing. These manifestations constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—frequently referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which is etiologically and clinically distinct from the congenital form related to PHOX2B gene mutations. Recent clinical recommendations highlight this association by advocating for routine polysomnography in individuals with CMI who present with possible sleep-disordered breathing. Importantly, posterior fossa decompression can normalize CSF flow dynamics and may alleviate respiratory symptoms, thereby reinforcing the clinical imperative to consider CMI in patients presenting with unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s attended our family medicine clinic with a 7-year history of recurrent, unpredictable choking spells that affected her during both meals and sleep, impacting ingestion of solids and liquids to a similar degree. She reported that these episodes were abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting her meals or waking her from sleep with intense coughing and gasping; however, she never experienced full airway blockage. Nocturnal occurrences were frequent, manifesting three to four times weekly, and sometimes happening twice in a single night. Despite multiple comprehensive investigations over the years—including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all yielding normal results—her symptoms remained unresolved. The condition took a toll on her social life, leading her to avoid restaurants and social events out of apprehension about choking in public. Consequently, her persistent anxiety and embarrassment contributed to growing social isolation and emotional difficulties.

A comprehensive review of her clinical background indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, subsequent to a minor automobile accident during which she experienced a brief period of unconsciousness. Not long after this incident, she began to experience continuous headaches centered at the occipital region and the vertex of her head. She characterized these headaches as constant and pressure-like, frequently confined to a coin-sized region at the vertex. The pain did not intensify with actions such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple healthcare providers diagnosed her with nummular headache and administered repeated local injections into the scalp, which provided only short-lived alleviation. A particularly notable aspect of her condition was severe allodynia at the vertex, such that even the presence of her hair would provoke discomfort. Eventually, she found that cutting her hair short alleviated her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10; however, this change conflicted with her cultural norms, adding to her psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient initiated self-assessment of her oxygen saturation levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Over a period of months, she consistently recorded asymptomatic declines in oxygen saturation, frequently reaching levels between 70% and 80%. These reductions were typically unnoticed by her until revealed by the device. On several occasions, emergency medical assessments attributed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly developed severe dizziness and nausea. Medical staff present at the scene measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prevented from boarding the plane. The introduction of supplemental oxygen rapidly alleviated her symptoms entirely within several minutes. Follow-up assessments, encompassing chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal findings.

In light of the persistent occipital and vertex headache symptoms, recurrent choking episodes, and unexplained episodes of hypoxia, efforts were made to identify a single neurological condition that could account for all manifestations. Upon examination of her prior medical documentation, it was found that a non-contrast CT scan of the head had been conducted 14 years prior in the aftermath of her accident, originally interpreted as showing no abnormalities. Nonetheless, a meticulous retrospective assessment of the imaging demonstrated a congested foramen magnum and absence of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the presence of choking symptoms, an initial suspicion was raised for gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux. However, this diagnosis was ruled out based on normal findings from pH monitoring and endoscopic examinations, as well as the absence of clinical improvement following intensive acid-suppressive therapy.

An assessment for myasthenia gravis was undertaken in light of bulbar-related symptoms. However, the diagnosis was considered improbable given the negative results for relevant antibodies, the stable symptom pattern without fatigability, and no evidence of symptom fluctuation throughout the day.

In response to episodes of hypoxemia, a thorough evaluation of primary lung pathology was conducted. Intrinsic pulmonary disease was ruled out on the basis of unremarkable imaging studies and normal pulmonary function test results.

A strong indication of central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was the retention of voluntary respiratory function, contrasted by compromised automatic respiration during periods of inattention or sleep. The onset subsequent to head trauma differentiated this acquired disorder from its congenital counterparts.

The underlying cause of all symptoms was determined to be brainstem pathology resulting from CMI, as it led to compression of both the respiratory centers and the lower cranial nerve nuclei, thus offering a comprehensive explanation for the diagnosis.

Discussion:

It is important to examine closely the timing between the patient’s involvement in a motor vehicle accident and the emergence of their symptoms. Chiari malformation type I may present as a congenital anomaly or may develop following head trauma and other neurological insults. Current aetiological frameworks (2022) reflect this range, differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that arise due to traumatic injury or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, the CT scan obtained right after the incident showed that the foramen magnum was crowded. However, due to the absence of imaging prior to the trauma, it is unclear whether this anatomical change was a direct consequence of the injury or if the trauma simply made a pre-existing condition apparent. The patient’s occipital headaches, which started immediately following the accident and were later accompanied by gradually worsening bulbar and respiratory symptoms spanning 14 years, may be explained by either: (1) trauma precipitating tonsillar herniation, or (2) trauma aggravating or bringing to light an underlying, asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory complications associated with CMI and contributes to a more nuanced appreciation of acquired central hypoventilation in the setting of structural brainstem involvement. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic forms of respiration is key to elucidating the respiratory pathophysiology seen in CMI. While voluntary respiration—governed by cortical networks—tends to be preserved in individuals with CMI, automatic respiration—regulated by brainstem centers and especially vital during sleep—is impaired owing to the compression of medullary regions by herniated tonsils. Our patient maintained the ability to breathe voluntarily but lost involuntary respiratory drive, mirroring the clinical presentation of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though rooted in distinct pathophysiological processes. The incident at the airport, in which transient inattention led to significant oxygen desaturation in the absence of dyspnoea, serves as a striking demonstration of this specific deficit in automatic regulation: the patient effectively ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea in the context of marked oxygen desaturation stems from a fundamental dysfunction within the brainstem. This hypothesised brainstem abnormality likely compromises the essential ascending afferent pathways required to transmit information to the cortex, thereby facilitating conscious perception. The sensation of breathlessness constitutes a specific form of interoceptive awareness, which arises from the integration of these afferent inputs by vital cortical structures, such as the anterior insular cortex. When these neural signals are disrupted within the brainstem, the cortex is deprived of the crucial information necessary for generating the subjective experience of air hunger. This mechanism accounts for the patient’s perilous unawareness of their own physiological compromise.

The considerable mismatch between the pronounced clinical symptoms exhibited by our patient and the modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. For practical reasons, type III polysomnography was employed instead of type I; notably, type III does not incorporate EEG monitoring. As a result, it was not possible to determine total sleep time accurately, which likely contributed to a marked underreporting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG, the AHI is derived from the entire period of monitoring rather than periods of actual sleep, inevitably including intervals when the patient was awake. Consequently, this approach likely yields an artificially low AHI, thereby underrepresenting the actual extent of sleep-disordered breathing in this case.

Although this limitation exists, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) a high proportion of central events (72%), even though the total AHI remains low; (2) observed episodes of oxygen desaturation down to 70%–80%, occurring without accompanying dyspnoea or subjective awareness by the patient (both in the airport and during standard evaluations); and (3) anatomical data revealing pronounced brainstem compression, characterized by 11 mm tonsillar descent.

A Type I polysomnography—including comprehensive EEG assessment, end-tidal capnography, and continuous video surveillance—has been scheduled at an external sleep center. The study has yet to be carried out due to the need for alignment among patient availability, consent to undergo the procedure, and available scheduling at the offsite location. Upon its completion, the assessment is expected to yield a clearer delineation of the patient’s sleep architecture, respiratory function, and to determine both the existence and extent of central hypoventilation.

It should be noted that, on the basis of presently available data, central hypoventilation should be regarded as a strong clinical consideration, but it has not been conclusively established as a diagnosis.

In our patient, the most troubling symptom was oropharyngeal dysphagia that involved equal impairment when swallowing both solids and liquids, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. In a prospective study employing standardized tools to assess swallowing, dysfunction was identified in 36% of cases, with neurogenic mechanisms being most frequent. Unlike mechanical dysphagia, neurogenic dysphagia is characterized by similar difficulty across all types of food and liquid consistencies. Despite thorough initial and repeated assessments by both gastroenterology and otolaryngology, which produced unremarkable results, our patient's diagnosis remained elusive for an extended period. This diagnostic delay underscores an essential clinical point: the presence of dysphagia or choking without structural abnormalities observed via endoscopy should raise suspicion for an underlying neurological disorder. Notably, a retrospective review reported the prevalence of CMI among children with chronic dysphagia to be 3.3%, a rate significantly exceeding the 0.1%–0.77% seen in the general pediatric population, implying that sustained dysphagia may serve as a crucial marker of underlying CMI.

The patient’s description of vertex pain alleviated by cutting hair highlights a notable yet underexplored occurrence. While occipital neuralgia, which has been observed alongside CMI, may present as scalp hypersensitivity and is diagnosed by the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia during innocuous scalp or hair stimulation’, its connection to CMI is infrequently documented in the literature. Furthermore, current evidence has not definitively demonstrated a causal relationship between occipital neuralgia and CMI.

A different perspective considers the newly identified ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by pain at the scalp’s crown, cutaneous allodynia, and symptoms that resist categorization within existing primary headache disorders such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology implicates small fibre nerve pathways within the scalp, and patient responses to greater occipital nerve block are inconsistent. For our patient, symptoms at the vertex might indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) a coexisting burning vertex syndrome; or (3) an unrelated primary headache entity. There remains a notable lack of systematic recording of cutaneous allodynia in individuals with CMI, illustrated by the prior absence of any validated tool to capture specific symptomatology in CMI prior to the creation of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Owing to this limited evidence base, we report this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding, not as a conclusive diagnostic indicator.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI, particularly when its manifestations are unusual or mimic other conditions, as in presentations involving central hypoventilation and episodes of choking—factors that contributed to a fourteen-year postponement of accurate diagnosis. The protracted and circuitous route toward identifying the neurological basis, marked initially by diagnostic uncertainty, mirrors patterns frequently reported in existing literature.
