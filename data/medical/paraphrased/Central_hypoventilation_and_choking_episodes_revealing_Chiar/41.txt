Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

A female patient in her early 40s presented with a 7-year history of repeated episodes of choking on both solids and liquids, as well as nocturnal choking episodes occurring 3 to 4 times per week. She also reported persistent occipital headaches that had been ongoing for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home pulse oximetry monitoring unexpectedly detected severe oxygen desaturation events (ranging from 70% to 80%) that were not accompanied by respiratory symptoms. Numerous laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations for her choking complaints yielded normal results. Reevaluation of a head CT scan performed 14 years prior for unrelated trauma identified a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck confirmed Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with a descent of the cerebellar tonsils measuring 11 mm. Polysomnography demonstrated mild sleep apnea (apnea-hypopnea index 6.0), characterized primarily by central events (72%). This case highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory presentations resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the need to include structural brain lesions in the differential diagnosis for patients presenting with unexplained respiratory abnormalities.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils measuring at least 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging studies indicate that CMI is present in 1% of adults; however, only about 0.1% of the general population exhibit symptoms. There is a slight female preponderance with a ratio of 1.3:1, and CMI most commonly manifests in individuals in their thirties.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa, leading to a disproportion between neural tissue and the confines of the compartment. Yet, recent findings question the longstanding view of CMI as inherently abnormal, suggesting it exists along a continuum ranging from benign anatomical variants to pronounced malformations causing clinical symptoms. Current volumetric analyses reveal notable demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions, which do not consistently align with the presence of symptoms. This evolution in perspective supports a tailored, symptom-focused clinical approach, emphasizing the need to differentiate non-symptomatic anatomical variations from those pathological forms that warrant surgical management.

Chiari syndrome is the clinical expression of CMI, marked by a specific group of neurological symptoms. The primary feature is episodic headache in the occipital region, frequently triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, which occurs in more than 80% of patients. Adult patients generally exhibit more pronounced symptoms than children, particularly in the presence of associated syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves has been observed in 15%–26% of cases, usually manifesting as oropharyngeal dysfunction including palatal weakness, tongue wasting, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary complications are now acknowledged as a growing concern in patients with CMI. In a prospective analysis involving 90 adult individuals diagnosed with CMI, half of them exhibited sleep-associated breathing disturbances. Among the cohort, a central apnoea index of at least 5 was detected in 6.7%, though true central sleep apnoea was only identified in one subject, possibly attributable to brainstem impingement. The spectrum of respiratory abnormalities is not restricted to disruptions during sleep; a case series highlighted occurrences of acute respiratory failure and critical hypoxaemia among patients with CMI.

Respiratory impairment arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, and disruption of central respiratory control mechanisms, resulting in episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which is separate from the congenital central hypoventilation syndrome seen in PHOX2B mutation cases. Contemporary recommendations highlight this association by advising that patients with Chiari malformation I (CMI) who exhibit indications of sleep-disordered breathing should undergo routine polysomnography. Importantly, posterior fossa decompression may restore physiological CSF dynamics and ameliorate respiratory symptoms, thus emphasizing the necessity of considering CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory compromise.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s came to our family medicine practice reporting a 7-year history of recurrent, unpredictable episodes of choking during both meals and sleep, affecting the ingestion of both solids and liquids equally. She characterized these events as abrupt and alarming, frequently interrupting her meals or causing her to awaken from sleep with intense coughing and gasping, but noted that she had never experienced complete blockage of her airway. Nocturnal incidents occurred approximately three to four times per week, with some nights affected twice. Over time, she had received numerous investigations, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, each of which yielded normal results. Despite thorough workup, her symptoms remained unresolved. The social consequences were considerable; she refrained from attending restaurants and social occasions due to apprehension about experiencing a choking episode in public. This persistent worry and embarrassment progressively led to greater social isolation and emotional burden.

A more comprehensive review of her clinical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, following a minor car accident in which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after, she started experiencing ongoing headaches located at the occipital region and vertex. She characterized the headaches as constant, with a pressure-like quality, frequently confined to a small, coin-sized area atop the vertex. The headaches did not intensify with coughing, straining, or any actions involving Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple healthcare providers diagnosed nummular headache, and she received numerous local scalp injections, but these only provided short-term relief. The most notable symptom was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, as even the pressure from her hair led to discomfort. Ultimately, she found that keeping her hair cut short lessened her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, although this choice conflicted with cultural norms and contributed to emotional distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started checking her oxygen saturation at home with a pulse oximeter. Over a period of months, she noted recurrent asymptomatic episodes where her saturation fell within the 70%–80% range, frequently going unnoticed until she used the device. Several emergency department assessments ultimately attributed her symptoms to anxiety.

An important event took place at an international airport where, as she awaited her flight, the patient suddenly developed severe dizziness and nausea. Medical staff present at the scene measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being unable to board the plane. Her symptoms entirely subsided within minutes after receiving supplemental oxygen. Later assessments of her lungs, including chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed no abnormalities.

Considering the coexistence of persistent headaches localized to the occipital region and vertex, recurrent episodes of choking, and unexplained low oxygen levels, an overarching neurological cause was investigated. Her prior medical history included a non-contrast head CT conducted 14 years earlier post-trauma, which was interpreted as normal at that time. On careful re-examination, the scan demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and obliteration of the cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected due to the presence of choking episodes. However, this was ruled out following normal pH monitoring, unremarkable endoscopic findings, and failure to improve with maximum acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered in light of bulbar symptomatology. However, negative serology, no evidence of fatigability, and no variation throughout the day argued against this diagnosis.

Given episodes of hypoxemia, a thorough assessment for underlying pulmonary disease was conducted. Imaging of the chest and pulmonary function assessments were unremarkable, effectively ruling out primary lung pathology.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly suspected based on maintained voluntary respiration while automatic respiratory regulation was compromised during periods of distraction or sleep. Its occurrence after head injury set it apart from the congenital variants.

Compression of the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei by brainstem involvement due to CMI ultimately accounted for all clinical manifestations, thereby serving as the overarching explanation for the patient's symptoms.

Discussion:

The close timing between the patient’s motor vehicle collision and the emergence of symptoms necessitates thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is known to present as a congenital anomaly, but it may also develop following head trauma or other neurologic injuries. Current etiological frameworks (2022) delineate this range, differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants attributed to traumatic events or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained shortly after the injury showed a narrowed foramen magnum. Since no pre-injury imaging was available, it is unclear whether this finding was precipitated by the trauma or if it simply exposed an existing anomaly. The patient experienced occipital headaches right after the accident, then gradually developed bulbar and respiratory manifestations over a span of 14 years. These symptoms may indicate either (1) tonsillar herniation resulting from the trauma, or (2) trauma aggravating or revealing an asymptomatic congenital variant.

This case illustrates the intricate respiratory features associated with CMI and provides further insight into acquired central hypoventilation patterns resulting from structural abnormalities in the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is vital for comprehending the respiratory pathophysiology seen in CMI. Voluntary respiration, which relies on cortical pathways, is generally unaffected in individuals with CMI. However, automatic breathing, which depends on brainstem centers and is critical during sleep, can be disrupted because of compression of medullary regions by herniated tonsils. In our case, the patient maintained the ability for voluntary respiration but experienced a loss of automatic ventilatory drive, mimicking the presentation of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though arising through a separate pathogenic process. The episode at the airport, during which a transient lapse in attention led to marked oxygen desaturation without the sensation of dyspnoea, underscores this selective impairment of automatic regulation: the patient, quite literally, ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea in the setting of marked hypoxaemia may be attributable to an underlying dysfunction within the brainstem. Such pathology likely interferes with essential ascending afferent pathways responsible for transmitting neural signals to the cortex, which are needed for conscious perception. Breathlessness represents a facet of interoceptive awareness that relies on these afferent signals being processed by regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If the afferent neural input is interrupted in the brainstem, the cortex fails to receive the information required to evoke the sensation of air hunger, potentially accounting for the patient’s unawareness of severe physiological compromise.

The pronounced contrast between the patient’s profound clinical symptoms and the relatively low apnea-hypopnea index (AHI 6.0) observed on polysomnography necessitates clarification. For practical reasons, we employed type III polysomnography in place of the standard type I, resulting in the omission of EEG data. Consequently, we were unable to accurately determine total sleep duration, which likely led to an appreciable underreporting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG information, the AHI was computed based on the entire period of recording, encompassing both sleep and wakefulness, rather than solely sleep intervals. This methodological constraint suggests the AHI of 6.0 does not accurately reflect the actual extent of sleep-disordered breathing in this case.

Nonetheless, there are multiple factors indicative of central hypoventilation: (1) a high proportion of central events (72%) observed even though the total AHI remains low; (2) episodes of oxygen desaturation reaching 70%–80% in the absence of dyspnoea or patient recognition (noted both at the airport and in regular clinical situations); and (3) imaging findings showing marked brainstem compression, as indicated by 11 mm of tonsillar herniation.

Comprehensive Type I polysomnography, incorporating full EEG assessment, end-tidal CO₂ measurement, and video documentation, has been scheduled at an outside sleep laboratory. The study has not yet been conducted due to the need for further arrangement regarding patient scheduling, willingness to undergo the procedure, and allocation of appointment slots at the external location. Upon completion, this evaluation is expected to yield clearer information about sleep stage distribution, breathing patterns during sleep, and to more accurately delineate both the occurrence and severity of central hypoventilation.

It should be noted that, based on currently available data, central hypoventilation remains a strongly considered clinical possibility, though it has not been definitively established as the diagnosis.

Our patient experienced oropharyngeal dysphagia involving comparable difficulties with both solid and liquid foods, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, which constituted her most troublesome symptom. A prospective assessment utilizing standardized swallowing evaluation tools identified dysfunction in 36% of cases, with neurogenic dysphagia being the predominant type. The inability to handle multiple food consistencies equally is characteristic of neurogenic dysphagia and helps distinguish it from mechanical causes. Despite this symptom being the patient’s most pressing concern, initial and repeated investigations focused on gastrointestinal and otolaryngological causes revealed no abnormalities; however, her diagnosis remained undetermined for several years. This protracted diagnostic process underlines a crucial clinical point: when endoscopic examination fails to reveal structural abnormalities in cases of dysphagia or choking, a neurological etiology should be considered. In a retrospective cohort of pediatric patients with chronic dysphagia, CMI was identified in 3.3%, a rate significantly higher than the general pediatric prevalence of 0.1%–0.77%. This elevated frequency indicates that ongoing dysphagia may serve as an important marker for underlying CMI.

The patient's description of discomfort localized to the vertex and improvement following hair cutting illustrates an unusual presentation that has not been extensively explored in the literature. Occipital neuralgia is known to be associated with CMI, leading to increased sensitivity of the scalp; diagnostic standards specify the presence of 'dysesthesia and/or allodynia evident during non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair.' Despite this, reports detailing the relationship between occipital neuralgia and CMI are infrequent, and definitive evidence confirming causality is lacking.

Another possibility to consider is the newly recognized ‘burning vertex syndrome,’ characterized by pain on the scalp, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align well with standard primary headache disorders such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. It is postulated that this condition might involve small fiber neural pathways within the scalp, and patient responses to greater occipital nerve blocks have been inconsistent. In our case, the symptoms at the vertex may be attributed to: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) a coincidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache disorder. There is a notable deficit in systematic reports describing patterns of cutaneous allodynia in CMI populations, highlighted by the lack of any published assessment tool for such symptoms prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In light of the limited evidence, we share this observation as a notable clinical finding, rather than a confirmed diagnostic characteristic.

This case demonstrates the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when its clinical presentation is unusual, including symptoms like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, which resulted in a delayed diagnosis over 14 years. The process of reaching the correct diagnosis, marked by early misinterpretation and a protracted approach towards identifying the underlying neurological disorder, frequently appears in published literature.
