Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year history of repeated episodes of choking involving both solid and liquid foods, frequent nighttime choking episodes occurring three to four times per week, and persistent occipital headaches for the past 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, at-home monitoring unexpectedly identified episodes of profound oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopy and endoscopy procedures undertaken to evaluate her choking symptoms produced normal findings. Reassessment of a head CT obtained 14 years prior for unrelated trauma found an overlooked crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI imaging of the brain and cervical region confirmed Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0) with a central predominance (72% of events). This case demonstrates that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the need to include structural abnormalities of the brain in the differential diagnosis for patients presenting with unexplained respiratory impairment.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by cerebellar tonsils descending a minimum of 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging studies indicate that CMI affects 1% of adults, but only about 0.1% exhibit clinical symptoms. There is a slight female bias, with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases manifest in individuals in their 30s.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, resulting in a discrepancy between the size of neural elements and the space provided. Nevertheless, recent data question the long-held view that CMI is invariably disease-causing, suggesting it may range from harmless anatomical differences to clinically relevant abnormalities. Modern volumetric analyses have revealed considerable differences in posterior fossa size across populations, yet these measurements often do not reliably align with symptom presentation. This evolving perspective prompts a shift toward personalized, symptom-guided clinical assessment, allowing clinicians to differentiate non-symptomatic anatomical variants from conditions that warrant surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome constitutes the clinical presentation associated with CMI, distinguished by a unique set of neurological symptoms. The primary symptom is episodic occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of affected individuals. Adults often exhibit more pronounced symptoms than children, particularly in the presence of concomitant syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves has been documented in 15%–26% of cases, frequently manifesting as oropharyngeal abnormalities including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary complications are now more frequently acknowledged as part of the clinical spectrum of CMI. A prospective analysis involving 90 adult patients with CMI revealed that half experienced sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. A central apnoea index of 5 or greater was identified in 6.7% of individuals, while isolated central sleep apnoea was detected in only a single patient, possibly indicative of brainstem involvement. Notably, these respiratory issues are not restricted to sleep; case reports have described episodes of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia in patients with CMI.

Respiratory dysfunction in this context is attributed to factors such as brainstem compression, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, and impairment of the neural centers regulating respiration, resulting in episodic breathing difficulties. These manifestations constitute an acquired type of central hypoventilation syndrome—occasionally referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which differs from the congenital variant associated with PHOX2B gene mutations. Current recommendations highlight this association, advising regular polysomnography for patients with CMI who are suspected to have sleep-disordered breathing. Notably, posterior fossa decompression may reestablish normal CSF flow and could resolve respiratory disturbances, highlighting the necessity of considering CMI in patients with unexplained respiratory symptoms.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early 40s came to our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year history of repeated choking incidents, which took place unpredictably during both eating and sleep and affected solid and liquid foods equally. She described these episodes as abrupt and upsetting, frequently causing her to stop eating or abruptly waking her from sleep with intense bouts of coughing and gasping. Although these events were distressing, they never resulted in complete blockage of her airway. She experienced nighttime occurrences three to four times each week, with some nights involving two episodes. Over several years, she underwent thorough evaluations, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies; all findings were normal. Her symptoms persisted without a clear diagnosis. The social consequences were substantial; she started to avoid dining in restaurants and participating in gatherings due to anxiety about choking in public. The persistent embarrassment and worry contributed to progressive social isolation and growing emotional distress.

A comprehensive review of her medical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, shortly after a minor car accident that resulted in a brief period of unconsciousness. Soon after, she started experiencing continuous headaches located at the occipital region and the vertex. She characterized these headaches as persistent, pressure-like, and frequently confined to a small, coin-sized area on the vertex. These headaches did not worsen with activities such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. She received a diagnosis of nummular headache from several clinicians and underwent numerous local scalp injections; however, these interventions provided only short-lived relief. The most striking symptom was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, such that even the mere presence of her hair caused discomfort. Eventually, she found that keeping her hair short lessened her average pain score from 6/10 to 3/10, although this choice went against cultural norms and led to significant emotional turmoil.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started tracking her oxygen levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Across several months, she consistently recorded periods where her oxygen saturation fell to between 70% and 80% without experiencing any symptoms. She frequently remained unaware of these occurrences until she consulted her monitoring device. Several emergency department visits ultimately associated her symptoms with anxiety.

A significant event transpired at a global airport. As she awaited her departure, she abruptly developed severe dizziness accompanied by nausea. The medical team present recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prevented from boarding the aircraft. Symptoms fully subsided within minutes following supplemental oxygen administration. Later assessment by pulmonary specialists, including chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed no abnormalities.

In light of her persistent occipital and vertex headaches, recurrent episodes of choking, and unexplained hypoxia, efforts were made to identify an underlying neurological cause. Her past medical history included a non-contrast head CT obtained 14 years previously after her trauma, which was originally interpreted as unremarkable. On closer re-examination, the CT demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and absent cerebrospinal fluid around the craniocervical junction, features indicative of CMI that were overlooked at the initial assessment. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the patient's choking episodes, gastroesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was contemplated as a possible diagnosis. However, normal results from pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations, combined with no improvement on maximal acid suppression therapy, ruled out this condition.

Assessment for myasthenia gravis was prompted by bulbar symptoms. However, negative results for antibodies, as well as the absence of fatigability and diurnal fluctuation, argued against this diagnosis.

Given the episodes of hypoxia, comprehensive evaluation for primary lung disease was performed. Findings from unremarkable chest imaging and pulmonary function studies ruled out underlying pulmonary pathology.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome, also known as acquired Ondine’s curse, was indicated by normal voluntary respiration while automatic breathing was deficient during periods of sleep or distraction. The onset after head injury differentiated this condition from congenital variants.

Pathology involving the brainstem as a result of CMI accounted for the patient's entire symptomatology by exerting pressure on the respiratory centers and nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby establishing the unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing between the patient's involvement in a motor vehicle collision and the emergence of symptoms requires thoughtful evaluation. Chiari malformation type I may arise as a congenital disorder or develop following head trauma or other neurologic insults. Current etiological frameworks (2022) delineate primary Chiari malformations, which are congenital, from secondary or acquired variants that are associated with trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, a CT scan obtained right after the accident showed a constricted foramen magnum. As there is no imaging available prior to the trauma, it remains unclear whether this anatomical feature resulted from the injury or if it was simply unveiled by the traumatic event. The acute presentation of occipital headache directly after the incident, and subsequent gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory manifestations spanning 14 years, may indicate: (1) trauma leading to tonsillar herniation, or (2) trauma precipitating or exposing an asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory symptoms associated with CMI, contributing to a deeper insight into acquired central hypoventilation patterns resulting from structural lesions in the brainstem. Differentiating voluntary from automatic breathing is fundamental to appreciating the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. Voluntary respiration, governed by cortical circuitry, is usually spared in individuals with CMI. In contrast, automatic respiration—regulated by brainstem centers and critical for breathing during sleep—becomes impaired due to compression of the medullary region by herniated cerebellar tonsils. Our patient maintained the ability to breathe voluntarily while lacking involuntary respiratory drive, a profile that parallels congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit arising from a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, where a brief lapse in conscious attention led to marked desaturation in the absence of subjective breathlessness, provides a compelling demonstration of this isolated automatic breathing deficit: the patient, quite literally, ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient's striking lack of dyspnoea despite marked hypoxaemia can be attributed to a primary dysfunction of the brainstem. This underlying brainstem abnormality likely impedes essential afferent neural signals from reaching cortical structures, which are required to elicit conscious perception. Breathlessness represents a form of interoceptive awareness, contingent upon the relay and processing of these signals in regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If the brainstem interferes with the transmission of these afferents, the cortex would fail to receive critical information needed to generate the sensation of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient's perilous unawareness of severe physiological compromise.

The considerable difference observed between the patient’s pronounced clinical symptoms and the modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. We conducted a type III polysomnography for practical reasons, rather than the type I study, which would have included EEG measurements. As EEG data was not collected, it was impossible to accurately determine the patient’s actual sleep duration, likely causing an appreciable undercount of respiratory disturbances. Instead, the AHI was derived from the total recording time, encompassing both sleep and awake intervals, rather than exclusive sleep periods. Consequently, this methodological shortcoming suggests that the AHI of 6.0 provided may substantially undervalue the actual extent of sleep-disordered breathing in this patient.

Although this limitation exists, multiple findings are consistent with central hypoventilation: (1) central apneas constituted 72% of all events, even though the total AHI was low; (2) oxygen saturation levels were observed to drop to 70%–80% in the absence of symptoms such as dyspnoea or patient recognition (both during travel at the airport and ordinary interactions); and (3) imaging demonstrated substantial brainstem compression, evidenced by an 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A Type I polysomnography incorporating comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video surveillance has been scheduled at an off-site sleep center. The execution of this evaluation is still outstanding due to ongoing coordination with the patient’s schedule, willingness to undergo the procedure, and the remote center’s timing. Upon completion, the study is expected to yield a more detailed assessment of sleep structure, breathing patterns, and to clarify the existence and extent of central hypoventilation.

It should be noted that, based on currently available data, central hypoventilation remains a strongly considered clinical possibility rather than an unequivocally established diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, impacting both solid and liquid foods to the same degree and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, was the most bothersome symptom experienced by our patient. When assessed prospectively using standardized swallowing evaluation tools, dysfunction was identified in 36% of subjects, with neurogenic etiologies being the most common. The comparable impairment across all food textures sets neurogenic dysphagia apart from mechanical causes. The patient’s primary symptom was examined multiple times by gastroenterology and otolaryngology, but all investigations yielded normal results; however, the ultimate diagnosis was postponed for several years. This diagnostic delay underscores an important clinical point: when dysphagia or choking occurs without structural abnormalities seen on endoscopic studies, neurological causes should be considered. In a retrospective cohort of pediatric patients with ongoing dysphagia, the prevalence of CMI was 3.3%, which is significantly greater than the 0.1%–0.77% frequency seen in the wider pediatric population. This finding indicates that persistent dysphagia may serve as a significant warning sign for underlying CMI.

The patient’s description of discomfort localized to the vertex region and improvement following hair cutting highlights a noteworthy, though largely unexplored, clinical observation. Occipital neuralgia has been documented in conjunction with CMI and is known to produce increased scalp sensitivity; its diagnostic standards stipulate the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia elicited by non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Despite this, the relationship between occipital neuralgia and CMI remains seldom cited in the literature, with definitive evidence connecting the two conditions still lacking.

Another possibility is the recently reported ‘burning vertex syndrome’, which is characterized by pain at the top of the scalp, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical characteristics that do not clearly align with recognized primary headache categories such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The proposed underlying mechanism may involve small fiber pathways stemming from the scalp, and responses to greater occipital nerve blocks have been inconsistent among patients. In this case, the patient's vertex symptoms could potentially indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) burning vertex syndrome occurring independently; or (3) a different primary headache disorder entirely. Comprehensive recording of cutaneous allodynia patterns in CMI cohorts is limited, illustrated by the lack of validated tools for assessing these specific symptoms in CMI individuals before the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Due to the scarcity of supporting data, we report this observation as a notable clinical finding rather than asserting it as a diagnostic criterion.

This case demonstrates the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI when symptoms are unusual or ambiguous, such as central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a 14-year lapse before the correct diagnosis was established. The process, marked by early misdiagnoses and a slow progression toward an accurate identification of neurological pathology, aligns with similar reports in the literature.
