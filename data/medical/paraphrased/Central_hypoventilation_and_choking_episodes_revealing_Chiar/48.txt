Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s presented with a 7-year duration of recurrent episodes of choking involving both solid and liquid foods, nocturnal choking occurring 3 to 4 times per week, and persistent occipital headaches that had been ongoing for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home monitoring unexpectedly revealed severe oxygen desaturation (ranging from 70%–80%) in the absence of any respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies, carried out in the evaluation of the choking episodes, yielded normal findings. Reevaluation of a CT head scan performed 14 years prior for unrelated trauma disclosed a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck identified Chiari malformation type I (CMI), marked by 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography showed mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0), primarily due to central events (72%). This report highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory complaints closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underlining the necessity to consider structural abnormalities of the brain in cases of unexplained respiratory compromise.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils by 5 mm or more below the foramen magnum as visualized on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI is present in 1% of adults, although only about 0.1% of people experience related symptoms. There is a slight female predilection, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases manifest clinically in individuals in their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between the size of neural elements and the limited cranial space. Yet, recent findings dispute the long-held belief that CMI is invariably abnormal, suggesting a continuum that ranges from benign anatomical differences to severe malformations with clinical consequences. Contemporary volumetric analyses reveal notable demographic variation in posterior fossa size, yet these differences do not reliably align with symptomatology. This evolving perspective underscores the importance of personalized, symptom-guided management, separating non-symptomatic anatomical variants from those pathological cases in which surgery is indicated.

Chiari syndrome is the clinical expression of CMI, marked by a recognizable cluster of neurological symptoms. The primary complaint is sudden onset occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, observed in more than 80% of patients. Adults often exhibit more pronounced symptoms than pediatric patients, particularly when syringomyelia occurs simultaneously. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is seen in 15%–26% of cases, most commonly manifesting as oropharyngeal impairment, including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now acknowledged as a more common complication associated with CMI. In a prospective analysis involving 90 adults diagnosed with CMI, half exhibited sleep-related breathing abnormalities. Specifically, a central apnoea index of 5 or higher was identified in 6.7% of the cohort, but isolated central sleep apnoea was recorded in just one individual, possibly indicative of brainstem compression. Respiratory complications in CMI are not limited to disturbances during sleep; a series of cases has highlighted the occurrence of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia posing a threat to life in affected patients.

Respiratory impairment in this context arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, and disturbances affecting the neural centers responsible for breathing regulation, resulting in episodic respiratory manifestations. These manifestations constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—occasionally referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—and are distinct from the congenital form related to PHOX2B gene mutations. Current recommendations highlight this association, advising that patients with CMI who are suspected of having sleep-related breathing disorders undergo routine polysomnography. Notably, surgical decompression of the posterior fossa may re-establish normal CSF dynamics and potentially alleviate respiratory symptoms, emphasizing the need to identify CMI as a cause of otherwise unexplained respiratory compromise.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s visited our family medicine clinic with a 7-year history of recurring choking spells, which happened unpredictably during both meals and sleep, involving solids and liquids to the same extent. She described these episodes as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting her meals or waking her at night with intense coughing and gasping; however, she never experienced complete airway blockage. Nighttime incidents occurred three to four times weekly, sometimes happening twice in one evening. Over time, she had been thoroughly evaluated, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all of which yielded normal findings. Despite extensive investigation, her symptoms continued without relief. The impact on her social life was substantial; she actively avoided restaurants and social events for fear of choking in public. This persistent worry and embarrassment resulted in marked social isolation and emotional strain.

Upon further evaluation, it was determined that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, following a minor car accident that resulted in a brief period of unconsciousness. Soon after this event, she experienced ongoing headaches centered on the occipital region and the vertex. She described these headaches as continuous, with a pressure-like sensation that frequently concentrated in a coin-sized area at the vertex. The pain was not aggravated by actions such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Several healthcare providers diagnosed her with nummular headache, and she received numerous local scalp injections; however, these treatments provided only temporary improvement. The most prominent symptom was significant allodynia at the vertex, where even the sensation of her hair resting on this area provoked discomfort. She eventually found that keeping her hair short decreased her baseline pain level from 6/10 to 3/10, but this choice conflicted with her cultural norms and contributed to emotional suffering.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started checking her oxygen levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Over a span of several months, she consistently recorded episodes where her oxygen saturation decreased to between 70% and 80%, despite being asymptomatic. She typically only became aware of these desaturations upon reviewing her oximeter readings. On several occasions, emergency assessments linked her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she developed sudden onset vertigo and nausea. Medical staff at the scene noted an oxygen saturation level of 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from boarding the aircraft. Her symptoms abated entirely within minutes after receiving supplemental oxygen. Later assessments of her pulmonary status, incorporating chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed no abnormalities.

Because she exhibited a longstanding pattern of headaches involving the occipital and vertex regions, as well as progressively worsening choking spells and unexplained hypoxemia, the evaluation focused on finding a single neurological explanation. Her past medical documentation included a non-contrast head CT conducted 14 years prior, subsequent to her traumatic event, which had originally been interpreted as unremarkable. On closer, repeat examination, the imaging actually showed foramen magnum crowding and obliteration of cerebrospinal fluid spaces at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastroesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was initially suspected due to the presence of choking episodes. However, normal findings on pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations, along with the absence of improvement despite maximal acid suppression therapy, ruled out this diagnosis.

Assessment for myasthenia gravis was prompted by bulbar symptoms. However, the diagnosis was considered improbable due to negative antibody assays, no observed fatigability, and the absence of diurnal symptom fluctuation.

The possibility of primary lung disease was thoroughly explored after episodes of hypoxia. Intrinsic pulmonary disorders were ruled out by unremarkable chest imaging and normal results on pulmonary function testing.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly suspected based on the presence of intact voluntary respiration alongside deficient automatic respiratory drive during periods of distraction or sleep. The post-traumatic onset differentiated this condition from congenital variants.

Compression of the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei due to brainstem pathology associated with CMI accounted for all of the patient’s symptoms, resulting in a comprehensive diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of the patient’s symptoms following the motor vehicle accident requires thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is described in the literature as having congenital origins as well as arising in response to head trauma or other neurological insults. Current classifications (2022) acknowledge this range, differentiating between primary Chiari malformations, which are congenital, and secondary or acquired variants associated with trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, the CT conducted right after the injury showed a congested foramen magnum. Since we lack imaging prior to the trauma, it remains unclear whether this appearance was precipitated by the injury or if it was an incidental discovery. The patient experienced occipital headache immediately post-trauma, with a gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory manifestations over 14 years. This clinical sequence might indicate either (1) tonsillar herniation resulting from the trauma or (2) trauma aggravating or exposing an underlying congenital anomaly that had been asymptomatic.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory complications associated with CMI and contributes to a deeper knowledge of how central hypoventilation can develop in the context of structural brainstem abnormalities. Recognizing the separation between voluntary and automatic breathing is essential for grasping the respiratory pathophysiology of CMI. In these patients, voluntary respiration, which depends on cortical pathways, is generally preserved. By contrast, the automatic regulation of breathing—managed by brainstem centers and critical during sleep—becomes impaired as a result of tonsillar compression affecting medullary regions. In our case, the patient maintained the capacity for voluntary respiration but lost the automatic respiratory drive, mirroring the presentation of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit through a distinct underlying process. The incident at the airport, where a brief lapse in attention led to severe desaturation in the absence of dyspnoea, strikingly demonstrates this particular deficit in automatic respiratory control: the patient quite literally “forgets to breathe.”

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea despite severe oxygen desaturation may result from a primary dysfunction within the brainstem. Such pathology is presumed to compromise vital afferent neural pathways responsible for transmitting signals to the cortex, which are required for the conscious perception of distress. The sensation of breathlessness constitutes an aspect of interoception, reliant on the integration of these afferent inputs by critical brain structures such as the anterior insular cortex. If these pathways are disrupted at the level of the brainstem, cortical regions would be deprived of essential information to trigger the awareness of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s perilous unawareness of hypoxaemia.

The significant gap between the patient’s pronounced clinical symptoms and the comparatively modest polysomnographic result (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. For pragmatic reasons, type III rather than type I polysomnography was performed, and this method does not incorporate EEG monitoring. The lack of EEG prevented a precise determination of actual sleep duration, most likely causing substantial underestimation of respiratory events. In the absence of EEG, the AHI calculation is based on total recording time, which encompasses intervals of wakefulness, instead of exclusively measuring sleep time. As a result, this limitation implies that the observed AHI of 6.0 probably fails to capture the true extent of sleep-disordered breathing in this patient.

In spite of this constraint, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) a central event dominance of 72% is noted, even though the total AHI remains low; (2) oxygen saturation levels were observed to fall to 70%–80% without any accompanying dyspnea or awareness by the patient (both at the airport and during routine evaluations); and (3) anatomical imaging demonstrates marked compression of the brainstem with 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A Type I polysomnography that includes comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation has been scheduled at an outside sleep center. The study is awaiting finalization, as it depends on aligning patient availability, obtaining patient consent for the procedure, and securing an appointment at the off-site location. The results, once obtained, are expected to yield a more thorough assessment of sleep staging, respiratory function during sleep, and to clarify both the presence and degree of central hypoventilation.

It is important to note that, based on the current available information, central hypoventilation should still be regarded as a strongly suspected but not definitively established diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia involving equal impairment with both solids and liquids, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, was the primary complaint for our patient. Prospective studies utilizing standardized swallowing assessment tools have demonstrated dysfunction in 36% of cases, most frequently showing neurogenic features. The comparable difficulty across all food textures is characteristic of neurogenic dysphagia, in contrast to mechanical causes. Despite thorough evaluation from gastroenterology and otolaryngology, which yielded normal results, the patient's most prominent symptom was repeatedly assessed, yet her diagnosis was postponed for several years. This protracted diagnostic timeline underscores the crucial point that unexplained dysphagia or choking—where endoscopic examination does not reveal a structural abnormality—should raise suspicion for neurological etiologies. In a retrospective review, children with ongoing dysphagia showed a 3.3% prevalence of CMI, markedly greater than the general pediatric rate of 0.1%–0.77%. This indicates that persistent dysphagia may serve as a significant marker for underlying CMI.

The patient describes pain localized to the vertex that is alleviated following hair cutting, which is a curious occurrence lacking thorough investigation in the literature. Occipital neuralgia has been noted in conjunction with CMI and may produce increased sensitivity of the scalp; according to diagnostic criteria, patients should exhibit dysesthesia or allodynia provoked by non-painful contact with the scalp or hair. Nonetheless, reports of occipital neuralgia in the context of CMI are infrequent, and definitive evidence for a causal relationship remains absent.

Another possibility to consider is the newly recognized ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by pain on the scalp, skin sensitivity to touch, and manifestations that do not align precisely with current primary headache categories like migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The suggested pathophysiology may involve small sensory nerve fibers in the scalp, and patient responses to greater occipital nerve block are inconsistent. For our case, the vertex-related complaints could indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) burning vertex syndrome occurring independently; or (3) a different primary headache disorder. Comprehensive characterization of cutaneous allodynia patterns in individuals with CMI remains limited, as reflected by the lack of a validated instrument to capture such symptoms prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Owing to this limited evidence base, we report this finding as a noteworthy clinical observation rather than assigning it a conclusive diagnostic significance.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI when it manifests through unusual or deceptive symptoms, such as central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, which resulted in a delayed diagnosis spanning 14 years. The progression towards accurate neurological identification, marked by early uncertainty and eventual clarity, is repeatedly described in the literature.
