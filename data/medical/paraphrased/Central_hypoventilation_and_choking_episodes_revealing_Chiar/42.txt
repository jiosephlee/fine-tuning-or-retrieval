Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year history of repeated choking episodes involving both solid and liquid foods, nocturnal choking events occurring 3–4 times per week, as well as persistent occipital headaches lasting for 14 years. During home oxygen monitoring in the COVID-19 pandemic, she was found to have pronounced oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) episodes, despite the absence of respiratory distress. Several laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations carried out to assess the choking complaints yielded normal results. A retrospective review of a head CT scan from 14 years prior, initially performed for unrelated trauma, revealed a congested foramen magnum that had not previously been noted. Subsequent MRI scans of the head and neck established a diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) featuring an 11 mm downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography detected mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, and a predominance of central events (72%). This case highlights that CMI may present with respiratory manifestations closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the need to consider structural brain lesions in patients with unexplained breathing difficulties.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward extension of the cerebellar tonsils measuring 5 mm or more beneath the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI can be identified in 1% of adults, yet only about 0.1% of the population exhibit clinical symptoms. The disorder is slightly more common in females, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, and most frequently manifests in individuals in their twenties.

The underlying mechanism involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between the size of neural elements and the limited surrounding space. Nevertheless, recent data disputes the classic view of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) as inherently abnormal, suggesting a continuum that ranges from benign anatomical differences to manifest disease. Contemporary volumetric analyses reveal marked differences in posterior fossa dimensions across populations, with no uniform association with clinical manifestations. This evolving perspective highlights the importance of tailoring clinical management based on patient symptoms, carefully differentiating incidental anatomical findings from conditions warranting operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome denotes the symptomatic presentation of CMI and is identified by a unique set of neurological signs. The primary symptom is sudden onset occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of affected individuals. Adult patients generally exhibit more pronounced symptoms than pediatric cases, particularly in the presence of concomitant syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is observed in 15%–26% of cases, most often manifesting as oropharyngeal disturbances, including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are being acknowledged as a growing complication associated with CMI. In a prospective analysis involving 90 adult patients with CMI, sleep-related breathing disorders were identified in half of the subjects. A central apnoea index equal to or greater than 5 was noted in 6.7% of cases, although exclusive central sleep apnoea was recorded in just a single patient, possibly indicating compression at the brainstem level. The respiratory involvement is not limited to sleep abnormalities; a case series reported acute respiratory failure and severe, life-threatening hypoxaemia among individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in this context arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and impairment of the neural centres responsible for regulating respiration, resulting in episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome, referred to by some as ‘Ondine’s curse’, and are distinct from the congenital central hypoventilation syndrome linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Current recommendations highlight this relationship by advocating for routine polysomnography in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who exhibit possible sleep-disordered breathing. Importantly, decompression of the posterior fossa can improve CSF dynamics and may resolve respiratory symptoms, emphasizing the need to consider CMI in cases of respiratory dysfunction with unclear etiology.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s attended our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year duration of unpredictable, recurrent choking incidents that took place both during meals and while asleep. Both solid foods and liquids were equally implicated in triggering these events. She characterized them as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting her meals or rousing her from sleep with intense coughing and difficulty breathing, but they never escalated to full airway blockage. These nighttime occurrences happened three or four times weekly, and occasionally up to twice in the same night. Throughout this period, she underwent numerous assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopy examinations and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all with normal findings. Her symptoms, however, continued unabated in the absence of an identified cause. The condition had a considerable effect on her social life; she began to shun dining out and attending social functions due to concern over experiencing choking in public. The combination of persistent worry and embarrassment contributed to her growing social isolation and emotional challenges.

A thorough review of her clinical history indicated that her symptoms first appeared 14 years prior, following a minor car accident during which she briefly lost consciousness. In the aftermath, she began experiencing ongoing headaches located at the occipital region and vertex. These headaches were described as continuous, having a sensation of pressure, and were often restricted to a coin-sized area at the vertex. They did not worsen with coughing, physical strain, or other Valsalva maneuvers. She was diagnosed by several clinicians with nummular headache and received multiple local injections to the scalp, which only provided short-term relief. The most notable symptom was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, where even the sensation of her hair could provoke discomfort. Eventually, she learned that keeping her hair short alleviated her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, although this conflicted with cultural norms and resulted in significant emotional distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started tracking her oxygen levels at home with a personal pulse oximeter. Over the course of several months, she consistently recorded episodes of low oxygen saturation—between 70% and 80%—despite not experiencing any symptoms. She frequently remained unaware of these events until measuring with her device. Several visits to the emergency department resulted in her symptoms being ascribed to anxiety.

A significant event took place at a major international airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly developed severe dizziness accompanied by nausea. Medical staff present recorded an oxygen saturation of 78%, resulting in her being prevented from boarding. After she was provided with supplemental oxygen, her symptoms resolved entirely within a few minutes. Later assessments by a pulmonary specialist—including chest imaging and lung function tests—yielded normal findings.

Considering the persistent occipital and vertex headaches, along with escalating choking spells and unexplained episodes of hypoxemia, a single neurological etiology was sought. Review of her prior health records indicated she had undergone a non-contrast head CT fourteen years ago post-accident, originally interpreted as normal. Upon closer inspection, however, this scan demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and obliteration of the cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Such imaging characteristics were consistent with CMI and were not recognized during the initial assessment. Table 1 outlines the full sequence of her clinical presentation.

Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

An initial diagnosis of gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was suggested due to the presence of choking episodes. However, this was ruled out based on normal pH monitoring, unremarkable endoscopic findings, and no clinical improvement despite maximal acid suppression therapy.

Evaluation for myasthenia gravis was undertaken because of bulbar involvement; however, the diagnosis was deemed improbable given negative antibody results, no evidence of fatigability, and the lack of fluctuation in symptoms throughout the day.

After episodes of hypoxemia, a thorough assessment for primary pulmonary disease was performed. Both imaging studies of the chest and pulmonary function testing were normal, effectively ruling out intrinsic pulmonary pathology.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (also known as acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by intact voluntary respiration alongside reduced automatic ventilatory drive observed during periods of distraction or sleep. The development of this condition after head injury set it apart from congenital variants.

Brainstem involvement resulting from CMI accounted for the clinical manifestations by exerting pressure on the respiratory centers and nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby offering a comprehensive diagnostic explanation.

Discussion:

It is important to closely examine the timing between the motor vehicle accident experienced by our patient and the emergence of symptoms. Chiari malformation type I is recognized as a disorder with both congenital origins and potential to develop following head trauma or other neurological insults. Recent aetiological classifications2022 differentiate this range by categorizing Chiari malformations as either primary (congenital) or secondary/acquired, which may occur due to traumatic injury or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan taken right after the injury showed a tightly packed foramen magnum. As we did not have imaging prior to the trauma, it is unclear if the accident was responsible for this feature or if it simply brought it to attention. The patient developed occipital headaches immediately after the trauma and subsequently experienced a gradual onset of bulbar and respiratory symptoms over the following 14 years. This clinical course could indicate either: (1) trauma resulting in tonsillar herniation, or (2) trauma that worsened or revealed a congenital abnormality that had not previously caused symptoms.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory complications associated with CMI and advances our comprehension of acquired central hypoventilation patterns arising from structural abnormalities in the brainstem. Recognizing the difference between voluntary and automatic respiration is pivotal for elucidating the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. The cortical pathways responsible for voluntary breathing generally remain unaffected in individuals with CMI. Conversely, the automatic regulation of respiration—governed by brainstem centers and critical during sleep—is impaired as a result of cerebellar tonsil-induced compression of the medulla. In this patient, voluntary ventilation was maintained, whereas the automatic drive to breathe was lost, paralleling the presentation of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome but stemming from a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, where transient inattention led to marked oxygen desaturation in the absence of breathlessness, serves as a clear demonstration of isolated automatic control dysfunction: the patient effectively ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient's striking lack of dyspnoea amid substantial oxygen desaturation stems from an underlying dysfunction within the brainstem. This suspected brainstem lesion is believed to impede crucial afferent neural pathways responsible for conveying sensory information to the cerebral cortex, required for conscious perception. The sensation of breathlessness is considered an aspect of interoception, reliant upon the integration of these incoming neural signals by specific cerebral regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If these afferent pathways are disrupted within the brainstem, the cortex would fail to receive the necessary input to evoke the sensation of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s perilous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The pronounced difference between the severity of our patient’s clinical symptoms and the modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. We performed type III polysomnography instead of type I because of logistical constraints; type III does not incorporate EEG recordings. Consequently, we could not accurately determine the patient’s total sleep time, which probably led to a substantial underrepresentation of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG monitoring, the AHI is based on total recording duration rather than actual sleep periods, inadvertently including episodes of wakefulness. As a result, the AHI value of 6.0 is probably a considerable underestimate of the patient’s true level of sleep-disordered breathing.

Although this limitation exists, multiple findings are consistent with central hypoventilation: (1) central events comprised 72% of the apnoeas, even though the total AHI remained low; (2) the patient exhibited oxygen saturation drops to 70%–80% without experiencing dyspnoea or being conscious of the episodes (noted both at the airport and during regular assessments); and (3) imaging revealed marked brainstem compression, demonstrated by 11 mm of tonsillar herniation.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography, including full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation, has been scheduled at an outside sleep facility. At present, the study has not yet been performed, pending alignment of the patient’s availability, consent to the procedure, and booking at the distant location. Upon completion, this assessment is expected to yield more conclusive information regarding the patient’s sleep architecture, respiratory dynamics, and the presence and degree of central hypoventilation.

It should be noted that, based on the current data, central hypoventilation is supported as a plausible clinical diagnosis, though it has not been unequivocally established.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, impacting both solid and liquid foods to the same degree and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, represented the most troubling symptom for our patient. Prospective assessment employing standardized swallowing evaluation tools revealed dysfunction in 36% of cases, with most showing neurogenic characteristics. The similar severity of dysphagia across all consistencies serves to differentiate neurogenic origins from mechanical causes. Initially, the patient’s principal symptom underwent repeated gastroenterological and otolaryngological investigation, all of which yielded normal results; despite this, her definitive diagnosis was postponed for several years. This prolonged diagnostic interval underscores a vital clinical point: when dysphagia or choking occurs without evident structural abnormalities on endoscopic examination, a neurologic etiology should be considered. In a retrospective series of children experiencing chronic dysphagia, CMI was identified in 3.3%—notably higher than the general pediatric prevalence range of 0.1%–0.77%. This observation indicates that ongoing dysphagia may be a significant marker for underlying CMI.

The patient's experience of vertex pain alleviated by cutting hair illustrates a noteworthy but insufficiently explored occurrence. Occipital neuralgia has been observed in conjunction with CMI and may contribute to increased sensitivity of the scalp. Its diagnostic criteria specify the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia during non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Nonetheless, connections between occipital neuralgia and CMI are infrequently documented, and definitive evidence of causation is lacking.

Another possibility is the recently characterized ‘burning vertex syndrome,’ which is marked by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and a clinical presentation that does not conform precisely to existing primary headache categories, such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology may involve small fiber pathways originating in the scalp, with patients demonstrating variable responses to greater occipital nerve blocks. For our patient, the symptoms at the vertex could potentially signify: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) burning vertex syndrome that happens to occur simultaneously; or (3) a distinct primary headache entity altogether. There is a notable lack of systematic reports describing patterns of cutaneous allodynia in CMI cohorts, as illustrated by the absence of any published tool for evaluating these specific symptoms in individuals with CMI prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In light of this limited data, we present this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than as a conclusive diagnostic sign.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI when it manifests with unusual or confusing symptoms, including central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, which resulted in a 14-year postponement of accurate identification. The diagnostic process, marked by early misinterpretations and a slow progression to the proper neurological diagnosis, frequently appears in published reports.
