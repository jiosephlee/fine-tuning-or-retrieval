Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a seven-year duration of repeated choking episodes involving both solid and liquid intake, frequent nocturnal choking occurring three to four times per week, and persistent occipital headaches lasting for fourteen years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home monitoring incidentally detected severe hypoxaemia episodes (oxygen saturation 70%–80%) in the absence of any respiratory discomfort. Despite several laryngoscopy and endoscopy procedures undertaken to evaluate the choking complaints, findings were unremarkable. Reassessment of a fourteen-year-old head CT scan performed due to unrelated trauma uncovered a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck identified Chiari malformation type I (CMI) characterized by an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0), predominantly central in origin (72%). This case demonstrates that CMI can manifest with respiratory symptoms strongly resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the necessity of evaluating for structural abnormalities in patients with unexplained respiratory complaints.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward movement of the cerebellar tonsils extending 5 mm or more beneath the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging surveys have found CMI in 1% of adults; however, only about 0.1% of the overall population experience symptoms. The disorder shows a slight female preponderance, with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most commonly becomes apparent in individuals during their thirties.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an undersized posterior fossa during development, resulting in a disproportion between neural components and surrounding space. Nonetheless, recent research disputes the conventional view of CMI as inherently pathological, suggesting it encompasses a continuum ranging from benign anatomical differences to those causing clinical manifestations. Volumetric analyses have revealed notable variations in posterior fossa dimensions across populations, yet these differences do not reliably correspond to symptoms. Consequently, this paradigm shift underscores the importance of personalized, symptom-focused assessment, differentiating anatomical variants without symptoms from conditions that necessitate operative management.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical presentation of CMI, marked by a characteristic set of neurological symptoms. The primary manifestation is a sudden onset occipital headache triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, which occurs in more than 80% of patients. In adults, symptoms are generally more pronounced than in paediatric cases, particularly when syringomyelia is also present. Impairment of the lower cranial nerves is observed in 15%–26% of individuals, commonly leading to oropharyngeal disturbances including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory complications are becoming more widely acknowledged in association with CMI. In a prospective analysis involving 90 adult patients diagnosed with CMI, half exhibited sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. Specifically, 6.7% demonstrated a central apnoea index of 5 or higher, while pure central sleep apnoea was present in only a single individual, possibly indicative of compression of the brainstem. The spectrum of respiratory involvement is not limited to sleep disruptions; a case series has described episodes of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia among CMI cases.

Respiratory impairment in these cases arises from brainstem compression, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow, and disruption of neural respiratory control centers, which manifests as episodic respiratory abnormalities. These symptoms constitute an acquired type of central hypoventilation syndrome—often referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—that differs from the congenital variant associated with PHOX2B gene mutations. Current recommendations highlight this relationship by advocating for routine polysomnography in patients with CMI who are suspected of having sleep-related breathing disorders. Notably, surgical decompression of the posterior fossa may restore normal CSF circulation and reverse respiratory manifestations, emphasizing the need to consider CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early 40s was seen in our family medicine clinic due to a 7-year duration of recurrent choking episodes that occurred unpredictably with both eating and sleeping, and involved both solid and liquid intake. She reported that these incidents were abrupt and distressing, frequently disrupting her meals or waking her at night with severe coughing and difficulty breathing; however, she never experienced total airway blockage. The nighttime events happened three to four times weekly, with occasional instances of two occurrences within the same night. Throughout this period, she had comprehensive assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Nevertheless, her symptoms persisted in the absence of any positive findings. The consequences for her social life were considerable, as she refrained from eating out or participating in gatherings due to the fear of having a choking event in public. This enduring anxiety and sense of humiliation resulted in progressive social isolation and growing psychological distress.

A more extensive evaluation of her history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, after a minor car accident in which she experienced a brief loss of consciousness. Soon thereafter, she began to experience ongoing headaches affecting the occipital region and the vertex. She characterized these headaches as unrelenting, pressure-like, and frequently confined to a coin-sized spot at the vertex. The headaches did not worsen with actions such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple physicians concluded she had nummular headache, and she received numerous localized scalp injections, but these only provided short-lived relief. The most notable characteristic was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, where even the presence of her hair caused discomfort. Ultimately, she realized that keeping her hair short alleviated her baseline pain score from 6/10 to 3/10; however, this choice conflicted with her cultural norms and added to her psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started checking her oxygen saturation at home with a personal pulse oximeter. Over a period of months, she consistently recorded episodes of low oxygen saturation, falling between 70% and 80%, despite not experiencing any symptoms. These occurrences typically went unnoticed until she used the device. Several emergency department visits resulted in her symptoms being ascribed to anxiety.

An important event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly developed severe dizziness and nausea. Medical staff present measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being refused permission to board. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms fully resolved within several minutes. Follow-up assessment of her lungs, including chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, demonstrated normal findings.

In light of her persistent occipital and vertex headaches, recurrent episodes of choking, and unexplained hypoxemia, clinicians considered a single neurological etiology. Review of her past medical documentation uncovered a non-contrast head CT performed 14 years prior, subsequent to her injury, which had initially been interpreted as normal. Nevertheless, careful reassessment of this imaging demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and effacement of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. An initial suspicion of gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux arose due to the patient's complaints of choking. However, this diagnosis was ruled out owing to normal findings on pH monitoring, unremarkable endoscopic evaluations, and an absence of clinical improvement despite maximum acid suppression therapy.

Following episodes of hypoxia, a thorough workup for primary pulmonary disease was performed. Imaging of the chest and pulmonary function studies were within normal limits, thereby ruling out intrinsic pathology of the lungs.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by the maintenance of intentional respiration while automatic breathing was compromised during sleep or when the patient was distracted. The onset after head injury differentiated this acquired variant from the congenital type.

Compression of the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei due to brainstem involvement from CMI ultimately accounted for all symptoms, serving as the comprehensive diagnosis.

Discussion:

It is important to closely evaluate the timing of the patient’s motor vehicle accident in relation to the appearance of symptoms. Chiari malformation type I is reported not only as a congenital anomaly but also as a possible outcome of head injuries and other neurological trauma. Recent etiological classification schemes2022 delineate this range, separating congenital (primary) Chiari malformations from those that are acquired or secondary, which may develop due to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained right after the incident showed an unusually crowded foramen magnum. Due to the absence of imaging prior to the injury, it is unclear if this abnormality was precipitated by the trauma itself or simply brought to light as a consequence of the event. The patient experienced occipital headaches immediately following the accident, subsequently developing bulbar and respiratory manifestations that worsened over the next 14 years. These clinical features may represent either (1) herniation of the cerebellar tonsils as a result of the injury, or (2) aggravation or disclosure of an asymptomatic congenital anomaly prompted by the trauma.

This report illustrates the intricate respiratory presentations associated with CMI and contributes to the recognition of acquired central hypoventilation patterns secondary to structural lesions in the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is key to interpreting the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. While voluntary ventilation—under cortical control—generally remains preserved in individuals with CMI, automatic respiration—regulated by brainstem centers and vital during sleep—becomes impaired owing to compression of medullary regions by herniated tonsils. The patient maintained voluntary control over ventilation but lost the intrinsic respiratory drive, closely paralleling the phenotype observed in congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit through a different underlying process. The airport incident, wherein a brief lapse of attention led to marked hypoxemia absent any sensation of breathlessness, sharply underscores the selective disruption of automatic breathing regulation: in such moments, the individual simply ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that a primary dysfunction within the brainstem underlies the patient's striking lack of dyspnoea despite episodes of marked oxygen desaturation. This brainstem pathology likely impedes the essential afferent neural pathways responsible for transmitting sensory signals to the cortex, which are necessary for conscious awareness. The perception of breathlessness represents an aspect of interoception that relies on these ascending afferent inputs being processed in critical regions such as the anterior insular cortex. When these pathways are disrupted at the brainstem level, cortical regions fail to receive the requisite signals to evoke air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of physiological compromise.

The significant gap between the pronounced clinical symptoms in our patient and the modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, we employed type III polysomnography instead of the more comprehensive type I, thereby omitting EEG monitoring. Without EEG, we could not accurately determine total sleep duration, which probably resulted in a notable underestimation of the frequency of respiratory disturbances. The absence of EEG meant the AHI was based on total recording time—including periods of wakefulness—rather than on actual sleep time. This methodological shortcoming suggests that the documented AHI of 6.0 most likely understates the true extent of sleep-disordered breathing in this patient.

Although this limitation exists, multiple factors indicate central hypoventilation: (1) a high proportion of central apneic events (72%) notwithstanding a modest total AHI; (2) observed oxygen saturations dropping to 70%–80% in the absence of dyspnoea or any patient awareness (noted both at the airport and during regular assessments); and (3) anatomical findings demonstrating notable brainstem compression with 11 mm of tonsillar herniation.

A Type I polysomnographic evaluation—including comprehensive EEG surveillance, end-tidal carbon dioxide monitoring, and video documentation—has been scheduled at an outside sleep center. Nevertheless, the study has not yet been carried out due to ongoing logistical arrangements concerning patient attendance, consent for the procedure, and availability at the distant institution. Upon its completion, the results will offer a clearer assessment of sleep structure, breathing dynamics, and both the existence and degree of central hypoventilation.

We recognize that, based on current available information, central hypoventilation is strongly suspected on clinical grounds but cannot yet be considered conclusively diagnosed.

The patient’s most bothersome symptom was oropharyngeal dysphagia, affecting both solid and liquid foods to the same extent and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. Prospective studies utilizing validated tools for swallowing assessment demonstrated impairment in 36% of patients, with neurogenic origins being most prevalent. The similar level of difficulty with all types of food textures serves to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical forms. Despite repeated and early investigations from gastroenterology and otolaryngology, which yielded normal results, the patient’s diagnosis was not made for several years. This prolonged diagnostic interval underscores an important clinical consideration: in cases where dysphagia or choking occurs and structural abnormalities are absent on endoscopic evaluation, a neurological etiology should be entertained. Retrospective data from children suffering from persistent dysphagia revealed a 3.3% prevalence of CMI, markedly exceeding the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence found in the broader pediatric population. This points to persistent dysphagia as a potentially significant marker for underlying CMI.

The patient described experiencing discomfort at the top of the head, which improved after hair cutting, highlighting a noteworthy yet insufficiently explored occurrence. Occipital neuralgia may be seen in conjunction with CMI, leading to increased scalp sensitivity. Its diagnostic guidelines specify that ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’ is required. Despite this, reports connecting occipital neuralgia with CMI are infrequent, and definitive evidence for a causal relationship remains lacking.

A newly recognized entity, termed ‘burning vertex syndrome’, has been proposed as a differential, characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical attributes that are not encompassed by classic categories such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The pathophysiology is thought to involve small fiber nerves originating from the scalp, and responses to greater occipital nerve blocks have been inconsistent among reported cases. For our patient, the vertex-related complaints could be explained by one of the following: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) a concurrent occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache disorder. Currently, there is a notable lack of structured assessment of cutaneous allodynia specifically in CMI populations, a gap highlighted by the previous lack of any standardized instrument addressing these features before the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In light of the limited available data, we describe this presentation as an intriguing clinical observation, rather than attributing definitive diagnostic significance.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in identifying CMI when it manifests through uncommon or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, resulting in a protracted diagnostic process spanning 14 years. The process, marked by early misdirection and slow progression toward the accurate neurological diagnosis, reflects a pattern frequently described in prior reports.
