Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early forties reported experiencing recurrent choking with both solid and liquid intake over seven years, as well as nocturnal choking episodes occurring three to four times per week, alongside persistent occipital headaches for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, she underwent home oxygen monitoring, which unexpectedly showed profound desaturations to 70%–80% in the absence of respiratory distress. Multiple evaluations with laryngoscopy and endoscopy were performed for the choking events, all of which were unremarkable. Retrospective assessment of a cranial CT conducted 14 years earlier for unrelated head trauma revealed an unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. MRI of the head and cervical spine identified Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnea (apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0), with central events comprising 72% of episodes. This report highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory complaints that resemble acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underlining the necessity of evaluating for structural brain anomalies in cases of unexplained respiratory impairment.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils measuring at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI imaging. Recent radiologic studies identify CMI in about 1% of adults, but only around 0.1% of the overall population display symptoms. There is a slight predominance in females, with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases are first recognized in the third decade of life.

The underlying mechanism involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, resulting in disproportion between neural elements and the confined space. Nevertheless, recent findings dispute the conventional view of CMI as inherently pathological, suggesting it exists along a continuum from normal anatomical differences to clinically relevant abnormalities. Current volumetric analyses reveal notable demographic differences in posterior fossa size that do not consistently correlate with symptoms. This evolving perspective underscores the importance of personalized, symptom-focused management, distinguishing between incidental anatomical variants and those conditions that warrant surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome constitutes the symptomatic expression of CMI, marked by a recognizable pattern of neurological deficits. The primary complaint is episodic occipital headache brought on by Valsalva maneuvers, which occurs in more than 80% of patients. In adults, clinical manifestations are generally more pronounced than in pediatric cases, particularly in the presence of coexisting syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves has been observed in 15%–26% of individuals, most often manifesting as oropharyngeal disturbances such as palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary symptoms are now acknowledged as a growing complication associated with CMI. In a prospective cohort involving 90 adult CMI patients, half exhibited disorders affecting respiration during sleep. A central apnoea index of 5 or greater was present in 6.7% of individuals; however, isolated central sleep apnoea appeared in just a single patient, likely attributable to compression of the brainstem. Respiratory impairment in CMI is not limited to nocturnal disruptions; a case series has also reported episodes of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia among individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment arises due to factors such as brainstem compression, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and interference with respiratory regulatory centers, which collectively contribute to episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired type of central hypoventilation syndrome, occasionally referred to as 'Ondine's curse', which should be differentiated from the congenital variant linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Updated recommendations highlight this association by advising routine polysomnography in CMI patients presenting with possible sleep-related breathing disorders. Importantly, performing posterior fossa decompression can reestablish normal CSF circulation and may reverse respiratory abnormalities, highlighting the need to consider CMI in cases of otherwise unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s came to our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year history of unpredictable, recurrent choking incidents that happened during both eating and sleep, with no distinction between solid foods or liquids. She characterized these episodes as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting meals or waking her from sleep, accompanied by severe coughing and gasping, though never progressing to full airway blockage. These nocturnal events occurred three to four times per week, and occasionally twice in a single night. Throughout this period, she underwent multiple assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all of which showed normal results. Her symptoms, however, continued unabated in spite of negative test findings. The impact on her social life was profound; she refrained from eating out or attending social events out of concern for having a public choking episode. The persistent worry and embarrassment resulted in growing social isolation and emotional suffering.

A comprehensive review of her clinical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, following a minor car accident that resulted in a brief loss of consciousness. Soon thereafter, she experienced ongoing headaches localized to the occipital region and the vertex. She characterized these headaches as continuous, with a pressure-like quality, and frequently limited to a small, coin-sized area at the vertex. The pain was not aggravated by activities such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple healthcare providers diagnosed her with nummular headache, leading to repeated local scalp injections, which provided only short-lived symptom relief. The most notable aspect was pronounced allodynia at the vertex—such that even her hair's weight provoked discomfort in that area. Ultimately, she found that keeping her hair short diminished her baseline pain intensity from 6/10 to 3/10; however, this conflicted with cultural norms and resulted in psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started using a pulse oximeter at home to track her oxygen saturation levels. Over the course of several months, she consistently recorded episodes where her oxygen saturation fell into the 70%–80% range, despite the absence of symptoms. She was frequently unaware of these events until she used the device. Several emergency assessments attributed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an overseas airport. As she awaited her departure, she suddenly developed pronounced dizziness and nausea. Medical staff at the scene recorded an oxygen saturation of 78%, resulting in her being prevented from boarding the plane. Her symptoms fully abated within minutes after receiving supplemental oxygen. Follow-up assessment by pulmonary specialists, encompassing both chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed normal findings.

Due to the presence of longstanding headaches located in the occipital and vertex regions, along with worsening episodes of choking and unexplained hypoxia, efforts were made to identify a single neurological explanation. Examination of her prior medical documentation indicated that a non-contrast head CT had been obtained 14 years earlier following her accident; at the time, the scan was interpreted as unremarkable. On closer, retrospective analysis, however, the CT demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and absence of cerebrospinal fluid around the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected due to the patient's complaints of choking. However, this diagnosis was ruled out because pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations were unremarkable, and there was no improvement with maximum acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was evaluated due to symptoms affecting the bulbar region. Negative antibody testing, lack of fatigability and absence of diurnal variation made this unlikely.

Primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated following hypoxic episodes. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests excluded intrinsic lung disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly suspected based on intact voluntary respiration accompanied by deficient automatic ventilation during periods of sleep or distraction. The onset after head trauma differentiated this condition from its congenital counterparts.

Brainstem pathology secondary to CMI ultimately explained all symptoms through compression of the respiratory centres and lower cranial nerve nuclei, providing the unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The close timing between the patient’s involvement in a motor vehicle accident and the later emergence of symptoms necessitates thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is recognized in the literature as occurring congenitally or developing as a consequence of head trauma or various neurological injuries. Modern etiological classifications as of 2022 delineate this continuum, differentiating between primary (congenital) Chiari malformations and secondary or acquired variants attributable to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained right after the incident revealed a narrowed foramen magnum. Because we lack imaging from before the injury, it remains unclear whether this feature resulted directly from the trauma or was simply exposed by it. The patient experienced occipital headaches immediately following the accident, with subsequent gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory manifestations over a 14-year period. This clinical course could be interpreted as either (1) trauma precipitating tonsillar descent, or (2) trauma aggravating or bringing to light an otherwise silent congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory presentations associated with CMI, expanding our knowledge of how acquired central hypoventilation arises in the context of structural abnormalities of the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is essential for interpreting the respiratory pathophysiology seen in CMI. In these patients, voluntary breathing, which relies on cortical neural pathways, usually remains unaffected. However, automatic respiration—regulated by brainstem centers and necessary for breathing during sleep—is impaired as a result of medullary structure compression by herniated tonsils. The individual described here maintained voluntary ventilatory capacity but experienced a loss of involuntary respiratory drive, displaying a pattern similar to that seen in congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though stemming from an alternative underlying mechanism. The episode in the airport, during which a brief lapse in attention led to significant oxygen desaturation without any subjective sensation of breathlessness, strikingly demonstrates the isolated impairment in automatic respiratory control—effectively, the patient ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea during profound episodes of hypoxaemia is attributable to a primary dysfunction of the brainstem. This suspected pathology appears to impair essential afferent neural pathways responsible for relaying information to the cortex, which is necessary for conscious perception. Breathlessness constitutes an aspect of interoception that relies on these ascending signals being processed by specific brain areas such as the anterior insular cortex. If such input is disrupted at the level of the brainstem, the cortex would not be furnished with the requisite sensory information to generate a sense of air hunger, offering an explanation for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their underlying physiological compromise.

The pronounced mismatch between the patient's significant clinical symptoms and the modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, we employed type III polysomnography instead of type I, which does not incorporate EEG monitoring. As a result, we were unable to reliably assess actual sleep duration, probably resulting in an undercount of respiratory events. In the absence of EEG, AHI is derived from total recording duration—including intervals when the patient may be awake—rather than authentic total sleep time. Consequently, this methodological shortcoming suggests that the measured AHI of 6.0 likely underrepresents the actual burden of sleep-disordered breathing in this case.

Nevertheless, there are multiple factors suggesting central hypoventilation: (1) a majority of central events (accounting for 72%) were observed, even though the total AHI remained low; (2) episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% occurred without subjective dyspnoea or patient recognition (noted both at the airport and in usual clinical settings); and (3) imaging revealed marked brainstem compression, with 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A comprehensive type I polysomnographic evaluation, including full electroencephalography, end-tidal CO2 measurement, and video monitoring, has been scheduled at a specialized sleep center offsite. However, the study has yet to be performed due to the need to coordinate the patient's availability, obtain patient consent for the procedure, and align with the scheduling constraints of the external facility. When conducted, this assessment will yield more definitive information regarding the patient’s sleep structure, breathing patterns during sleep, and the extent and severity of any central hypoventilation.

We acknowledge that, given the current evidence, central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis, rather than a definitively confirmed diagnosis.

Our patient’s most prominent and troubling complaint was oropharyngeal dysphagia involving comparable difficulty with both solids and liquids, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. In a prospective study that utilized standardized swallowing evaluations, 36% of individuals exhibited dysfunction, with most displaying neurogenic features. Neurogenic dysphagia is characterized by uniform impairment across all food consistencies, in contrast to the pattern seen in mechanical dysphagia. Despite repeated and initial assessments by gastroenterology and otolaryngology that revealed no abnormalities, her main symptom was thoroughly investigated from these perspectives, resulting in a diagnosis only after several years. This prolonged period prior to diagnosis underscores a critical clinical consideration: when patients present with dysphagia or episodes of choking and endoscopy shows no structural abnormality, a neurological cause should be considered. In a retrospective analysis of children with long-standing dysphagia, the frequency of CMI was 3.3%, markedly higher than the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence found in the general pediatric population, pointing to persistent dysphagia as a significant potential indicator of underlying CMI.

The patient’s description of vertex pain alleviated by cutting hair is a noteworthy, though poorly understood, observation. Occipital neuralgia may present alongside CMI and can manifest as increased scalp sensitivity; established diagnostic guidelines stipulate the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia during non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Nonetheless, reports documenting a connection between occipital neuralgia and CMI are infrequent, and definitive evidence confirming a causal relationship is lacking.

Another possibility is the newly recognized 'burning vertex syndrome', characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and atypical clinical features that do not align well with established categories of primary headaches such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology may involve dysfunction of small fiber nerve pathways within the scalp, and patient responses to greater occipital nerve blocks have been inconsistent. In our case, the symptoms localized to the vertex could be attributable to: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) a coincidental presentation of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct, separate primary headache disorder. There is a notable gap in the systematic reporting of cutaneous allodynia patterns among individuals with CMI, illustrated by the lack of validated assessment tools for specific CMI-related symptoms prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Due to this limited body of evidence, we describe this finding as a noteworthy clinical observation, rather than ascribing to it a specific diagnostic significance.

This case underscores the considerable diagnostic difficulties associated with Chiari malformation type I (CMI), particularly when it manifests through uncommon or ambiguous symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a protracted 14-year interval before definitive diagnosis. Such a diagnostic process, often marked by early uncertainty and a slow progression toward accurate identification of the underlying neurological disorder, is frequently observed in published accounts.
