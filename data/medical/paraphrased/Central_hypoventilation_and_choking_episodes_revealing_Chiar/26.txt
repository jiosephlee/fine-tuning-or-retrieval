Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female in her early 40s reported a 7-year course of recurrent episodes of choking on both solids and liquids, as well as nocturnal choking events occurring 3–4 times per week, accompanied by a 14-year history of persistent occipital headaches. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home oximetry unexpectedly identified marked hypoxemia (oxygen saturation 70%–80%) in the absence of any respiratory distress. Numerous laryngoscopies and endoscopies performed in the workup for her choking episodes were unremarkable. A review of a non-contrast CT brain scan, performed 14 years earlier for unrelated head trauma, disclosed a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain and cervical spine subsequently identified Chiari malformation type I (CMI), with 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography demonstrated mild sleep apnea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0 and a predominance (72%) of central apneas. This case demonstrates that CMI may present with respiratory manifestations resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to consider structural lesions of the brain in cases of otherwise unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized on sagittal MRI by cerebellar tonsils descending at least 5 mm inferior to the foramen magnum. Current imaging data indicate that CMI occurs in about 1% of adults; however, only around 0.1% of people display clinical symptoms. The disorder is somewhat more frequent in females, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, and most commonly becomes apparent in the third decade of life.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa size, resulting in a disproportion between neural components and their spatial capacity. Nonetheless, newer research has questioned the conventional notion that Chiari Malformation type I (CMI) is inherently disease-causing, suggesting it may encompass a continuum ranging from benign anatomical differences to meaningful malformations. Contemporary volumetric analyses have shown pronounced demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions, yet these do not reliably correlate with clinical manifestations. This evolving perspective emphasizes the importance of patient-specific, symptom-oriented management, separating asymptomatic structural variations from abnormalities warranting operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome is the symptomatic expression of CMI, marked by a recognizable set of neurological symptoms. The primary feature is intermittent occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, observed in more than 80% of patients. Adults often exhibit more pronounced symptoms than children, particularly when syringomyelia is also present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in 15%–26% of individuals and most commonly manifests as oropharyngeal impairments, including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are being acknowledged more frequently as complications associated with CMI. In a prospective evaluation involving 90 adult individuals with CMI, half were diagnosed with sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. A central apnoea index of 5 or greater was detected in 6.7% of participants, but true central sleep apnoea was reported in only a single patient, possibly indicating compression of the brainstem. These respiratory issues are not restricted to sleep disorders; a series of cases highlighted instances of acute respiratory insufficiency and severe hypoxaemia in persons with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in this context arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, and disruption of the neural respiratory control centers, which produces episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome, often referred to as 'Ondine’s curse', and are distinct from the congenital central hypoventilation syndrome caused by PHOX2B gene mutations. Contemporary guidelines highlight the significance of this relationship by advising routine polysomnography for individuals with CMI who exhibit possible sleep-related breathing disorders. Importantly, decompression of the posterior fossa may restore normal CSF dynamics and improve respiratory symptoms, illustrating the critical need to identify CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory disturbances.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early forties visited our family medicine clinic, reporting a seven-year history of recurring episodes of choking that happened unpredictably during both meals and sleep. These events involved both solid and liquid foods equally. She characterized the episodes as abrupt and distressing, frequently disrupting her meals or awakening her with forceful coughing and gasping for air, though she never experienced total airway blockage. Nocturnal episodes occurred three to four times weekly, and at times occurred twice within a single night. Over the years, she underwent extensive investigations, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all of which yielded normal findings. Despite these negative results, her symptoms remained unchanged. Social consequences were substantial; she avoided dining out and social events due to concern about choking in public. The persistent anxiety and embarrassment contributed to progressive social isolation and increasing emotional discomfort.

A more comprehensive review of her medical history showed that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, after she was involved in a minor car accident during which she briefly lost consciousness. In the aftermath, she began to experience ongoing headaches in the occipital and vertex regions. These headaches were described as being steady, having a sensation of pressure, and were frequently confined to a coin-sized spot at the vertex. Activities such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers did not worsen the headaches. She was seen by several physicians, who diagnosed her with nummular headache and administered numerous local scalp injections; these treatments provided only short-term improvement. The most notable aspect was significant allodynia at the vertex, such that the mere presence of her hair was enough to provoke discomfort. She eventually learned that keeping her hair cut short reduced her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10. However, this choice ran counter to cultural norms and led to feelings of emotional distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started tracking her oxygen levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Throughout several months, she frequently recorded episodes in which her oxygen saturation fell to between 70% and 80% without any accompanying symptoms. She generally became aware of these events only upon checking her pulse oximeter. Various emergency department visits ultimately attributed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly developed pronounced dizziness accompanied by nausea. Medical staff at the scene recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in refusal to board the aircraft. The provision of supplemental oxygen rapidly alleviated all symptoms in a matter of minutes. Later assessments by pulmonology, including chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal findings.

Considering the longstanding occipital and vertex headache, along with escalating choking spells and unaccounted hypoxia, a search for a single neurological explanation was initiated. Her past medical documentation included a non-contrast cranial CT conducted 14 years prior after her accident, which had originally been interpreted as unremarkable. On closer re-examination, however, the imaging demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and an absence of cerebrospinal fluid around the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with accompanying laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected due to the presence of choking manifestations. However, the diagnosis was ruled out based on unremarkable pH monitoring, unremarkable endoscopic examinations, and failure to improve after comprehensive acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered because of bulbar symptoms. However, the diagnosis was deemed improbable given negative antibody results, no evidence of fatigability, and no diurnal symptom fluctuation.

Comprehensive assessment for primary pulmonary disease was undertaken after hypoxic events. Intrinsic lung pathology was ruled out based on unremarkable chest imaging and pulmonary function test findings.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly indicated by the maintenance of intentional respiration while involuntary breathing was compromised during periods of distraction or sleep. Its onset after head trauma set it apart from congenital variants.

CMI-related brainstem involvement accounted for the entire clinical presentation by causing pressure on the respiratory centers and nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby delivering a cohesive explanation for the diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of our patient’s symptoms following a motor vehicle accident merits thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is recognized in the literature as both a developmental disorder present from birth and as a consequence of head injuries or other neurological events. Modern etiological frameworks (2022) describe this range, differentiating between primary (congenital) Chiari malformations and those that are secondary or acquired due to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained right after the incident showed a densely packed foramen magnum. Due to the absence of imaging prior to the trauma, it is unclear if this result was directly caused by the injury or simply disclosed a preexisting condition. The rapid emergence of occipital headache immediately after the accident, with subsequent gradual appearance of bulbar and respiratory manifestations over a span of 14 years, may indicate either (1) herniation of the cerebellar tonsils as a direct result of trauma or (2) trauma aggravating or revealing an otherwise silent congenital anomaly.

This case illustrates the intricate respiratory features associated with CMI and contributes to our comprehension of acquired central hypoventilation patterns arising from structural pathology in the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic breathing is pivotal for interpreting the respiratory physiology of CMI. In individuals with CMI, cortical pathways responsible for voluntary respiration are usually preserved. Conversely, automatic breathing—governed by brainstem centers and crucial during sleep—is impaired owing to compression of medullary regions by tonsillar herniation. Our patient maintained the capacity for voluntary ventilation but lacked the drive for automatic respiration, a presentation that mirrors congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though stemming from an alternate underlying process. The incident at the airport, in which a brief lapse in attention led to severe desaturation absent any sensation of dyspnea, powerfully demonstrates this targeted breakdown of automatic control: the patient, in effect, “forgets to breathe.”

We propose that underlying dysfunction within the brainstem accounts for this patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea, even during marked episodes of oxygen desaturation. The suspected brainstem abnormality most likely impedes essential ascending neural pathways that transmit sensory information to the cortex, where conscious perception is generated. Breathlessness is a component of interoception, requiring afferent signals to be integrated by major brain areas such as the anterior insular cortex. Interruption of these signals within the brainstem could prevent the cortex from receiving adequate input, thereby explaining the absence of the sensation of air hunger and the patient’s dangerous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The significant gap between the intensity of this patient’s symptoms and the modest polysomnographic result (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was employed instead of the more comprehensive type I, resulting in omission of EEG monitoring. Consequently, the lack of EEG prevented precise determination of the total time the patient was asleep, probably causing notable underreporting of respiratory events. In the absence of EEG, AHI is calculated using total recording time, encompassing both sleep and wakefulness, rather than being based solely on actual sleep duration. As a result, this methodological limitation means the AHI value of 6.0 almost certainly underrepresents the actual extent of sleep-disordered breathing.

In spite of this limitation, multiple findings are suggestive of central hypoventilation: (1) a majority of the events were central in nature (72%) even though the total AHI was low; (2) there were recorded instances of oxygen saturations falling to 70%–80% without dyspnoea or the patient being aware (observed at the airport and in typical clinical settings); and (3) imaging revealed notable anatomical compromise of the brainstem, specifically an 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A Type I polysomnography—including comprehensive EEG assessment, end-tidal CO2 monitoring, and video documentation—has been scheduled at an outside sleep laboratory. This evaluation is awaiting finalization, as it depends on aligning the patient’s availability, the patient’s consent to undergo testing, and available appointment times at the remote centre. Upon completion, this study is expected to yield more conclusive data on sleep structure, respiratory dynamics, and to clarify both the presence and the extent of central hypoventilation.

We note that, based on current information, central hypoventilation remains a strongly supported clinical consideration rather than an unequivocally established diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, impacting both solid and liquid foods to the same extent and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, was the patient's most troubling complaint. A prospective analysis utilizing standardized swallowing assessments demonstrated dysfunction in 36% of cases, with neurogenic causes being most frequently observed. The uniformity in difficulty across various consistencies helps to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical forms. The patient's principal symptom underwent repeated evaluations by both gastroenterology and otolaryngology, which yielded normal results; however, a definitive diagnosis was not established for several years. This diagnostic delay underscores a crucial clinical point: in the absence of identifiable structural abnormalities on endoscopy, dysphagia or choking should raise suspicion for a neurological etiology. According to a retrospective study, the incidence of CMI among children with ongoing dysphagia was 3.3%, significantly greater than the 0.1%–0.77% reported in the general pediatric population. These findings imply that sustained dysphagia may serve as a prominent marker for underlying CMI.

The patient described experiencing pain at the vertex of the scalp, which improved after cutting her hair—an observation that is intriguing yet not extensively explored in the literature. Occipital neuralgia may present alongside CMI and is known to produce hypersensitivity of the scalp. The diagnostic guidelines specify the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia in response to harmless stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Despite this, occipital neuralgia’s relationship to CMI is infrequently documented, and definitive evidence confirming a causal connection remains absent.

A recently identified condition termed ‘burning vertex syndrome’ offers another explanation, characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align clearly with established primary headache disorders such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The underlying mechanism is thought to involve small fiber nerve pathways within the scalp, and patients show mixed responses to greater occipital nerve block interventions. In the present case, the vertex-related manifestations could be attributed to: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) an incidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) an unrelated primary headache entity. There is a notable deficit in systematic reporting of cutaneous allodynia within CMI cohorts, highlighted by the previous lack of assessment tools targeting specific symptomatology in CMI prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Owing to this limited evidence base, we offer this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than asserting it as a definitive diagnostic criterion.

This case demonstrates the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when its presentation includes unusual or ambiguous symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a 14-year period before the correct diagnosis was reached. The process involved early uncertainty and a gradual progression toward accurately identifying the underlying neurological disorder, which is frequently observed in published accounts.
