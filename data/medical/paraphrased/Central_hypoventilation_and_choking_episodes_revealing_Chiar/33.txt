Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early 40s experienced recurrent episodes of choking with both solid and liquid intake over a 7-year period, along with nocturnal choking events occurring 3 to 4 times per week, and had a history of chronic occipital headaches for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home monitoring unexpectedly identified severe episodes of oxygen desaturation (to 70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Repeated laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations to assess the cause of choking did not show any abnormalities. A retrospective review of a cranial CT scan from 14 years earlier—originally performed for trauma—identified an overlooked crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the brain and cervical spine established a diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with cerebellar tonsillar herniation of 11 mm. Polysomnography detected mild sleep apnoea, reflected by an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, mostly due to central events (72%). This report underscores that CMI may present with respiratory symptoms closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), and highlights the necessity of evaluating for structural brain lesions in patients presenting with unexplained respiratory problems.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending 5 mm or more below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI is present in about 1% of adults, while clinically apparent cases occur in roughly 0.1% of the overall population. There is a slight predominance among females, with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases are identified in individuals in their twenties.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between the size of neural elements and the space provided. Nevertheless, recent findings question the classic view of CMI as inherently abnormal, suggesting the presence of a continuum that spans from benign anatomical differences to significant malformations with clinical impact. Modern volumetric analyses indicate notable demographic variation in posterior fossa dimensions, yet these differences often lack a clear association with symptoms. This evolving perspective calls for tailored, symptom-focused clinical approaches, separating asymptomatic anatomical variations from true pathological conditions that warrant surgical management.

Chiari syndrome reflects the clinical manifestation of CMI, displaying a specific set of neurological symptoms. The principal complaint is an episodic occipital headache triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, which occurs in more than 80% of patients. Adults usually exhibit more pronounced symptoms than pediatric patients, particularly when syringomyelia is also present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves has been observed in 15%–26% of cases, most often appearing as oropharyngeal impairment, including palatal weakness, tongue wasting, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary involvement is now acknowledged as an emerging complication associated with CMI. In a prospective cohort involving 90 adults diagnosed with CMI, half demonstrated sleep-associated respiratory abnormalities. Specifically, 6.7% of subjects exhibited a central apnoea index equal to or greater than 5; however, isolated central sleep apnoea was identified in just a single patient, possibly indicative of direct brainstem compromise. Respiratory disorders in CMI are not confined to disturbances during sleep; a case series reported episodes of acute respiratory insufficiency and severe hypoxaemia among affected individuals.

Respiratory dysfunction in this context arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow, and impairment of the respiratory control centers, which results in episodic respiratory symptoms. These manifestations constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—occasionally referred to as 'Ondine’s curse'—and are clinically separate from the congenital form linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Recent recommendations highlight this relationship, advising routine polysomnography in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) presenting with possible sleep-disordered breathing. Importantly, posterior fossa decompression can restore physiological CSF circulation and potentially alleviate respiratory disturbances, reinforcing the necessity to consider CMI when evaluating unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A female in her early 40s visited our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes, which manifested unpredictably during eating and sleeping and affected both solid and liquid foods equally. She characterized these events as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting her meals or awakening her from sleep with intense bouts of coughing and gasping; however, she had never experienced complete airway obstruction. Nocturnal occurrences were noted three to four times weekly, occasionally happening twice in a single night. Throughout the years, she underwent multiple diagnostic assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Despite thorough investigation and absence of abnormal findings, her symptoms remained unchanged. These persistent episodes had considerable social ramifications, prompting her to avoid dining out and social events out of concern for choking in public. This continued fear and humiliation contributed to increasing isolation and psychological distress.

A comprehensive clinical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, after a minor motor vehicle accident in which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon afterwards, she experienced continuous headaches located in the occipital and vertex regions. She characterized these as constant, pressure-like, and frequently confined to a coin-sized spot at the vertex. The headaches were not aggravated by actions such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Several clinicians diagnosed her with nummular headache, and she received numerous local scalp injections, which only provided temporary alleviation. The most notable symptom was significant allodynia at the vertex; even the sensation of her hair's weight would provoke discomfort. Eventually, she found that trimming her hair short diminished her average pain from 6/10 to 3/10, although this choice conflicted with cultural norms and caused her emotional hardship.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient initiated self-assessment of her oxygen saturation levels utilizing a pulse oximeter at home. Across several months, she frequently recorded asymptomatic instances where her oxygen saturation decreased to values between 70% and 80%. She typically became aware of these episodes only after measuring with the device. Several visits to the emergency department resulted in her symptoms being ascribed to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. The patient developed sudden dizziness and nausea as she awaited her flight. Airport medical staff recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being refused boarding. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms entirely subsided within several minutes. Later pulmonary assessment, encompassing chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal findings.

Considering the persistent occipital and vertex headaches alongside increasing episodes of choking and unexplained hypoxia, clinicians sought a single neurological explanation. Upon examination of her earlier medical records, it was found that a non-contrast CT head scan had been carried out 14 years prior, after her injury, and was originally interpreted as unremarkable. On thorough retrospective analysis, this scan showed evidence of a crowded foramen magnum and absence of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the choking episodes, gastro-esophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was suspected at first. However, this was ruled out due to normal findings on pH monitoring and endoscopic examination, as well as no improvement after maximal acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered as a potential diagnosis because the patient exhibited bulbar symptoms. However, the diagnosis was deemed improbable given the negative antibody results, absence of fatigability, and no diurnal fluctuation of symptoms.

After episodes of hypoxia, a thorough assessment for primary pulmonary conditions was conducted. Normal findings on chest imaging and pulmonary function testing ruled out intrinsic pulmonary disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome, also known as acquired Ondine’s curse, was highly suspected given that voluntary respiration remained intact, whereas automatic respiratory drive was compromised when the patient was distracted or asleep. The onset after head trauma indicated an acquired etiology, differentiating it from congenital variants.

The symptoms were ultimately accounted for by brainstem involvement resulting from CMI, which compressed the respiratory centers and nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby offering a cohesive diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of this patient’s symptoms following the motor vehicle accident merits thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is known to occur congenitally, but it can also develop following head injuries or other neurological events. Current etiological frameworks (2022) reflect this range by differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired types that are associated with trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, a CT scan conducted right after the incident showed a congested foramen magnum. Because there is no imaging available from before the trauma, it is unclear whether this finding is a result of the injury or was simply exposed by it. The acute occurrence of occipital headache immediately after the accident, with gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory symptoms over a 14-year period, may indicate either (1) tonsillar herniation caused by the trauma, or (2) trauma precipitating or revealing a pre-existing, asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory complications associated with CMI and contributes to a deeper understanding of acquired central hypoventilation seen in structural brainstem disease. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is essential when examining the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. While voluntary respiration, directed by cortical pathways, generally remains unaffected in individuals with CMI, the automatic drive to breathe—governed by brainstem centers and necessary for respiration during sleep—is impaired due to tonsillar impingement on medullary regions. Our patient demonstrated preserved voluntary control of breathing but lacked the automatic respiratory drive, a pattern resembling congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit via a distinct mechanism. The incident at the airport, where an episode of brief distraction led to severe oxygen desaturation without any sensation of breathlessness, starkly illustrates this isolated automatic dysfunction: in effect, the patient "forgets to breathe."

We propose that an underlying dysfunction within the brainstem accounts for the patient’s concerning lack of dyspnoea during marked episodes of oxygen desaturation. This brainstem abnormality likely impairs essential afferent neural pathways that travel to the cerebral cortex, which are required to produce conscious awareness. Breathlessness represents a form of interoceptive sensation that relies on the transmission and processing of these afferent signals by crucial cerebral structures such as the anterior insular cortex. By compromising the afferent pathways as they traverse the brainstem, the input necessary for the cortex to produce the sensation of air hunger would not be received, thereby elucidating the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The pronounced gap between the patient’s severe clinical manifestations and the relatively low polysomnographic result (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. For practical reasons, type III polysomnography was performed rather than type I, resulting in the absence of EEG monitoring. Consequently, it was not possible to determine the actual duration of sleep, which most likely led to substantial underreporting of respiratory disturbances. The lack of EEG means that AHI is based on the entire recorded duration, encompassing both sleep and wakefulness, rather than exclusively on sleep periods. As a result of this methodological constraint, the documented AHI of 6.0 is presumed to considerably underrepresent the true extent of sleep-disordered breathing.

In spite of this constraint, multiple findings are indicative of central hypoventilation: (1) central events constitute the majority (72%) despite the relatively low total AHI; (2) recorded oxygen saturation drops to 70%–80% occurred without dyspnoea or the patient's subjective awareness (both at the airport and during standard assessments); and (3) imaging demonstrates substantial brainstem compression, specifically with 11 mm of tonsillar herniation.

A Type I polysomnographic assessment—including comprehensive EEG surveillance, end-tidal CO2 monitoring, and video documentation—has been scheduled at an off-site sleep facility. However, this evaluation has yet to be performed, as it is contingent upon aligning patient availability, patient consent for the procedure, and appointment slots at the distant location. Upon completion, the study will yield more conclusive information regarding sleep stage architecture, breathing dynamics during sleep, and the existence and magnitude of central hypoventilation.

We recognize that, based on the currently available data, central hypoventilation remains a strong clinical suspicion rather than an unequivocally established diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, characterized by equivalent difficulty swallowing both solid and liquid foods and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, proved to be the patient’s most troubling complaint. Prospective studies utilizing standardized swallowing assessments have demonstrated dysfunction in 36% of cases, with neurogenic mechanisms being most frequently observed. The fact that trouble occurs with all types of consistencies points to a neurogenic etiology, as opposed to mechanical dysphagia. Initially, the patient’s primary symptom was extensively examined by gastroenterology and otolaryngology specialists, and all assessments yielded normal results; despite this, her diagnosis remained undetermined for a number of years. Such a delay underscores an essential clinical teaching point: when endoscopic evaluation reveals no structural cause for dysphagia or episodes of choking, a neurological cause should be considered. In a retrospective analysis of pediatric patients experiencing chronic dysphagia, the prevalence of CMI was found to be 3.3%, notably exceeding the reported prevalence in the broader pediatric population, which ranges from 0.1% to 0.77%. This finding suggests that ongoing dysphagia may serve as a significant marker for underlying CMI.

The patient’s experience of vertex pain alleviated by hair cutting illustrates a notable, yet little researched occurrence. Occipital neuralgia may present alongside CMI, often resulting in increased sensitivity of the scalp. Diagnostic criteria for this condition stipulate the presence of dysesthesia and/or allodynia elicited by gentle stimulation of the scalp or hair. Nonetheless, reports linking occipital neuralgia to CMI remain uncommon, and definitive evidence demonstrating a causal relationship is lacking.

Another possibility to consider is the recently identified 'burning vertex syndrome', characterized by scalp pain, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align with recognized primary headache disorders, including migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The underlying pathophysiology is thought to involve small fiber sensory pathways from the scalp, and patient responses to greater occipital nerve blockade have been inconsistent. In this case, the symptoms at the vertex could be attributed to: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) a coincidental presentation of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct, unrelated primary headache disorder. There is a notable gap in the literature concerning systematic evaluation of cutaneous allodynia in CMI populations, as indicated by the prior lack of specific assessment tools for these symptoms before the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Owing to this limited evidence base, we describe this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than a conclusive diagnostic hallmark.

This case illustrates the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when its manifestations are unusual or deceptive, including symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, which resulted in a 14-year lapse before the condition was identified. The process of reaching an accurate neurological diagnosis was marked by early misinterpretation and a slow progression toward recognition, a pattern frequently reported in the literature.
