Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early forties presented with a 7-year history of repeated choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, along with nocturnal choking occurring 3–4 times per week, and chronic occipital headaches present for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home monitoring unexpectedly identified episodes of marked oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies were performed to investigate her choking, but yielded normal results. Retrospective examination of a head CT obtained 14 years earlier for unrelated trauma revealed an unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. MRI of the brain and cervical spine subsequently demonstrated Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography identified mild sleep apnea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0), predominantly with central events (72%). This case highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underlining the importance of evaluating for structural brain abnormalities in patients with unexplained respiratory compromise.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by a downward extension of the cerebellar tonsils measuring at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI is present in about 1% of adults, yet only around 0.1% of the general population experience symptoms. The disorder exhibits a slight female bias with a ratio of 1.3:1 and most often becomes clinically apparent in individuals in their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between the neural elements and the space provided. Nonetheless, recent research calls into question the long-held view of CMI as inherently abnormal, suggesting it may represent a continuum ranging from benign anatomical variations to malformations with clinical relevance. Modern volumetric analyses reveal marked demographic diversity in posterior fossa size without reliable association with clinical symptoms. This evolving perspective emphasizes the need for tailored, symptom-oriented management, differentiating incidental anatomical findings from conditions warranting operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the set of symptoms caused by CMI, marked by a characteristic pattern of neurological signs. The most prominent clinical feature is an episodic occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, observed in more than 80% of patients. Symptom severity tends to be greater in adults, particularly when syringomyelia is also present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in 15%–26% of individuals, often manifesting as oropharyngeal issues such as weakness of the palate, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary involvement is being acknowledged with growing frequency as a complication of CMI. In a prospective cohort of 90 adults diagnosed with CMI, half were identified as having sleep-associated breathing disturbances. A central apnoea index of 5 or greater was detected in 6.7% of individuals, though isolated central sleep apnoea was reported in just one patient, possibly due to compression of the brainstem. Such respiratory dysfunction is not limited to disordered sleep; a case series has also reported episodes of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia in patients with CMI.

Respiratory impairment arises from factors such as compression of the brainstem, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and interruption of centers regulating breathing, which result in episodic respiratory disturbances. These clinical features constitute an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, occasionally referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’, which should be differentiated from the congenital variant of central hypoventilation syndrome linked to PHOX2B mutations. Contemporary recommendations highlight this association by advocating for routine polysomnography in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who exhibit suspected sleep-disordered breathing. Notably, surgical decompression of the posterior fossa can re-establish normal CSF circulation and may alleviate respiratory manifestations, highlighting the significance of identifying CMI in individuals with unexplained respiratory failure.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early forties attended our family medicine practice, reporting a seven-year history of unpredictable choking spells during both meals and sleep, equally triggered by solids and liquids. She characterized these episodes as abrupt and alarming, frequently interrupting her eating or waking her from sleep with bouts of forceful coughing and gasping, but never progressing to total airway blockage. Episodes at night occurred three to four times weekly, and occasionally twice within a single night. Throughout the years, she underwent multiple assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two esophagogastroduodenoscopies, all of which were unremarkable. Her symptoms, however, remained unchanged. The effect on her personal life was notable; she began avoiding dining out and attending social events out of concern for experiencing choking publicly. As a consequence, her ongoing anxiety and embarrassment led to progressive social isolation and psychological distress.

A more comprehensive clinical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, after she experienced a minor motor vehicle collision resulting in a brief loss of consciousness. Soon after, she developed continuous headaches centered over the occipital and vertex regions. She characterized the pain as steady, with a sensation of pressure, typically confined to a coin-sized spot at the vertex. The headaches were not worsened by activities such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. She received a diagnosis of nummular headache from several clinicians and underwent repeated local scalp injections, which provided only short-lived relief. Notably, she experienced pronounced allodynia at the vertex, so much so that even the sensation of her hair resting on the area was uncomfortable. Ultimately, she found that cutting her hair short decreased her usual pain intensity from 6/10 to 3/10, but this choice was at odds with cultural norms and led to additional psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started checking her oxygen saturation levels at home with a personal pulse oximeter. For several months, she consistently recorded asymptomatic drops in saturation, with values falling into the 70%–80% range. She was frequently unaware of these desaturations until measuring with her device. On numerous occasions, emergency assessments ascribed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at a foreign airport. As she awaited boarding, she developed sudden onset vertigo and nausea. Medical staff on location measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, which resulted in her being prohibited from boarding the flight. Her symptoms fully abated within minutes following the application of supplemental oxygen. Later assessment by a pulmonologist, which included imaging of the chest and pulmonary function tests, revealed no abnormalities.

In light of her persistent occipital and vertex headache, increasingly frequent episodes of choking, and unexplained hypoxia, clinicians sought a single neurological explanation for her symptoms. On reviewing her prior medical documentation, it was found that she had undergone a non-contrast head CT 14 years before, in the aftermath of her accident, and at that time, the imaging was interpreted as unremarkable. Nonetheless, upon a meticulous reassessment, the scan demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and diminished cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. These imaging features pointed towards Chiari malformation type I (CMI), which had not been recognized in the initial interpretation. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the patient's choking symptoms, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected. However, this diagnosis was ruled out based on normal findings from pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations, as well as the absence of improvement despite maximal acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was evaluated due to symptoms affecting the bulbar region. Negative antibody testing, lack of fatigability and absence of diurnal variation made this unlikely.

Primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated following hypoxic episodes. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests excluded intrinsic lung disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly suspected due to maintained voluntary respiration with disrupted automatic respiratory drive during periods of sleep or distraction. Its onset after head trauma set it apart from the congenital variants.

Brainstem pathology secondary to CMI ultimately explained all symptoms through compression of the respiratory centres and lower cranial nerve nuclei, providing the unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The close timing of this patient’s symptoms following the motor vehicle collision merits thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is recognized to occur as a congenital disorder, but it has also been reported following head injuries or other neurologic trauma. Current etiological frameworks (2022) delineate this range by differentiating congenital, or primary, Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired types that may arise due to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained directly following the injury revealed a crowded foramen magnum. As there are no imaging studies from prior to the trauma, it is unclear whether this abnormality was precipitated by the incident or if the injury simply brought a pre-existing condition to attention. The abrupt appearance of occipital headaches after the accident, with subsequent gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory symptoms over a 14-year period, may indicate either: (1) trauma-related tonsillar herniation, or (2) trauma amplifying or disclosing a congenital anomaly that was previously without symptoms.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory features associated with CMI and contributes to a broader comprehension of acquired central hypoventilation patterns arising from structural lesions of the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is pivotal to appreciating the respiratory pathophysiology of CMI. While voluntary respiration, governed by the cerebral cortex, is generally preserved in individuals with CMI, automatic breathing—regulated by brainstem centers and critical during sleep—becomes impaired as a result of tonsillar herniation compressing medullary regions. Our patient maintained the ability for voluntary ventilation, yet exhibited absent automatic respiratory drive, a clinical picture that parallels congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit stemming from a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, where a brief distraction resulted in severe oxygen desaturation in the absence of dyspnea, dramatically demonstrates this isolated disruption of automatic respiratory control: the patient, in effect, 'forgets to breathe.'

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea, even during marked hypoxaemia, results from a fundamental dysfunction within the brainstem. Such a disturbance likely interferes with essential ascending sensory pathways required to reach cortical areas responsible for conscious perception. Breathlessness represents an interoceptive sensation that depends upon the transmission and integration of these sensory signals within specific brain structures, such as the anterior insular cortex. If these afferent inputs are disrupted as they pass through the brainstem, the cortex fails to receive the information needed to perceive air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s perilous unawareness of significant respiratory compromise.

The substantial mismatch between the pronounced clinical manifestations in our patient and the modest polysomnography results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was utilized instead of type I; consequently, EEG data were not obtained. The lack of EEG monitoring precluded precise assessment of total sleep duration, probably resulting in a notable undercount of respiratory events. Since EEG was not available, AHI was determined based on the total recording time, which incorporates intervals of wakefulness and not just actual sleep. As a result, this methodological constraint suggests that the reported AHI of 6.0 likely fails to capture the full extent of the patient's sleep-related respiratory disturbances.

Nevertheless, there are multiple findings suggestive of central hypoventilation: (1) a majority of respiratory events were central in origin (72%), even though the total AHI was relatively low; (2) recorded episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% occurred without symptoms of dyspnoea or the patient's recognition (noted both in the airport setting and during regular evaluations); and (3) imaging revealed marked brainstem compression, characterized by an 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A Type I polysomnographic evaluation, incorporating comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation, has been scheduled at an offsite sleep center. The study has not yet been conducted, as it requires alignment of the patient's schedule, consent to undergo the procedure, and appointment availability at the external location. Upon completion, this assessment will yield more detailed information regarding sleep structure, respiratory activity, and the extent as well as the degree of central hypoventilation.

We acknowledge that, given the current evidence, central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis, rather than a definitively confirmed diagnosis.

Our patient experienced oropharyngeal dysphagia involving similar difficulty with both solid and liquid foods, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing; this was her most troubling complaint. A prospective study utilizing validated tools for swallowing evaluation revealed that 36% of patients showed evidence of dysfunction, with neurogenic types being most common. The presence of equal impairment across different food textures helps differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical forms. Despite this, the patient's primary and most bothersome symptom was initially—and on repeated occasions—evaluated by both gastroenterology and otolaryngology services, with findings consistently within normal limits; this led to several years’ delay in establishing the correct diagnosis. The prolonged time to diagnosis underscores a critical clinical teaching point: when dysphagia or episodes of choking persist and endoscopic examination does not reveal a structural abnormality, a neurologic etiology should be suspected. In a retrospective series, the rate of CMI among children with chronic dysphagia was found to be 3.3%, much greater than the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence documented for the broader paediatric population. This difference supports the idea that ongoing dysphagia may serve as a significant sign of possible underlying CMI.

The patient's description of vertex pain alleviated by cutting hair highlights an unusual and insufficiently explored observation. While occipital neuralgia has been documented in connection with CMI and may lead to scalp hypersensitivity, diagnosis mandates the presence of "dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair." Nonetheless, reports linking occipital neuralgia to CMI are infrequent, and there is currently no definitive evidence confirming a causal relationship.

Another diagnostic possibility is the recently characterized ‘burning vertex syndrome’, which manifests as pain on the scalp, cutaneous allodynia, and symptoms that do not align clearly with recognized primary headache categories such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology may involve small fiber sensory pathways originating from the scalp, and clinical response to greater occipital nerve blockade appears to be inconsistent among patients. In the current case, symptoms at the vertex could indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) a coincidental presentation of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache entity altogether. There is a notable lack of systematic reporting on patterns of cutaneous allodynia in individuals with CMI, which is underscored by the prior absence of a validated tool for evaluating these symptoms in CMI prior to the advent of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Due to these limitations in available evidence, we report this finding as a noteworthy clinical observation rather than assigning it as a specific diagnostic marker.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI when it manifests with uncommon or confusing clinical features, such as central hypoventilation and choking episodes, which in this instance resulted in a 14-year delay before the correct diagnosis was reached. The trajectory toward identifying the neurological cause, marked by early diagnostic uncertainty followed by eventual clarification, has frequently been described in previous reports.
