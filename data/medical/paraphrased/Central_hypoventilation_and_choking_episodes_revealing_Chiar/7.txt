Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early 40s experienced recurrent choking on both solids and liquids over a 7-year period, accompanied by nocturnal choking episodes 3–4 times per week, as well as a 14-year history of persistent occipital headaches. During home oxygen monitoring conducted amid the COVID-19 pandemic, she was found to have intermittent episodes of marked oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) in the absence of apparent respiratory distress. Despite multiple normal laryngoscopic and endoscopic examinations undertaken for her choking symptoms, no abnormalities were detected. Retrospective review of a cranial CT scan, obtained 14 years earlier following head trauma, revealed a crowded foramen magnum that had previously gone unrecognized. Subsequent MRI imaging of the brain and cervical spine confirmed Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with cerebellar tonsils herniating 11 mm below the foramen magnum. Polysomnography showed mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0 and a predominance of central respiratory events (72%). This case underscores that CMI may manifest primarily with respiratory symptoms closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the necessity of evaluating for underlying structural brain pathologies in cases of otherwise unexplained respiratory disturbances.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the descent of the cerebellar tonsils extending at least 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum as observed on sagittal MRI scans. Recent imaging data indicate a prevalence of CMI in about 1% of adults, whereas clinically significant symptoms are observed in roughly 0.1% of all individuals. The disorder is seen more frequently in females, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, and most often manifests in individuals during their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, which leads to a spatial discrepancy between neural elements and the space enclosing them. Nonetheless, modern research calls into question the conventional view of CMI as inherently pathological, instead suggesting that it exists along a continuum ranging from normal anatomical variation to serious clinical malformations. Recent analyses employing volumetric techniques have revealed marked demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions that do not reliably correspond with symptom presentation. This evolving perspective underscores the importance of tailoring clinical decisions to the individual, emphasizing assessment based on symptoms to differentiate between benign anatomical variants and those pathological cases that warrant surgical management.

Chiari syndrome constitutes the clinical presentation of CMI, marked by a specific set of neurological symptoms. The most prominent feature is an episodic headache localized to the occipital region, often triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, and occurs in more than 80% of individuals. Symptom severity tends to be greater in adults compared to pediatric patients, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in 15%–26% of cases, most commonly manifesting as oropharyngeal dysfunction, which may include palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now more widely acknowledged as a complication associated with CMI. In a prospective cohort of 90 adult individuals diagnosed with CMI, half were identified as having sleep-associated breathing disorders. A central apnoea index of 5 or higher was detected in 6.7% of participants, though isolated central sleep apnoea was noted in just one instance, which may be indicative of compression at the brainstem. Importantly, respiratory complications are not confined to nocturnal disturbances; one case series highlighted the occurrence of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal, hypoxaemia among patients with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in this context is driven by mechanisms such as compression of the brainstem, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and interference with the neural centres responsible for respiratory regulation, resulting in episodic respiratory disturbances. These manifestations correspond to an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which differs from the congenital form that is linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Current guidelines highlight this association by advocating for systematic use of polysomnography in individuals with CMI who present with suspected sleep-related breathing disorders. Notably, posterior fossa decompression has been shown to normalize CSF dynamics and may alleviate respiratory symptoms, thereby highlighting the clinical significance of identifying CMI in cases of otherwise unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s visited our family medicine clinic with a longstanding, 7-year complaint of unpredictable choking episodes that took place during both eating and sleeping. These events affected the ingestion of both solid foods and liquids, without preference. She described the episodes as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting her meals or awakening her at night with forceful bouts of coughing and attempts to catch her breath; however, she had never experienced a complete blockage of her airway. Nighttime occurrences happened approximately three to four times per week, with some nights involving two episodes. Throughout this period, she underwent numerous diagnostic procedures, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Nevertheless, her symptoms showed no improvement. The effects on her social life were considerable—her fear of experiencing a choking episode in public led her to shun dining out and attending social events. This persistent worry and sense of embarrassment progressively contributed to her social isolation and emotional suffering.

A thorough review of her clinical background indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, subsequent to a minor car accident in which she briefly lost consciousness. In the aftermath, she experienced ongoing headaches localized to the occipital and vertex regions. She characterized the pain as continuous, with a pressure-like sensation, frequently confined to a coin-sized spot at the vertex. These headaches did not intensify with activities such as coughing, straining, or performing Valsalva manoeuvres. Multiple healthcare providers assessed her as having nummular headache, leading to several local scalp injections, which produced only short-lived symptom improvement. Notably, she exhibited significant allodynia at the vertex, such that even the weight of her hair provoked discomfort. Ultimately, she found that cutting her hair short lessened her baseline pain intensity from 6/10 to 3/10, although this choice ran counter to cultural norms and was a source of psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started tracking her oxygen saturation at home with a pulse oximeter. Over the course of several months, she consistently recorded instances of asymptomatic oxygen saturation falling into the 70%–80% range. She frequently became aware of these episodes only upon consulting her device. On several occasions, emergency assessments concluded that her symptoms were related to anxiety.

An important event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her departure, she was suddenly struck by severe dizziness and nausea. Medical staff on the scene measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being refused boarding. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms abated entirely in a matter of minutes. Follow-up assessment of her lungs, encompassing chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed no abnormalities.

In light of her persistent occipital and vertex headaches, escalating episodes of choking, and unexplained hypoxia, efforts were made to identify a single neurological explanation for her symptoms. Examination of her prior medical history showed that a non-contrast CT head scan had been conducted 14 years prior, after her accident, and had originally been interpreted as normal. Nevertheless, upon a meticulous retrospective review, the scan demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum along with a reduction in cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the presence of choking symptoms, an initial diagnosis of gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was contemplated. However, this possibility was ruled out due to unremarkable pH monitoring, unremarkable findings on endoscopy, and no improvement with comprehensive acid-suppressive therapy.

Assessment for myasthenia gravis was undertaken owing to the presence of bulbar symptoms. However, this diagnosis was considered improbable because antibody assays were negative, there was no observed fatigability, and symptoms did not exhibit diurnal fluctuation.

Episodes of hypoxia prompted thorough evaluation for primary pulmonary pathology. As chest imaging and pulmonary function assessments were within normal limits, intrinsic lung disease was effectively ruled out.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by intact voluntary respiration alongside compromised automatic breathing during periods of sleep or when the patient was distracted. Its onset subsequent to head trauma set it apart from congenital variants of the condition.

The underlying cause of all symptoms was attributed to brainstem pathology resulting from CMI, as this led to compression of the respiratory centers and the nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby offering a comprehensive explanation for the clinical presentation.

Discussion:

It is important to examine closely the timing of our patient’s symptom development following the motor vehicle accident. Chiari malformation type I is recognized not only as a congenital disorder but also as a possible consequence of head trauma or other forms of neurological injury. Recent aetiological classifications2022 acknowledge this range, differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that arise due to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, the CT scan obtained directly following the accident showed a narrowed foramen magnum. Due to the absence of pre-injury imaging, it is not possible to ascertain if this was a pre-existing condition that the trauma exposed, or if the injury itself led to its development. The abrupt appearance of occipital headaches right after the incident, and the subsequent gradual onset of bulbar and respiratory symptoms across a 14-year period, may be indicative of either: (1) tonsillar herniation triggered by the trauma, or (2) trauma that either aggravated or made apparent an otherwise latent congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the multifaceted respiratory complications associated with CMI, providing deeper insight into the mechanisms underlying acquired central hypoventilation in the context of structural brainstem abnormalities. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiratory control is key to interpreting the respiratory dysfunctions seen in CMI. While voluntary respiration—governed by cortical circuits—tends to be preserved among individuals with CMI, automatic respiration, which depends on brainstem centers and is especially critical during sleep, is impaired as a result of compression of medullary regions by tonsillar herniation. In our patient, voluntary respiratory effort was maintained, but the capacity for automatic breathing was absent, producing a clinical picture analogous to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome but stemming from a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, during which a brief lapse in attention led to significant oxygen desaturation in the absence of dyspnoea, starkly demonstrates the selective breakdown of automatic respiratory control—effectively, the patient ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient's striking lack of dyspnoea in the face of marked oxygen desaturation stems from a fundamental dysfunction within the brainstem. Such a brainstem abnormality likely disrupts essential ascending afferent neural pathways, preventing these signals from reaching the cortex and generating conscious awareness. The perception of breathlessness represents an aspect of interoception that relies upon the transmission and processing of these afferent signals by specific cortical areas, notably the anterior insular cortex. If these pathways are compromised within the brainstem, the cortex would fail to receive sufficient afferent input required to evoke the sensation of air hunger, thus accounting for the patient’s dangerously diminished recognition of their physiological compromise.

The pronounced gap between the intensity of our patient’s clinical symptoms and the comparatively low AHI value of 6.0 observed on polysomnography requires clarification. For logistical reasons, we employed type III polysomnography rather than the more comprehensive type I, which incorporates EEG monitoring. The lack of EEG data impeded precise measurement of actual sleep duration, thereby increasing the risk of substantially undercounting respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG, the AHI is determined based on the entire recording period, which encompasses both sleep and wakefulness, as opposed to being restricted to periods of sleep. Consequently, this methodological constraint suggests that the documented AHI of 6.0 does not accurately reflect the true burden of the patient's sleep-disordered breathing.

In spite of this limitation, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central events comprise the majority (72%) even though the total AHI remains low; (2) observed oxygen desaturations reaching 70%–80% occur without reported dyspnoea or patient consciousness of these episodes (as noted at the airport and during standard evaluations); and (3) anatomical imaging reveals substantial brainstem compression, with tonsillar herniation measuring 11 mm.

A Type I polysomnography, incorporating comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation, has been scheduled at an outside sleep laboratory. The execution of this assessment, however, is awaiting synchronization of the patient's schedule, their consent to the procedure, and appointment availability at the distant location. Upon its completion, the study will yield more conclusive insights into the patient's sleep structure, breathing patterns, and the extent and severity of central hypoventilation.

It should be noted that, based on the currently available data, central hypoventilation remains a robustly supported clinical suspicion, rather than an unequivocally established diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, characterized by comparable difficulty in swallowing both solids and liquids and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, emerged as the predominant and most troubling symptom for our patient. A prospective assessment utilizing standardized swallowing evaluation tools identified dysfunction in 36% of cases, with the majority displaying neurogenic features. The fact that all food textures are equally problematic serves to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from its mechanical counterpart. Despite repeated investigations by both gastroenterology and otolaryngology specialists, and in the absence of abnormal findings, the patient’s diagnosis remained elusive for several years. This protracted diagnostic timeline underlines an essential clinical point: in instances where dysphagia or choking occurs without an identifiable structural lesion during endoscopy, a neurological etiology should be considered. In one retrospective cohort of children suffering from persistent dysphagia, the prevalence of CMI was documented at 3.3%—significantly surpassing the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence seen in the broader pediatric demographic. These data underscore that ongoing dysphagia may serve as an important clinical clue pointing to undiagnosed CMI.

The patient's account of experiencing pain localized to the vertex, which subsides following haircuts, highlights a curious yet largely unexplored clinical feature. Occipital neuralgia has been observed alongside CMI, sometimes manifesting as heightened scalp sensitivity. According to diagnostic standards, the condition involves 'dysesthesia and/or allodynia elicited by benign stimulation of the scalp and/or hair.' Nonetheless, reports connecting occipital neuralgia specifically to CMI are infrequent, and there remains a lack of definitive evidence to support a direct causal relationship.

Another perspective to consider is the newly recognized ‘burning vertex syndrome,’ which is characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align precisely with existing primary headache categories, such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The underlying pathophysiology is proposed to involve small fiber nerve pathways originating within the scalp, with patient responses to greater occipital nerve blockade being inconsistent. For our patient, the symptoms localized to the vertex could be indicative of: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) a coincidental presentation of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct, unrelated primary headache disorder. Comprehensive characterization of cutaneous allodynia in CMI cohorts remains limited, highlighted by the lack of standardized assessment tools prior to the creation of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Owing to this notable gap in the literature, we report this observation as a noteworthy clinical occurrence rather than a conclusive diagnostic indicator.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties clinicians face in diagnosing CMI, particularly when the condition manifests through uncommon or ambiguous symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a prolonged diagnostic delay of 14 years. The trajectory toward accurate neurological identification, marked by early uncertainty and eventual clarification, aligns with patterns frequently documented in the literature.
