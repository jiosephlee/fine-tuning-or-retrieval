Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year duration of recurrent choking spells with both solids and liquids, as well as nocturnal choking episodes occurring 3–4 times per week, alongside a 14-year history of persistent occipital headaches. During home monitoring conducted amidst the COVID-19 pandemic, she was found to have episodes of marked oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Repeated laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations performed to determine the cause of the choking episodes yielded normal findings. On retrospective review, a CT head scan acquired 14 years prior for unrelated trauma showed evidence of a crowded foramen magnum that had not been previously recognized. Further assessment with MRI of the head and neck demonstrated Chiari malformation type I (CMI), with a descent of the cerebellar tonsils by 11 mm. Polysomnography identified mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, consisting mainly of central events (72%). This report highlights that CMI may present with respiratory symptoms resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), and underscores the need to evaluate for possible structural brain lesions in patients presenting with unexplained disturbances of respiration.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by cerebellar tonsils extending 5 mm or more below the foramen magnum on sagittal MRI imaging. Recent imaging data show that CMI affects about 1% of adults, yet only around 0.1% of individuals present with symptoms. There is a slight female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, with most cases manifesting in the third decade of life.

The pathophysiology is primarily attributed to a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, resulting in an imbalance between neural tissue volume and spatial capacity. Nonetheless, current research questions the longstanding view that CMI is invariably abnormal, suggesting a continuum ranging from benign anatomical variations to disorders with clinical consequences. Volumetric analyses have revealed notable demographic differences in posterior fossa size, with no uniform relationship to presenting symptoms. This evolving perspective underscores the importance of tailoring clinical management to each patient’s symptoms, carefully differentiating between incidental anatomical findings and those malformations warranting surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical presentation of CMI, marked by a specific array of neurological symptoms. The principal complaint is sudden-onset headache localized to the occipital region, often triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, and occurring in more than 80% of affected individuals. Adults are usually afflicted with more pronounced manifestations than children, particularly if syringomyelia is also present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves has been observed in 15%–26% of cases, most commonly manifesting as oropharyngeal abnormalities including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are becoming more widely acknowledged as complications associated with CMI. In a prospective analysis involving 90 adult patients diagnosed with CMI, half exhibited sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. A central apnoea index of 5 or greater was detected in 6.7% of participants, yet isolated central sleep apnoea was reported in just one individual, which may indicate compression at the brainstem. Respiratory dysfunction in CMI is not confined to disruptions during sleep; a case series reported acute respiratory failure and critical hypoxaemia among patients with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in this context arises from factors such as compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and interference with the central respiratory control centers, resulting in episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, also referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse,’ which differs from the congenital variant linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Recent recommendations highlight this relationship, advocating for routine polysomnography in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who show indications of sleep-related breathing disturbances. Importantly, posterior fossa decompression may normalize CSF flow and ameliorate respiratory symptoms, highlighting the necessity of considering CMI in patients presenting with unexplained respiratory abnormalities.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s visited our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year history of sporadic choking episodes. These incidents took place unexpectedly while eating or sleeping and affected both solid and liquid foods. She described the events as abrupt and alarming, frequently interrupting meals or waking her from sleep with intense coughing and gasping. While the episodes were disruptive, they did not cause complete airway blockage. Nocturnal choking happened three to four times weekly, with some nights involving two episodes. Throughout the years, she underwent numerous assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all of which yielded normal results. Her symptoms endured in spite of negative diagnostic findings. The consequences socially were considerable; she refrained from visiting restaurants and social events due to apprehension about choking in public. This ongoing worry and embarrassment resulted in progressive social isolation and emotional suffering.

A thorough review of her clinical history indicated that her symptoms first appeared 14 years prior, after she experienced a minor car accident accompanied by a brief episode of loss of consciousness. Soon thereafter, she began having ongoing headaches located in the occipital region and at the vertex. She characterized these headaches as continuous, pressure-like, and frequently confined to a small, coin-sized area at the vertex. The headaches did not worsen with activities such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple clinicians diagnosed her with nummular headache, and she received repeated local scalp injections, which provided only temporary alleviation. The most remarkable symptom was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, such that even the mere weight of her hair would provoke discomfort. Eventually, she learned that keeping her hair cut short lowered her baseline pain from 6 out of 10 to 3 out of 10, although doing so conflicted with her cultural norms and caused her significant emotional strain.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient used a pulse oximeter at home to regularly check her oxygen levels. Over a period of months, she consistently recorded asymptomatic declines in oxygen saturation to values between 70% and 80%. She frequently was unaware of these drops until measuring with her device. Several emergency department visits led clinicians to associate her symptoms with anxiety.

A significant event took place at a major international airport. As she awaited her departure, she suddenly developed severe dizziness accompanied by nausea. Airport medical staff recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prevented from boarding the aircraft. When given supplemental oxygen, her symptoms fully subsided within a few minutes. Later assessments of her pulmonary status, including chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, showed no abnormalities.

In light of her longstanding occipital and vertex headache, worsening episodes of choking, and unexplained episodes of hypoxia, clinicians sought a single neurological explanation. Upon review of her past medical records, it was noted that a non-contrast head CT conducted 14 years prior, post-accident, had been originally interpreted as normal. Nonetheless, a careful reassessment of this imaging later identified a crowded foramen magnum along with diminished cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the patient's choking symptoms, an initial consideration was gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux. However, this diagnosis was ruled out due to normal pH monitoring, unremarkable endoscopic examinations, and absence of improvement despite maximal acid suppressive therapy.

Assessment for myasthenia gravis was performed because of involvement of the bulbar area. However, negative results for antibody testing, no evidence of fatigability, and no diurnal fluctuation in symptoms diminished suspicion for this diagnosis.

Given episodes of hypoxia, underlying lung disorders were thoroughly considered. With unremarkable chest imaging and pulmonary function studies, intrinsic pulmonary disease was ruled out.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by the maintenance of voluntary respiration alongside defective automatic breathing when the patient was distracted or asleep. Its onset after head injury set it apart from the congenital variants.

All symptoms were ultimately accounted for by brainstem involvement due to CMI, as compression of the lower cranial nerve nuclei and respiratory centers supplied a single, comprehensive diagnosis.

Discussion:

The close timing between the patient’s car accident and the development of symptoms requires thoughtful analysis. Chiari malformation type I is reported to occur as a congenital disorder as well as following head injuries and various neurological traumas. Recent aetiological frameworks2022 acknowledge this range by differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that are associated with trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, a CT scan obtained directly after the incident showed a congested foramen magnum. Lacking imaging prior to the trauma, it is impossible to ascertain if the injury was responsible for this observation or simply exposed an existing condition. The prompt appearance of occipital headaches post-trauma, alongside gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory issues spanning 14 years, may indicate either: (1) a tonsillar herniation precipitated by trauma or (2) trauma amplifying or uncovering an asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case demonstrates the intricate respiratory presentations associated with CMI and contributes to a deeper insight into the patterns of acquired central hypoventilation stemming from structural abnormalities in the brainstem. Recognizing the differentiation between voluntary and automatic respiration is key to comprehending the respiratory pathophysiology seen in CMI. In CMI patients, voluntary respiration, reliant on cortical pathways, generally remains functional. Conversely, automatic respiration—which is regulated by brainstem centers and is vital during sleep—can be impaired as a result of compression of medullary structures by the tonsils. Our patient retained the capacity for voluntary breathing but lost automatic respiratory function, presenting a clinical picture similar to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit through a distinct underlying mechanism. The airport incident, wherein a brief moment of inattention led to significant oxygen desaturation without the sensation of breathlessness, serves as a striking example of this selective breakdown of automatic regulation: the patient, in essence, simply 'forgot to breathe'.

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea despite marked episodes of hypoxaemia is attributable to a primary dysfunction within the brainstem. This underlying brainstem abnormality most likely interferes with essential ascending neural pathways responsible for conveying afferent signals to the cortex, which are required for conscious perception. The sensation of breathlessness constitutes a form of interoception, dependent upon the processing of such signals by specific brain regions, notably the anterior insular cortex. Interruption of these afferent pathways at the level of the brainstem would prevent the cortex from receiving the inputs necessary to generate the perception of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The notable gap between the patient’s pronounced clinical manifestations and the modest polysomnography result (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was employed instead of type I; the former does not provide EEG data. The lack of EEG prevented precise measurement of actual sleep duration and probably resulted in substantial undercounting of respiratory disturbances. Since EEG was unavailable, the AHI was calculated based on the total time the device was recording, which includes wake periods, rather than exclusively actual sleep time. Consequently, this methodological shortcoming suggests that the reported AHI of 6.0 does not fully reflect the extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

In spite of this limitation, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central apneic events constitute the majority (72%) even though the total AHI remains low; (2) observed episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% occurred without accompanying dyspnea or the patient's awareness (noted both at the airport and during standard evaluations); and (3) imaging reveals substantial brainstem compression, with an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography—including full EEG, end-tidal CO2 monitoring, and video documentation—has been scheduled at an outside sleep center. Finalization of the assessment awaits alignment of patient scheduling, consent to the procedure, and coordination with the external site. Upon completion, the study is expected to yield clearer delineation of the patient’s sleep structure, breathing dynamics, and the extent and existence of central hypoventilation.

It is recognized that, based on existing information, central hypoventilation is still best considered a strongly supported clinical suspicion, not a definitively established diagnosis.

Our patient’s most troubling complaint was oropharyngeal dysphagia involving both solids and liquids, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. Prospective studies using validated tools to assess swallowing have identified dysfunction in 36% of patients, most commonly displaying a neurogenic pattern. Difficulty swallowing all types of food and liquid, rather than just one consistency, characterizes neurogenic dysphagia and differentiates it from mechanical causes. Despite being the most prominent symptom, repeated evaluations by gastroenterology and otolaryngology revealed no abnormalities, resulting in a prolonged delay in diagnosis spanning several years. This prolonged diagnostic process illustrates a critical point: when dysphagia or choking occurs without apparent structural abnormalities on endoscopic examination, clinicians should consider a neurological etiology. A retrospective review reported the prevalence of CMI in children with persistent dysphagia to be 3.3%, notably exceeding the 0.1%–0.77% range seen in the general pediatric population. This finding supports persistent dysphagia as a potential key clinical clue to underlying CMI.

The patient's description of experiencing pain at the vertex, which improves following hair cutting, illustrates a compelling but largely unexplored clinical presentation. Occipital neuralgia is sometimes observed in conjunction with CMI and may manifest as hypersensitivity of the scalp. Diagnostic guidelines stipulate that patients should exhibit dysesthesia and/or allodynia that become evident upon non-painful contact with the scalp or hair. Nevertheless, reports linking occipital neuralgia with CMI are infrequent, and definitive evidence confirming a causal relationship remains lacking.

A potential alternative diagnosis is the recently identified 'burning vertex syndrome', characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align precisely with established primary headache disorders such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. It is postulated that the underlying pathophysiology involves small fiber sensory pathways originating in the scalp, and patients exhibit variable responses to greater occipital nerve blockade. In this case, the symptoms at the vertex could correspond to: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) burning vertex syndrome occurring independently; or (3) another distinct primary headache syndrome. There remains a significant lack of systematic documentation regarding the distribution of cutaneous allodynia in CMI cohorts, which is exemplified by the fact that, until the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile, no standardized tool existed to evaluate specific symptoms among CMI patients. In light of this limited evidence base, we describe this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding, rather than assigning it diagnostic significance.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI, particularly when it manifests with uncommon or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a 14-year period before accurate identification. The diagnostic process was marked by early uncertainty and a slow progression toward a correct neurologic diagnosis, a pattern frequently described in the literature.
