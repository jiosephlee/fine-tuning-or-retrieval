Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early forties reported a 7-year history of repeated choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, as well as nocturnal choking that occurred three to four times per week, and persistent occipital headaches over the preceding 14 years. While self-monitoring during the COVID-19 pandemic, she unexpectedly discovered instances of marked oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations aimed at elucidating the cause of the choking episodes were unremarkable. Retrospective examination of a cranial CT obtained 14 years prior due to unrelated trauma identified a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the brain and cervical spine demonstrated Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with cerebellar tonsillar herniation measuring 11 mm. Polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnoea with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, and the majority (72%) were central in nature. This case underscores that CMI may present with respiratory complaints resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the necessity to evaluate for structural brain abnormalities in patients presenting with otherwise unexplained respiratory compromise.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum, as seen on sagittal MRI. Current imaging data indicate that CMI is present in 1% of adults, while symptoms are evident in about 0.1% of individuals. There is a slight female predominance, with a ratio of 1.3:1, and the disorder most frequently manifests in patients in their twenties.

The underlying mechanism involves a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, leading to a disproportion between neural elements and the space they occupy. Nonetheless, recent research questions the long-standing view of CMI as inherently abnormal, suggesting instead a continuum ranging from benign anatomical variations to disorders with clinical implications. Contemporary volumetric analyses have revealed considerable demographic differences in posterior fossa size, often without direct association to clinical symptoms. As a result, this evolving perspective emphasizes the need for personalized, symptom-focused management strategies, differentiating non-symptomatic anatomic findings from those pathologies that merit surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the symptomatic presentation of CMI, distinguished by a specific set of neurological symptoms. The primary clinical feature is intermittent occipital headache that is triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of affected individuals. Symptom severity in adults is generally greater than in pediatric cases, particularly when syringomyelia is also present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in 15%–26% of cases, most often manifesting as oropharyngeal impairments such as palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory involvement is now more frequently acknowledged as a complication of CMI. In a prospective cohort of 90 adult CMI patients, sleep-disordered breathing was detected in half of the cases. An elevated central apnoea index (≥5) was identified in 6.7% of subjects, with only a single patient demonstrating isolated central sleep apnoea, likely as a result of compression at the level of the brainstem. These respiratory issues are not confined to disturbances during sleep; one case series reported incidents of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia in individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in these cases is attributed to compression of the brainstem, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation, and interference with the central respiratory regulatory centers, resulting in periodic respiratory symptoms. These manifestations correspond to an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome, sometimes called 'Ondine’s curse,' which is different from the congenital form linked to PHOX2B mutations. Contemporary guidelines highlight the relevance of this association by advising routine polysomnography in individuals with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who are suspected of having sleep-related breathing disorders. Importantly, decompression of the posterior fossa can reestablish normal CSF dynamics and may ameliorate respiratory disturbances, emphasizing the necessity to identify CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early forties presented to our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year course of recurrent choking incidents. These episodes emerged unpredictably, occurring both during meals and at night, with both solid foods and liquids implicated equally. She characterized these events as abrupt and highly distressing, frequently disrupting her eating and sometimes waking her from sleep with forceful coughing and difficulty breathing, although she had never experienced full airway blockage. Episodes occurring at night happened three to four times each week, and sometimes twice within a single night. Throughout the years, she had been subjected to multiple assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all of which yielded unremarkable results. Nonetheless, her symptoms endured despite the absence of any diagnostic abnormalities. The impact on her daily life was pronounced; she avoided dining out or attending social events out of apprehension regarding potential public choking incidents. This persistent anxiety and sense of embarrassment contributed to a progressive retreat from social interactions and increased psychological distress.

A more comprehensive review of her clinical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, subsequent to a minor automobile collision in which she experienced a brief loss of consciousness. Soon after the incident, she began having ongoing headaches located at the occipital region and the vertex. The headaches were described as unremitting, having a pressure-like quality, and frequently restricted to a coin-sized area at the vertex. They were not aggravated by actions such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple healthcare providers diagnosed her with nummular headache, and she received a series of local scalp injections that yielded only temporary benefit. The most notable symptom was pronounced allodynia at the vertex; even the weight of her hair provoked discomfort. She eventually found that having her hair cut short lessened her baseline pain level from 6 out of 10 to 3 out of 10, although making this change was at odds with cultural norms and resulted in emotional distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient initiated home pulse oximetry to track her oxygen saturation. Over a period of several months, she frequently recorded asymptomatic decreases in oxygen saturation to levels between 70% and 80%. These occurrences typically went unnoticed until she reviewed her oximeter readings. Several emergency department assessments subsequently ascribed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at a major international airport. As she awaited her flight, she developed sudden onset dizziness accompanied by nausea. Medical staff present at the location measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, after which she was not permitted to board the plane. Her symptoms fully abated within minutes after she received supplemental oxygen. Follow-up assessment by a pulmonologist, which encompassed chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal results.

In light of her chronic occipital and vertex headaches, episodes of worsening choking, and unexplained hypoxia, clinicians sought a single neurological explanation for her symptoms. Review of her past medical history uncovered a non-contrast head CT, performed 14 years before after her trauma, which was originally read as unremarkable. On closer re-examination, the scan demonstrated crowding at the foramen magnum and diminished cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastroesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was initially suspected due to the patient's episodes of choking. However, this diagnosis was ruled out as pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations were unremarkable, and there was no improvement despite maximal acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was evaluated due to symptoms affecting the bulbar region. Negative antibody testing, lack of fatigability and absence of diurnal variation made this unlikely.

Primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated following hypoxic episodes. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests excluded intrinsic lung disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by the patient’s maintained ability for voluntary respiration, while automatic respiratory control was compromised during periods of distraction or sleep. The temporal association with head trauma differentiated this case from congenital variants.

Brainstem pathology secondary to CMI ultimately explained all symptoms through compression of the respiratory centres and lower cranial nerve nuclei, providing the unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of the patient’s motor vehicle collision in relation to the emergence of her symptoms requires thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is recognized in the literature as a congenital disorder as well as a potential consequence of cranial trauma or other neurological insults. Recent etiological frameworks2022 reflect this diversity by differentiating between primary (congenital) Chiari malformations and secondary or acquired variants that arise following trauma or elevations in intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained directly following the trauma revealed a narrowed foramen magnum. Due to the lack of imaging prior to the injury, it is unclear if the trauma resulted in this abnormality or simply exposed a pre-existing condition. The patient experienced occipital headache onset immediately after the incident, with bulbar and respiratory symptoms emerging progressively over a span of 14 years. This sequence may suggest either (1) trauma leading to tonsillar herniation, or (2) the injury worsening or revealing an undiagnosed, asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory complications associated with CMI, shedding light on acquired forms of central hypoventilation that occur secondary to structural abnormalities within the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is pivotal when considering the respiratory pathophysiology linked to CMI. While voluntary respiration—relying on cortical neural circuits—tends to be preserved in these individuals, automatic respiration—regulated by brainstem centers and vital for maintaining ventilation during sleep—can be disrupted by compression of the medullary region by herniated cerebellar tonsils. In our patient, voluntary breathing was maintained, but the automatic respiratory mechanism was absent, mirroring the clinical profile of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though arising through an alternative pathogenic process. The incident at the airport, during which transient inattention led to marked oxygen desaturation in the absence of subjective dyspnoea, serves as a striking demonstration of isolated impairment of automatic respiratory function: the patient quite literally ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the striking absence of dyspnoea observed in this patient during marked episodes of hypoxaemia may be attributable to an underlying primary brainstem dysfunction. Such a lesion could impair the essential afferent neural pathways responsible for transmitting signals from the periphery to the cortex, which are necessary for conscious awareness. Breathlessness represents a specific aspect of interoception, reliant on afferent input processed by regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If these neural signals are disrupted at the brainstem level, the cortical centers involved would not receive adequate information to perceive air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s profoundly diminished perception of respiratory distress despite severe hypoxaemia.

The significant gap observed between the patient’s pronounced clinical symptoms and the relatively low AHI of 6.0 on polysomnography requires further clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was performed instead of the more comprehensive type I, resulting in the omission of EEG recordings. Without EEG, total sleep time could not be accurately measured, potentially resulting in an appreciable undercount of respiratory disturbances. In this context, the AHI is derived from total recording time—which also encompasses periods when the patient was awake—rather than actual sleep duration. Consequently, this methodological shortcoming suggests that the recorded AHI of 6.0 does not fully reflect the extent of the patient’s sleep-related breathing disorder.

Despite this restriction, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) a high proportion of central events (72%) relative to the modest total AHI; (2) observed episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% occurring without symptoms of dyspnoea or patient awareness (noted both at the airport and in standard evaluations); and (3) anatomical findings showing marked brainstem compression, with 11 mm of tonsillar descent.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnographic evaluation, including full electroencephalogram monitoring, end-tidal carbon dioxide measurement, and video documentation, has been scheduled at an offsite sleep center. Nevertheless, finalization of this assessment is contingent upon aligning the patient's schedule, obtaining their consent, and arranging the necessary time at the distant facility. Upon completion, this study will yield a more thorough assessment of sleep structure, breathing patterns, and both the existence and extent of central hypoventilation.

We acknowledge that, given the current evidence, central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis, rather than a definitively confirmed diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia involving both solids and liquids, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, was the predominant and most troubling complaint in this patient. Validated swallow evaluations performed prospectively revealed dysfunction in 36% of cases, most commonly displaying neurogenic patterns. Neurogenic dysphagia is distinguished by similar impairment across all food and liquid textures, as opposed to mechanical forms. Despite this, repeated assessments by gastroenterology and otolaryngology produced unremarkable results, causing a prolonged delay in establishing her diagnosis. The protracted interval before diagnosis serves as a crucial reminder that in the absence of identifiable structural abnormalities on endoscopy, dysphagia or choking should raise suspicion for a neurologic etiology. In one retrospective review, Chiari malformation type I (CMI) was found in 3.3% of pediatric patients with ongoing dysphagia—a notable increase compared to the 0.1%–0.77% rate observed in the wider pediatric population—suggesting that chronic dysphagia may be a significant clinical clue for underlying CMI.

The patient's description of vertex pain alleviated by cutting hair highlights an unusual and insufficiently explored observation. Occipital neuralgia has been reported alongside CMI and is known to produce hypersensitivity of the scalp; its diagnostic criteria include “dysesthesia and/or allodynia evident upon non-noxious stimulation of the scalp and/or hair.” Nevertheless, reports connecting occipital neuralgia with CMI are uncommon, and definitive evidence demonstrating a causal relationship remains lacking.

Another possible explanation is the newly reported ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align precisely with established primary headache types, such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headaches. The suggested underlying mechanism implicates involvement of small fiber nerve pathways from the scalp, and patients have shown mixed responses to greater occipital nerve block treatments. For our patient, the vertex manifestations could be interpreted as: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) a coincidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a separate, primary headache condition. There remains a notable gap in the systematic recording of cutaneous allodynia presentations within the CMI patient population, illustrated by the prior lack of any published tool to evaluate these symptoms before the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Because of the limited data available, we report this observation primarily as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than as a definitive feature for diagnosis.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI, especially when it manifests with unusual or ambiguous symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a delayed diagnosis over 14 years. The process of diagnosis—marked by early uncertainty and a slow progression to recognizing the true neurological condition—has been frequently described in existing literature.
