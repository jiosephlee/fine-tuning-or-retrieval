Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female in her early forties presented with a 7-year history characterized by recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, frequent nocturnal choking occurring 3 to 4 times per week, and persistent occipital headaches dating back 14 years. Home pulse oximetry during the COVID-19 pandemic incidentally detected marked oxygen desaturation (ranging from 70% to 80%), despite the absence of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations were performed for her choking complaints and yielded normal findings. A review of a head CT obtained 14 years earlier for unrelated trauma disclosed a crowded foramen magnum that had been previously unrecognized. MRI of the brain and cervical spine revealed Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with an 11 mm downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography showed mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0 and primarily central events (72%). This case demonstrates that CMI may present with respiratory manifestations resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the necessity of considering structural brain lesions in patients presenting with otherwise unexplained respiratory disturbances.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils of at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate a prevalence of CMI in 1% of adults, but only around 0.1% of individuals experience symptoms related to the malformation. The disorder shows a slight female bias with a ratio of 1.3:1 and most commonly manifests in patients during their thirties.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa size, resulting in a disparity between the volume of neural elements and the spatial confines. Nevertheless, current data dispute the longstanding notion that CMI is inherently pathological, suggesting it exists along a continuum that spans from benign anatomical differences to meaningful malformations causing clinical symptoms. Recent volumetric analyses reveal marked demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions, which do not reliably predict the presence of symptoms. This evolving perspective calls for a tailored, symptom-driven approach to management, enabling clinicians to differentiate asymptomatic anatomical findings from those pathologies that warrant operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome is the clinical expression of CMI and is distinguished by a unique set of neurological symptoms. The primary manifestation is episodic occipital headache that is triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of patients. Adults generally exhibit more pronounced symptoms than children, particularly when syringomyelia coexists. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves has been observed in 15%–26% of cases, often presenting as oropharyngeal abnormalities, including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary involvement is now more commonly acknowledged as a complication of CMI. In a prospective cohort involving 90 adult patients with CMI, half were identified as having sleep-disordered breathing. A central apnoea index of 5 or greater was documented in 6.7% of subjects, but isolated central sleep apnoea was present in only a single patient, which may be attributable to brainstem compression. Notably, the respiratory dysfunction seen in CMI is not restricted to disturbances during sleep; a series of reported cases has highlighted the occurrence of acute respiratory insufficiency and severe hypoxaemia posing immediate threats to life in individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in these cases arises from brainstem compression, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and disruption of the respiratory control centers, which can cause periodic respiratory symptoms. These manifestations correspond to an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—and are separate from the congenital central hypoventilation syndrome linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Recent recommendations highlight this association by advocating for routine polysomnography in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) suspected of having sleep-related breathing disorders. Importantly, posterior fossa decompression has the potential to restore CSF circulation and alleviate respiratory manifestations, emphasizing the necessity of considering CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory disturbances.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early forties came to our family medicine practice reporting a seven-year duration of repeated choking events that happened unpredictably during both eating and sleeping, impacting intake of solids and liquids alike. She characterized the events as abrupt and alarming, frequently interrupting her meals or waking her at night with intense coughing and difficulty breathing, although she never experienced complete blockage of her airway. Nocturnal incidents occurred three to four times a week and occasionally happened twice in a single night. Throughout this period, she had multiple assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all of which revealed normal findings. Nonetheless, her symptoms persisted without explanation. The impact on her life was considerable, as she avoided dining out or social gatherings from a fear of choking in front of others. The continued anxiety and embarrassment contributed to increased social isolation and worsening emotional distress.

Upon further exploration of her medical history, it was found that her symptoms initially emerged 14 years prior, following a minor car accident in which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after this event, she began to experience persistent headaches in the occipital and vertex regions. These headaches were described as constant in nature, with a sensation of pressure, and frequently confined to a small, coin-sized area on the vertex. The headaches did not worsen with actions such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. She was diagnosed with nummular headache by several healthcare providers, and received numerous local scalp injections; however, these interventions only provided temporary relief. A prominent characteristic was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, such that even her hair’s weight caused discomfort. Eventually, she learned that keeping her hair short lowered her usual pain intensity from 6/10 to 3/10, although this choice conflicted with her cultural norms and resulted in additional psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient initiated self-monitoring of her oxygen levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Over the course of several months, she consistently recorded episodes of decreased oxygen saturation into the 70%–80% range, despite the absence of symptoms. These occurrences frequently went unnoticed unless she measured her levels. Several visits to the emergency department resulted in her symptoms being ascribed to anxiety.

An important event took place at an international airport. As she was awaiting her flight, she suddenly developed severe dizziness accompanied by nausea. Medical staff present recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prevented from boarding. Symptoms resolved entirely within minutes following the delivery of supplemental oxygen. Later pulmonary assessment, which involved chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal results.

In light of the patient's persistent occipital and vertex headaches, escalating episodes of choking, and unexplained episodes of hypoxia, clinicians sought an overarching neurological explanation. Upon reviewing her historical medical documentation, it was found that a non-contrast head CT had been completed 14 years prior, post-accident, and interpreted as unremarkable. Nonetheless, closer examination of this scan identified a crowded foramen magnum and absence of cerebrospinal fluid within the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

An initial diagnosis of gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was made due to the patient’s choking episodes. However, this was ruled out after pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations returned normal findings and there was no improvement with high-dose acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered as a possible diagnosis because of bulbar symptoms. However, the likelihood was reduced by negative antibody assays, no evidence of fatigue on examination, and no fluctuations in symptom severity throughout the day.

Comprehensive assessment for a primary lung disorder was undertaken after episodes of hypoxemia. Normal findings on chest imaging and pulmonary function tests effectively ruled out intrinsic pulmonary disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by normal voluntary respiration, but disrupted automatic breathing whenever the patient was distracted or asleep. The onset after head injury differentiated it from the congenital variants.

Compression of the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei by brainstem pathology associated with CMI ultimately accounted for all symptoms, offering a single, cohesive diagnosis.

Discussion:

It is important to closely examine the timing between our patient’s motor vehicle accident and the later emergence of symptoms. Chiari malformation type I is noted in the literature as having a congenital origin, but it may also arise following head injuries or other types of neurological trauma. Recent etiological frameworks2022 acknowledge this range by categorizing Chiari malformations into primary forms that are congenital and secondary or acquired variants that are attributed to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, the CT scan obtained directly after the incident showed a constricted foramen magnum. Because there is no imaging available from before the trauma, it is unclear if the injury produced this anomaly or simply uncovered an existing condition. The acute emergence of occipital headaches right after the accident, with subsequent gradual worsening of bulbar and respiratory issues over a period of 14 years, may indicate either: (1) tonsillar herniation resulting from trauma, or (2) trauma aggravating or exposing an asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory presentations associated with CMI and broadens insight into central hypoventilation acquired secondary to structural lesions of the brainstem. A critical aspect of CMI respiratory pathophysiology involves differentiating between voluntary and automatic breathing. While the cortical circuits responsible for voluntary respiration are generally preserved in individuals with CMI, the brainstem centers responsible for automatic ventilation—particularly vital during sleep—become impaired owing to compression of the medullary region by tonsillar herniation. In this instance, the patient maintained voluntary respiratory capacity but exhibited a loss of automatic drive, producing a clinical picture similar to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit with a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, where attentional distraction led to severe oxygen desaturation in the absence of dyspnea, provides a stark example of this targeted dysfunction in automatic breathing: the patient, in effect, “forgets to breathe.”

We propose that the striking lack of dyspnoea observed in this patient during marked episodes of oxygen desaturation can be attributed to an underlying dysfunction within the brainstem. Such pathology would plausibly disrupt the essential afferent neural pathways responsible for transmitting information to the cortex, which is needed for conscious sensory perception. Breathlessness represents a facet of interoceptive awareness, requiring proper processing of afferent input by specific cortical regions such as the anterior insular cortex. Interruption of these signals as they traverse the brainstem would prevent the cortex from receiving the input necessary to elicit the sensation of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s failure to recognize their own physiological compromise.

The significant difference observed between the patient’s pronounced clinical manifestations and the modest polysomnographic outcome (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. We conducted a type III polysomnographic study instead of the type I variant because of practical constraints, which resulted in the absence of EEG monitoring. This omission hindered an accurate assessment of total sleep time, almost certainly causing considerable underrepresentation of respiratory disturbances. When EEG data are unavailable, AHI is derived from total recording duration—including periods when the patient is awake—rather than exclusively during actual sleep. As a result, this approach causes the reported AHI value of 6.0 to understate the genuine extent of sleep-disordered breathing in our patient.

Although this limitation exists, multiple factors indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central apneic events constituted the majority (72%) even though the total AHI was low; (2) recorded oxygen saturation drops to 70%–80% occurred without dyspnoea or the patient noticing (both at the airport and during routine interactions); and (3) anatomical findings revealed considerable brainstem compression, with 11 mm of tonsillar herniation.

A Type I polysomnographic assessment incorporating comprehensive EEG surveillance, end-tidal capnography, and continuous video monitoring has been scheduled at a specialized sleep facility offsite. The performance of this evaluation is currently awaiting alignment of the patient’s schedule, their consent to undergo the procedure, and availability at the referring location. Upon finalization, the study is expected to yield more conclusive information regarding sleep structure, patterns of respiration during sleep, as well as identification and quantification of central hypoventilation.

It is important to note that, based on presently available data, central hypoventilation stands as a strongly substantiated clinical consideration, but has not yet been unequivocally diagnosed.

Our patient’s most troublesome symptom was oropharyngeal dysphagia, which affected solids and liquids to the same degree and was accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. Prospective assessments utilizing validated swallowing tests identified dysfunction in 36% of cases, predominantly showing neurogenic characteristics. The fact that difficulties occurred equally with all types of food consistency serves to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical causes. The patient underwent multiple evaluations from both gastroenterology and otolaryngology, all of which yielded normal results, leading to a delayed diagnosis spanning several years. This prolonged interval before diagnosis underscores a key clinical principle: when dysphagia or choking occurs in the absence of a clear structural abnormality found on endoscopy, a neurological etiology should be considered. A retrospective review in children with persistent dysphagia revealed a 3.3% prevalence of CMI, markedly higher than the general paediatric population prevalence of 0.1%–0.77%. Therefore, ongoing dysphagia may serve as an important sign pointing to underlying CMI.

The patient’s experience of vertex pain alleviated by hair cutting highlights a curious, yet largely unexplored, clinical presentation. Occipital neuralgia, which may occur in connection with CMI, can lead to heightened sensitivity of the scalp. Its diagnostic requirements include ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia provoked by non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair.’ Despite this, reports of occipital neuralgia concomitant with CMI are uncommon, and a definitive causative relationship remains unsubstantiated.

A further diagnostic possibility is ‘burning vertex syndrome,’ a recently recognized condition characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical attributes that do not correspond with conventional primary headache disorders such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headaches. The proposed pathophysiology implicates small fiber neural pathways originating within the scalp, and affected individuals exhibit variable responses to greater occipital nerve block interventions. In the case under discussion, the vertex symptoms might be explained by one of the following: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) a coincidental manifestation of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache disorder. There is a notable deficiency in systematic documentation of cutaneous allodynia presentations in CMI cohorts, highlighted by the lack of any validated assessment tool for CMI-specific symptoms prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Due to limited supporting data, we offer this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than asserting it as a conclusive diagnostic criterion.

This case illustrates the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI, particularly when it manifests with unusual or confusing symptoms like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, resulting in a 14-year lag before accurate identification. The process toward a proper neurological diagnosis, marked by early ambiguity and slow progression to clarity, commonly appears in previously reported cases.
