Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early forties reported a 7-year pattern of recurrent choking events with both solids and liquids, along with nocturnal choking episodes occurring 3–4 times per week, in addition to chronic occipital headaches persisting over a 14-year period. During the COVID-19 pandemic, at-home monitoring unexpectedly detected marked episodes of hypoxemia (oxygen saturation of 70%–80%) in the absence of overt respiratory compromise. Repeated laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations undertaken to explore the etiology of the choking episodes were unremarkable. Retrospective assessment of a computed tomography scan of the head obtained 14 years prior, following unrelated trauma, identified an overlooked crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck verified the presence of Chiari malformation type I (CMI), characterized by an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography documented mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0), predominantly comprised of central events (72%). This report highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory complaints closely resembling those of acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the need to assess for structural abnormalities of the brain in patients exhibiting unexplained respiratory pathology.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending a minimum of 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum, as observed on sagittal MRI scans. Recent imaging research indicates that CMI can be detected in 1% of adults, whereas only about 0.1% of the overall population experience clinical symptoms. The disorder shows a slight predominance in females, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, and it most frequently becomes apparent in individuals in their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa, resulting in disproportion between the size of neural components and the space accommodating them. Nonetheless, more recent findings dispute the conventional view of CMI as inherently pathological, suggesting it exists along a continuum ranging from benign anatomical variations to severe, symptomatic malformations. Modern volumetric analyses have revealed notable demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions, yet these differences do not reliably predict clinical symptoms. This paradigm shift underscores the need for patient-specific, symptom-driven management strategies that discern incidental anatomical variants from those warranting surgical treatment due to their pathological nature.

Chiari syndrome denotes the symptomatic clinical expression of CMI, marked by a characteristic array of neurological deficits. The principal clinical feature is an episodic occipital headache triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, which occurs in more than 80% of individuals. Adults generally experience a more pronounced symptom burden than children, particularly when syringomyelia is also present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is observed in approximately 15%–26% of cases and usually manifests as oropharyngeal disturbances, including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary complications are being identified more frequently as significant sequelae of CMI. In a forward-looking cohort of 90 adult patients with CMI, half exhibited some form of sleep-associated respiratory disturbance. Among these, 6.7% had a central apnoea index of at least 5, though isolated central sleep apnoea was rare, occurring in only a single individual—an observation possibly attributable to compression at the level of the brainstem. Respiratory dysfunction in CMI is not confined to sleep abnormalities; reports in the literature have described instances of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia in individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in this context arises from several mechanisms, including compression of the brainstem, obstruction to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and disturbance of neural networks responsible for regulating respiration. These factors collectively result in episodic respiratory manifestations. Clinically, such manifestations constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—often referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which differs from the inherited central hypoventilation syndrome associated with PHOX2B gene mutations. Contemporary recommendations highlight this relationship, advocating for routine use of polysomnography in patients with CMI who present with possible sleep-disordered breathing. Notably, surgical intervention via posterior fossa decompression has the capacity to reestablish normal CSF circulation and may alleviate respiratory dysfunction, thus illustrating the necessity of evaluating CMI as a possible cause in cases of otherwise unexplained respiratory issues.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early forties visited our family medicine clinic reporting a seven-year history of intermittent choking incidents that manifested unpredictably both during eating and while asleep. These events involved difficulty with both solid foods and liquids, and were described as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting meals or rousing her from sleep with severe coughing and difficulty breathing. Despite their severity, the episodes never progressed to a total blockage of her airway. Nocturnal occurrences were common, happening three to four times weekly, and occasionally as frequently as twice in a single night. She had previously undergone multiple assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, none of which revealed any abnormalities. Nonetheless, her symptoms remained unchanged. The effect on her social life was profound; she refrained from dining out and avoided social events out of fear of experiencing an episode in public. This chronic apprehension and embarrassment contributed to marked social isolation and emotional burden.

A more thorough review of her clinical background indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, immediately after a minor car accident in which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after this event, she began experiencing unremitting headaches centered at the occipital region and vertex. She characterized these headaches as constant, with a sensation of pressure, frequently restricted to a small, coin-sized area on the vertex of her scalp. Notably, the headaches were not worsened by actions such as coughing, straining, or performing other Valsalva maneuvers. She was subsequently diagnosed by multiple clinicians with nummular headache and received repeated localized scalp injections, though these only provided temporary alleviation. The most striking aspect of her condition was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, so severe that even the weight of her hair induced pain. In the end, she found that keeping her hair short lowered her average pain level from 6/10 to 3/10; however, this adaptation ran counter to cultural norms and resulted in considerable psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient initiated regular monitoring of her oxygen saturation at home with a personal pulse oximeter. Throughout several months, she consistently recorded episodes where her oxygen saturation fell into the 70%–80% range, despite the absence of symptoms. These occurrences frequently went unnoticed until detected through use of the device. Numerous emergency assessments subsequently ascribed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly developed severe dizziness and nausea. Medical staff present at the airport recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, leading to her being prevented from boarding the plane. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms subsided entirely within a few minutes. Further assessment of her lungs, comprising both chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed no abnormalities.

In light of her persistent headaches localized to the occipital and vertex regions, coupled with recurrent choking episodes and episodes of unexplained hypoxia, clinicians sought a single neurological explanation for her symptoms. Examination of her historical medical documentation uncovered that a non-contrast CT head scan had been performed 14 years prior, subsequent to her accident, and was initially interpreted as unremarkable. Upon meticulous retrospective analysis, the scan demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and an absence of cerebrospinal fluid at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the presence of choking symptoms, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected. However, this diagnosis was ruled out due to normal results from pH monitoring, unremarkable endoscopic examinations, and no improvement following intensive acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered as a differential diagnosis in light of bulbar symptoms. However, the diagnosis was deemed improbable given the negative antibody results, no observed fatigability, and no diurnal fluctuations in symptoms.

Comprehensive evaluation for primary pulmonary pathology was performed after episodes of hypoxemia. The absence of abnormalities on chest imaging and normal pulmonary function test outcomes ruled out primary lung disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by the maintenance of voluntary respiration, whereas automatic breathing control was disrupted during periods of sleep or distraction. The onset after head trauma differentiated this case from the congenital variants.

Pathological changes in the brainstem resulting from CMI accounted for the entire spectrum of symptoms by exerting pressure on both the respiratory centers and the nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thus yielding a comprehensive diagnostic explanation.

Discussion:

It is important to closely evaluate the timing of our patient's motor vehicle accident in relation to when their symptoms began. Chiari malformation type I is known to arise congenitally but can also develop following head trauma or various neurological insults. Modern aetiological frameworks2022 reflect this variability, separating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired types that emerge due to traumatic events or elevations in intracranial pressure.

In this instance, a CT scan conducted right after the accident showed a congested foramen magnum. Due to the lack of imaging before the trauma, it is unclear whether the trauma was responsible for producing this anomaly or simply exposed a pre-existing condition. The patient experienced an abrupt onset of occipital headaches after the incident, subsequently developing bulbar and respiratory symptoms gradually over a 14-year period. This progression may indicate either: (1) the trauma directly precipitated tonsillar herniation, or (2) the traumatic event worsened or brought to light an otherwise asymptomatic congenital abnormality.

This case highlights the intricate nature of respiratory symptoms associated with CMI and broadens our knowledge of how acquired central hypoventilation can present in the setting of structural lesions affecting the brainstem. A key point in the respiratory physiology of CMI is the differentiation between voluntary and automatic breathing. The voluntary component, which depends on cortical circuits, is generally preserved in individuals with CMI. Conversely, automatic respiration—which is governed by brainstem centers and predominates during sleep—can be impaired as a consequence of tonsillar herniation impacting the medulla. In this instance, the patient maintained the ability for voluntary breathing but experienced a loss of automatic respiratory drive, displaying a clinical picture similar to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit with a different underlying pathogenesis. The episode in the airport, wherein an incidental distraction led to severe oxygen desaturation without the perception of breathlessness, clearly demonstrates this selective disruption of automatic regulation: the patient, in effect, ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea, even during periods of marked oxygen desaturation, originates from an underlying dysfunction within the brainstem. Such a pathological process in the brainstem could impede essential afferent neural pathways required for transmitting signals to the cortex, where conscious awareness arises. The perception of breathlessness constitutes a specific facet of interoception that hinges on these afferent pathways being processed by critical brain structures, notably the anterior insular cortex. Interruption of these ascending signals within the brainstem would prevent the cortex from receiving the information necessary to elicit the sensation of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s perilous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The notable gap between the patient's pronounced clinical manifestations and the modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Constraints in available resources necessitated the use of type III polysomnography in place of type I, resulting in the absence of EEG data. The lack of EEG prevented precise determination of actual sleep duration, which may have led to a considerable underrepresentation of respiratory disturbances. In this context, AHI was calculated based on the entire monitoring period—including intervals of wakefulness—rather than the actual time spent asleep. Consequently, this methodological shortcoming suggests that the recorded AHI of 6.0 likely fails to reflect the actual severity of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Nonetheless, a number of observations indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central apnoeic events constitute the majority (72%) even though the total AHI remains low; (2) observed oxygen saturation drops to 70%–80% occurred in the absence of dyspnoea or patient consciousness of these events (noted both in the airport setting and during standard clinical assessments); and (3) imaging reveals marked compression of the brainstem, with a tonsillar herniation measuring 11 mm.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography—including full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation—has been scheduled at an external sleep facility. The study has yet to be performed, as it is dependent upon alignment of the patient's schedule, their consent to the procedure, and available appointment times at the off-site location. Upon completion, this assessment is expected to yield clearer insights into the patient’s sleep architecture, respiratory dynamics, and the extent as well as severity of central hypoventilation.

We recognize that, based on the current data, central hypoventilation remains a clinically plausible but not yet conclusively established diagnosis.

In our patient, the most troubling manifestation was oropharyngeal dysphagia, which impacted the intake of both solids and liquids equally and was accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. When validated tools for assessing swallowing were used in a prospective study, 36% of individuals exhibited swallowing dysfunction, with neurogenic origins being most prevalent. The absence of variation in swallowing difficulty between different food consistencies serves to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from its mechanical counterpart. Despite comprehensive investigations targeting gastroenterological and otolaryngological etiologies—none of which identified any abnormalities—the patient's most prominent symptom persisted, resulting in a diagnostic delay spanning several years. This protracted timeline underscores a critical point for clinical practice: in instances where dysphagia or choking is present without evident structural abnormalities on endoscopic evaluation, neurologic causes should be entertained. Notably, a retrospective review revealed that 3.3% of pediatric patients with chronic dysphagia were diagnosed with CMI, a rate markedly exceeding the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence noted among the broader pediatric demographic. These findings point toward persistent dysphagia as an important clinical sign that may indicate underlying CMI.

The case in which the patient describes vertex pain alleviated by hair cutting highlights a phenomenon that has received little scholarly attention. In the context of CMI, occipital neuralgia may manifest and lead to heightened scalp sensitivity; established diagnostic guidelines necessitate ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia elicited by otherwise innocuous scalp or hair stimulation’. Nonetheless, reports detailing the connection between occipital neuralgia and CMI are infrequent, and definitive evidence supporting a causal relationship remains lacking.

Another possibility to consider is the newly characterized ‘burning vertex syndrome’, which is marked by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and a constellation of features that do not conform to conventional primary headache categories such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The underlying pathophysiology is postulated to involve small fiber afferents arising from the scalp, and treatment responses to greater occipital nerve blocks are inconsistent among patients. In the case we report, the vertex pain may be attributable to: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) an incidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct and unrelated primary headache condition. Comprehensive recording of cutaneous allodynia characteristics within CMI cohorts remains insufficient, as reflected by the lack of validated tools to evaluate these specific manifestations in CMI patients prior to the advent of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In view of these evidentiary gaps, we interpret this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding, rather than establishing it as a diagnostic hallmark.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI when it manifests through non-classical or confusing symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a protracted 14-year interval before a definitive diagnosis was made. The progression from misdiagnosis to eventual neurological recognition mirrors patterns frequently reported in scholarly literature.
