Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early 40s experienced recurrent episodes of choking on both solids and liquids over a period of 7 years, as well as nocturnal choking incidents occurring 3 to 4 times per week, accompanied by chronic occipital headaches that had persisted for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home health monitoring unexpectedly identified episodes of marked oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) in the absence of overt respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopic evaluations conducted to identify the cause of the choking yielded normal findings. Retrospective examination of a cranial CT scan performed 14 years prior for unrelated trauma uncovered a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the brain and cervical spine confirmed the presence of Chiari malformation type I (CMI), with cerebellar tonsillar herniation measuring 11 mm. Polysomnography indicated mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0) with a predominance of central events (72%). This case highlights that CMI may present with respiratory manifestations that are indistinguishable from acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the need to assess for structural abnormalities of the brain in cases of unexplained respiratory compromise.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending at least 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum, as visualized on sagittal MRI scans. Modern imaging data indicate that CMI is observed in roughly 1% of adults, but only about 0.1% of the population exhibit clinical symptoms. The disorder is somewhat more prevalent in females, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, and most frequently manifests in individuals in their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, resulting in a discrepancy between the volume of neural tissues and the surrounding bony space. Nevertheless, recent findings call into question the long-held view that CMI invariably represents a pathological condition. Instead, current perspectives suggest a continuum ranging from benign anatomical differences to marked malformations with clinical consequences. Volumetric analyses have revealed considerable variation in posterior fossa dimensions across populations, often without a direct relationship to clinical symptoms. As a result, there is an increasing emphasis on tailoring clinical decisions to the individual, guided by the presence and severity of symptoms, and differentiating incidental anatomical variants from cases that warrant surgical management.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical expression of CMI, marked by a characteristic pattern of neurological signs and symptoms. The hallmark feature is a sudden onset occipital headache triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, observed in more than 80% of affected individuals. Adult patients generally exhibit more pronounced clinical manifestations than pediatric patients, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in approximately 15%–26% of cases and commonly presents as oropharyngeal impairment, including symptoms like weakness of the palate, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory complications are being acknowledged with greater frequency as sequelae of CMI. In a forward-looking analysis involving 90 adult CMI patients, half exhibited sleep-disordered breathing. Notably, a central apnoea index of at least 5 was detected in 6.7% of subjects, though isolated central sleep apnoea was identified in just one individual, which may be attributable to compression of the brainstem. The scope of these respiratory issues is not restricted to disturbances during sleep; one series of cases described instances of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia among individuals with CMI.

Respiratory dysfunction in this context is attributed to several factors, including compression of the brainstem, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) egress, and impairment of respiratory regulatory centers, all of which contribute to episodic respiratory disturbances. These disturbances constitute an acquired type of central hypoventilation syndrome, colloquially known as ‘Ondine’s curse’, which should be differentiated from the congenital variant associated with PHOX2B gene mutations. In light of this, current clinical guidelines advocate for routine polysomnography in patients with CMI presenting with possible sleep-related breathing abnormalities. Importantly, surgical intervention via posterior fossa decompression can reestablish proper CSF circulation and may alleviate respiratory symptoms, highlighting the critical role of identifying CMI as a potential cause in cases of otherwise unexplained respiratory impairment.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s sought care at our family medicine practice due to a 7-year history of recurrent, unpredictable choking events occurring during both meals and sleep, with no clear distinction between solids and liquids as triggers. She characterized these incidents as abrupt and unsettling, frequently halting her eating or abruptly awakening her at night accompanied by intense coughing and difficulty breathing; however, she never experienced total airway blockage. Nocturnal episodes typically happened three to four times weekly and occasionally occurred twice in one night. Despite undergoing multiple thorough assessments—including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies—which consistently yielded normal results, her complaints continued unabated. The repercussions on her daily life were profound: out of concern for experiencing choking in public, she refrained from dining out or attending social gatherings, leading to mounting isolation and psychological distress as anxiety and embarrassment grew.

A comprehensive review of her medical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, subsequent to a minor motor vehicle collision that resulted in a brief episode of unconsciousness. Shortly thereafter, she began experiencing ongoing headaches located at the occipital and vertex regions. She characterized these headaches as unremitting, pressure-like, and typically restricted to a coin-sized region at the vertex. The pain was not provoked by coughing, straining, or other Valsalva manoeuvres. Multiple clinicians diagnosed her with nummular headache and administered several local injections to the scalp, which yielded only short-term improvement. The most prominent symptom was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, to the extent that the mere weight of her hair provoked discomfort. Ultimately, she found that keeping her hair short alleviated her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, though this measure conflicted with prevailing cultural norms and led to emotional distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started to regularly track her oxygen saturation at home with a pulse oximeter. Over the course of several months, she consistently recorded asymptomatic drops in oxygen saturation to levels between 70% and 80%. She typically became aware of these episodes only upon measuring her saturation. Despite seeking care in emergency settings on several occasions, her symptoms were repeatedly ascribed to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she was suddenly struck by severe dizziness and nausea. Medical staff present at the scene measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, prompting the decision to prevent her from boarding. When given supplemental oxygen, her symptoms subsided entirely in a matter of minutes. Further assessment of her pulmonary status—including chest imaging and pulmonary function tests—revealed no abnormalities.

Considering the co-occurrence of persistent occipital and vertex headaches, recurrent episodes of choking, and unexplained episodes of hypoxia, an integrative neurological explanation was sought. Upon examining her historical medical documentation, it was noted that a non-contrast CT scan of the head had been obtained 14 years prior, after her injury, and was previously interpreted as unremarkable. Nevertheless, a thorough re-examination of the imaging identified features including crowding at the foramen magnum and obliteration of the cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the presence of choking episodes, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected. However, this diagnosis was ruled out due to normal findings on pH monitoring and endoscopy, as well as the absence of improvement with maximal acid-suppressive therapy.

The possibility of myasthenia gravis was considered in light of bulbar symptoms. However, the diagnosis was deemed improbable given the absence of specific antibodies, no evidence of fatigable weakness, and stable symptom patterns throughout the day.

Comprehensive assessment for primary pulmonary disease was undertaken after occurrences of hypoxemia. Findings from both imaging studies of the chest and pulmonary function tests were within normal limits, thereby ruling out inherent lung pathology.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by intact voluntary respiratory effort, contrasted with diminished automatic respiratory control when the patient was distracted or asleep. The syndrome's onset after head trauma set it apart from congenital variants.

The entirety of the patient’s symptoms could be attributed to brainstem pathology arising from CMI, as this resulted in compression of both the respiratory centers and the nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby serving as a comprehensive explanation.

Discussion:

It is important to closely examine the timing between our patient’s involvement in a motor vehicle accident and the emergence of symptoms. Chiari malformation type I is recognized not only as a congenital anomaly but also as a potential consequence of traumatic brain injury or other neurological insults. Current aetiological frameworks2022 acknowledge this range, categorizing Chiari malformations into primary forms, which are present at birth, and secondary or acquired types that develop following trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, the CT scan obtained directly after the injury showed a crowded foramen magnum. Due to the absence of imaging prior to the traumatic event, it remains unclear if this anatomical feature was a consequence of the trauma itself or if the injury simply brought it to light. The immediate appearance of occipital headaches post-trauma, succeeded by a gradual progression to bulbar and respiratory symptoms over a span of 14 years, may indicate either (1) herniation of the tonsils triggered by the trauma or (2) trauma intensifying or revealing an underlying, previously asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory features associated with CMI and contributes to the broader understanding of how structural lesions of the brainstem can give rise to acquired forms of central hypoventilation. Central to this understanding is the differentiation between automatic and voluntary breathing. While voluntary respiration, governed by the cortex, often remains unaffected in individuals with CMI, automatic breathing—which relies on the integrity of brainstem centers and predominates during sleep—can be disrupted by compression of medullary regions by herniated tonsils. In this patient, voluntary control of breathing was preserved; however, automatic respiratory drive was absent, producing a clinical presentation that parallels congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit through a distinct underlying process. The incident at the airport, where transient distraction led to marked oxygen desaturation in the absence of dyspnoea, starkly demonstrates this dissociation: the patient, in effect, ceases to breathe automatically when not consciously attending to respiration.

We propose that the patient's striking lack of dyspnoea during marked episodes of hypoxaemia is attributable to an underlying dysfunction in the brainstem. Such a pathological process in the brainstem could interfere with essential ascending neural pathways required to transmit afferent information to the cortex, where conscious awareness is generated. Breathlessness constitutes an aspect of interoceptive processing, reliant on the relay and integration of these sensory inputs by cortical structures, such as the anterior insular cortex. When these afferent signals are disrupted at the level of the brainstem, the cortical regions are deprived of the necessary information to elicit the perception of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of physiological compromise.

The notable divergence between the patient’s pronounced clinical symptoms and the modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. For logistical reasons, type III polysomnography was performed instead of type I, resulting in the absence of EEG monitoring. This omission made it impossible to accurately determine total sleep time and thus likely caused a substantial undercounting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG, the AHI is computed based on the entire duration of the recording, encompassing both sleep and wake states, rather than exclusive periods of actual sleep. Consequently, this methodological constraint suggests that the measured AHI of 6.0 significantly underrepresents the actual severity of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Although this limitation exists, multiple findings point toward central hypoventilation: (1) a high proportion of central events (72%) was observed even though the overall AHI was low; (2) there were recorded episodes of oxygen desaturation down to 70%–80% in the absence of dyspnoea or subjective awareness by the patient (noted both at the airport and during routine assessments); and (3) anatomical imaging revealed marked brainstem compression, indicated by an 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A Type I polysomnography, incorporating comprehensive EEG surveillance, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation, has been scheduled at an off-site sleep facility. Nevertheless, the execution of this assessment is contingent upon aligning the patient’s schedule, securing the patient’s willingness to undergo the procedure, and organizing a suitable appointment with the external centre. Upon completion, this evaluation is expected to yield a more precise assessment of the patient’s sleep architecture, respiratory dynamics, and the extent and severity of central hypoventilation.

It is important to note that, based on currently available data, central hypoventilation continues to represent a robust clinical suspicion, but it has not yet been incontrovertibly established as a diagnosis.

In our patient, oropharyngeal dysphagia—which manifested as equal difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids and was accompanied by episodes of coughing and choking—proved to be the most troublesome symptom. Prospective analysis utilizing standardized swallowing evaluations revealed swallowing dysfunction in 36% of subjects, with neurogenic etiology being the most common. Neurogenic dysphagia can be differentiated from mechanical forms by the uniform impairment across all food and liquid textures. Despite the prominence of the dysphagia, initial investigations focusing on gastroenterological and otolaryngological etiologies returned unremarkable results, and the diagnosis remained elusive for several years. This prolonged interval before diagnosis underscores a key clinical principle: in cases where dysphagia or choking occurs without identifiable anatomical abnormalities on endoscopic examination, clinicians should have a heightened suspicion for neurological causes. Notably, a retrospective review indicated that the prevalence of CMI among children presenting with persistent dysphagia was 3.3%—substantially exceeding the 0.1%–0.77% reported within the general pediatric population. This increased prevalence highlights that ongoing dysphagia may serve as an important clinical marker for underlying CMI.

The observation that the patient experienced pain at the vertex region, which was alleviated by cutting hair, highlights a curious yet insufficiently researched occurrence. In cases of CMI, occipital neuralgia may be present and is known to induce hypersensitivity of the scalp; its diagnostic criteria include “dysesthesia and/or allodynia provoked by non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair.” Despite this, there are infrequent reports documenting the coexistence of occipital neuralgia and CMI, and definitive evidence of a causal relationship remains lacking.

Another possibility to consider is the recently characterized ‘burning vertex syndrome,’ which manifests as scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that do not align clearly with established primary headache entities such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiological basis implicates small fibre pathways from the scalp, and responses to greater occipital nerve blockade have been inconsistent among affected individuals. For our patient, the symptoms at the vertex could reflect one of the following: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) a coincident presentation of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a different primary headache disorder altogether. Notably, the systematic recording of cutaneous allodynia patterns within CMI cohorts remains insufficient, which is underscored by the lack of validated assessment tools specifically designed for these symptoms prior to the advent of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In light of the limited data available, we report this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than as evidence of a conclusive diagnostic sign.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI, particularly when the condition manifests with unusual or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a delayed diagnosis spanning 14 years. The process of arriving at the correct neurological diagnosis, often marked by initial misdirection and a slow progression toward clarity, is frequently documented in existing literature.
