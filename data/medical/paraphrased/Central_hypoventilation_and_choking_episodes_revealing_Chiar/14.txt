Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early forties reported a seven-year history of repeated choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, frequent nighttime choking occurring three to four times per week, and longstanding occipital headaches persisting for 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home pulse oximetry incidentally detected severe episodes of oxygen desaturation (with levels dropping to 70%–80%), though she remained free of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations for her choking episodes were unremarkable. Re-examination of a head CT scan obtained 14 years prior, following an unrelated traumatic event, disclosed a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. MRI of the head and neck confirmed a diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I (CMI), showing 11 mm herniation of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, predominantly central in origin (72% of events). This report highlights that CMI may present with respiratory features closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the importance of evaluating for structural lesions in patients with unexplained respiratory abnormalities.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils measuring 5 mm or more beneath the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging surveys indicate a prevalence of CMI in 1% of adults, while only about 0.1% of the overall population develop clinical symptoms. There is a slight female preponderance with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases present in individuals in their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between neural tissue and cranial space. Nevertheless, recent findings dispute the conventional view of CMI as inherently pathological, suggesting it exists on a continuum ranging from benign anatomical variants to disorders of clinical consequence. Contemporary volumetric analyses reveal significant demographic differences in posterior fossa size, with no uniform relationship to symptom manifestation. This evolving understanding underscores the importance of a personalised approach to clinical management, focusing on symptoms to differentiate incidental anatomical findings from malformations warranting surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome denotes the clinical expression of CMI, marked by a specific group of neurological symptoms. The principal complaint is a sudden onset occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of individuals. Adults usually experience more pronounced symptoms than pediatric patients, particularly if syringomyelia is also present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is observed in 15%–26% of cases, often manifesting as oropharyngeal deficits, including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary complications are being identified more frequently in patients with CMI. A prospective analysis involving 90 adult individuals with CMI revealed that half exhibited sleep-disordered breathing. In this cohort, 6.7% demonstrated a central apnoea index of 5 or greater, while isolated central sleep apnoea was identified in just a single patient, possibly attributable to compression of the brainstem. The respiratory issues associated with CMI are not limited to sleep; one case series reported occurrences of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia among individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in this context arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and impairment of neural centers governing respiration, which together result in episodic breathing abnormalities. These manifestations are characteristic of an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, sometimes referred to as 'Ondine’s curse,' which differs from the congenital form associated with PHOX2B gene mutations. Recent consensus statements highlight this association by advocating for the use of routine polysomnography in individuals with CMI who present with possible sleep-related breathing disorders. Importantly, posterior fossa decompression surgery has the potential to reestablish physiological CSF circulation and alleviate respiratory dysfunction, highlighting the significance of considering CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory compromise.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s came to our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year history of recurrent, unpredictable choking spells that arose during both eating and sleeping. These episodes involved both solid foods and liquids, occurring without a clear pattern. She characterized the incidents as abrupt and highly distressing, frequently interrupting her meals or awakening her at night with episodes of intense coughing and gasping; however, she never experienced total airway obstruction. Nocturnal events happened three to four times per week and, on occasion, occurred twice in one night. Despite multiple assessments over several years—including three flexible laryngoscopies and two esophagogastroduodenoscopies, all yielding normal results—her symptoms continued unabated. The social consequences were substantial: she refrained from dining out or attending gatherings out of concern for experiencing a choking event in public. This persistent worry and embarrassment resulted in greater social isolation and worsening psychological distress.

A more thorough review of her clinical background showed that her symptoms began 14 years prior, after experiencing a minor car accident that resulted in a brief loss of consciousness. Soon thereafter, she started to have ongoing headaches in the occipital region and at the vertex. She characterized these headaches as continuous, pressure-like, and typically confined to a coin-sized region at the vertex. The headaches did not worsen with coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. She received a diagnosis of nummular headache from several different physicians and underwent numerous local scalp injections, though these only provided short-lived relief. Notably, she exhibited pronounced allodynia at the vertex, to the extent that even the sensation of her hair was uncomfortable. Ultimately, she found that keeping her hair short decreased her baseline pain level from 6/10 to 3/10, but this choice was at odds with her cultural norms and led to psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient initiated home monitoring of her oxygen saturation with a personal pulse oximeter. She consistently recorded episodes over several months where her saturation levels declined to 70%–80% without experiencing any symptoms. Frequently, these low readings went unnoticed until she reviewed her device. Several emergency assessments ascribed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event transpired at a global airport, where, during a layover, the patient suddenly developed severe vertigo and nausea. Airport medical staff recorded her oxygen saturation at 78% and prohibited her from boarding the aircraft. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms resolved entirely within a few minutes. Further assessment by pulmonary specialists, including chest imaging and spirometry, revealed no abnormalities.

Due to the presence of longstanding occipital and vertex headaches, increasing episodes of choking, and unexplained episodes of low oxygen saturation, a comprehensive neurological etiology was investigated. Examination of her historical medical documentation uncovered a head CT without contrast that had been carried out 14 years prior, after her accident; at that time, the imaging was interpreted as normal. On careful retrospective evaluation, however, the scan demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and an absence of cerebrospinal fluid around the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

An initial diagnosis of gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was entertained due to the presence of choking episodes. However, this was ruled out as pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations were within normal limits, and there was no improvement despite maximal acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was evaluated due to symptoms affecting the bulbar region. Negative antibody testing, lack of fatigability and absence of diurnal variation made this unlikely.

Primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated following hypoxic episodes. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests excluded intrinsic lung disease.

A diagnosis of central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (also known as acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly likely, as evidenced by intact voluntary respiration but deficient automatic ventilatory drive when the patient was distracted or asleep. Its onset after head trauma set it apart from the congenital variants.

Brainstem pathology secondary to CMI ultimately explained all symptoms through compression of the respiratory centres and lower cranial nerve nuclei, providing the unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

It is important to closely examine the timing between this patient’s involvement in a motor vehicle accident and the development of symptoms. Chiari malformation type I is recognized not only as a congenital anomaly but also as a potential outcome of head trauma or other neurological insults. Recent aetiological frameworks (2022) acknowledge this range, differentiating between primary (congenital) Chiari malformations and secondary (acquired) variants that arise after trauma or elevations in intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained right after the incident showed a narrowed foramen magnum. As pre-injury imaging was not available, it is unclear whether this condition resulted from the trauma or was simply identified because of it. The rapid appearance of occipital headache following the trauma, together with a gradual onset of bulbar and respiratory manifestations over a 14-year period, may suggest two possibilities: (1) the trauma led to herniation of the cerebellar tonsils, or (2) the trauma worsened or exposed an underlying, previously silent, congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory complications seen in CMI and contributes to our comprehension of acquired central hypoventilation patterns associated with structural lesions in the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is key to elucidating the respiratory pathophysiology in CMI. Volitional breathing, regulated by cortical circuits, is often preserved in individuals with CMI. Conversely, automatic respiration—which relies on brainstem centers and is critical during sleep—can be impaired as a result of medullary compression by the cerebellar tonsils. In our patient, the ability for conscious ventilation was retained, but there was a loss of automatic respiratory drive, creating a clinical picture akin to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though with a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, where a brief lapse in attention led to significant desaturation in the absence of dyspnoea, vividly demonstrates this selective impairment of automatic breathing—essentially, the patient ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient's concerning lack of dyspnoea during episodes of marked oxygen desaturation can be attributed to a primary dysfunction within the brainstem. Such a brainstem abnormality would be expected to interfere with crucial ascending afferent pathways responsible for transmitting sensory input to the cortex, where conscious awareness is produced. The perception of breathlessness represents a specific aspect of interoception, relying on the relay and cortical processing of these signals, particularly within regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If these afferent pathways are disrupted at the brainstem level, the cortex is deprived of the input required to generate a sensation of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The notable difference observed between the pronounced clinical symptoms in our patient and the comparatively low AHI value of 6.0 on polysomnography requires clarification. For logistical reasons, type III polysomnography was performed instead of the gold standard type I, resulting in the absence of EEG data. Without EEG monitoring, precise measurement of total sleep time was not feasible, which likely resulted in undercounting of respiratory disturbances. Because EEG was not available, AHI was calculated using the total duration of the recording—including awake periods—rather than the actual time spent asleep. As a result, the reported AHI of 6.0 probably understates the actual burden of sleep-disordered breathing due to this methodological constraint.

Despite this constraint, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central events accounted for 72% of the total, even though the overall AHI was low; (2) oxygen saturation levels were recorded as low as 70%–80% in the absence of dyspnoea or the patient's awareness, observed both in an airport setting and during regular assessments; and (3) anatomical imaging revealed pronounced brainstem compression, with the tonsils descended by 11 mm.

A Type I polysomnographic assessment, incorporating comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation, has been scheduled at an external sleep laboratory. At present, the study has yet to be performed due to the need to coordinate patient scheduling, ensure the patient's agreement to undergo the procedure, and align with the availability of the remote center. Upon its completion, the evaluation will yield a more precise delineation of the patient’s sleep structure, breathing patterns during sleep, and will assess both the occurrence and extent of central hypoventilation.

We acknowledge that, given the current evidence, central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis, rather than a definitively confirmed diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, presenting with comparable difficulty in swallowing both solids and liquids and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, represented the patient’s most troublesome complaint. In a prospective analysis utilizing standardized tools to assess swallowing, dysfunction was identified in 36% of patients, with neurogenic etiologies being the most common. The similar degree of impairment across all food and liquid consistencies is characteristic of neurogenic dysphagia, distinguishing it from mechanical types. The symptom most troublesome to the patient underwent multiple evaluations by gastroenterology and otolaryngology specialists, all of which were unremarkable; however, this led to a prolonged delay in establishing the correct diagnosis. Such a delay underscores an important clinical point: when dysphagia or choking occurs without an apparent anatomical explanation on endoscopic examination, neurological causes should be considered. In a retrospective cohort of children with chronic dysphagia, the prevalence of CMI was 3.3%, markedly higher than the 0.1%–0.77% reported in the broader pediatric population. This observation supports the notion that chronic dysphagia may be an important clinical marker for underlying CMI.

The patient's account of experiencing pain localized to the vertex that improves after hair cutting highlights a notable yet insufficiently researched observation. Occipital neuralgia has been observed in conjunction with CMI and is known to lead to heightened scalp sensitivity; its diagnostic standards stipulate “dysesthesia and/or allodynia triggered by non-noxious stimulation of the scalp and/or hair.” Nonetheless, reports describing the relationship between occipital neuralgia and CMI are infrequent, and definitive evidence supporting a causal connection remains lacking.

Another possible explanation is the newly identified ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and symptoms that do not conform precisely to traditional primary headache categories, including migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headaches. This condition is theorized to involve small fiber pathways arising from the scalp, and patient responses to greater occipital nerve blocks have been inconsistent. In the current case, the symptoms at the vertex could be due to: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) a coincidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) an unrelated primary headache disorder. There is a notable deficiency in systematic documentation of cutaneous allodynia patterns among CMI patients, as demonstrated by the lack of a published tool to assess these specific symptoms prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Owing to these evidentiary gaps, we report this finding as a noteworthy clinical observation rather than as a definitive diagnostic sign.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI, particularly when it manifests with unusual or deceptive signs like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, resulting in a 14-year postponement before a definitive diagnosis was reached. The process, marked by early uncertainty and a slow progression towards the accurate neurological identification, reflects patterns frequently documented in existing literature.
