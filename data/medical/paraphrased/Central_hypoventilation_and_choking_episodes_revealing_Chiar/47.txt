Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year history of frequent choking episodes triggered by both solids and liquids, as well as nocturnal choking occurring three to four times per week. She also experienced persistent occipital headaches for 14 years. During at-home monitoring amid the COVID-19 pandemic, she was found to have marked oxygen desaturation events (ranging from 70% to 80%) despite the absence of respiratory symptoms. Several laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations to determine the cause of the choking yielded normal findings. A review of a head CT scan conducted 14 years earlier for unrelated trauma disclosed an overlooked crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck identified Chiari malformation type I (CMI), characterized by a cerebellar tonsillar descent of 11 mm. A polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0 and a predominance of central events (72%). This case demonstrates that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to investigate structural brain anomalies in individuals with unexplained respiratory issues.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by cerebellar tonsils extending downward at least 5 mm past the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI can be identified in 1% of adults, but only around 0.1% of individuals develop symptoms. There is a slight female predilection with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases manifest in patients during their 30s.

The underlying mechanism involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa, which leads to a disproportionate relationship between the size of neural elements and the space they occupy. Despite this, recent findings question the conventional view that Chiari Malformation Type I is invariably pathological; rather, it may encompass a continuum from benign anatomical variations to clinically relevant deformities. Contemporary volumetric analyses reveal marked demographic differences in posterior fossa size that do not consistently align with symptom presence. This shift in perspective emphasizes the importance of tailoring clinical decisions to the patient’s specific symptoms, separating incidental anatomical findings from malformations that warrant surgical management.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical presentation of CMI, marked by a characteristic set of neurological symptoms. The primary feature is sudden onset occipital headache, often triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, and observed in more than 80% of cases. Compared to pediatric cases, adults generally exhibit more pronounced symptoms, particularly when syringomyelia is also present. Lower cranial nerve impairment has been documented in 15%–26% of individuals, commonly manifesting as oropharyngeal disturbances such as palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary symptoms are now acknowledged as a more frequent complication of CMI. In a prospective analysis involving 90 adult patients with CMI, half exhibited sleep-associated respiratory disorders. Among these individuals, a central apnoea index of at least 5 was present in 6.7%, though isolated central sleep apnoea was identified in only one patient, possibly attributable to compression of the brainstem. This form of respiratory dysfunction is not limited to sleep abnormalities; a series of cases reported episodes of acute respiratory insufficiency and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia among patients with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in these cases is attributed to compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) egress, and disturbances in the respiratory regulatory centers, resulting in episodic respiratory manifestations. These manifestations correspond to a secondary form of central hypoventilation syndrome—occasionally referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which differs from the congenital type linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Recent recommendations highlight the significance of this association by advocating for routine polysomnography in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who display possible sleep-related breathing abnormalities. Importantly, posterior fossa decompression may normalize CSF circulation and alleviate respiratory disturbances, reinforcing the need to consider CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early forties came to our family medicine clinic with a 7-year history of sporadic choking episodes. These episodes occurred unexpectedly during both meals and sleep, affecting solids and liquids without preference. She characterized the events as abrupt and unsettling, frequently interrupting eating or awakening her with forceful coughing and gasping; however, they had never resulted in full airway blockage. Nighttime events were reported three to four times each week, and sometimes happened twice in a single night. Over the years, she had received thorough workups, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two esophagogastroduodenoscopies, all yielding normal findings. Nonetheless, her symptoms continued. The repercussions on her social life were pronounced—she avoided restaurants and social events out of concern for having a choking episode in public. This ongoing worry and embarrassment contributed to escalating social isolation and psychological distress.

A more comprehensive review of her clinical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, subsequent to a minor motor vehicle collision during which she experienced a brief loss of consciousness. Soon after the incident, she began to have ongoing headaches affecting the occipital and vertex regions. These headaches were described as constant, with a pressure sensation, and typically confined to a coin-sized spot at the vertex. They were not worsened by coughing, straining, or performing Valsalva maneuvers. She was evaluated by several clinicians, who diagnosed her with nummular headache, and she received numerous local scalp injections, though these provided only fleeting improvement. Notably, she reported pronounced allodynia at the vertex, such that even the mere presence of her hair would provoke discomfort. Eventually, she found that keeping her hair short reduced her baseline pain from 6 out of 10 to 3 out of 10, but this choice conflicted with cultural norms and led to significant psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started tracking her oxygen levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Over the course of several months, she consistently recorded asymptomatic instances where her oxygen saturation decreased to between 70% and 80%. She typically became aware of these occurrences only when she used the device. Several emergency department assessments ultimately linked her symptoms to anxiety.

An important event took place at a foreign airport. As she waited to board her plane, she developed sudden onset vertigo and nausea. Medical staff present assessed her and recorded an oxygen saturation level of 78%. She was subsequently prevented from boarding the aircraft. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms resolved entirely within a few minutes. Later evaluation of her lungs, which included imaging studies and tests of pulmonary function, showed normal results.

In light of her longstanding occipital and vertex headaches, along with increasing episodes of choking and unexplained hypoxemia, a comprehensive neurological diagnosis was sought. Examination of her past medical documentation identified a non-contrast head CT obtained 14 years prior, subsequent to her trauma, which was originally interpreted as unremarkable. On closer re-examination, the scan demonstrated foramen magnum crowding and an absence of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastroesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was initially suspected due to the presence of choking episodes. However, the diagnosis was ruled out owing to unremarkable pH monitoring, normal findings on endoscopy, and absence of improvement despite maximum acid suppression therapy.

The possibility of myasthenia gravis was considered in light of the patient's bulbar symptoms. However, the diagnosis was deemed improbable given negative antibody results, the patient’s absence of fatigability, and no observed fluctuation in symptoms over the course of the day.

An in-depth workup for primary lung pathology was conducted after episodes of hypoxia. Intrinsic pulmonary disease was ruled out based on unremarkable chest imaging and normal results on pulmonary function testing.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly indicated by the patient’s ability to breathe voluntarily while displaying abnormal automatic respiratory control when distracted or asleep. The onset after head injury differentiated it from congenital variants.

The underlying brainstem pathology due to CMI accounted for the patient's symptoms by exerting pressure on the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei, thereby offering a comprehensive diagnosis.

Discussion:

It is important to thoroughly evaluate the timing between this patient’s motor vehicle collision and the appearance of their symptoms. Chiari malformation type I can occur as an inborn abnormality or develop following head trauma or other neurological insults. Modern aetiological schemes (2022) acknowledge this range, differentiating between congenital or primary Chiari malformations and secondary, acquired variants linked to injury or elevations in intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained directly after the injury showed a congested foramen magnum. Since imaging prior to the trauma is unavailable, it is unclear if the trauma resulted in this finding or simply exposed a pre-existing condition. The abrupt appearance of occipital headaches immediately post-injury, with subsequent gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory symptoms spanning 14 years, may suggest either: (1) trauma triggering tonsillar herniation, or (2) trauma aggravating or disclosing an otherwise asymptomatic congenital abnormality.

This case highlights the multifaceted respiratory presentations associated with CMI and contributes to the knowledge of central hypoventilation acquired from structural lesions of the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is essential to appreciating the respiratory pathophysiology of CMI. In such cases, voluntary respiration, which is governed by cortical networks, is generally preserved. Conversely, the automatic regulation of breathing—dependent on brainstem centers and particularly vital during sleep—is impaired owing to the compression of medullary regions by herniated cerebellar tonsils. In the described patient, voluntary breathing was maintained; however, automatic respiratory drive was absent, producing a clinical pattern similar to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit resulting from a distinct underlying cause. The airport event, during which the patient experienced profound oxygen desaturation after briefly becoming distracted but did not perceive breathlessness, clearly demonstrates the isolated disruption of automatic respiratory control: the individual effectively 'forgets to breathe.'

We propose that the underlying mechanism for this patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea during marked episodes of hypoxaemia is a primary dysfunction within the brainstem. This presumed pathological process in the brainstem likely impairs essential ascending afferent pathways required for transmitting sensory information to the cortex, thereby enabling conscious perception. Breathlessness constitutes an interoceptive sensation that relies on the effective processing of these incoming signals by critical brain structures, such as the anterior insular cortex. Interruption of these neural pathways within the brainstem would prevent the cortex from receiving adequate sensory input, leading to an absence of the awareness of air hunger and potentially resulting in hazardous disregard for the patient’s physiological impairment.

The pronounced mismatch between the patient's significant clinical manifestations and the modest polysomnographic result (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, we performed type III rather than type I polysomnography, which does not provide EEG monitoring. The lack of EEG prevented precise assessment of actual sleep duration, likely resulting in considerable underreporting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG, AHI is determined based on the total duration of recording, which encompasses wakeful periods in addition to sleep, rather than exclusive sleep time. Consequently, this methodological issue suggests the recorded AHI of 6.0 probably does not reflect the true extent of sleep-disordered breathing.

Although this limitation exists, there are multiple findings indicating central hypoventilation: (1) central events accounted for 72% of occurrences, even though the total AHI was low; (2) observed oxygen saturation dropped to between 70% and 80% without inducing dyspnoea or patient consciousness, both at the airport and in typical interactions; and (3) anatomical imaging revealed substantial compression of the brainstem, including an 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A Type I polysomnography, incorporating comprehensive EEG surveillance, end-tidal CO₂ measurements, and video documentation, has been scheduled at a sleep center outside our institution. At present, the execution of this assessment is delayed, awaiting alignment of the patient’s schedule, procedural consent, and appointment availability at the off-site location. Following its completion, this evaluation will offer more precise delineation of sleep structure, breathing dynamics, and identification and quantification of central hypoventilation.

We recognize that, based on the current data, central hypoventilation continues to be a strongly substantiated clinical suspicion rather than an unequivocally established diagnosis.

Our patient’s most prominent complaint was oropharyngeal dysphagia impacting both solid and liquid foods, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. Prospective analysis with standardized swallowing evaluation tools identified abnormal swallowing function in 36% of subjects, primarily displaying neurogenic characteristics. The uniform challenge with all types of food textures helps differentiate neurogenic from mechanical forms of dysphagia. Gastroenterology and otolaryngology assessments were repeatedly pursued for the patient’s primary symptom, but all results remained within normal limits; despite this, her definitive diagnosis was postponed for several years. This protracted diagnostic period emphasizes a critical clinical point: when dysphagia or choking occurs without structural anomalies detected via endoscopy, clinicians should consider possible neurologic etiologies. A retrospective survey in pediatric patients experiencing persistent dysphagia revealed a 3.3% rate of CMI, which substantially exceeds the general pediatric prevalence range of 0.1%–0.77%. This finding supports that ongoing dysphagia may represent an important clue to underlying CMI.

The patient described discomfort localized to the vertex, which was alleviated following hair cutting; this observation is notable yet insufficiently explored in the literature. Occipital neuralgia has been documented in conjunction with Chiari malformation type I (CMI), sometimes resulting in heightened scalp sensitivity. For diagnosis, criteria stipulate the presence of 'dysesthesia and/or allodynia during non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair'. Nevertheless, occurrences of occipital neuralgia related to CMI are uncommon, and definitive evidence for a causal relationship is lacking.

Another potential diagnosis is the recently reported ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and symptoms that do not conform to well-defined primary headache types such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. This condition is thought to be mediated by small fibre pathways arising from the scalp, and patients show mixed responses to greater occipital nerve block treatments. In the current case, the symptoms at the vertex could reflect: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with Chiari malformation type I (CMI); (2) a coincidental case of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a completely different primary headache condition. Comprehensive data on the distribution of cutaneous allodynia within CMI cohorts remains insufficient, which is highlighted by the lack of a published tool to evaluate symptomatology in CMI before the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Due to the limited available evidence, we report this as a notable clinical observation rather than establishing it as a distinct diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI, particularly when it manifests with uncommon or confusing symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, which resulted in a 14-year lapse before the correct diagnosis was made. The process of reaching the appropriate neurological diagnosis was marked by early uncertainty and a progressive clarification, a pattern frequently reported in previous studies.
