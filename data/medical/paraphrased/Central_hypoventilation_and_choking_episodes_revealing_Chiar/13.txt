Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early forties reported a 7-year history marked by recurrent choking episodes with both solids and liquids, nocturnal choking episodes occurring three to four times weekly, and persistent occipital headaches present for 14 years. During home monitoring initiated amidst the COVID-19 pandemic, she was incidentally found to have severe episodes of oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) despite the absence of respiratory distress. Repeated laryngoscopies and endoscopies conducted in pursuit of an etiology for the choking events were unremarkable. Re-examination of a cranial CT obtained 14 years prior, originally for head trauma, identified an overlooked crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the brain and cervical spine demonstrated Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with cerebellar tonsillar herniation measuring 11 mm. A polysomnography study revealed mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0), characterized predominantly by central events (72%). This case underscores that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to assess for structural brain lesions in patients presenting with unexplained respiratory abnormalities.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils measuring 5 mm or more beneath the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate a prevalence of CMI in 1% of adults, but clinical symptoms are reported in roughly 0.1% of the overall population. There is a slight female bias with a ratio of 1.3 to 1, and onset most commonly occurs in individuals in their twenties.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, leading to disproportion between the size of neural elements and the capacity of the surrounding compartment. Nevertheless, recent research disputes the long-held notion of CMI as inherently abnormal, suggesting a continuum ranging from benign anatomical differences to forms that cause clinical problems. New volumetric analyses have shown notable population differences in posterior fossa dimensions that do not reliably correspond with symptoms. As a result, this evolving perspective underscores the need for personalized, symptom-oriented clinical management, carefully differentiating benign asymptomatic variants from those malformations necessitating surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical syndrome resulting from CMI, marked by a characteristic group of neurological symptoms. The most prominent feature is episodic occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of individuals. Symptom severity is generally greater in adults than in pediatric patients, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves has been documented in 15%–26% of cases, usually manifesting as oropharyngeal deficits such as weakness of the palate, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory complications are being identified more frequently in association with CMI. In a prospective analysis involving 90 adult individuals diagnosed with CMI, half exhibited sleep-related breathing abnormalities. A central apnoea index of 5 or higher was present in 6.7% of participants, while only a single case involved isolated central sleep apnoea, which may be attributable to compression of the brainstem. These respiratory issues are not limited to nocturnal symptoms; in one case series, CMI was linked to episodes of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia.

Respiratory impairment in this context arises from factors such as compression of the brainstem, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and interruption of neural centers that regulate respiration, all of which can lead to episodic breathing disturbances. These symptoms correspond to an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome, occasionally referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse,’ and are clinically distinct from the congenital syndrome form linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Newer recommendations stress this relationship by advising routine polysomnographic evaluation in CMI patients presenting with probable sleep-related breathing disorders. Notably, surgical decompression of the posterior fossa can reestablish normal CSF circulation and may reverse respiratory issues, highlighting the necessity of considering CMI in the differential diagnosis of unexplained respiratory compromise.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early forties attended our family medicine clinic with a 7-year history of recurrent choking incidents that happened unpredictably during both eating and sleeping. These episodes involved difficulty with both solid and liquid foods, occurring with equal frequency. She described the events as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting meals or abruptly awakening her from sleep with episodes of intense coughing and difficulty breathing, but never leading to complete blockage of her airway. Nocturnal occurrences were reported three to four times weekly, with occasional nights experiencing two separate episodes. Over time, she underwent an extensive workup, which included three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies; all test results were within normal limits. Despite no abnormal findings, her symptoms persisted. The social consequences were profound, as she began to avoid eating out and attending social gatherings, fearing an episode might happen in public. Persistent anxiety and embarrassment from her symptoms led to growing social isolation and psychological distress.

A more comprehensive clinical history indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, after a minor car accident during which she experienced a brief loss of consciousness. Soon after, she began having constant headaches centered on the occipital region and vertex. She characterized the pain as persistent and pressure-like, frequently confined to a coin-sized region at the vertex. The headaches were not intensified by coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Several healthcare providers diagnosed her with nummular headache, for which she received numerous local scalp injections that provided only short-lived symptom relief. The most notable aspect was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, such that even the sensation of her hair resting on the area provoked discomfort. She eventually found that cutting her hair short decreased her usual pain level from 6/10 to 3/10, but this choice conflicted with cultural norms and led to psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient initiated self-assessment of her oxygen saturation at home with a pulse oximeter. Across several months, she consistently recorded episodes in which her oxygen saturation decreased to values between 70% and 80%, despite the absence of symptoms. She was frequently unaware of these events prior to consulting her pulse oximeter. Repeated emergency department visits led to her symptoms being ascribed to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly developed pronounced dizziness accompanied by nausea. Airport medical staff recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being refused permission to board the plane. Providing supplemental oxygen promptly relieved her symptoms in a matter of minutes. Follow-up pulmonary assessment, which involved chest imaging and spirometry, revealed no abnormalities.

In light of the persistent occipital and vertex headaches, repeated choking incidents, and unexplained hypoxemia, efforts were made to identify a single neurological disorder that could explain her symptoms. Examination of her prior medical documentation showed that a non-contrast head CT had been obtained 14 years earlier after her accident and was originally read as normal. On thorough re-examination, however, the scan demonstrated a narrowed foramen magnum and absence of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. These radiologic features were consistent with Chiari malformation type I (CMI), which had initially gone unrecognized. The entire sequence of events for her clinical course is detailed in table 1.

Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Initially, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was suspected due to the presence of choking episodes. However, this diagnosis was ruled out after pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations yielded normal results, and the patient showed no improvement with maximum acid-suppressive therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was evaluated due to symptoms affecting the bulbar region. Negative antibody testing, lack of fatigability and absence of diurnal variation made this unlikely.

Primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated following hypoxic episodes. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests excluded intrinsic lung disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by intact voluntary respiration alongside compromised involuntary respiratory drive during periods of distraction or sleep. The onset after head injury differentiated it from congenital variants.

Brainstem pathology secondary to CMI ultimately explained all symptoms through compression of the respiratory centres and lower cranial nerve nuclei, providing the unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

It is important to assess the timing of this patient’s symptom emergence in relation to the motor vehicle accident. Chiari malformation type I is recognized both as a congenital disorder and as a possible outcome following cranial trauma or neurological insult. Recent aetiological frameworks2022 delineate this range, differentiating between primary (congenital) Chiari malformations and secondary, acquired variants that develop due to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, the CT imaging obtained right after the incident showed a crowded foramen magnum. However, lacking baseline imaging prior to the trauma, it remains unclear if the injury produced this finding or simply brought a preexisting condition to light. The acute appearance of occipital headaches after the accident, along with the gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory complaints over a 14-year period, may suggest one of two possibilities: (1) trauma directly led to tonsillar herniation, or (2) the injury aggravated or exposed a congenital abnormality that was previously without symptoms.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory presentations associated with CMI, contributing to our knowledge of how structural brainstem abnormalities can produce acquired central hypoventilation. Recognizing the difference between voluntary and automatic breathing is fundamental to interpreting the respiratory pathophysiology of CMI. In patients with CMI, voluntary breathing—governed by cortical networks—usually remains unaffected. However, the capacity for automatic breathing, which relies on brainstem centers and is particularly vital during sleep, is impaired as a result of compression of medullary regions by herniated tonsils. The individual described maintained the ability to breathe voluntarily but lacked spontaneous automatic respiratory effort, a clinical presentation akin to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome but arising through a separate pathological process. The incident at the airport, during which a brief period of inattention led to severe oxygen desaturation without subjective breathlessness, serves as a stark example of this isolated deficit in automatic respiration: the patient, in effect, ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that a fundamental dysfunction within the brainstem underlies the patient's striking lack of dyspnoea despite episodes of marked hypoxaemia. This suspected brainstem abnormality probably disrupts essential ascending afferent pathways required to reach the cortex and produce conscious perception. Breathlessness represents a form of interoception reliant on the cortical processing—particularly within regions such as the anterior insular cortex—of these ascending neural signals. If such signals are interrupted in their passage through the brainstem, the cortical areas involved would not receive the necessary input to elicit awareness of air hunger, providing a rationale for the patient's dangerous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

A notable difference exists between the patient’s profound clinical symptoms and the relatively low AHI of 6.0 observed on polysomnography, which requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, a type III sleep study, as opposed to a type I study, was utilized; this version does not incorporate EEG monitoring. As a result, we were unable to measure actual total sleep time, almost certainly leading to a significant underreporting of respiratory events. In the absence of EEG data, the AHI is derived from the total duration of the recording—which encompasses both sleep and wakefulness—instead of periods of sleep alone. Consequently, this approach likely yields a falsely low AHI value, meaning that the documented index of 6.0 underrepresents the true burden of sleep-disordered breathing in this patient.

In spite of this constraint, multiple findings suggest central hypoventilation: (1) central events accounted for the majority (72%) even though the total AHI remained low; (2) oxygen saturation levels were observed to fall to 70%–80% without the patient experiencing dyspnoea or being aware of these episodes (noted both at the airport and during regular assessments); and (3) imaging revealed substantial compression of the brainstem, demonstrated by an 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A comprehensive sleep study (Type I polysomnography) incorporating full EEG monitoring, end-tidal CO₂ measurement, and video surveillance has been scheduled at an outside sleep center. However, the test has not yet been conducted, as it depends on organizing the patient’s availability, securing their agreement to undergo the procedure, and scheduling at the external site. Once this assessment is performed, it is expected to yield a more precise evaluation of sleep stages, breathing patterns, and the existence and extent of central hypoventilation.

We acknowledge that, given the current evidence, central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis, rather than a definitively confirmed diagnosis.

The patient’s most troubling complaint was oropharyngeal dysphagia, which impacted both solid and liquid foods to the same degree and was accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. A prospective study utilizing standardized swallowing evaluations identified dysfunction in 36% of cases, predominantly displaying neurogenic characteristics. Difficulty swallowing both solids and liquids equally helps differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical causes. The patient's primary symptom was investigated multiple times by gastroenterology and otolaryngology, and evaluations yielded normal results; nonetheless, there was a significant delay of several years before a diagnosis was established. This extended diagnostic process underscores a key clinical point: when dysphagia or choking occurs without evident structural abnormalities on endoscopy, a neurological cause should be suspected. In a retrospective review, the rate of CMI among children experiencing chronic dysphagia was 3.3%, considerably exceeding the reported prevalence of 0.1%–0.77% in the wider pediatric population. These findings indicate that prolonged dysphagia may serve as a significant clinical marker of underlying CMI.

The patient's description of vertex pain alleviated by cutting their hair highlights a curious and insufficiently explored clinical observation. Scalp hypersensitivity may be observed with occipital neuralgia in the context of CMI; criteria for diagnosing occipital neuralgia include "dysesthesia and/or allodynia evident upon innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair." Nonetheless, reports connecting occipital neuralgia with CMI are uncommon, and definitive evidence for a causal relationship is lacking.

Another possibility to consider is the more recently recognized 'burning vertex syndrome,' characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and symptoms that do not align precisely with established categories of primary headaches like migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The underlying pathophysiology is thought to involve small fiber pathways within the scalp, and patient responses to greater occipital nerve blockade have been inconsistent. In this case, the vertex-related symptoms could be attributed to: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) an incidental coexistence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache disorder entirely. There is a notable lack of systematic reporting of cutaneous allodynia in individuals with CMI, reflected in the previous unavailability of specific instruments to evaluate these symptoms before the advent of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Given the limited evidence base, we report this observation as an intriguing clinical finding rather than establishing it as a diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI, particularly when it manifests with unusual or deceptive signs such as central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a fourteen-year interval before an accurate diagnosis was established. The process, marked by early misinterpretations and a progressive approach toward identifying the neurological etiology, is a scenario often described in published reports.
