Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early 40s was evaluated for a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes with both solids and liquids, including nocturnal attacks occurring 3–4 times per week, as well as ongoing occipital headaches that had persisted for 14 years. While monitoring at home during the COVID-19 pandemic, she was found to have episodes of marked oxygen desaturation (ranging from 70% to 80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Repeated laryngoscopy and endoscopy examinations investigating the choking did not reveal any abnormalities. Reassessment of a CT scan of the head from 14 years earlier, initially conducted for trauma, uncovered an overlooked crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI imaging of the head and neck demonstrated Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with a descent of the cerebellar tonsils measuring 11 mm. Polysomnography identified mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0), predominantly due to central events (72%). This report underscores that CMI can manifest with respiratory symptoms closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the necessity of evaluating for structural brain anomalies in patients presenting with unexplained respiratory symptoms.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils measuring at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI is observed in 1% of adults, yet only about 0.1% of people develop clinical symptoms. There is a slight female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, with most cases becoming apparent in patients during their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an undersized posterior fossa during development, resulting in a disparity between the volume of neural components and the confined area. Yet, recent data call into question the classic view of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) as inherently abnormal, suggesting instead a range spanning benign anatomical variations to clinically impactful anomalies. Advanced volumetric analyses indicate marked demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions, with no reliable association with clinical manifestations. This evolving perspective underscores the need for tailored, symptom-oriented management strategies, allowing clinicians to separate asymptomatic anatomical variants from cases necessitating operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical presentation associated with CMI, marked by a specific set of neurological symptoms. The primary symptom is episodic occipital headache that is triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of patients. In adults, symptoms are generally more pronounced compared to children, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is documented in 15%–26% of cases, most often manifesting as oropharyngeal difficulties, including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary symptoms are now acknowledged as a growing complication associated with CMI. In a prospective analysis involving 90 adults diagnosed with CMI, half of the cohort exhibited sleep-associated breathing disorders. A central apnoea index of at least 5 was detected in 6.7% of subjects, but purely central sleep apnoea was noted in just a single patient, which may indicate underlying brainstem compression. The scope of this respiratory impairment goes beyond disturbances during sleep; a series of cases has reported episodes of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia among individuals with CMI.

Respiratory dysfunction in this context arises from brainstem compression, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and impairment of the neural centres responsible for respiratory regulation, producing intermittent breathing disturbances. These respiratory issues constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome, often referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’, which differs from the congenital form related to PHOX2B gene mutations. Recent recommendations highlight the significance of this association by advising routine polysomnography in patients with CMI who exhibit signs suggestive of sleep-related breathing disorders. Notably, decompression of the posterior fossa can normalize CSF circulation and potentially ameliorate respiratory symptoms, highlighting the critical need to consider CMI when evaluating unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A female in her early 40s came to our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year history of sporadic choking events that happened unexpectedly during both meals and sleep, involving both solid and liquid foods. She characterized the episodes as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting meals or waking her from sleep with forceful coughing and gasping, but never resulting in complete airway blockage. Nighttime episodes were reported three to four times weekly, and sometimes occurred twice in a single night. Throughout these years, she had several assessments including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all of which yielded normal findings. Nonetheless, her symptoms continued unabated. The effect on her social life was considerable; she avoided dining out and attending social events out of concern for choking in public. As a result, ongoing worry and shame led to progressive social isolation and heightened emotional distress.

A more comprehensive account of her medical history indicated that her symptoms first appeared 14 years ago after a minor car accident that resulted in a brief period of unconsciousness. Soon after, she began experiencing ongoing headaches in the occipital and vertex regions. She characterized these headaches as constant, with a sensation of pressure, and frequently confined to a coin-sized spot at the vertex. The headaches did not worsen with actions such as coughing, straining, or performing Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple clinicians identified her condition as nummular headache, and she received numerous localized scalp injections, which only provided temporary alleviation. The most notable aspect was pronounced allodynia at the vertex; even the simple act of her hair resting on this area would provoke discomfort. She eventually learned that keeping her hair cut short could lower her baseline pain rating from 6 out of 10 to 3 out of 10, although this conflicted with cultural norms and contributed to psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started using a home pulse oximeter to regularly check her oxygen levels. Over a span of months, she consistently recorded asymptomatic reductions in oxygen saturation falling into the 70%–80% range. She typically remained unaware of these occurrences unless she measured with the device. Several visits to the emergency department resulted in her symptoms being ascribed to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she was waiting to board, she developed sudden dizziness and nausea. Medical staff on the scene measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, after which she was not permitted to fly. She received supplemental oxygen, resulting in total resolution of her symptoms within several minutes. Later assessment by a pulmonary specialist, involving chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal findings.

Considering the coexistence of persistent headaches localized to the occipital and vertex regions, recurrent choking spells, and otherwise unaccounted for hypoxemia, clinicians sought a single neurological explanation. A review of her prior medical history uncovered a non-contrast head CT performed 14 years prior, subsequent to her accident, which had initially been interpreted as within normal limits. Nevertheless, on thorough retrospective analysis, the scan demonstrated a tightly packed foramen magnum and obliteration of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Given the presence of choking episodes, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected. However, this diagnosis was ruled out due to unremarkable pH monitoring results, normal findings on endoscopic examination, and the absence of improvement with maximum acid-suppressive therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered because of bulbar symptoms. However, negative results for antibodies, no evidence of fatigability, and the stable nature of symptoms throughout the day argued against this diagnosis.

Following hypoxic episodes, thorough evaluation was performed to rule out primary lung disease. Both chest imaging and pulmonary function assessments were unremarkable, effectively excluding intrinsic pulmonary pathology.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (also known as acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by the maintenance of conscious, voluntary respiration with a loss of involuntary respiratory drive when the patient was distracted or asleep. Its development after head injury set it apart from the congenital variants.

Compression of the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei due to brainstem involvement from CMI accounted for all clinical manifestations, thereby offering a single, cohesive diagnosis.

Discussion:

The close timing between the patient’s motor vehicle collision and the emergence of symptoms requires thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is known to present as a congenital disorder, but it can also develop following head trauma or other neurologic insults. Recent aetiological frameworks (2022) delineate this range, separating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary, or acquired, variants attributed to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained right after the injury showed a narrowed foramen magnum. Because pre-injury imaging was unavailable, it is unclear whether this feature was a direct result of the trauma or if it existed beforehand and was only uncovered by the event. The patient experienced sudden occipital headache after the accident, and subsequently developed gradually worsening bulbar and respiratory symptoms over the next 14 years. These clinical changes may be attributable to either: (1) the trauma leading to new tonsillar herniation, or (2) the trauma aggravating or exposing an otherwise silent congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory features associated with CMI, contributing to our knowledge of acquired central hypoventilation in the context of structural lesions of the brainstem. Recognizing the separation between voluntary and automatic respiratory control is fundamental to comprehending the respiratory pathophysiology seen in CMI. In these patients, voluntary ventilation, which is governed by cortical circuitry, is usually preserved. However, automatic breathing, orchestrated by brainstem centers and vital during sleep, can be disrupted by compression of medullary regions due to cerebellar tonsillar descent. Our patient demonstrated retained ability for voluntary ventilation but lacked automatic respiratory drive, a pattern bearing resemblance to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though driven by a different underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, during which a transient lapse in attention led to significant desaturation without associated dyspnea, starkly demonstrates this specific deficit in automatic control—here, the patient ‘forgets to breathe’ when not consciously aware.

We propose that an underlying brainstem dysfunction accounts for the patient’s striking lack of breathlessness during periods of marked hypoxaemia. Such brainstem pathology may compromise the essential ascending afferent pathways required for generating conscious respiratory sensation. Breathlessness constitutes a type of interoceptive perception, which relies upon the relay and cortical processing of these sensory signals—particularly within areas such as the anterior insular cortex. If these afferent messages are interrupted as they pass through the brainstem, the cortical centers would not receive the information needed to produce the sensation of dyspnoea, thereby explaining the patient’s perilous unawareness of their respiratory compromise.

The considerable gap between the pronounced clinical manifestations observed in our patient and the relatively low apnea-hypopnea index (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, we employed a type III polysomnography instead of the more comprehensive type I study; the former does not provide electroencephalographic (EEG) monitoring. Consequently, we could not determine actual sleep duration, which probably led to substantial underreporting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG data, the AHI calculation is based on the entire recording period, encompassing both sleep and wakefulness, rather than restricted to genuine sleep intervals. This methodological constraint suggests that the documented AHI of 6.0 is likely an underestimate of the true extent of sleep-disordered breathing.

Although this limitation exists, multiple findings point toward central hypoventilation: (1) a majority of central events (72%) occur even though the total AHI remains low; (2) there are observed episodes of oxygen saturation dropping to 70%–80% without symptoms such as dyspnoea or any awareness by the patient (noted both at the airport and in standard assessments); and (3) imaging shows marked compression at the brainstem, with tonsillar descent measuring 11 mm.

A Type I polysomnographic evaluation—including comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal CO₂ measurement, and video surveillance—has been scheduled at an offsite sleep facility. Nevertheless, the study has not yet been performed due to ongoing efforts to coordinate patient availability, obtain patient consent, and secure an appointment at the external centre. Upon completion, this assessment is expected to yield more conclusive information regarding sleep structure, respiratory function during sleep, and the identification and assessment of central hypoventilation.

It is recognized that, based on current data, central hypoventilation continues to be a strongly considered clinical possibility, though it is not yet definitively established as a diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, impacting both solid and liquid foods to the same degree and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, represented the patient’s most troubling complaint. A prospective study utilizing validated swallowing assessment tools identified dysfunction in 36% of patients, with neurogenic etiologies being the most common pattern observed. The presence of similar difficulties across all food consistencies helps differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from its mechanical counterpart. The patient’s primary symptom was repeatedly and initially evaluated by gastroenterology and otolaryngology specialists, with results showing no abnormalities; however, a definitive diagnosis was not made for several years. This delay underscores an important clinical point: when dysphagia or choking occurs without evidence of anatomical abnormality on endoscopic examination, clinicians should consider neurological causes. According to a retrospective study, the prevalence of CMI in children with chronic dysphagia was 3.3%, which is significantly greater than the prevalence of 0.1%–0.77% found in the general pediatric population. This implies that persistent dysphagia may serve as a significant indicator for underlying CMI.

The patient's description of vertex pain that improves following hair cutting highlights a compelling yet little-explored clinical presentation. Occipital neuralgia may manifest alongside CMI, frequently resulting in increased scalp sensitivity. Its diagnostic criteria include “dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair.” Nonetheless, documentation connecting occipital neuralgia and CMI in the literature is uncommon, and there is currently a lack of definitive evidence supporting a causal relationship.

Another possible explanation is the recently identified ‘burning vertex syndrome,’ characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features that are not clearly aligned with conventional primary headache categories like migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology involves small fibre pathways within the scalp, and patients have exhibited inconsistent responses to greater occipital nerve blocks. In the case described here, the vertex manifestations may indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) coincidental burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct, unrelated primary headache condition. There is a notable deficiency in systematic reports detailing cutaneous allodynia patterns among those with CMI, demonstrated by the lack of available tools to evaluate these symptoms in CMI patients prior to the advent of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Considering this limited body of evidence, we report this phenomenon as an intriguing clinical observation, rather than a conclusive diagnostic attribute.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI, particularly when it manifests with unusual or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a delayed diagnosis spanning 14 years. The process of diagnosis—marked by early uncertainty and a slow progression toward the accurate neurological identification—echoes similar patterns described in published accounts.
