Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early 40s reported a 7-year duration of recurrent choking episodes with both solids and liquids, along with nocturnal choking occurring 3–4 times per week, and longstanding occipital headaches present for 14 years. While self-monitoring at home during the COVID-19 pandemic, she incidentally discovered episodes of marked oxygen desaturation (ranging from 70% to 80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies, undertaken to evaluate her choking, showed no abnormalities. Retrospective evaluation of a CT head scan from 14 years prior (initially performed following unrelated head trauma) identified a previously missed crowded foramen magnum. MRI of the head and neck later demonstrated Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with cerebellar tonsils descending 11 mm. Polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnoea with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, predominantly central in origin (72%). This report underscores that CMI may present with respiratory features closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to include structural intracranial abnormalities in the differential diagnosis of unexplained respiratory symptoms.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI images. Recent imaging research indicates that CMI is present in around 1% of adults, but only about 0.1% of people develop symptoms. There is a slight female preponderance with a ratio of 1.3:1, and the disorder most often manifests in the third decade of life.

The underlying mechanism involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa size, which leads to a disproportion between neural elements and the surrounding intracranial compartment. Yet, recent research questions the long-held view that Chiari Malformation I (CMI) is invariably pathological, suggesting it may represent a continuum from benign anatomical differences to abnormalities with clinical impact. Modern volumetric analyses reveal significant variation in posterior fossa dimensions across populations, with no reliable association to patient symptoms. This evolving perspective calls for personalized, symptom-driven management strategies that differentiate between incidental anatomical variations and clinically relevant conditions that merit surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome denotes the clinical expression of CMI, marked by a specific cluster of neurological signs and symptoms. The principal complaint is an episodic occipital headache that is triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of patients. Compared to children, adults often experience more pronounced symptoms, particularly in the presence of associated syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is seen in 15%–26% of individuals and usually manifests as oropharyngeal issues, including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Pulmonary symptoms are being identified more frequently as a complication associated with CMI. In a forward-looking cohort of 90 adult individuals diagnosed with CMI, half exhibited sleep-disordered breathing. Among these, 6.7% demonstrated a central apnoea index of 5 or greater, though isolated central sleep apnoea was identified in just one individual, possibly indicating compression at the brainstem. These respiratory issues are not limited to nocturnal events; a published case series described instances of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia in patients affected by CMI.

Respiratory dysfunction arises due to compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and impairment of centers responsible for respiratory regulation, which results in episodic respiratory manifestations. These respiratory features constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome, sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’, which is different from the congenital form linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Contemporary recommendations highlight the significance of this association, advising routine polysomnographic evaluation in CMI patients with suspected sleep-related breathing disturbances. Notably, posterior fossa decompression may normalize CSF flow and lead to resolution of respiratory symptoms, highlighting the need to identify CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory compromise.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s came to our family medicine practice reporting a 7-year duration of recurrent choking incidents that happened unpredictably during eating and at night, with both solid and liquid foods equally implicated. She characterized these events as abrupt and distressing, frequently interrupting her meals or waking her at night with severe coughing and gasping, though she never experienced full airway blockage. Nighttime occurrences happened three to four times weekly, and sometimes twice in the same night. Over time, she underwent multiple assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two esophagogastroduodenoscopies, all yielding normal results. Despite these normal investigations, her symptoms persisted. The condition significantly affected her social life, leading her to avoid dining out or attending events from fear of choking publicly. The resulting worry and embarrassment progressively caused her to withdraw socially and contributed to emotional hardship.

A more comprehensive assessment of her medical history indicated that her symptoms started 14 years prior, immediately following a minor car accident in which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after, she experienced persistent headaches localized to the occipital region and the vertex. She characterized the pain as unremitting, with a pressure-like sensation, typically confined to an area about the size of a coin at the vertex. The headaches were not worsened by coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple clinicians subsequently diagnosed her with nummular headache, and she received several localized scalp injections, providing only temporary alleviation. A particularly notable symptom was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, such that even the mere presence of her hair would provoke discomfort. She eventually realized that cutting her hair short decreased her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, although doing so conflicted with her cultural norms and led to psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started using a home pulse oximeter to check her oxygen levels. Over a period of several months, she consistently recorded episodes of asymptomatic oxygen saturation falling into the 70%–80% range. She typically only became aware of these events when she measured with the device. Several emergency department assessments ascribed her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport when, prior to boarding, she developed sudden dizziness accompanied by nausea. Emergency staff on location noted her oxygen saturation was 78%, resulting in her being unable to board her flight. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms fully subsided within a few minutes. Later assessment by pulmonary specialists—including imaging of the chest and lung function tests—revealed no abnormalities.

In light of her chronic headaches localized to the occipital and vertex regions, recurring episodes of choking, and episodes of unexplained hypoxia, clinicians sought to identify a single neurological cause. A review of her previous medical documentation disclosed that she had undergone a non-contrast CT head scan 14 years prior, subsequent to her accident, which was originally interpreted as unremarkable. Nevertheless, careful retrospective examination of this imaging identified features such as narrowing of the foramen magnum and obliteration of the cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. These radiological signs were consistent with CMI and had not been recognized at the time. Table 1 outlines the detailed sequence of her clinical presentation.

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the patient's choking episodes, gastroesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was suspected at first. However, this condition was ruled out due to normal pH monitoring results, unremarkable endoscopic findings, and no improvement despite the use of high-dose acid suppressive therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was evaluated due to symptoms affecting the bulbar region. Negative antibody testing, lack of fatigability and absence of diurnal variation made this unlikely.

Primary pulmonary pathology was extensively investigated following hypoxic episodes. Normal chest imaging and pulmonary function tests excluded intrinsic lung disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly suspected due to the retention of voluntary respiration contrasted with loss of automatic breathing regulation during periods of sleep or distraction. Its emergence after cranial injury differentiated it from the congenital variants.

Brainstem pathology secondary to CMI ultimately explained all symptoms through compression of the respiratory centres and lower cranial nerve nuclei, providing the unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of this patient’s motor vehicle collision relative to the development of symptoms requires thorough assessment. Chiari malformation type I is known to occur as a congenital anomaly but may also arise following head injury or other neurological insults. Current etiological frameworks2022 reflect this range, differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants associated with trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, the CT scan obtained directly after the injury showed a narrowed foramen magnum. Since there is no imaging from before the trauma, it remains unclear if this abnormality resulted from the injury itself or if the event simply uncovered an existing condition. The patient’s sudden onset of occipital headaches immediately post-trauma, with subsequent gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory manifestations spanning 14 years, may indicate either (1) cerebellar tonsillar herniation triggered by the injury, or (2) trauma that aggravated or exposed an otherwise silent congenital anomaly.

This report highlights the intricate respiratory manifestations associated with CMI and contributes to our comprehension of acquired patterns of central hypoventilation in the context of structural lesions involving the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is pivotal to elucidating the respiratory pathophysiology seen in CMI. Cortically mediated voluntary breathing is typically preserved in individuals with CMI. Conversely, automatic respiration—which depends on brainstem centers and is vital during sleep—can be impaired as a result of cerebellar tonsil-induced compression of the medullary region. In this case, the patient retained the ability for voluntary ventilation but exhibited a loss of automatic respiratory drive, demonstrating a clinical profile analogous to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit arising from a different underlying process. The incident at the airport, where a brief lapse in attention led to severe oxygen desaturation without the sensation of dyspnea, starkly demonstrates this isolated failure of automatic respiratory regulation: the patient, in effect, “forgets to breathe.”

We propose that an underlying dysfunction within the brainstem is responsible for the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea despite marked episodes of hypoxaemia. Such brainstem pathology would plausibly impair essential afferent neural pathways that ascend to the cortex and are necessary for conscious sensory perception. The subjective sense of breathlessness represents an interoceptive process that relies on afferent signalling to specific cerebral regions, notably the anterior insular cortex. Interruption of these signals as they traverse the brainstem would prevent the cortex from obtaining adequate information to elicit the sensation of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The notable difference observed between the patient's pronounced clinical symptoms and the relatively low apnea-hypopnea index (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to practical constraints, type III polysomnography was employed instead of type I, resulting in the absence of EEG monitoring. Without EEG, it was not possible to precisely determine total sleep time, which likely caused substantial underreporting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG information, the AHI was derived from the total duration of the recording, encompassing both sleep and wakefulness, instead of actual sleep periods exclusively. This methodological shortcoming suggests that the measured AHI of 6.0 probably does not fully reflect the actual extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Nonetheless, certain findings are indicative of central hypoventilation: (1) a majority of central events (72%) occur, even though the total AHI remains low; (2) oxygen saturation levels fell to 70%–80% in the absence of dyspnea or any awareness by the patient (both at the airport and during standard assessments); and (3) anatomical imaging demonstrated marked brainstem compression, with 11 mm of tonsillar herniation.

A comprehensive sleep study (Type I polysomnography) including full electroencephalography, end-tidal capnography, and video monitoring has been scheduled at an outside sleep facility. The study has not yet been completed due to the need to coordinate the patient’s availability, obtain the patient's agreement to undergo the procedure, and secure an appropriate time at the remote location. Upon completion, the results are expected to yield a more precise evaluation of sleep structure, breathing patterns, and the existence and extent of central hypoventilation.

We acknowledge that, given the current evidence, central hypoventilation remains a well-supported clinical hypothesis, rather than a definitively confirmed diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, impacting both solids and liquids to the same extent and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, was the most troubling symptom for our patient. Prospective evaluation with standardized swallowing tests demonstrated dysfunction in 36% of patients, with neurogenic causes being the most common. Neurogenic dysphagia is characterized by equal impairment across all consistencies, in contrast to mechanical dysphagia. The patient's primary and most bothersome symptom was explored repeatedly through gastroenterological and otolaryngological assessments, all of which yielded unremarkable results; nonetheless, the diagnosis was not made for several years. This prolonged time to diagnosis underscores a critical clinical point: when dysphagia or choking persists in the absence of an identifiable structural abnormality on endoscopic evaluation, a neurological etiology should be considered. In a retrospective study, the frequency of CMI in children presenting with persistent dysphagia was found to be 3.3%, significantly greater than the reported 0.1%–0.77% prevalence seen in the general pediatric population. This finding implies that persistent dysphagia may serve as an important marker for underlying CMI.

The patient’s account of experiencing vertex pain that improves following a haircut highlights a curious but insufficiently explored clinical observation. Occipital neuralgia, which may present alongside Chiari Malformation type I (CMI), is known to produce hypersensitivity of the scalp; its diagnostic features include “dysesthesia and/or allodynia evident when the scalp and/or hair is subjected to non-painful stimulation.” Nonetheless, reports connecting occipital neuralgia with CMI are uncommon, and current evidence does not definitively confirm a causal relationship.

Another possibility to consider is the recently identified ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by pain in the scalp, cutaneous allodynia, and features that are not easily classified within traditional categories of primary headaches, such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology may involve small fiber pathways from the scalp, and patients may exhibit varied responses to greater occipital nerve block treatment. In the case described, the symptoms at the vertex could signify: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) an unrelated coincident case of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache disorder. Comprehensive documentation of patterns of cutaneous allodynia in individuals with CMI is notably scarce, as demonstrated by the lack of any dedicated assessment tool for such symptoms in CMI patients before the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Owing to this limited body of evidence, we present this observation as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than establishing it as a clear diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI, particularly when it manifests with unusual or confusing symptoms like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, which in this instance contributed to a 14-year period before definitive diagnosis was established. The prolonged and convoluted process that eventually led to accurate neurological identification echoes patterns reported in the literature.
