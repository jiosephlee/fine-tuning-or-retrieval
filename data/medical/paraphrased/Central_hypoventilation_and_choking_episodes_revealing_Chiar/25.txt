Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year duration of recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, in addition to nocturnal choking occurring 3–4 times per week and chronic occipital headaches persisting for 14 years. During home monitoring implemented amid the COVID-19 pandemic, severe oxygen desaturation events (70%–80%) were noted in the absence of visible respiratory distress. Serial laryngoscopies and endoscopies conducted to explore the etiology of her choking episodes yielded unremarkable findings. Re-evaluation of a head CT obtained 14 years prior, originally performed due to trauma, identified an overlooked crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck demonstrated Chiari malformation type I (CMI) characterized by 11 mm herniation of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, and a predominance (72%) of central events. This case highlights that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underscoring the need to evaluate for structural anomalies of the brain in individuals with unexplained respiratory symptoms.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending a minimum of 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum as visualized on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate a prevalence of CMI in 1% of adults, whereas only around 0.1% of the general population exhibit symptoms. There is a slight female predominance with a ratio of 1.3:1, and clinical presentation most often occurs in individuals in their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology is attributed to a congenitally reduced posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between neural elements and the spatial confines. Nonetheless, recent research has questioned the longstanding notion of Chiari Malformation Type I (CMI) being inherently pathological, suggesting that it represents a continuum ranging from normal anatomical variants to conditions that are clinically relevant. Current volumetric analyses reveal marked demographic differences in posterior fossa size that do not consistently correspond to clinical symptoms. This shift in perspective mandates a personalized, symptom-driven approach to management, wherein clinicians must differentiate incidental anatomical findings from pathological conditions that warrant operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinical presentation associated with CMI and is marked by a particular set of neurological symptoms. The primary feature is intermittent occipital headache triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of patients. Adults generally display more pronounced symptoms than pediatric cases, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves has been documented in 15%–26% of individuals, commonly manifesting as oropharyngeal disturbances including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now more frequently acknowledged as complications of CMI. A prospective investigation involving 90 adults diagnosed with CMI revealed that half exhibited sleep-related breathing abnormalities. Among these, a central apnoea index of 5 or greater was detected in 6.7% of participants; however, true central sleep apnoea was identified in only a single individual, possibly indicating compression at the brainstem. The spectrum of respiratory dysfunction goes further than disturbed sleep patterns; a series of cases highlighted occurrences of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia in individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in these cases arises from compression of the brainstem, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, and disturbances affecting the respiratory regulatory centers, ultimately causing episodic breathing abnormalities. These manifestations constitute an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome—referred to by some as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which is distinct from the congenital form linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Recent recommendations stress the significance of this association by suggesting routine polysomnography for individuals with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who are suspected to have sleep-related breathing disorders. Importantly, performing posterior fossa decompression may restore CSF circulation and alleviate respiratory manifestations, highlighting the critical need to consider CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory symptoms.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s came to our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year history of recurring episodes of choking that happened unpredictably during both meals and sleep, affecting ingestion of solids and liquids to the same degree. She characterized these events as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting her meals or waking her abruptly from sleep with forceful coughing and difficulty breathing, though she never experienced a fully blocked airway. Nighttime episodes occurred between three and four times per week, with some nights featuring two occurrences. During this time, she had been subjected to multiple investigations, including three flexible laryngoscopy procedures and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Despite the absence of findings on these tests, her symptoms continued. The impact on her social life was considerable; she deliberately avoided eating out or attending social gatherings out of concern over public choking incidents. As a result, her ongoing anxiety and sense of embarrassment contributed to progressive social isolation and emotional distress.

A comprehensive review of her clinical background indicated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, following a minor car accident in which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after the incident, she began to experience ongoing headaches located in the occipital and vertex regions. She characterized these headaches as constant, with a pressure sensation, frequently restricted to a small, coin-sized area at the vertex. The pain was not intensified by actions such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Various clinicians concluded she was suffering from nummular headache, leading to repeated localized scalp injections that provided only short-term improvement. The most prominent symptom was pronounced allodynia over the vertex, where even her hair's weight precipitated discomfort. Eventually, she found that keeping her hair short lowered her average pain from 6/10 to 3/10, although this choice conflicted with cultural norms and resulted in emotional strain.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started checking her oxygen levels at home with a personal pulse oximeter. Throughout several months, she consistently recorded episodes where her oxygen saturation decreased to between 70% and 80% without any accompanying symptoms. She often was unaware of these events until she used the pulse oximeter. Several emergency department assessments attributed her symptoms to anxiety.

An important event took place at an international airport. As she was awaiting her flight, she developed sudden onset of vertigo and nausea. Medical staff at the scene recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from boarding. Her symptoms subsided entirely within minutes following the application of supplemental oxygen. Later assessments, which included chest radiography and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal findings.

Considering her persistent occipital and vertex headaches, recurrent choking spells, and idiopathic hypoxemia, a central neurological etiology was sought. Review of her prior medical documentation showed that a non-contrast head CT had been obtained 14 years prior after her accident, with an initial interpretation of normal findings. Nevertheless, closer reassessment of the imaging demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and obliteration of cerebrospinal fluid spaces at the craniocervical junction. Such imaging features were consistent with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) and had not been identified in the original assessment. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Initially, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was suspected due to the presence of choking episodes. However, normal pH monitoring, unremarkable endoscopic findings, and absence of improvement following maximum acid suppression therapy ruled out this condition.

Myasthenia gravis was considered because of bulbar symptoms. However, negative results on antibody assays, as well as the absence of fatigability or diurnal fluctuations, argued against this diagnosis.

Comprehensive evaluation of pulmonary causes was performed after incidents of hypoxemia. Both chest imaging and pulmonary function assessments were within normal limits, effectively ruling out primary pulmonary disease.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by intact voluntary respiration alongside compromised automatic breathing when the patient was distracted or asleep. Its onset after head trauma differentiated it from congenital variants.

All symptoms were ultimately attributed to brainstem involvement from CMI, which resulted in compression of the respiratory centers and the nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby offering a comprehensive explanation for the clinical presentation.

Discussion:

It is important to closely evaluate the timing between this patient’s motor vehicle accident and the emergence of her symptoms. Chiari malformation type I is reported to occur as a congenital disorder, but may also arise following head trauma or various other neurological insults. Recent etiological classification systems in 2022 reflect this range, differentiating primary congenital Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that are attributed to traumatic events or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, a CT scan obtained directly after the incident showed an overcrowded foramen magnum. Since there are no pre-injury imaging studies, it is unclear if the trauma was responsible for this feature or simply brought it to attention. The acute onset of occipital headache immediately following the accident, with subsequent gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory manifestations over the ensuing 14 years, might represent either (1) tonsillar herniation resulting from trauma or (2) trauma aggravating or disclosing a previously silent congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory features associated with CMI and broadens our comprehension of acquired central hypoventilation observed in structural brainstem disorders. Differentiating voluntary from automatic breathing is essential when considering the respiratory physiology of CMI. While voluntary respiration, which relies on cortical circuits, usually remains unaffected in CMI individuals, automatic breathing—regulated by brainstem centers and critical during sleep—can be impaired as a result of medullary compression by the tonsils. In this patient, voluntary breathing was retained, yet automatic respiratory control was absent, presenting a pattern reminiscent of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome but arising through a distinct pathological process. The airport incident, where a fleeting lapse in attention resulted in severe oxygen desaturation without the sensation of dyspnoea, compellingly demonstrates this selective impairment of automatic regulation: the patient effectively 'forgot to breathe.'

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea during marked episodes of oxygen desaturation stems from a primary dysfunction of the brainstem. This underlying brainstem abnormality likely interferes with the essential ascending afferent pathways responsible for transmitting signals to the cortex that elicit conscious awareness. The sensation of breathlessness represents an aspect of interoception, requiring appropriate processing of these afferent messages by specific cortical areas, such as the anterior insular cortex. Disruption of these neural signals as they pass through the brainstem would prevent cortical reception of inputs necessary for the perception of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s perilous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The substantial gap between the patient’s pronounced clinical manifestations and the relatively modest polysomnography results (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was employed instead of type I, which does not provide EEG assessment. As a result, precise measurement of actual sleep duration was unattainable, probably leading to considerable undervaluation of respiratory disturbances. With no EEG, the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) was calculated based on the entire recording period—including wakefulness—rather than just sleep time. Consequently, this methodological limitation suggests that the documented AHI of 6.0 likely underrepresents the true extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Nevertheless, certain findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) a high proportion of central events (72%) even with a low total AHI; (2) observed oxygen saturation drops to 70%–80% occurring without reported dyspnea or patient awareness (both at the airport and in typical settings); and (3) anatomical findings of marked brainstem compression, specifically an 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography—including full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video surveillance—has been scheduled at an outside sleep facility. Completion of this evaluation is contingent upon aligning the patient's availability, gaining patient consent for the procedure, and arranging an appropriate appointment at the remote site. Upon completion, this assessment is expected to yield clearer information regarding sleep structure, breathing patterns during sleep, and the existence and degree of central hypoventilation.

It is recognized that, based on current findings, central hypoventilation is best considered a strongly supported clinical suspicion rather than an unequivocal diagnosis.

The patient’s most bothersome complaint was oropharyngeal dysphagia that impaired ingestion of both solid and liquid foods to an equal degree, and was accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. Validated tools for swallowing function used in a prospective cohort showed dysfunction in 36% of cases, with the majority demonstrating neurogenic types of impairment. The lack of distinction between food consistencies further supports neurogenic rather than mechanical causes of dysphagia. Despite multiple assessments from gastroenterology and otolaryngology, which revealed no abnormalities, the patient’s underlying condition remained unidentified for several years. This prolonged time to diagnosis underscores a key clinical consideration: when dysphagia or choking occurs and no structural cause is found on endoscopy, a neurological etiology should be evaluated. In a retrospective series, the incidence of CMI among children with prolonged dysphagia was found to be 3.3%, much higher than the general paediatric incidence of 0.1%–0.77%. These findings point to persistent dysphagia as a potentially important marker for CMI.

The patient’s description of experiencing pain localized to the vertex, which is alleviated by cutting their hair, illustrates an intriguing but sparsely researched observation. Occipital neuralgia has been documented in connection with CMI and may lead to heightened sensitivity of the scalp; its diagnostic guidelines include ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia evident with non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Nonetheless, reports addressing the correlation between occipital neuralgia and CMI are infrequent, and definitive evidence supporting a causal relationship is lacking.

A recent entity known as ‘burning vertex syndrome’ should also be considered; this syndrome is characterized by scalp discomfort, skin hypersensitivity, and clinical features that do not align precisely with traditional primary headache disorders such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology implicates small nerve fiber involvement originating within the scalp, and patient response to greater occipital nerve blockade has been variable. In our case, the sensations at the vertex could potentially be explained by: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) a coincidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a different primary headache condition altogether. There is a clear lack of systematic evaluation for patterns of cutaneous allodynia in individuals with CMI, as no tools existed for assessing these symptoms prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Due to this significant gap in the literature, we report this as a notable clinical observation rather than assign it diagnostic significance.

This case demonstrates the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when it manifests through uncommon or confusing clinical features like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, which resulted in a 14-year postponement of accurate identification. The progression toward a correct neurological diagnosis was marked by early uncertainty and gradual clarification, echoing patterns described in the literature.
