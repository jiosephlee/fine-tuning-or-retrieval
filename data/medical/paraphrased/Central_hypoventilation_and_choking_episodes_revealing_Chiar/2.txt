Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

A woman in her early 40s reported a 7-year progression of recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, along with nocturnal choking occurring 3–4 times each week, and persistent occipital headaches persisting over the past 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, self-monitoring at home unexpectedly identified significant drops in oxygen saturation (to 70%–80%), occurring without signs of respiratory distress. Extensive investigations, including repeated laryngoscopies and endoscopies, failed to reveal a cause for her choking episodes. Upon reevaluation of a cranial CT scan obtained 14 years prior for unrelated trauma, a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum was detected. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck established the diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I (CMI), showing an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography indicated mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0), mainly due to central events (72%). This report highlights that CMI may present with respiratory features closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), underlining the necessity of assessing for structural brain lesions in patients with unexplained respiratory issues.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by cerebellar tonsils extending at least 5 mm beneath the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI scans. Recent imaging research indicates a prevalence of CMI in 1% of adults, but only about 0.1% of individuals in the general population exhibit symptoms. The disorder displays a slight female preponderance with a ratio of 1.3:1 and most frequently manifests in the third decade of life.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally undersized posterior fossa, resulting in a disproportion between the volume of neural components and the space enclosing them. Nevertheless, recent findings have questioned the classic view of CMI as invariably pathological, instead suggesting a continuum that spans from typical anatomical variations to cases with clinical importance. Modern volumetric analyses reveal marked demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions, often lacking a clear association with clinical symptoms. This evolving perspective underscores the importance of tailored, symptom-oriented management, enabling clinicians to differentiate benign anatomical differences from pathological cases that warrant operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome constitutes the clinical presentation of CMI, marked by a specific pattern of neurological symptoms. The principal complaint is episodic occipital headache triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, occurring in more than 80% of patients. Symptom severity is generally greater in adults than in paediatric cases, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Lower cranial nerve dysfunction occurs in approximately 15%–26% of individuals, frequently manifesting as oropharyngeal deficits, including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory involvement is now acknowledged as a notable complication associated with CMI. A prospective investigation involving 90 adults diagnosed with CMI revealed that half of the participants experienced sleep-related respiratory disorders. Among these individuals, 6.7% exhibited a central apnoea index of ≥5, though isolated central sleep apnoea was identified in only a single patient, possibly indicating underlying brainstem compression. Importantly, the respiratory dysfunction linked to CMI is not limited to abnormalities during sleep; case reports have described instances of acute respiratory failure and severe, life-threatening hypoxaemia in patients affected by CMI.

Respiratory dysfunction in this context is primarily driven by compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) pathways, and disturbances within the respiratory regulatory centers, resulting in episodic respiratory disturbances. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—occasionally referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—which is etiologically and clinically distinct from the congenital central hypoventilation syndrome observed with PHOX2B gene mutations. Contemporary clinical recommendations underscore the significance of this association by advocating for systematic polysomnographic assessment in CMI patients who are suspected to have sleep-related breathing disorders. Importantly, posterior fossa decompression surgery may reestablish proper CSF dynamics and ameliorate respiratory dysfunction, highlighting the critical role of diagnosing CMI in individuals with otherwise unexplained respiratory compromise.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early forties was seen in our family medicine clinic with a longstanding, seven-year history of unpredictable choking spells that arose during both eating and sleeping, affecting solids and liquids to the same extent. She reported that these episodes were abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting meals or awakening her at night with severe coughing and gasping, though there was never a total blockage of her airway. The nocturnal events happened three to four times weekly, with some nights involving two separate occurrences. Throughout these years, she underwent several thorough assessments—including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies—none of which revealed any abnormalities. Nevertheless, her symptoms continued without relief. The impact on her personal life was profound; she refrained from dining out or attending social gatherings out of concern for experiencing an episode in public. This persistent anxiety and embarrassment progressively led her to withdraw socially and experience significant emotional hardship.

A comprehensive review of her clinical background uncovered that her symptoms had originated 14 years prior, subsequent to a minor car accident during which she briefly lost consciousness. In the aftermath, she experienced ongoing headaches located in the occipital region and at the vertex. She characterized these headaches as constant and pressure-like, frequently confined to a small, coin-sized spot on the vertex. The headaches did not worsen with actions such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva manoeuvres. Multiple healthcare providers diagnosed her with nummular headache, leading to a series of localized scalp injections that provided only temporary alleviation. Notably, she experienced pronounced allodynia at the vertex, to the extent that even the sensation of her hair resting on the area caused pain. Ultimately, she found that keeping her hair short led to a reduction in her baseline pain levels from 6/10 to 3/10. However, this approach conflicted with her cultural norms and resulted in significant emotional discomfort.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient initiated regular self-assessment of her oxygen saturation at home with a personal pulse oximeter. Across a span of several months, she consistently recorded episodes where her oxygen saturation fell to between 70% and 80%, despite the absence of symptoms. She frequently became aware of these events only upon consulting her device readings. Numerous visits to the emergency department ultimately attributed her manifestations to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her departure, she was suddenly overcome by intense dizziness and nausea. Medical staff present at the airport measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from boarding the plane. After she received supplemental oxygen, her symptoms resolved entirely within a few minutes. Later assessments, which included chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal results.

In light of her persistent occipital and vertex headaches, recurrent choking spells, and episodes of unexplained hypoxia, a search for a comprehensive neurological explanation was initiated. Examination of her prior medical history uncovered a head CT without contrast conducted 14 years prior, post-accident, which was originally interpreted as unremarkable. Nonetheless, a meticulous re-examination of that CT identified evidence of a crowded foramen magnum and diminished cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the presence of choking symptoms, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was first suspected. However, the diagnosis was ruled out due to unremarkable pH monitoring, normal endoscopic findings, and the absence of improvement with maximal acid suppression therapy.

Assessment for myasthenia gravis was prompted by the presence of bulbar symptoms. However, the diagnosis was deemed improbable given the negative antibody results, absence of fatigability, and no diurnal symptom fluctuation.

Following incidents of hypoxia, a comprehensive evaluation for primary pulmonary disease was undertaken. The absence of abnormalities on chest imaging and normal results from pulmonary function tests ruled out intrinsic pulmonary pathology.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by the presence of normal voluntary respiration alongside deficient automatic respiratory control when the patient was distracted or asleep. The onset after head trauma set this acquired condition apart from congenital variants.

The compression of the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei caused by brainstem pathology secondary to CMI ultimately accounted for all observed symptoms, thereby offering a comprehensive diagnostic explanation.

Discussion:

It is important to examine the timing between the patient's involvement in a motor vehicle accident and the emergence of symptoms. Chiari malformation type I may present congenitally or may develop following head trauma or other neurological insults. Recent aetiological frameworks2022 reflect this diversity, differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that develop as a consequence of trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, a CT scan obtained directly following the accident revealed a narrowed foramen magnum. As there is no imaging available from before the trauma, it is unclear whether the trauma was the causative factor or simply brought attention to a pre-existing condition. The patient's immediate experience of occipital headaches after the accident, together with the gradual onset of bulbar and respiratory symptoms across a 14-year period, may be indicative of either: (1) tonsillar herniation triggered by the trauma, or (2) trauma that aggravated or exposed an underlying, asymptomatic congenital abnormality.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory presentations seen in CMI and contributes to a deeper comprehension of the acquired central hypoventilation profiles resulting from structural abnormalities of the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is essential for interpreting the respiratory pathophysiology associated with CMI. Cortical mechanisms, which govern voluntary respiration, are usually preserved in individuals with CMI. Conversely, the automatic control of breathing—managed by brainstem centers and especially vital during sleep—is impaired due to the compression of medullary structures by tonsillar herniation. Our patient exhibited maintenance of voluntary ventilatory capacity while demonstrating an absence of intrinsic automatic respiratory drive, a clinical picture analogous to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit arising from a separate underlying process. The episode at the airport, in which brief inattentiveness led to severe oxygen desaturation without any sensation of breathlessness, provides a clear demonstration of the isolated deficit in automatic respiratory regulation: the patient, in effect, simply ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of dyspnoea amid severe episodes of oxygen desaturation stems from an underlying dysfunction of the brainstem. Such a brainstem abnormality would plausibly interfere with vital afferent pathways, impeding the transmission of sensory information required for conscious perception to the cortex. The subjective sensation of breathlessness represents an aspect of interoception, contingent upon the processing of ascending neural signals by specific regions such as the anterior insular cortex. When these afferent inputs are disrupted within the brainstem, the cortical regions responsible for generating the perception of air hunger are deprived of essential information, thereby accounting for the patient’s perilous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The notable mismatch between the patient’s pronounced clinical symptoms and the comparatively low polysomnographic score (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was employed instead of the more comprehensive type I, resulting in the absence of EEG measurements. As a consequence, precise assessment of total sleep duration was not possible, probably causing substantial undercounting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG, the AHI is based on the entire recording period—which includes both sleep and wakefulness—rather than exclusively on actual sleep duration. This methodological shortcoming suggests that the reported AHI of 6.0 does not accurately reflect the true extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Nonetheless, there are multiple findings that indicate central hypoventilation: (1) central events account for 72% of the occurrences even though the total AHI is relatively low; (2) observed episodes of oxygen desaturation reaching 70%–80% occurred without accompanying dyspnoea or any reported awareness by the patient (such as during airport transit and standard evaluations); and (3) anatomical imaging reveals marked compression of the brainstem, evidenced by an 11 mm tonsillar descent.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography, incorporating full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation, has been scheduled at a separate sleep facility. The execution of this assessment is contingent upon aligning patient availability, obtaining patient consent for the procedure, and securing a slot at the offsite location. Upon completion, the study will yield a more precise delineation of sleep structure, breathing patterns, and the degree and occurrence of central hypoventilation.

It should be noted that, based on the information currently at hand, central hypoventilation remains a strongly supported clinical consideration, though it has not yet been conclusively established as a diagnosis.

Our patient’s principal complaint was oropharyngeal dysphagia, characterized by equal impairment when consuming both solids and liquids, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. This symptom was evaluated prospectively with standardized swallowing tests, which revealed dysfunction in 36% of individuals, predominantly reflecting neurogenic etiologies. The similar degree of impairment across various food consistencies serves to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical forms. Despite multiple assessments by gastroenterology and otolaryngology specialists—each yielding normal results—the patient’s most troubling symptom was initially, and repeatedly, explored from a non-neurological standpoint, resulting in a significant diagnostic delay over several years. This case underscores a crucial clinical point: when dysphagia or episodes of choking present without an identifiable structural abnormality on endoscopy, clinicians should consider a neurological basis for the symptoms. Notably, in a retrospective analysis of children with prolonged dysphagia, the prevalence of CMI was 3.3%, which stands in stark contrast to the reported general paediatric prevalence of 0.1%–0.77%. These findings indicate that persistent dysphagia may serve as a key clinical clue to the presence of underlying CMI.

The patient’s description of experiencing pain localized to the vertex, alleviated by hair cutting, highlights a curious and relatively unexplored clinical observation. In cases of CMI, occipital neuralgia may present, often manifesting as increased scalp sensitivity. According to diagnostic guidelines, symptoms such as dysesthesia or allodynia must be triggered by non-painful stimulation of the scalp or hair. Despite this, reports directly connecting occipital neuralgia with CMI remain infrequent, and definitive evidence supporting a causal relationship is lacking.

Another possible explanation is the more recently characterized ‘burning vertex syndrome’, distinguished by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and a constellation of symptoms that do not align precisely with traditional primary headache disorders such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The underlying pathophysiology is thought to involve small fiber pathways emanating from the scalp, and therapeutic response to greater occipital nerve blockade has been inconsistent among affected individuals. In the present case, the vertex complaints might be attributed to: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) the coincidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) an unrelated primary headache condition. Notably, there is a deficit in rigorous documentation concerning the distribution of cutaneous allodynia in CMI cohorts, underscored by the lack of any validated tool for symptom assessment in CMI patients prior to the creation of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In view of these evidentiary limitations, we report this observation as a noteworthy clinical phenomenon rather than as an established diagnostic marker.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties encountered in diagnosing CMI when it manifests with unusual or deceptive symptoms, such as central hypoventilation and choking, which in our instance resulted in a 14-year delay before reaching an accurate diagnosis. The progression towards identifying the correct neurological condition was marked by early misinterpretation and a prolonged course, a pattern commonly reflected in published reports.
