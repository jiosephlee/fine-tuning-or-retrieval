Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year history of repeated choking incidents involving both solid and liquid foods, as well as nocturnal choking episodes occurring three to four times per week, in addition to persistent occipital headaches lasting 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home pulse oximetry unexpectedly detected episodes of significant oxygen desaturation (70%–80%), despite the absence of respiratory distress. Diagnostic investigations, including multiple laryngoscopies and endoscopies to evaluate her choking, yielded normal results. A retrospective assessment of a head CT scan conducted 14 years prior for unrelated head trauma identified a crowded foramen magnum, which had previously been missed. Subsequent MRI scans of the head and neck confirmed Chiari malformation type I (CMI), showing an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography indicated mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0 and predominantly central events (72%). This case demonstrates that CMI may manifest with respiratory symptoms resembling those of acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to consider structural anomalies of the brain in the evaluation of patients presenting with unexplained respiratory issues.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by downward movement of the cerebellar tonsils measuring 5 mm or more beneath the foramen magnum, as observed on sagittal MRI scans. Current imaging data indicate that CMI is present in about 1% of adults, but only 0.1% of the overall population exhibit clinical symptoms. There is a slight female bias in prevalence, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, and most cases arise during the third decade of life.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa size, resulting in disproportion between the neural elements and the confined space. Nonetheless, current research questions the conventional notion that CMI is invariably abnormal, suggesting it spans from benign anatomical variations to clinically relevant anomalies. Newer volumetric analyses reveal considerable differences in posterior fossa dimensions across populations, often lacking reliable association with clinical manifestations. This evolving perspective requires a personalized approach, basing management on symptom presentation and differentiating incidental structural findings from those that necessitate operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome reflects the clinical expression of CMI, marked by a specific set of neurological signs and symptoms. The primary feature is an episodic headache located in the occipital region, which is often triggered by Valsalva maneuvers, occurring in more than 80% of affected individuals. Symptom severity tends to be greater in adults compared to pediatric patients, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is observed in 15%–26% of cases, most often manifesting as oropharyngeal impairments including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now acknowledged as a growing complication of CMI. In a prospective evaluation involving 90 adults diagnosed with CMI, half exhibited sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. A central apnoea index of 5 or higher was reported in 6.7% of participants, while isolated central sleep apnoea was identified in just one individual, which may indicate brainstem compression. The range of respiratory involvement is not limited to sleep disorders; a case series has reported incidents of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia among patients with CMI.

Respiratory impairment arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) drainage, and disturbance of central respiratory regulation, which results in episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms constitute an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—sometimes referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—and differ from the congenital form linked to PHOX2B mutations. Contemporary guidelines highlight this association by advising routine polysomnography for individuals with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who are suspected of having sleep-related breathing disorders. Notably, decompression of the posterior fossa can reestablish normal CSF circulation and may ameliorate respiratory symptoms, accentuating the necessity for considering CMI in cases of respiratory dysfunction without apparent cause.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early 40s was seen at our family medicine practice reporting a 7-year history of episodic choking, which appeared randomly during eating or sleep and involved both solid foods and liquids. She described these incidents as abrupt and alarming, frequently causing her to stop meals or wake up suddenly at night coughing violently and struggling for breath, but never resulting in total airway blockage. The nighttime episodes occurred three or four times each week, with some nights featuring two attacks. Throughout this period, she had extensive workup including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all yielding normal findings. Her symptoms continued despite the absence of any abnormal results. The episodes had considerable effects on her social life; she refrained from dining out or attending social events for fear of choking in public. The persistent anxiety and sense of shame led her to progressively withdraw socially and experience heightened emotional distress.

A thorough review of her medical history indicated that her symptoms initially appeared 14 years prior, after a minor car accident that resulted in a brief period of unconsciousness. Soon afterwards, she began to experience ongoing headaches in the occipital region and at the vertex. She described these headaches as unremitting, with a sensation of pressure, frequently confined to a small, coin-sized spot at the vertex. The headaches were unaffected by actions such as coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Multiple clinicians diagnosed her condition as nummular headache, and she received repeated scalp injections in the area, which provided only temporary alleviation. The most notable aspect of her presentation was significant allodynia at the vertex, such that even the presence of her hair caused discomfort. Eventually, she found that keeping her hair short lowered her baseline pain from 6/10 to 3/10, but this choice clashed with cultural norms and resulted in additional psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started tracking her oxygen levels at home with a pulse oximeter. Over a period of months, she frequently recorded asymptomatic drops in oxygen saturation to between 70% and 80%. These events were typically unnoticed until she consulted her device. On several occasions, emergency assessments connected her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at a foreign airport. As she awaited her plane, she developed sudden onset dizziness and nausea. Medical staff on location recorded an oxygen saturation of 78%, resulting in her being refused permission to board. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms resolved entirely within a few minutes. Later, comprehensive pulmonary assessment—including chest imaging and pulmonary function tests—showed no abnormalities.

Considering the persistent occipital and vertex headaches, recurrent episodes of choking, and unexplained hypoxia, clinicians sought a comprehensive neurological explanation. Review of previous medical documentation identified a non-contrast head CT scan performed 14 years prior after her accident; this scan had originally been interpreted as normal. Nonetheless, upon meticulous reassessment, the imaging demonstrated a constricted foramen magnum and absence of cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Gastroesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was initially suspected due to the presence of choking episodes. However, the diagnosis was ruled out based on normal pH monitoring, unremarkable endoscopic findings, and absence of clinical improvement despite maximal acid suppression therapy.

Assessment for myasthenia gravis was prompted by bulbar symptomatology. However, this diagnosis was considered improbable because antibody assays were negative, there was no evidence of muscle fatigability, and symptoms did not fluctuate over the course of the day.

A thorough evaluation of possible primary pulmonary disorders was conducted after episodes of hypoxia. Intrinsic lung disease was ruled out as chest imaging and pulmonary function testing returned normal results.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly suspected due to intact voluntary respiration, whereas automatic respiratory drive was deficient when the patient was distracted or asleep. The development of symptoms after head trauma differentiated this from congenital variants.

Compression of the respiratory centers and lower cranial nerve nuclei by brainstem pathology resulting from CMI accounted for all clinical manifestations, thus offering a comprehensive explanation for the patient’s presentation.

Discussion:

The timing of symptom emergence following our patient's motor vehicle accident merits thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is known to occur as a congenital anomaly, but it may also develop secondary to head trauma or other neurological insults. Current etiological frameworks2022 acknowledge this continuum, separating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that arise due to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained directly after the trauma showed a crowded foramen magnum. Since pre-injury imaging is unavailable, it is unclear if the trauma was responsible for this observation or simply brought it to attention. The patient experienced the sudden onset of occipital headaches right after the incident, which later evolved over 14 years into gradually worsening bulbar and respiratory complaints. This progression could be due either to (1) traumatic tonsillar herniation or (2) trauma that precipitated symptoms or unveiled an asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory features associated with CMI and broadens our perspective on the patterns of acquired central hypoventilation that emerge from structural lesions within the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic respiration is fundamental in clarifying the respiratory pathophysiology observed in CMI. Voluntary respiration, under cortical control, generally persists unaffected in individuals with CMI. Conversely, automatic breathing—which depends on brainstem centers and is particularly vital during sleep—can be impaired owing to compression of the medulla by herniated tonsils. In this patient, voluntary ventilation was maintained, but there was a loss of automatic respiratory drive, producing a clinical presentation similar to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though by a distinct underlying mechanism. The incident at the airport, where brief distraction led to significant desaturation in the absence of dyspnea, strikingly demonstrates this isolated disruption in automatic respiratory control, as the patient essentially ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that an underlying dysfunction within the brainstem accounts for the patient's striking lack of dyspnoea despite severe episodes of oxygen desaturation. This suspected brainstem lesion likely interferes with essential afferent neural pathways responsible for transmitting sensory information to the cortex, which is required for conscious perception. Breathlessness is an aspect of interoception and relies on the integration of these afferent impulses by pivotal brain regions such as the anterior insular cortex. If these signals are disrupted during their passage through the brainstem, the cortex would not be provided with the inputs necessary to trigger the sensation of air hunger, thereby explaining the patient's hazardous unawareness of their physiological compromise.

The substantial gap observed between this patient’s pronounced clinical manifestations and the comparatively modest polysomnographic result (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was employed instead of type I, resulting in the lack of EEG monitoring. The omission of EEG precluded precise determination of total sleep duration and likely contributed to considerable underreporting of respiratory disturbances. Since EEG information was not available, the AHI was determined based on total recording time, which encompasses wakefulness as well as sleep, rather than restricting calculation to periods of actual sleep. Consequently, this methodological limitation suggests the documented AHI of 6.0 is an underestimate of the true extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

In spite of this limitation, multiple findings are consistent with central hypoventilation: (1) central events accounted for 72% of respiratory events, even though the total AHI was low; (2) oxygen saturations were observed to decrease to 70%–80% in the absence of dyspnoea or the patient's recognition of these episodes (both in the airport and during standard assessments); and (3) imaging showed substantial brainstem compression, with 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A Type I polysomnography incorporating comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation has been scheduled at an external sleep facility. Nevertheless, finalization of this assessment is awaiting alignment of patient scheduling, consent to the procedure, and appointment availability at the off-site center. Upon completion, the study is expected to yield clearer assessment of sleep architecture, respiratory behaviors, and both the existence and intensity of central hypoventilation.

We recognize that, based on available data, central hypoventilation continues to represent a robust clinical presumption rather than an unequivocally established diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, presenting with equal difficulty swallowing both solids and liquids and accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, represented the patient’s most bothersome complaint. A prospective study utilizing validated tools to assess swallowing function revealed dysfunction in 36% of cases, with neurogenic patterns being the most common. The uniform impairment across all food consistencies serves to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical types. The patient’s primary symptom underwent repeated evaluation by gastroenterology and otolaryngology, each yielding normal results; as a consequence, her diagnosis was postponed for several years. This diagnostic delay underscores an important clinical insight: when dysphagia or choking is observed in the absence of structural abnormalities on endoscopic examination, a neurological etiology should be considered. According to a retrospective review, the frequency of CMI among children with persistent dysphagia was found to be 3.3%, notably higher than the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence seen in the broader pediatric population. This finding implies that ongoing dysphagia may be a significant marker for underlying CMI.

The patient described experiencing pain localized to the vertex region, which improved following hair cutting, highlighting an intriguing yet insufficiently explored occurrence. Occipital neuralgia may present alongside CMI and is known to produce heightened sensitivity of the scalp; diagnostic guidelines stipulate the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Despite this, reports connecting occipital neuralgia with CMI are infrequent, and definitive evidence confirming a causal relationship is lacking.

Another possible explanation is the recently reported ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical features not well-aligned with recognized primary headache disorders such as migraine, tension-type, cluster, or nummular headaches. The proposed underlying mechanism likely involves small fiber sensory pathways from the scalp, and therapeutic response to greater occipital nerve blockade has been inconsistent among affected individuals. In the present case, symptoms at the vertex might indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) an independent occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache entity. Comprehensive recording of cutaneous allodynia characteristics within CMI cohorts is notably absent, as no assessment tool targeting these symptoms in CMI existed prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Given this substantial gap in the literature, we describe our observation as a noteworthy clinical finding rather than as conclusive evidence for diagnosis.

This case underscores the considerable difficulties in diagnosing CMI, especially when it manifests through unusual or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, resulting in a delayed diagnosis of 14 years. The process involved early misinterpretation and slowly advancing towards the appropriate neurological diagnosis, a pattern commonly reported in the literature.
