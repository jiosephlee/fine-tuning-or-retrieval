Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early forties reported a 7-year pattern of recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, as well as nocturnal choking events occurring 3 to 4 times per week, in addition to a 14-year history of persistent occipital headaches. During the COVID-19 pandemic, at-home monitoring unexpectedly detected marked oxygen desaturation episodes (70%–80%), despite the absence of respiratory distress. Multiple laryngoscopic and endoscopic evaluations undertaken to explore the cause of her choking did not identify any abnormalities. Reassessment of a head CT scan obtained 14 years earlier, following an unrelated traumatic incident, revealed a crowded foramen magnum that had previously been missed. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck demonstrated Chiari malformation type I (CMI), showing 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography indicated mild sleep apnoea, with an apnoea–hypopnoea index of 6.0, characterized predominantly by central events (72%). This case underscores that CMI can manifest with respiratory complaints closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to evaluate for structural brain lesions in patients with unexplained breathing disturbances.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by cerebellar tonsils descending at least 5 mm below the foramen magnum, as visualized on sagittal MRI scans. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI is present in around 1% of adults, whereas only about 0.1% of individuals in the general population exhibit clinical symptoms. There is a slight predominance in females, with a ratio of 1.3:1, and the onset most frequently occurs in individuals in their third decade.

The underlying pathophysiology involves a congenitally reduced posterior fossa volume, resulting in disproportion between neural components and the spatial capacity. Yet, recent research has questioned the longstanding notion of CMI as inherently pathological, suggesting it exists along a continuum ranging from benign anatomical variations to disorders that manifest clinical symptoms. Volumetric analyses in contemporary literature reveal marked demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions, but these do not reliably predict symptomatology. As a result, there is an increasing emphasis on personalized, symptom-driven management, with careful differentiation between non-symptomatic anatomical deviations and those pathological cases necessitating surgical treatment.

Chiari syndrome constitutes the clinical presentation of CMI and is defined by a characteristic set of neurological symptoms. The principal feature is episodic occipital headache triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, occurring in more than 80% of affected individuals. Adults often experience more pronounced symptoms than paediatric patients, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Lower cranial nerve involvement has been documented in 15%–26% of cases, most frequently manifesting as oropharyngeal dysfunction including palatal weakness, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory complications are being identified with greater frequency in individuals with CMI. In a forward-looking investigation involving 90 adult patients diagnosed with CMI, half were found to experience sleep-disordered breathing. Specifically, 6.7% of these participants exhibited a central apnoea index of at least 5, though only a single patient had pure central sleep apnoea, which may suggest the involvement of brainstem compression. These respiratory issues are not confined solely to disruptions during sleep; a series of case reports has described instances of acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia in patients suffering from CMI.

Respiratory dysfunction in this context arises due to factors such as compression of the brainstem, impediment of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation, and interference with the neural centers responsible for regulating respiration, collectively resulting in episodic breathing disturbances. These respiratory manifestations are considered to be an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—often referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—and are pathophysiologically distinct from the congenital form of central hypoventilation syndrome that is linked to mutations in PHOX2B. Contemporary clinical guidelines highlight the significance of this association by advocating for the routine use of polysomnography in individuals with CMI who exhibit suspected sleep-related breathing abnormalities. Notably, surgical intervention via posterior fossa decompression has the capacity to re-establish physiological CSF dynamics and may lead to resolution of the associated respiratory symptoms, thereby emphasising the need for heightened awareness of CMI in cases of otherwise unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early forties sought care at our family medicine clinic, reporting a 7-year history of unpredictable episodes of choking during both meals and sleep, with no clear pattern regarding the type of food or drink involved. She characterized these episodes as abrupt and alarming, frequently interrupting meals or waking her from sleep with bouts of forceful coughing and gasping; however, her airway was never fully blocked. She experienced these nocturnal events three to four times weekly, and occasionally twice in one night. Throughout the years, she underwent multiple assessments—including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies—all of which yielded normal findings. Nonetheless, her symptoms remained unchanged. The consequences on her daily life were profound; apprehension about choking in public led her to avoid restaurants and social occasions, ultimately resulting in heightened social isolation and emotional suffering.

Upon obtaining a more comprehensive clinical history, it became evident that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, immediately after experiencing a minor motor vehicle accident associated with a brief episode of unconsciousness. Soon thereafter, she began suffering from ongoing headaches located in the occipital and vertex regions. She characterized these headaches as unremitting, with a pressure-like quality, and predominantly confined to a coin-sized region at the vertex. Notably, the headaches did not intensify with activities such as coughing, straining, or during other Valsalva manoeuvres. Multiple clinicians rendered a diagnosis of nummular headache, for which she received repeated local injections into the scalp; however, these interventions only afforded short-lived alleviation. A particularly striking aspect of her presentation was pronounced allodynia at the vertex, such that even minimal stimulation, like the mere presence of her hair, provoked pain. Eventually, she discovered that cutting her hair short lessened her baseline pain score from 6/10 to 3/10, though this solution conflicted with cultural norms and was a source of psychological distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient initiated home-based monitoring of her oxygen saturation levels with a personal pulse oximeter. Over a period of months, she consistently recorded episodes in which her oxygen saturation declined asymptomatically to between 70% and 80%. Such decreases were typically identified only when she measured her levels with the device. Several emergency assessments linked her symptoms to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she was suddenly overcome by severe dizziness and nausea. Medical staff present at the scene recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from boarding the plane. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms subsided entirely in a matter of minutes. Follow-up assessment of her lungs, encompassing both chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal results.

In light of the patient’s longstanding occipital and vertex headaches, coupled with recurrent episodes of choking and unexplained hypoxemia, clinicians sought an integrated neurological explanation. Examination of her prior medical documentation uncovered a non-contrast head CT, completed 14 years prior after her injury, which had originally been interpreted as within normal limits. Upon closer re-examination, however, the scan demonstrated features such as a narrowed foramen magnum and diminished cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the presence of choking symptoms, an initial diagnosis of gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with laryngopharyngeal reflux was proposed. However, this was ruled out due to normal findings in pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations, as well as the absence of symptom improvement following comprehensive acid suppression therapy.

Assessment for myasthenia gravis was prompted by the presence of bulbar symptoms. However, the diagnosis was considered improbable because antibody tests were negative, there was no evidence of fatigability, and diurnal fluctuation was not observed.

Given the occurrence of hypoxic events, a thorough workup for primary pulmonary disorders was conducted. Intrinsic lung pathology was ruled out based on unremarkable chest imaging and normal results from pulmonary function assessments.

The diagnosis of central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was highly supported by the observation that voluntary respiration remained intact, whereas involuntary respiratory drive was compromised during periods of distraction or sleep. Its onset after head trauma set it apart from congenital variants.

All symptoms were ultimately accounted for by brainstem pathology resulting from CMI, as it caused compression of the respiratory centers and the nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby offering a comprehensive explanation for the clinical presentation.

Discussion:

It is important to closely examine the timing of symptom emergence following the patient’s motor vehicle accident. Chiari malformation type I is recognized not only as a congenital disorder but also as a condition that can develop after traumatic brain injury or other neurologic insults. Modern classifications of aetiology (2022) reflect this range by differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary or acquired variants that occur in response to trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, the CT scan conducted directly following the incident showed a crowded foramen magnum. As there are no pre-injury images available, it remains unclear if this was a pre-existing condition unveiled by the trauma or a direct result of the event. The patient experienced occipital headaches immediately after the accident, which gradually evolved into bulbar and respiratory symptoms over a 14-year period. This clinical progression may be attributed to either (1) trauma-induced tonsillar herniation or (2) trauma acting as a trigger for the manifestation or worsening of an asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the multifaceted respiratory presentations associated with CMI and broadens our comprehension of central hypoventilation patterns resulting from structural abnormalities within the brainstem. Differentiating between voluntary and automatic breathing is essential to clarify the respiratory pathophysiology of CMI. While voluntary respiration—governed by cortical circuits—remains largely unaffected in individuals with CMI, automatic respiration—which is reliant on brainstem centers and critical during periods of sleep—can be impaired by compression of medullary structures by the cerebellar tonsils. In the presented patient, the capacity for voluntary breathing was retained, whereas the automatic drive to breathe was abolished, a presentation akin to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though stemming from a distinct etiological process. The incident at the airport, wherein brief inattention resulted in significant oxygen desaturation absent of dyspnoea, provides a striking example of the isolated deficit in automatic breathing: the patient effectively 'forgets to breathe'.

We propose that the striking lack of dyspnoea observed in the patient during marked hypoxaemic episodes stems from an underlying dysfunction in the brainstem. This suspected brainstem abnormality likely impairs the essential ascending afferent neural pathways required for transmitting sensory information to the cortex, which is necessary for conscious perception. Breathlessness represents a manifestation of interoception, relying on the integration of these afferent inputs by critical regions such as the anterior insular cortex. Interruption of the signals at the level of the brainstem would prevent their arrival at the cortical areas responsible for generating the subjective sensation of breathlessness, thereby accounting for the patient’s unawareness of their compromised physiological state.

The notable gap observed between the patient’s pronounced clinical symptoms and the relatively low polysomnographic result (AHI 6.0) merits further consideration. Owing to logistical constraints, a type III polysomnography was employed instead of the gold-standard type I; importantly, type III recordings do not include EEG monitoring. As a result, precise determination of total sleep time was not possible, which likely resulted in an appreciable underrepresentation of the actual frequency of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG-derived sleep staging, AHI is determined based on total recording time—including periods when the patient was awake—rather than being restricted to genuine sleep periods. Consequently, this methodological shortcoming likely caused the measured AHI of 6.0 to underestimate the real extent of sleep-disordered breathing in this case.

Nonetheless, multiple factors point toward central hypoventilation: (1) a high proportion of central events (72%) even in the context of a relatively low overall AHI; (2) observed oxygen desaturation episodes dropping to 70%–80%, occurring without accompanying dyspnoea or the patient’s awareness—both in an airport setting and during regular evaluations; and (3) anatomical findings demonstrating notable brainstem compression, with tonsillar descent measuring 11 mm.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography—including full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video surveillance—has been scheduled at an external sleep center. The execution of this assessment is awaiting coordination, specifically in aligning patient availability, obtaining patient consent for the procedure, and securing an appropriate time slot at the external facility. Upon completion, this study will enable a more precise delineation of sleep architecture, respiratory dynamics, and the extent as well as the presence of central hypoventilation.

It is important to note that, based on the information currently available, central hypoventilation is best regarded as a strongly supported clinical suspicion rather than a diagnosis that has been conclusively established.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, characterized by comparable impairment when swallowing both solids and liquids and frequently accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing, emerged as the patient’s most troublesome symptom. Systematic, prospective swallowing evaluations utilizing validated tools identified swallowing dysfunction in 36% of individuals, with neurogenic mechanisms being the most common. The simultaneous involvement of multiple food consistencies serves to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical forms. Despite the patient's significant symptom burden, multiple initial assessments by gastroenterology and otolaryngology specialists yielded normal results, leading to a protracted diagnostic process lasting several years. This case underscores a critical clinical insight: in situations where endoscopic examination reveals no structural abnormalities, clinicians should maintain suspicion for a neurological etiology in cases of dysphagia or choking. Notably, in a retrospective analysis, 3.3% of children exhibiting persistent dysphagia were found to have CMI, a rate notably higher than the general pediatric prevalence of 0.1%–0.77%. These findings indicate that ongoing dysphagia may serve as an important clinical marker for underlying CMI.

The patient’s description of vertex pain alleviated by hair cutting illustrates a notable, yet insufficiently explored, clinical observation. In the context of CMI, occipital neuralgia may manifest, producing heightened scalp sensitivity; according to diagnostic guidelines, this requires the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia elicited by non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Nonetheless, documented cases connecting occipital neuralgia to CMI are uncommon, and there is a lack of definitive evidence supporting a causal relationship.

Another possibility to consider is the recently identified ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical manifestations that do not align precisely with recognized categories of primary headache such as migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headaches. The underlying pathophysiology is believed to involve small fiber neural pathways from the scalp, and therapeutic responses to greater occipital nerve blocks have been inconsistent among reported cases. For our patient, the vertex complaints could plausibly be attributed to: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) coincidental manifestation of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct, unrelated primary headache syndrome. There is a notable lack of systematic data regarding the pattern of cutaneous allodynia among individuals with CMI, underscored by the historical absence of assessment tools targeting these symptoms prior to the advent of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In light of this limited evidence base, we share this finding primarily as an intriguing clinical observation rather than ascribing it definitive diagnostic weight.

This case exemplifies the considerable difficulties encountered in diagnosing CMI, particularly when its manifestation includes uncommon or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and episodes of choking, which, in this instance, resulted in a protracted diagnostic delay of 14 years. The sequence of misdiagnoses followed by eventual identification of the correct neurological disorder mirrors patterns repeatedly documented in the literature.
