Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her early 40s reported a 7-year duration of repeated choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, as well as nighttime choking events occurring 3–4 times per week. Additionally, she had experienced persistent occipital headaches for the past 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home monitoring unexpectedly identified marked drops in oxygen saturation (70%–80%), although she showed no signs of respiratory distress. Several laryngoscopies and endoscopies aimed at uncovering the cause of her choking events yielded unremarkable results. Reevaluation of a cranial CT obtained 14 years prior for an unrelated head injury disclosed a previously missed crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI imaging of the head and neck verified the diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. A polysomnography revealed mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0), predominantly central in nature (72%). This case demonstrates that CMI may present with respiratory symptoms that can be easily confused with acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to investigate potential structural abnormalities of the brain in patients with otherwise unexplained respiratory problems.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending 5 mm or more beneath the foramen magnum as visualized on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging data indicate a prevalence of CMI in about 1% of adults, but only around 0.1% of the overall population develop symptoms. The disorder shows a slight female bias, with a female-to-male ratio of 1.3:1, and most frequently manifests clinically in individuals in their twenties.

The underlying pathophysiology involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, which results in a disproportion between neural elements and the confined cranial space. Nonetheless, more recent research calls into question the conventional view of CMI as invariably pathological, instead positing a continuum ranging from normal anatomical differences to true clinical malformations. Advanced volumetric analyses reveal notable variation in posterior fossa size across different demographic groups, with no robust association to symptomatic presentation. This evolving perspective underscores the need for personalized, symptom-oriented management, emphasizing the importance of differentiating benign anatomical variations from disorders that necessitate operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome constitutes the clinical presentation of CMI, marked by a specific set of neurological symptoms. The primary feature is an episodic headache located in the occipital region, usually triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, occurring in more than 80% of affected individuals. Symptom severity tends to be greater in adults than in paediatric patients, particularly in the presence of syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in approximately 15%–26% of cases, often manifesting as oropharyngeal disturbances such as palatal paresis, atrophy of the tongue, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory involvement is now acknowledged as a notable complication associated with CMI. In a prospective cohort of 90 adult CMI patients, half demonstrated sleep-disordered breathing. A central apnoea index of 5 or greater was detected in 6.7% of individuals, with exclusively central sleep apnoea identified in a single patient—possibly indicating compression at the level of the brainstem. The scope of these respiratory issues is not limited to nocturnal symptoms; one case series reported instances of acute respiratory failure and severe hypoxaemia among patients with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in these cases arises from factors such as brainstem compression, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) egress, and impairment of the central respiratory regulatory regions, all of which contribute to episodic breathing disturbances. This constellation of respiratory abnormalities is considered an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—commonly referred to as ‘Ondine’s curse’—and should be distinguished from the congenital form related to PHOX2B gene mutations. In light of this association, updated clinical guidelines advocate for the routine use of polysomnography in individuals with CMI who are suspected of having sleep-related breathing disorders. Notably, surgical intervention via posterior fossa decompression has the capacity to normalize CSF flow and alleviate respiratory symptoms, highlighting the clinical significance of identifying CMI as a potential cause in patients with otherwise unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s attended our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year history marked by unpredictable episodes of choking that occurred during both meals and sleep. These incidents affected her intake of solids and liquids without preference. She characterized the attacks as abrupt and alarming, frequently disrupting her eating or abruptly waking her at night with intense coughing and difficulty breathing, but never causing a full airway blockage. Nocturnal occurrences took place three or four times each week, and on occasion as often as twice in one night. During the course of her illness, she had multiple comprehensive assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all yielding normal results. Nevertheless, her symptoms remained unresolved. The impact on her daily life was considerable; she refrained from dining out or participating in social functions due to anxiety over experiencing an episode publicly. The resulting persistent fear and humiliation contributed to progressive social isolation and considerable psychological distress.

A more comprehensive clinical evaluation indicated that her symptoms initially appeared 14 years prior, following a minor car accident that resulted in a brief loss of consciousness. Soon after the incident, she began to experience unremitting headaches located at the occipital region and vertex. She described these headaches as unvarying, with a sensation of pressure, frequently confined to a small, coin-sized area at the vertex. The headaches were unaffected by actions such as coughing, straining, or performing Valsalva manoeuvres. Multiple healthcare providers identified her condition as nummular headache, leading to numerous local scalp injections; however, these interventions only produced short-lived improvement. Notably, she exhibited pronounced allodynia at the vertex, where even the sensation of her hair’s weight could provoke pain. She eventually learned that maintaining a short hairstyle diminished her usual pain intensity from 6/10 to 3/10, though this solution conflicted with cultural norms and resulted in significant emotional difficulty.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient initiated self-assessment of her oxygen saturation levels with a personal pulse oximeter at home. Throughout several months, she consistently recorded episodes of decreased oxygen saturation, falling within the 70%–80% range, despite lacking symptoms. These occurrences typically went unnoticed by the patient until she utilized her oximeter. Several visits to emergency services resulted in the conclusion that her symptoms were related to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly developed severe dizziness and nausea. Medical staff at the scene measured her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from boarding. After receiving supplemental oxygen, her symptoms abated entirely within minutes. Further assessment of her lungs, which included chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, revealed no abnormalities.

In light of her persistent occipital and vertex headaches, recurrent episodes of choking, and episodes of hypoxia with no clear etiology, clinicians sought a single neurological explanation for her symptoms. Review of her prior medical documentation identified a head CT without contrast performed 14 years prior, after her injury, which had initially been interpreted as unremarkable. Upon close re-examination, this scan demonstrated foramen magnum crowding and an absence of cerebrospinal fluid at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the presence of choking symptoms, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease with associated laryngopharyngeal reflux was initially suspected. However, this diagnosis was ruled out based on normal findings from pH monitoring and endoscopic evaluations, as well as no clinical improvement with maximal acid suppression therapy.

Assessment for myasthenia gravis was prompted by bulbar symptomatology. However, this diagnosis was considered improbable given negative antibody results, no evidence of fatigable weakness, and the absence of symptom fluctuation throughout the day.

A thorough evaluation for underlying pulmonary disease was undertaken after incidents of hypoxia. Unremarkable chest imaging alongside normal pulmonary function testing ruled out intrinsic pulmonary pathology.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by the maintenance of voluntary respiratory effort while automatic respiratory regulation was compromised during periods of sleep or distraction. The occurrence of this syndrome subsequent to head trauma differentiated it from its congenital variants.

The collective symptoms were ultimately attributed to brainstem pathology resulting from CMI, which led to compression of both the lower cranial nerve nuclei and the respiratory centres, thereby offering a comprehensive diagnostic explanation.

Discussion:

It is important to closely examine the timing of our patient’s symptom development in relation to their motor vehicle accident. Chiari malformation type I is known to present congenitally, but cases have also been observed following head trauma or other neurological injuries. Recent classifications of etiology as of 2022 reflect this range, differentiating primary (congenital) Chiari malformations from secondary, or acquired, variants that arise due to factors such as trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, the CT scan obtained directly following the accident revealed a crowded foramen magnum. However, lacking imaging from prior to the trauma, it is impossible to ascertain whether this anatomical feature was precipitated by the trauma or simply exposed by it. The abrupt emergence of occipital headaches right after the incident, and the subsequent, gradual appearance of bulbar and respiratory manifestations across a 14-year span, may indicate either: (1) tonsillar herniation directly resulting from the trauma, or (2) trauma aggravating or bringing to light an underlying, asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory complications associated with CMI, deepening insight into the presentation of acquired central hypoventilation arising from structural abnormalities in the brainstem. A critical aspect in elucidating the respiratory pathophysiology of CMI is differentiating between voluntary and automatic breathing. While voluntary respiration—underpinned by cortical circuits—often remains functional in those with CMI, the capacity for automatic breathing, governed by brainstem centres and indispensable during sleep, becomes impaired owing to compression of the medullary regions by the cerebellar tonsils. Our patient demonstrated preserved voluntary control of breathing but lacked automatic respiratory drive, mirroring the clinical picture seen in congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, albeit through a distinct pathophysiological route. The incident at the airport, during which an incidental lapse in attention led to severe hypoxaemia in the absence of dyspnoea, offers a striking example of isolated failure in automatic respiratory regulation—illustrating a scenario in which the patient, in effect, simply ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient’s notable lack of dyspnoea during marked episodes of oxygen desaturation is primarily attributable to a dysfunction localized in the brainstem. Such a pathology is likely to impair essential afferent neural pathways responsible for transmitting sensory information to the cortex, which is crucial for the conscious perception of internal bodily states. The sensation of breathlessness constitutes a component of interoception and depends upon the appropriate relay of afferent input to specific cortical structures, such as the anterior insular cortex. Interruption of these neural signals at the level of the brainstem would prevent their successful relay to the cortex, thereby impeding the generation of the subjective sensation of air hunger and accounting for the patient’s perilous unawareness of significant physiological compromise.

The significant mismatch observed between the pronounced clinical symptoms exhibited by our patient and the modest polysomnographic results (AHI 6.0) requires further clarification. Owing to logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was employed instead of type I, resulting in the omission of EEG monitoring. This lack of EEG precluded precise assessment of total sleep time and probably caused substantial underreporting of respiratory disturbances. Since EEG was unavailable, the AHI was derived based on total recording duration—which includes both sleep and awake periods—rather than strictly sleep time. Consequently, this methodological constraint indicates that the documented AHI of 6.0 most likely does not reflect the actual severity of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Nevertheless, certain observations are indicative of central hypoventilation: (1) central events make up 72% of the total, even though the aggregate AHI is low; (2) recorded episodes of oxygen saturation falling to 70%–80% occur without accompanying dyspnoea or patient recognition (both at the airport and in routine assessments); and (3) imaging reveals marked brainstem compression, demonstrated by an 11 mm descent of the tonsils.

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography—including full EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation—has been scheduled at an external sleep laboratory. The finalization of this assessment is contingent upon aligning the patient's schedule, securing their consent for the procedure, and arranging a suitable time at the offsite location. Upon completion, this investigation is expected to yield a more precise evaluation of sleep architecture, breathing patterns, and the extent and severity of central hypoventilation.

It should be noted that, based on available data, central hypoventilation continues to be a strong clinical consideration rather than an unequivocally established diagnosis.

Oropharyngeal dysphagia, characterized by comparable impairment in swallowing both solids and liquids as well as episodes of choking and coughing, represented the most troubling symptom for our patient. Prospective assessment employing standardized swallowing evaluation tools revealed dysfunction in 36% of cases, with neurogenic etiologies being most common. The comparable difficulty across all textures serves to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from its mechanical counterpart. Initial and repeated evaluations of the patient’s predominant symptom were performed from gastroenterological and otolaryngological viewpoints, both yielding unremarkable results; despite this, her correct diagnosis was significantly postponed. This diagnostic lag underscores an essential clinical point: in cases of dysphagia or choking where endoscopic examination fails to identify a structural abnormality, neurologic causes should be considered. In a retrospective study of children with persistent dysphagia, the prevalence of CMI was found to be 3.3%, markedly exceeding the general paediatric rate of 0.1%–0.77%. This finding implies that sustained dysphagia may serve as an important marker of underlying CMI.

The description of pain localized to the vertex that improves following hair cutting illustrates a notable yet relatively unexplored clinical observation. Occipital neuralgia, which has been observed in connection with CMI, may produce heightened sensitivity of the scalp. The diagnostic framework for occipital neuralgia specifies the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia apparent during innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Nevertheless, the occurrence of occipital neuralgia in patients with CMI is infrequently documented in the literature, and current evidence does not definitively support a direct causal relationship.

A different perspective involves the recently identified ‘burning vertex syndrome’, characterized by pain at the scalp’s vertex, cutaneous allodynia, and a constellation of signs that do not correspond well with standard primary headache disorders, including migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headaches. Proposed pathophysiology suggests small fibre neural circuits within the scalp may be implicated, with variable responses observed following greater occipital nerve blockade. For our patient, the symptoms at the vertex could reflect: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) an incidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a separate, unrelated primary headache condition. Notably, there is a significant lack of systematic data concerning the distribution of cutaneous allodynia among those with CMI, underscored by the lack of validated instruments designed to assess such symptoms in CMI prior to the advent of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In light of this limited evidence base, we describe this observation as an intriguing clinical note rather than ascribing to it definite diagnostic significance.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when it manifests through unusual or ambiguous symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a protracted 14-year period before the condition was correctly identified. Such a prolonged and circuitous diagnostic process, marked by initial uncertainty and eventual neurological clarification, is frequently documented in previous reports.
