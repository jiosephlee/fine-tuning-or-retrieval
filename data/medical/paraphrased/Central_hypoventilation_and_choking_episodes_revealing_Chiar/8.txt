Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early forties reported a 7-year duration of recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, in addition to nocturnal choking incidents that happened 3–4 times per week and longstanding occipital headaches present for 14 years. During home surveillance in the COVID-19 pandemic, she was unexpectedly found to experience severe oxygen desaturation (70%–80%) episodes in the absence of overt respiratory distress. Several laryngoscopic and endoscopic examinations aimed at evaluating her choking symptoms yielded normal results. Retrospective analysis of a head CT scan from 14 years earlier—initially performed due to unrelated trauma—revealed an unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI of the head and neck established a diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I (CMI) with an 11 mm descent of the cerebellar tonsils. Polysomnography indicated mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0), predominantly of central origin (72%). This case underscores the potential for CMI to present with respiratory manifestations closely resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to investigate possible structural brain abnormalities in patients exhibiting unexplained respiratory impairment.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by the downward herniation of the cerebellar tonsils extending 5 mm or more beneath the foramen magnum, as seen on sagittal MRI scans. Recent imaging data indicate that CMI can be detected in roughly 1% of adults, yet clinical symptoms manifest in only about 0.1% of the population. There is a slight female predominance in CMI cases, with a ratio of 1.3:1, and the disorder most frequently emerges in individuals in their third decade of life.

The underlying pathophysiology primarily involves an undersized posterior fossa due to developmental factors, leading to a discrepancy between the volume of neural tissue and the capacity of the compartment. Nonetheless, recent research has questioned the classical view of CMI as inherently abnormal, suggesting instead that it represents a continuum ranging from typical anatomical differences to overtly symptomatic malformations. Volumetric analyses have revealed notable demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions that do not consistently align with the presence or severity of clinical symptoms. This evolving understanding underscores the need for a tailored, symptom-oriented clinical approach, carefully separating asymptomatic structural variants from cases of genuine pathology warranting operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome refers to the clinically significant presentation of CMI, marked by a characteristic set of neurological symptoms. The primary feature is an episodic occipital headache, often triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, and observed in more than 80% of patients. Symptom severity tends to be greater in adults compared to paediatric patients, particularly in those who also develop syringomyelia. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves occurs in 15%–26% of individuals, commonly manifesting as oropharyngeal impairments including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now understood to be a significant and rising complication associated with CMI. In a forward-looking investigation involving 90 adult patients diagnosed with CMI, half were found to exhibit sleep-related respiratory disorders. Specifically, a central apnoea index of at least 5 was identified in 6.7% of participants, though isolated central sleep apnoea was noted in just one instance, likely indicative of direct brainstem compression. Importantly, these respiratory issues are not confined to sleep disruptions alone; case reports have described both acute respiratory failure and severe, potentially fatal hypoxaemia among individuals with CMI.

Respiratory dysfunction in this context arises due to factors such as brainstem compression, blockage of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) outflow, and disturbance of key respiratory control centres, all contributing to episodic breathing difficulties. These manifestations are characteristic of an acquired central hypoventilation syndrome, also referred to as 'Ondine’s curse', which should be distinguished from the congenital form linked to PHOX2B mutations. Contemporary guidelines highlight this association by advocating for routine polysomnography in patients with CMI suspected of having sleep-disordered breathing. Notably, posterior fossa decompression has the potential to normalize CSF flow and alleviate respiratory symptoms, thus illustrating the need for clinicians to consider CMI as an underlying cause in cases of unexplained respiratory impairment.

Case presentation:

A woman in her early 40s came to our family medicine clinic with a 7-year history of recurrent choking episodes that happened unpredictably, affecting her during both meals and sleep. The episodes were equally triggered by solids and liquids. She described these events as abrupt and alarming, frequently causing her to stop eating or suddenly awaken with intense coughing and gasping for air, but never resulting in complete blockage of her airway. The nocturnal episodes occurred approximately three to four times per week, with occasional instances of two episodes in a single night. Throughout the years, she had received extensive workups, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper gastrointestinal endoscopies, all yielding normal findings. Despite these normal test results, her symptoms continued without improvement. The condition substantially impacted her social life; she refrained from dining out or attending gatherings because of the fear of experiencing a choking incident in public. As a consequence, she suffered from growing anxiety and embarrassment, ultimately leading to increased social isolation and emotional distress.

Upon further evaluation, it was uncovered that her clinical manifestations had started 14 years prior, after a minor motor vehicle collision during which she briefly lost consciousness. Soon after the incident, she experienced continuous headaches located in the occipital and vertex regions. She described these headaches as persistent, pressure-like sensations, frequently confined to a coin-sized area at the vertex. Activities such as coughing, straining, or performing other Valsalva manoeuvres did not worsen the pain. Multiple clinicians attributed her condition to nummular headache, and she received numerous local scalp injections that only temporarily alleviated her symptoms. Notably, she suffered from pronounced allodynia at the vertex, to the extent that even her hair's weight would precipitate discomfort. Ultimately, she found that maintaining short hair significantly lowered her usual pain intensity from 6/10 to 3/10; however, this adjustment was at odds with cultural norms and resulted in emotional difficulty.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started regularly measuring her oxygen saturation at home with a personal pulse oximeter. Across a span of several months, she recorded numerous instances of asymptomatic desaturation, with readings frequently falling into the 70%–80% range. Typically, these low saturation episodes went unnoticed until she actively used the device to check her levels. On several occasions, she sought emergency medical care, but her symptoms were consistently ascribed to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited her flight, she suddenly developed severe dizziness and nausea. Airport medical staff recorded her oxygen saturation at 78%, resulting in her being prohibited from boarding. When given supplemental oxygen, her symptoms abated entirely in a matter of minutes. Follow-up pulmonary assessments, incorporating chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, yielded normal results.

Considering the presence of persistent occipital and vertex headaches alongside recurrent choking incidents and unexplained episodes of hypoxia, efforts were made to identify a singular neurological cause. Examination of her prior medical documentation disclosed that a non-contrast CT scan of the head had been carried out 14 years prior, post-accident, and was interpreted as normal at that time. Upon meticulous retrospective analysis, however, the scan showed evidence of a crowded foramen magnum and diminished cerebrospinal fluid space at the craniocervical junction. Differential diagnosis:

The constellation of choking affecting solids and liquids equally, unexplained hypoxia and chronic headaches prompted systematic evaluation:

Given the presence of choking symptoms, an initial diagnosis of gastro-oesophageal reflux disease accompanied by laryngopharyngeal reflux was contemplated. However, this possibility was ruled out due to unremarkable pH monitoring results, normal findings on endoscopic examination, and the absence of clinical improvement despite maximal acid suppression therapy.

Myasthenia gravis was considered in light of bulbar symptomatology. However, this diagnosis became improbable given the negative results from antibody assays, no evidence of fatigable weakness, and the absence of variation in symptoms throughout the day.

Given episodes of hypoxia, comprehensive assessment for underlying pulmonary pathology was pursued. Intrinsic pulmonary disease was ruled out, as both chest imaging and pulmonary function assessments yielded normal findings.

The presentation of preserved voluntary respiration alongside compromised automatic breathing during periods of distraction or sleep strongly indicated central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse). Its onset after head trauma differentiated this acquired condition from congenital variants.

Pathological changes in the brainstem resulting from CMI accounted for the entirety of symptoms by exerting pressure on both the respiratory centers and nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thereby offering a comprehensive diagnostic explanation.

Discussion:

The close timing of the patient’s motor vehicle accident and the emergence of their symptoms merits thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is recognized not only as a congenital anomaly but also as a possible outcome following head trauma or various neurological insults. Recent aetiological frameworks2022 reflect this range by differentiating between primary (congenital) Chiari malformations and secondary or acquired variants, which are associated with traumatic events or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this instance, the CT scan obtained right after the accident revealed a foramen magnum that appeared crowded. Since we lack imaging from before the trauma, it is unclear whether this condition resulted directly from the injury or if the trauma simply uncovered a preexisting anomaly. The patient’s abrupt development of occipital headaches following the incident, with subsequent gradual emergence of bulbar and respiratory symptoms over a 14-year period, may be indicative of either: (1) tonsillar herniation directly triggered by trauma or (2) trauma that aggravated or exposed an otherwise silent congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory complications associated with CMI, offering deeper insight into the mechanisms underlying acquired central hypoventilation in the context of structural lesions of the brainstem. Understanding the differential involvement of voluntary versus automatic breathing is essential for interpreting the respiratory physiology in CMI. While voluntary respiration, governed by cortical circuits, is generally preserved in individuals with CMI, automatic respiration—which is orchestrated by brainstem centers and becomes critical during sleep—can be disrupted due to compression of the medullary region by cerebellar tonsils. In our case, the patient maintained the ability to breathe voluntarily but experienced a loss of involuntary respiratory drive, a pattern reminiscent of congenital central hypoventilation syndrome, though with a distinct underlying pathophysiology. The incident at the airport, wherein a brief lapse in attention led to severe oxygen desaturation in the absence of breathlessness, starkly demonstrates the specific impairment of automatic respiratory control, effectively illustrating how the patient can ‘forget to breathe’.

We propose that an underlying dysfunction within the brainstem is responsible for the patient’s notable lack of dyspnoea despite experiencing substantial episodes of oxygen desaturation. This suspected brainstem abnormality likely impedes essential afferent neural pathways that normally convey information to the cortex, facilitating conscious perception. Breathlessness exemplifies interoception, a process reliant on the transmission and interpretation of these sensory signals by specific brain areas, notably the anterior insular cortex. If these afferent signals are interrupted at the brainstem level, the cortical structures would fail to receive the requisite input necessary for the perception of air hunger, thereby accounting for the patient’s perilous unawareness of their compromised physiological state.

The pronounced difference observed between the patient’s significant clinical symptoms and the comparatively low AHI value of 6.0 on polysomnography requires clarification. For logistical reasons, we employed type III instead of type I polysomnography, omitting EEG measurements. The absence of EEG precluded determination of actual total sleep time, which in turn likely resulted in considerable underreporting of respiratory disturbances. Because the AHI is calculated based on total recording time—encompassing both wakefulness and sleep—instead of genuine sleep duration when EEG is not used, the figure of 6.0 probably fails to capture the true extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

In spite of this constraint, multiple aspects point toward central hypoventilation: (1) a high proportion of central events (72%) relative to a modest total AHI; (2) observed episodes of oxygen desaturation to 70%–80% occurring in the absence of dyspnoea or the patient’s recognition (noted both at the airport and in standard assessments); and (3) structural findings indicating considerable compression of the brainstem (11 mm descent of the tonsils).

A comprehensive Type I polysomnography—including complete EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video documentation—has been scheduled to take place at an external sleep centre. Nevertheless, the study has not yet been conducted due to the need to coordinate the patient’s availability, secure their consent to undergo the procedure, and arrange a suitable time with the remote facility. Upon completion, this assessment is expected to yield a more conclusive evaluation of the patient’s sleep architecture, respiratory activity, and to clarify both the presence and degree of central hypoventilation.

It should be noted that, based on the current data, central hypoventilation is still considered a clinically plausible hypothesis, but has not yet been confirmed by definitive diagnostic findings.

Our patient’s primary and most troubling complaint was oropharyngeal dysphagia, characterized by comparable difficulty swallowing both solids and liquids, and frequently accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. In a prospective study utilizing standardized swallowing evaluations, dysfunction was identified in 36% of cases, with neurogenic etiologies being most common. Notably, the presence of uniform impairment across different food textures helps to differentiate neurogenic dysphagia from mechanical types. Despite being the central concern, the patient’s symptom was repeatedly explored through gastroenterological and otolaryngological investigations, all of which yielded normal results; as a consequence, her correct diagnosis was substantially delayed. This diagnostic delay underscores a crucial clinical insight: when dysphagia or recurrent choking cannot be explained by abnormalities detected on endoscopic examination, neurological causes should be considered. In a retrospective analysis, the prevalence of CMI among children with long-standing dysphagia was 3.3%, markedly exceeding the 0.1%–0.77% rate documented in the general paediatric population. Thus, persistent dysphagia may serve as an important marker for undiagnosed CMI.

The observation that the patient experiences pain localized to the vertex and finds relief after hair cutting illustrates a noteworthy yet insufficiently explored clinical feature. While occipital neuralgia has been documented in connection with CMI and is known to elicit hypersensitivity of the scalp, its diagnostic definition stipulates the presence of ‘dysesthesia and/or allodynia provoked by innocuous stimulation of the scalp and/or hair’. Nevertheless, reports detailing the co-occurrence of occipital neuralgia with CMI remain sparse, and current evidence does not definitively confirm a causal relationship.

Another possible explanation involves the newly characterized ‘burning vertex syndrome’, which is marked by scalp discomfort, cutaneous allodynia, and clinical characteristics that fall outside the typical categories of primary headache disorders, including migraine, cluster, tension-type, or nummular headache. The proposed pathophysiology may pertain to dysfunction in small fibre pathways emanating from the scalp, with some patients exhibiting a variable response to greater occipital nerve blocks. For our case, the vertex-related symptoms could signify: (1) occipital neuralgia secondary to CMI; (2) coincidental presentation of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct and unrelated primary headache condition. Notably, there is a scarcity of systematic reporting on cutaneous allodynia patterns among those with CMI, highlighted by the previous lack of validated instruments designed to capture such symptoms in CMI patients before the creation of the Chiari Symptom Profile. Due to the limited available evidence, we share this observation as a noteworthy clinical feature rather than a conclusive diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI, particularly when its clinical presentation includes unusual or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, which in this instance resulted in a 14-year diagnostic delay. The trajectory from early misdiagnosis to eventual identification of the neurological etiology reflects a common pattern documented in existing literature.
