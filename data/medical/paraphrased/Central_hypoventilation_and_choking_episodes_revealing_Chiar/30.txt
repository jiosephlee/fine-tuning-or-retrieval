Title: Central hypoventilation and choking episodes revealing Chiari malformation type I

Ali Mohamed Mousa, Mervat Mahmoud, Ghaida Mubarak AlShuraiaan, Zouheir Ibrahim Bitar

Abstract:

Summary: A female patient in her early 40s reported a 7-year duration of recurrent choking episodes involving both solids and liquids, frequent nocturnal choking episodes occurring three to four times per week, as well as chronic occipital headaches persisting over 14 years. During the COVID-19 pandemic, home oxygen monitoring incidentally detected severe desaturation events (ranging from 70%–80%) in the absence of respiratory distress. Repeated laryngoscopic and endoscopic examinations to evaluate the choking complaints yielded normal findings. A re-examination of a 14-year-old head CT, originally performed for unrelated trauma, revealed a previously unrecognized crowded foramen magnum. Subsequent MRI imaging of the head and neck established the diagnosis of Chiari malformation type I (CMI), with cerebellar tonsillar descent measured at 11 mm. Polysomnography indicated mild sleep apnoea (apnoea–hypopnoea index 6.0), predominantly of central origin (accounting for 72% of events). This case demonstrates that CMI may manifest as respiratory symptoms resembling acquired central hypoventilation syndrome (Ondine’s curse), highlighting the need to consider structural brain pathology in individuals with unexplained respiratory dysfunction.

Background:

Chiari malformation type I (CMI) is characterized by cerebellar tonsils descending 5 mm or more below the foramen magnum as seen on sagittal MRI. Recent imaging research indicates that CMI affects 1% of adults, while only about 0.1% of people experience symptoms. The disorder has a slight female bias, with a ratio of 1.3:1, and most often arises in individuals in their thirties.

The underlying mechanism involves an underdeveloped posterior fossa, resulting in a discrepancy between the size of neural tissue and the space accommodating it. Yet, newer research contests the longstanding view that CMI is inherently pathological, suggesting it represents a continuum ranging from benign anatomical differences to clinically relevant abnormalities. Current volumetric analyses reveal significant demographic differences in posterior fossa dimensions, often lacking clear association with clinical symptoms. This evolving perspective calls for tailored, symptom-driven management strategies, separating incidental anatomical variations from those malformations that warrant operative treatment.

Chiari syndrome is the symptomatic expression of CMI, distinguished by a unique cluster of neurological symptoms. The primary feature is intermittent occipital headache triggered by Valsalva manoeuvres, observed in more than 80% of patients. Adults generally exhibit more pronounced symptoms compared to pediatric cases, particularly if syringomyelia is present. Involvement of the lower cranial nerves is documented in 15%–26% of cases, most often manifesting as oropharyngeal disturbances, including palatal weakness, tongue atrophy, or cricopharyngeal achalasia.

Respiratory symptoms are now acknowledged as a growing complication associated with CMI. In a prospective analysis of 90 adult patients diagnosed with CMI, half exhibited sleep-associated breathing abnormalities. An elevated central apnoea index (≥5) was identified in 6.7% of these individuals; however, exclusively central sleep apnoea was reported in only a single patient, possibly indicating direct brainstem involvement. The spectrum of respiratory issues is not confined to disruptions in sleep, as illustrated by a case series describing episodes of acute respiratory failure and critical hypoxaemia among individuals with CMI.

Respiratory impairment in these cases arises from compression of the brainstem, obstruction of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) circulation, and disturbance of the respiratory regulatory centres, resulting in episodic respiratory manifestations. These symptoms correspond to an acquired variant of central hypoventilation syndrome—occasionally referred to as 'Ondine’s curse'—which is separate from the congenital central hypoventilation syndrome linked to PHOX2B gene mutations. Current recommendations highlight this relationship by advising regular polysomnography in patients with Chiari malformation type I (CMI) who exhibit possible sleep-related breathing disorders. Importantly, surgical decompression of the posterior fossa can re-establish normal CSF flow and may ameliorate the respiratory symptoms, thereby stressing the need to identify CMI in cases of unexplained respiratory compromise.

Case presentation:

A female patient in her early forties visited our family medicine clinic reporting a 7-year history of unpredictable choking incidents that arose during meals and while sleeping, with both solid and liquid foods being equally implicated. She characterized these occurrences as abrupt and highly distressing, frequently interrupting meals or awakening her at night with intense coughing and gasping, although she had never experienced complete airway obstruction. Nocturnal episodes happened three to four times weekly, and sometimes twice in a single night. Throughout the years, she had several assessments, including three flexible laryngoscopies and two upper GI endoscopies, all yielding normal findings. Despite these negative evaluations, her symptoms continued unabated. Social consequences were substantial; she abstained from restaurants and social events out of concern for experiencing choking episodes in public. The persistent fear and humiliation associated with these events resulted in progressive social isolation and heightened emotional discomfort.

A thorough clinical history demonstrated that her symptoms originated 14 years prior, immediately after a minor motor vehicle accident during which she briefly lost consciousness. Subsequently, she experienced ongoing headaches affecting the occipital region and vertex. These headaches were characterized as continuous, pressure-like, and frequently confined to a coin-sized region at the vertex. They did not worsen with coughing, straining, or other Valsalva maneuvers. Several practitioners diagnosed her condition as nummular headache, and she received numerous localized scalp injections, providing only temporary relief. The most notable feature was pronounced allodynia at the vertex; even the sensation of her hair was sufficient to provoke discomfort. Ultimately, she found that keeping her hair short reduced her typical pain level from 6/10 to 3/10, although this choice conflicted with cultural norms and caused her emotional distress.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the patient started using a pulse oximeter at home to track her oxygen saturation. Across several months, she consistently recorded episodes of low oxygen saturation falling into the 70%–80% range, despite being asymptomatic. These events were frequently unnoticed except when she assessed her oximeter readings. Several visits to emergency services resulted in her symptoms being ascribed to anxiety.

A significant event took place at an international airport. As she awaited departure, she developed sudden-onset dizziness accompanied by nausea. Airport medical staff recorded an oxygen saturation level of 78%, resulting in her being unable to board the plane. Her symptoms abated entirely within a few minutes after receiving supplemental oxygen. Follow-up assessment by pulmonology, which included chest imaging and pulmonary function tests, did not reveal any abnormalities.

In light of her persistent occipital and vertex headaches, recurrent episodes of choking, and unexplained hypoxia, clinicians sought a single neurological condition that could explain all her symptoms. Upon examination of her past medical documentation, a non-contrast head CT scan performed 14 years prior, after her accident, was noted to have been interpreted as unremarkable at that time. Nonetheless, upon careful retrospective assessment, the imaging demonstrated a crowded foramen magnum and absence of cerebrospinal fluid around the craniocervical region. After the patient experienced hypoxic events, comprehensive assessment for primary pulmonary disease was carried out. Chest imaging and pulmonary function tests were within normal limits, ruling out inherent lung disorders.

Central alveolar hypoventilation syndrome (acquired Ondine’s curse) was indicated by intact voluntary respiration contrasted with defective automatic regulation when the patient was distracted or asleep. Its development after head injury differentiated it from congenital variants.

The clinical manifestations were fully accounted for by brainstem dysfunction resulting from CMI, which led to direct pressure on the respiratory centers and nuclei of the lower cranial nerves, thus establishing a singular unifying diagnosis.

Discussion:

The timing of the motor vehicle accident in relation to the appearance of symptoms in our patient necessitates thorough evaluation. Chiari malformation type I is known to exist as a congenital abnormality but has also been reported following head injuries and other neurological trauma. Recent etiological frameworks2022 address this range by differentiating between primary (congenital) Chiari malformations and secondary or acquired variants that emerge as a consequence of trauma or elevated intracranial pressure.

In this patient, a CT scan obtained promptly after the injury showed a narrowed foramen magnum. Because there are no baseline images prior to the trauma, it is unclear if this anatomical change resulted directly from the injury or was simply brought to attention by it. The patient experienced sudden occipital headaches following the accident and gradually developed bulbar and respiratory symptoms over a 14-year period. This clinical course may suggest either: (1) trauma causing new tonsillar herniation, or (2) the injury exacerbating or exposing an underlying, asymptomatic congenital anomaly.

This case highlights the intricate respiratory features associated with CMI and broadens insight into acquired patterns of central hypoventilation resulting from structural lesions in the brainstem. Differentiating voluntary from automatic breathing is essential when interpreting the respiratory pathophysiology seen in CMI. While voluntary respiration, governed by cortical circuits, is generally preserved in individuals with CMI, automatic breathing—managed by brainstem centers and especially critical during sleep—can be impaired because of compression of medullary regions by the cerebellar tonsils. In this patient, voluntary control of breathing was maintained, whereas automatic respiratory drive was absent, a clinical presentation similar to congenital central hypoventilation syndrome but stemming from a different pathophysiological cause. The incident at the airport, wherein transient inattention led to significant oxygen desaturation without subjective dyspnea, starkly demonstrates this specific deficit in automatic respiratory regulation: the patient effectively ‘forgets to breathe’.

We propose that the patient’s striking lack of breathlessness during periods of pronounced oxygen desaturation results from underlying dysfunction within the brainstem. This brainstem abnormality may block essential afferent neural transmissions that are required to reach the cerebral cortex in order to elicit conscious perception. Breathlessness represents an interoceptive process that relies on these signals being processed by critical cortical areas, notably the anterior insular cortex. If these afferent pathways are interrupted while traversing the brainstem, the cortex fails to receive adequate input for the sensation of air hunger, which accounts for the patient’s hazardous unawareness of their physiologic compromise.

The significant contrast between the patient’s pronounced clinical manifestations and the modest polysomnographic outcome (AHI 6.0) requires clarification. Because of logistical constraints, type III polysomnography was employed instead of type I, and this method does not incorporate EEG monitoring. The lack of EEG precludes precise determination of actual sleep duration and, as a result, probably results in substantial underreporting of respiratory disturbances. In the absence of EEG data, the AHI calculation is based on the entire monitoring period, which encompasses both sleep and wakefulness, rather than exclusively on periods of sleep. Consequently, this methodological limitation suggests that the documented AHI of 6.0 most likely understates the genuine extent of the patient’s sleep-disordered breathing.

Nevertheless, multiple findings indicate central hypoventilation: (1) a majority of the observed events were central in nature (72%), even though the total AHI was low; (2) episodes of oxygen saturation falling to 70%–80% were noted, occurring without dyspnea or the patient’s recognition (such as at the airport and during routine assessments); and (3) imaging revealed notable compression of the brainstem, with 11 mm of tonsillar descent.

A Type I polysomnography incorporating comprehensive EEG monitoring, end-tidal capnography, and video surveillance has been scheduled at an outside sleep facility. Nonetheless, the study has not yet been carried out due to ongoing arrangements involving the patient’s availability, consent for the procedure, and the sleep centre’s scheduling constraints. Upon completion, the results are expected to yield a more conclusive assessment of sleep architecture, respiratory function, and to delineate the existence and degree of central hypoventilation.

We recognize that, based on the data currently available, central hypoventilation is best regarded as a strongly supported clinical consideration rather than an unequivocally established diagnosis.

The most troublesome symptom experienced by our patient was oropharyngeal dysphagia, characterized by equally impaired swallowing of both solids and liquids, accompanied by episodes of choking and coughing. When prospectively assessed with validated swallowing tests, swallowing impairment was identified in 36% of cases, predominantly demonstrating neurogenic features. The comparable difficulty across all food textures serves to differentiate neurogenic from mechanical dysphagia. The patient’s chief complaint was explored multiple times from both gastroenterological and otolaryngological angles, yielding normal results; nonetheless, her diagnosis was postponed for several years. This delay underscores a crucial point in clinical practice: in cases where dysphagia or choking occurs in the absence of an evident structural lesion on endoscopic evaluation, a neurological cause should be considered. A retrospective review of children suffering from persistent dysphagia reported a prevalence of CMI at 3.3%, a rate substantially higher than the 0.1%–0.77% prevalence in the broader paediatric population. These data indicate that sustained dysphagia may serve as a significant marker for underlying CMI.

The patient’s description of vertex pain that improves following haircutting highlights a curious, yet largely unexamined, clinical observation. Occipital neuralgia is sometimes seen in conjunction with CMI and may lead to increased scalp sensitivity. Diagnostic guidelines stipulate the presence of 'dysesthesia and/or allodynia in response to non-painful stimulation of the scalp and/or hair.' Despite this, reports of occipital neuralgia co-occurring with CMI are infrequent, and current evidence does not confirm a direct causal relationship.

Another possibility is the newly characterized ‘burning vertex syndrome,’ which manifests with scalp discomfort, skin sensitivity to touch, and symptom profiles that do not conform precisely to established categories of primary headaches such as migraine, cluster headache, tension-type headache, or nummular headache. It has been proposed that this syndrome may result from disturbances in small nerve fiber pathways within the scalp, and therapeutic response to greater occipital nerve blocks among patients has been inconsistent. For our patient, the vertex complaints could indicate: (1) occipital neuralgia associated with CMI; (2) a coincidental occurrence of burning vertex syndrome; or (3) a distinct primary headache disorder unrelated to the above. There remains a lack of comprehensive documentation of cutaneous allodynia in individuals with CMI, a deficiency underscored by the historical absence of validated tools for symptom evaluation in this population prior to the introduction of the Chiari Symptom Profile. In view of the limited data, we report this observation as a notable clinical finding rather than an established diagnostic criterion.

This case underscores the considerable difficulty in diagnosing CMI when the presentation involves unusual or deceptive symptoms like central hypoventilation and choking, resulting in a 14-year lag before an accurate diagnosis was made. The process was marked by early misinterpretation and a slow progression toward identifying the correct neurological disorder—a pattern frequently described in published reports.
