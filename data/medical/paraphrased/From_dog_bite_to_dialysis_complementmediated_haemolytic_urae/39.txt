Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical issues developed rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She experienced disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, progressing to complete anuria and the need for dialysis. Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia was identified through blood cultures. While she initially responded to antibiotic therapy and supportive measures, she subsequently developed abrupt isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory testing and renal biopsy revealed a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Plasma exchange and treatment with eculizumab resulted in haematological remission and partial restoration of renal function. This report demonstrates that C. canimorsus infection can rarely precipitate CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, and discusses the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the challenges in distinguishing among diagnoses, as well as the intricate clinical progression and management considerations for TMA arising in the setting of sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is commonly present in the normal oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Human infection with this organism is infrequent, with transmission possible through bites, scratches, or close proximity to these animals. The clinical manifestations are diverse, ranging from isolated meningitis to severe sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organs, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although the organism exhibits susceptibility to a wide range of antibiotics, mortality rates can be as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often associated with invasive zoonotic disease in humans. Because of its fastidious growth and slow development in culture, diagnosis may be delayed, increasing reliance on molecular diagnostics, including real-time nanopore sequencing.

In this case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock accompanied by pronounced leukopaenia and coagulopathy typical of DIC. While early progression was primarily driven by sepsis and multiorgan dysfunction, subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopaenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated a co-existing TMA, illustrating how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be masked during the initial phases of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues and two previous uneventful pregnancies arrived at the emergency department with acutely deteriorating symptoms. Four days earlier, her dog had bitten her upper lip, after which she received tetanus prophylaxis. She was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) on the same day, but did not start antibiotic therapy as she was unable to pick up the medication. Subsequently, she developed a high-grade fever (>40 °C), generalized malaise, decreased oral intake, and sought further care after the onset of numbness and discoloration in her feet. Evaluation at the primary-care emergency unit revealed hypoglycemia that did not respond to oral glucose or glucagon, with the lowest measured level at 0.6 mmol/L, leading to her urgent referral to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and communicative but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness in the lower extremities. She denied any use of herbal products or other substances, and no family members had experienced similar symptoms. Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated profound systemic abnormalities with significant inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated hepatic transaminases, and coagulation abnormalities. Arterial blood gas testing indicated metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a raised lactate concentration of 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). The patient persisted with hypoglycaemia even after administration of large amounts of intravenous glucose, attributed to septic processes. Upon arrival, her urine output was adequate; urinalysis detected proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her range of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—was assessed as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis (qSOFA score 1 out of 3). After both clinical and radiological assessment, surgical etiologies for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded. Because of the severity of her initial clinical picture, she was started on broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) to provide coverage against pathogens associated with dog bites, including Capnocytophaga species. Importantly, C. canimorsus was suspected from the outset and was definitively identified seven days later in both aerobic and anaerobic blood culture samples. She was then transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive surveillance.

Due to her pancytopenia and coagulopathy, the haematology service was engaged. Differential diagnoses considered included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also evaluated but was deemed less probable because of the presence of leukopaenia, inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and absence of compensatory reticulocytosis. Flow cytometry analysis was performed, with plans to proceed with a bone marrow biopsy; however, the normal findings—which showed no increase in CD34+ blasts or abnormal phenotypes—led to the cancellation of the procedure. The clinical features aligned most closely with sepsis. While awaiting diagnostic results, supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were provided.

Following her admission to the ICU, the patient became completely anuric and experienced a swift decline in renal function. Attempts at aggressive intravenous fluid administration combined with loop diuretic therapy proved ineffective. She later progressed to septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by fluid overload, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including ANA and ANCA, were negative.

By ICU day 3, she developed widespread cutaneous bleeding with ecchymoses, along with cyanotic and ischemic changes in multiple distal toes and fingers. Notably, distal pulses and sensation remained intact. These manifestations were interpreted as evidence of microthrombotic processes secondary to DIC. In spite of involvement of multiple organ systems, the patient never needed vasopressors except for stress-dose corticosteroids used during the early septic episode. She was also treated with albumin and additional blood product transfusions.

Following 8 days of hospitalization, she was moved to the nephrology unit due to persistent dialysis-requiring AKI and the need for continued intravenous antibiotic therapy. Upon arrival, the patient was haemodynamically stable but remained anuric, displayed diffuse oedema, and had multiple haematomas; she was, however, alert and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was subsequently stepped down to intravenous ampicillin and later switched to oral Apocillin in accordance with blood culture findings.

While in the nephrology ward, laboratory assessments demonstrated a favourable trend with reduction in inflammatory markers, a reactive leukocytosis, an upward trend in platelet count, and stable haemoglobin levels. Renal function tests continued to reveal elevated azotaemic indices, but coagulation studies had normalised, pointing to resolution of her prior coagulopathy. These laboratory data are summarised in Table 2.

Following a period of initial progress, her clinical course altered when she experienced an abrupt, isolated decrease in haemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days) despite absence of clinically apparent bleeding or haemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin became undetectable, and LDH levels increased, all consistent with ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) was positive for IgG, indicating possible autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. The patient was started on high-dose prednisolone in addition to standard prophylactic measures, and received multiple blood transfusions over a two-day period. Despite this, the haemolytic process did not improve with corticosteroid treatment and platelet counts remained low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), prompting further investigation. Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, which is consistent with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) suggestive of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). It was determined that the initial DAT result was likely falsely positive due to penicillin or previous transfusions; subsequent testing was negative.

Complement studies and ADAMTS13 activity were requested. The locally available standard complement assays (C3/C4) were normal, and functional tests for the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways were also within reference ranges. ADAMTS13 activity was reported soon afterward at 43.5%, which effectively excluded thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP usually manifests with activity below 10%. During the interval while these results were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was performed as a bridging intervention ahead of administering the initial dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia with the presence of fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, all of which are indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy images are not included, as immunofluorescence showed absence of immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy did not demonstrate features of a primary glomerular disorder.

Hemolytic uremic syndrome related to Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC-HUS) was excluded due to a negative stool PCR result.

After the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), the patient's spontaneous urine output resumed. The initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B)—were administered before starting eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg intravenously per week for a total of 4 weeks). The follow-up vaccine doses were scheduled to be given as outpatient therapy 8 weeks later. Apocillin prophylaxis was prescribed because the meningococcal vaccines had not been provided at least 2 weeks prior to initiating complement inhibition.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement testing was sent to Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. For reasons of logistics and patient safety, the patient received three Octaplas PEX sessions and a single induction dose of eculizumab before sample collection. Functional complement analysis demonstrated a significantly reduced quantitative alternative pathway activity (AH50) at 6%, with normal levels of complement factors H, I, and B, and negative antifactor H antibody results.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intricate medical treatment, the patient maintains a stable overall state and satisfactory urine production. Her renal function remains compromised, as indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and ongoing monitoring will occur in the nephrology outpatient setting (figure 4). Eculizumab therapy continues at a maintenance dose of 1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks, sustained for four months following induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of complement-related genes failed to reveal any pathogenic mutations; preliminary verbal results were obtained from the reference laboratory, though the definitive written report was not available at the time of manuscript submission. Given these findings, complement inhibition therapy is being sustained due to the seriousness of her clinical presentation, with plans to reconsider cessation if renal function achieves stability. Should pathogenic complement gene mutations have been detected, longer-term treatment with vigilant surveillance for recurrence would be indicated. In this patient, however, no such variants were present.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

The foremost concern in adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI is ruling out thrombotic TTP, characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score are useful for initial triage pending laboratory confirmation. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicative of a low pretest likelihood for TTP, and ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, diminishing the probability of TTP and informing subsequent evaluation.

Consideration of STEC-HUS is also essential. Shiga toxin PCR testing for the patient was negative, and there was no preceding gastrointestinal illness, both findings against STEC-HUS.

The combination of ongoing haemolysis, schistocyte presence, dialysis-requiring AKI, and renal biopsy findings consistent with TMA supported the diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). Exclusion of STEC via PCR and notable clinical improvement following eculizumab therapy further reinforced this diagnosis. With no significant past medical history to suggest an underlying complement disorder necessitating a ‘second hit,’ the clinical picture favors infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is primarily a clinicopathological diagnosis that is reached by ruling out other causes, and patients may exhibit normal complement levels (C3/C4) throughout the illness. Significantly, the presence of normal complement values (C3/C4) does not eliminate the possibility of CM-HUS, since these parameters can remain within normal limits during the disease. Importantly, the onset of haemolysis was detected only after improvement in sepsis, indicating that the CM-HUS was most likely induced by infection rather than being a pre-existing disorder.

For our patient, initial complement assays (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were measured just prior to the first therapeutic plasma exchange (PEX). Additional, more specialized complement analysis was conducted eight days following the last PEX treatment and four days subsequent to the first administration of eculizumab. The decreased function observed in the alternative pathway aligns predominantly with the effects of therapy as opposed to inherent complement deficiency or increased activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS’, results from unregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway and often occurs in individuals with a genetic susceptibility. Genetic mutations or autoantibodies that impair regulation of complement proteins—including factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are detected in approximately 50% to 70% of affected patients. Nevertheless, the disease exhibits incomplete penetrance, with clinical onset generally necessitating an external precipitating factor, such as infections, pregnancy, autoimmune processes, or certain drugs.

Though infrequently documented, Capnocytophaga canimorsus is capable of activating complement cascades, especially in individuals with underlying vulnerability. Importantly, C. canimorsus demonstrates varied sensitivity to complement-mediated lysis; while the majority of strains are complement-sensitive, certain encapsulated variants exhibit some resistance, highlighting the crucial role of complement in host defense. This situation poses a clinical dilemma: activation of complement can assist in pathogen clearance yet may simultaneously promote uncontrolled endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in susceptible hosts.

The present case contributes to a limited but increasing number of reports associating C. canimorsus with complement-driven TMAs. Bjørkto et al., for instance, detailed a patient who developed CM-HUS due to C. canimorsus infection and was found to have a complement factor B mutation, responding well to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that case, genetic testing in our patient did not reveal pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene abnormalities, suggesting infection-induced CM-HUS in the absence of detectable inherited complement dysfunction.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS in the setting of active infection poses distinct difficulties. Eculizumab—a monoclonal antibody directed against complement component 5—is considered the standard therapy for CM-HUS, and significantly enhances both haematologic and renal outcomes, especially with early administration. Initiating eculizumab late is linked to incomplete renal restoration, thereby elevating the likelihood of progressing to end-stage renal disease.

Conversely, plasma exchange (PEX) is crucial in TTP and can be life-preserving, but in CM-HUS it offers only fleeting benefit, chiefly by eliminating circulating complement fragments, inflammatory cytokines, or autoantibodies, without resolving the fundamental complement dysregulation. In this patient, diuresis resumed following the last PEX sessions but ahead of eculizumab initiation, demonstrating merely temporary stabilization. Persistent recovery of renal and haematologic parameters was achieved only subsequent to complement inhibition.

An important therapeutic challenge is employing complement inhibitors when bacteraemia is ongoing, as terminal complement factors are vital for host defense against encapsulated pathogens—such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga—which raises concern about infection risk related to eculizumab. Here, eculizumab treatment commenced once blood cultures no longer indicated infection, reducing the chance of uncontrolled sepsis. Immunization, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring are essential components whenever complement inhibitors are administered.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition represent key factors linked to better outcomes. In this case, the patient attained haematologic remission and partial recovery of renal function, aligning with existing data indicating that prognosis is largely determined by baseline renal status and the interval prior to starting eculizumab therapy.

While CM-HUS triggered by C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of considering infectious etiologies—including atypical pathogens—in individuals with new presentations of TMA. Increased clinical vigilance and the use of molecular diagnostic methods could facilitate recognition of additional similar cases.
