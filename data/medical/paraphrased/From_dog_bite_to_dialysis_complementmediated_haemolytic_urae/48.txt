Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after being bitten by a dog. Her condition was complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury that progressed to complete anuria requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia. After initially responding to antibiotics and supportive measures, she experienced abrupt isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory studies and a renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) was established. Hematological remission was achieved following plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab, with partial renal recovery. This report underscores C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, and details the patient’s diagnostic process, difficulties in differential diagnosis, and the clinical and therapeutic complexities associated with TMA after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow-growing, gram-negative rod that resides within the normal oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Infections in humans are uncommon, but transmission may occur through bites, scratches, or even close proximity. The clinical manifestations vary considerably, spanning from isolated meningitis to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiple organ dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although it is generally susceptible to a wide range of antibiotics, the associated mortality rate can reach up to 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in severe zoonotic infections in humans. Its fastidious growth and prolonged incubation time in cultures often delay identification, thus molecular diagnostic approaches, including real-time nanopore sequencing, are being increasingly employed.

In this report, the presentation of C. canimorsus infection involved septic shock, pronounced leukopaenia, and coagulation abnormalities compatible with DIC. The early phase was chiefly characterized by sepsis with multiorgan involvement; however, the subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and progressive acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated a secondary TMA, demonstrating that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be masked during the initial period of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, with no previous health issues and a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department in a state of rapidly declining health. Four days before admission, she sustained a bite from her dog to her upper lip and was subsequently given a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed on the day of the injury; however, she did not begin antibiotic therapy due to an inability to collect the medication. She subsequently developed a high fever exceeding 40 °C, general malaise, and decreased oral intake. She later presented for medical evaluation following the onset of numbness and discoloration in her feet. Upon assessment in primary-care emergency, she was noted to have hypoglycemia that did not respond to oral glucose or glucagon, with a lowest recorded glucose level of 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating emergent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and communicative, yet demonstrated signs of sepsis, including mottled and cool lower limbs. She denied the use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of similar symptoms among her family members. Initial laboratory assessment showed profound systemic disturbance with intense inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas analysis indicated metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference: 7.35–7.43) and an increased lactate level of 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). The patient persisted in a hypoglycaemic state despite administration of large doses of intravenous glucose, suspected to be a consequence of sepsis. Upon presentation, she was still producing urine adequately; urinalysis detected protein at 2+ and blood at 3+.

Her combination of clinical findings—including elevated temperature, decreased blood cell counts, coagulation abnormalities accompanied by acute kidney injury and lactic acidosis—was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early septic state (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1 of 3). Potential surgical etiologies for the mottling of her limbs, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded following thorough clinical assessment and radiological studies. Owing to the seriousness of her illness, empiric intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to provide coverage against canine-associated organisms, including Capnocytophaga species. Of note, C. canimorsus was suspected from the beginning and was definitively identified seven days later through both aerobic and anaerobic blood culture analyses. She was subsequently moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced surveillance.

Given her pancytopenia and coagulopathy, haematology input was sought. Differential considerations included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also assessed but was deemed less likely on account of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and a lack of compensatory reticulocyte response. Flow cytometric analysis was performed and a bone marrow biopsy was intended; however, findings demonstrating normal parameters with no increase in CD34+ blasts or abnormal phenotypic populations led to cancellation of the biopsy. The clinical scenario supported a diagnosis of sepsis. Pending further laboratory results, the patient received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient developed complete anuria alongside a swift decline in kidney function. Efforts to restore renal output with intensive intravenous fluids and simultaneous loop diuretic use were unsuccessful. She then progressed to septic cardiomyopathy, resulting in volume overload, necessitating commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Serological screening for autoimmunity—including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA)—yielded negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, the patient manifested widespread cutaneous bleeding characterized by ecchymosis, as well as peripheral cyanosis and ischemic alterations involving multiple toes and fingers, while distal pulses and sensory function were intact. These signs were indicative of microvascular thromboses associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although multiple organ systems were compromised, she did not require vasopressor administration apart from stress-dose corticosteroids used in the early septic period. Albumin and other blood component infusions were also administered.

Following eight days of hospital admission, she was moved to the nephrology unit for continued management of dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, the patient was stable from a clinical perspective but remained anuric, exhibited widespread swelling and multiple bruises, and was awake and oriented. Based on blood culture findings, antibiotics were reduced to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently to oral Apocillin.

During her stay in the nephrology unit, laboratory investigations demonstrated a decreasing inflammatory marker trend, leukocytosis consistent with a reactive process, platelet count began to recover, and haemoglobin remained unchanged. Tests of renal function continued to reveal azotaemia, though coagulation studies returned to normal, reflecting an improvement in coagulopathy. Despite some initial improvement, her condition evolved when a sudden and isolated decline in haemoglobin was noted, dropping from 111 to 68 g/L across six days, without any signs of bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently reduced (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin levels became undetectable, and LDH increased, all consistent with ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, returned positive for IgG, prompting concern for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was administered, accompanied by standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were performed over the course of two days. Since the haemolysis did not improve with corticosteroid treatment and platelet counts persisted at 41–46 × 10⁹/L, additional diagnostic workup was indicated. Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of MAHA and consistent with TMA (figure 2). The initial DAT result was probably falsely positive due to penicillin exposure or earlier transfusions; subsequent DAT testing yielded a negative result.

Complement testing and ADAMTS13 activity levels were requested. Routine complement panels (C3/C4) were found to be within reference ranges, and functional evaluations of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways also demonstrated normal findings. Shortly thereafter, ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, thereby excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP characteristically shows activity less than 10%. In the interim while awaiting these data, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced to provide interim therapy before administration of the initial dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, findings indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy findings are not presented; immunofluorescence testing detected no deposition of immune complexes, and electron microscopy did not identify evidence of a primary glomerular disorder.

Stool PCR testing was negative for Shiga toxin, thereby excluding Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS).

After the patient underwent the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) procedures, spontaneous urine output resumed. Initial meningococcal vaccinations—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B)—were administered before starting eculizumab induction (900 mg intravenously weekly for 4 weeks). The booster vaccine doses were scheduled to be given after 8 weeks, arranged for outpatient follow-up. Since the vaccines had not been provided at least two weeks prior to initiating complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement analysis was ordered from Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Due to logistical challenges and patient safety, three Octaplas-based PEX procedures and a single induction dose of eculizumab were given before sample collection. Functional complement assessment measured alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which was significantly decreased (6%), whereas complement factors H, I, and B remained within normal ranges, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After the intricate medical interventions, the patient demonstrates overall good health and satisfactory urine production. Renal function remains impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and ongoing care will be provided in the nephrology outpatient setting (figure 4). She persists with eculizumab maintenance therapy (1200 mg IV every 2 weeks), extending to 4 months following induction treatment. Comprehensive complement genetic analysis failed to reveal any disease-causing variants; the reference laboratory shared preliminary verbal findings, with the formal written documentation pending submission. Given these results, complement inhibition is being sustained because of the disease’s severity, with intentions to reassess and possibly cease therapy if kidney function reaches stability. Detection of pathogenic complement gene mutations would warrant longer-term treatment and careful surveillance for recurrence; nonetheless, such variants were not observed in this patient.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adults who present with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI), the foremost step is to rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Tools such as the PLASMIC score can aid in clinical assessment while awaiting definitive laboratory data, though confirmation depends on test results. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, with ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, thereby making TTP improbable and prompting additional diagnostic evaluation.

Another diagnosis to consider is STEC-HUS. However, Shiga toxin PCR testing was negative for this patient. Furthermore, the lack of antecedent gastrointestinal symptoms and negative microbiological tests diminishes the possibility of STEC-HUS.

In conclusion, ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, AKI requiring dialysis, and renal biopsy findings indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) all favored a diagnosis of complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) (figure 5). Exclusion of STEC infection via PCR and her swift improvement following initiation of eculizumab further reinforced the diagnosis. The patient's minimal medical background lessens the argument for an underlying complement disorder needing an additional trigger, supporting the theory of infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is identified clinically and histopathologically after ruling out other conditions, and complement concentrations (C3/C4) can remain within normal limits throughout the illness. It is essential to note that CM-HUS cannot be excluded solely based on normal C3/C4 values, as these may not fall outside the typical range during active disease. Importantly, the onset of haemolysis was noted only after sepsis began to resolve, indicating CM-HUS initiated by infection rather than an underlying disorder.

For this patient, baseline complement assessments (C3/C4) and measurement of ADAMTS13 activity were conducted immediately prior to the initial PEX procedure. Additional complement evaluation was carried out 8 days following the last PEX and 4 days after administration of the first eculizumab dose. The decrease noted in alternative pathway activity most likely reflects therapeutic intervention rather than inherent complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS, formerly known as ‘atypical HUS,’ results from improper regulation of the alternative complement pathway, typically seen in individuals with an underlying genetic predisposition. Genetic alterations or autoantibodies impacting complement regulatory proteins—including factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are found in 50–70% of cases. Nevertheless, clinical onset is not universal among carriers; a secondary trigger such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or exposure to specific drugs is usually required for the disease to manifest.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus has rarely been shown to initiate complement activation, especially in vulnerable hosts. Of note, C. canimorsus varies in susceptibility to serum, with many strains readily affected by complement and some, particularly those with capsules, exhibiting partial resistance, underscoring complement function’s central role in host immunity. This scenario presents a clinical dilemma: while complement activation is important for pathogen elimination, in those genetically predisposed, it can result in excessive endothelial injury and TMA.

This case further supports the association between C. canimorsus infection and complement-mediated TMAs, building upon limited existing reports. For example, Bjørkto et al documented a patient with C. canimorsus-associated CM-HUS who carried a complement factor B variant and responded well to eculizumab therapy. In contrast, our patient did not possess any detected pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene mutations, highlighting the potential for CM-HUS to occur following infection even in the absence of an identifiable genetic complement disorder.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection presents distinct difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5, represents the standard therapy for CM-HUS and significantly enhances hematological and renal outcomes, particularly when administered promptly. Late initiation correlates with incomplete renal recovery and subsequently elevated likelihood of developing end-stage kidney disease.

Conversely, plasma exchange (PEX) is life-preserving in TTP, but confers only transient advantage in CM-HUS, chiefly by clearing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory molecules, or autoantibodies, and does not resolve the fundamental complement system defect. In the described patient, diuresis resumed following the final PEX treatments but before eculizumab was administered, indicating temporary stabilization. Prolonged hematologic and renal improvement was observed exclusively after complement inhibition.

An important therapeutic quandary involves administering complement blockade during active bacteremia, since terminal complement elements are indispensable for host defense against encapsulated organisms—including Neisseria and Capnocytophaga—thereby heightening apprehensions regarding infectious risks associated with eculizumab. In this instance, eculizumab was started once blood cultures were negative, thus reducing the chance of uncontrolled infection. Immunization, antibiotic prophylaxis, and vigilant observation remain crucial when utilizing complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition are key factors associated with positive outcomes. In this case, the patient attained haematological remission and partial improvement in kidney function, aligning with existing data that indicate renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab are primary prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus infection is exceptionally uncommon, this report underscores the need to consider infectious etiologies—including atypical organisms—in patients presenting with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and advances in molecular diagnostics may facilitate detection of additional instances.
