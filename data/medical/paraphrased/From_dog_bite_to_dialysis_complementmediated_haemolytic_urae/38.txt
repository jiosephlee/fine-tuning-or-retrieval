Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical conditions presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She subsequently developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which advanced to complete anuria and dependence on dialysis. Blood cultures grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus, confirming bacteraemia. Although her condition initially improved with antibiotic treatment and supportive care, she experienced sudden isolated haemolysis along with ongoing thrombocytopenia. Laboratory investigations and a renal biopsy identified a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Treatment with plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab resulted in haematological remission and partial recovery of renal function. This case underscores C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults and details the patient's diagnostic evaluation, the diagnostic challenges encountered, and the complexity of management in TMA following sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow-growing, gram-negative bacillus commonly present as part of the oropharyngeal flora in dogs and cats. Infections in humans are uncommon and may be acquired not only through bites and scratches but also from close contact with these animals. The clinical spectrum ranges considerably, with presentations spanning from isolated cases of meningitis to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although the organism is generally susceptible to a wide array of antibiotics, reported mortality rates may reach 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often associated with invasive zoonotic illness in humans. Because the pathogen is fastidious and requires extended incubation for culture growth, diagnosis may be delayed, thus necessitating increased use of advanced molecular diagnostics, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the case presented, infection with C. canimorsus manifested initially with septic shock, profound leukopaenia, and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. While the early phase was marked by sepsis involving multiple organ systems, subsequent development of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and progressive acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed toward concomitant TMA. This underscores that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can remain concealed during the initial presentation dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two previously uneventful pregnancies came to the emergency department due to an acutely deteriorating condition. She had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip four days before presentation and received prophylactic tetanus immunization. She was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) on the day of the bite but did not start the antibiotic because she was unable to obtain her prescription. Over the following days, she developed a high fever exceeding 40 °C, general malaise, decreased oral intake, and subsequently experienced numbness and discoloration in her feet, at which point she sought medical care. At a primary-care emergency facility, she was found to be hypoglycemic, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a lowest measured glucose of 0.6 mmol/L, which led to urgent referral to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and communicative, though she demonstrated signs of sepsis, including mottled and cool skin over the lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of similar illness among family members. Initial laboratory investigations indicated significant systemic dysfunction, including pronounced inflammatory response, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated hepatic transaminases, and coagulopathy. Arterial blood gases showed a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43), along with an increased lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). The patient persisted with hypoglycaemia in spite of administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, suspected to be secondary to sepsis. Upon presentation, she displayed sufficient urine output; urinalysis was notable for proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her clinical presentation—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury and lactic acidosis—was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) attributed to bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3). A surgical etiology for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded following both clinical assessment and imaging studies. Due to the seriousness of her case, empirical broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to provide coverage against dog-related pathogens, including Capnocytophaga species. Importantly, suspicion of C. canimorsus was present from the start and this organism was confirmed after seven days through both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring.

Hematology was brought in for consultation because of her pancytopenia and coagulopathy. The differential diagnosis included acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, while haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was entertained but considered less probable given the leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and low reticulocyte count. Flow cytometry was conducted, and bone marrow biopsy was considered; however, the absence of CD34+ blast expansion or aberrant phenotypes on flow cytometry prompted cancellation of the planned biopsy. Clinically, her features aligned with sepsis. In the interim, supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were administered while laboratory results were pending.

Upon admission to the ICU, the patient became completely anuric and experienced a marked and rapid decline in renal function. Intensive intravenous fluid administration in combination with loop diuretics was undertaken, but these interventions failed to improve her status. She later developed septic cardiomyopathy complicated by significant fluid overload, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune evaluations for antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) returned negative results.

By the third ICU day, the patient exhibited extensive cutaneous bleeding with the appearance of ecchymoses, along with peripheral cyanosis and ischemia impacting multiple toes and fingers; nevertheless, peripheral pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical features were interpreted as evidence of microthrombotic processes related to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). In spite of the involvement of multiple organ systems, the patient did not require vasopressor therapy except for stress-dose corticosteroids used during the early septic period. In addition to other transfused blood components, she was administered intravenous albumin.

Following eight days of inpatient care, the patient was moved to the nephrology ward due to dialysis-requiring acute kidney injury and for continued intravenous antibiotic treatment. At the time of transfer, she exhibited clinical stability, remained anuric, had diffuse edema and several hematomas, but was fully alert and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was stepped down to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently transitioned to oral Apocillin in accordance with the results from blood cultures.

During her stay on the nephrology service, laboratory studies demonstrated a declining inflammatory response, leukocytosis indicative of a reactive process, recovery of platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin concentration. Renal function testing continued to reveal azotemia, while coagulation studies returned to normal, suggesting improvement in the prior coagulopathy. Her laboratory data during this period are outlined in Table 2.

Despite an initial trend toward recovery, her clinical course altered as she experienced an abrupt, isolated decline in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over a six-day period) without clinical signs of bleeding or circulatory compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently decreased (30–40×10⁹/L). Haptoglobin became undetectable and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels increased, together reflecting active hemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, suggesting possible autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was commenced with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple transfusions were administered over the ensuing 2 days. As the haemolysis did not improve following corticosteroid therapy and platelet counts remained decreased (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional investigation was pursued. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, consistent with MAHA and indicative of TMA (figure 2). The initial positive DAT was probably spurious, potentially attributable to penicillin exposure or preceding transfusions; subsequent testing yielded negative results.

Complement evaluation and ADAMTS13 activity assessments were performed. Locally available tests for complement (C3/C4) showed values within the reference range, and functional studies of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways similarly revealed normal results. Shortly thereafter, ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, effectively excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP is usually characterized by activity below 10%. During the interval awaiting these analyses, plasma exchange (PEX) was begun as a temporizing measure prior to administration of eculizumab.

Following an increase in platelet count to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia with fibrin thrombi, and areas of crescent formation, findings that are characteristic of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Light microscopy findings, as depicted in figure 3, most clearly illustrate the diagnostic features observed in this case. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy results are not displayed; immunofluorescence did not identify immune complex deposits, and electron microscopy showed no indications of primary glomerular disease.

Infection with Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC-HUS) was excluded, as stool PCR testing yielded negative results.

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, the patient experienced a return of spontaneous urine output. Eculizumab was started for induction therapy after administration of the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (against serogroup B)—at a dosage of 900 mg intravenously once weekly for four weeks. Plans were made for the second vaccine doses to be given eight weeks later in the outpatient setting. Since the vaccines had not been given two weeks prior to initiating complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement testing was ordered from Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden) to exclude inherited complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). For reasons related to patient safety and logistical constraints, three PEX procedures utilizing Octaplas and a single induction dose of eculizumab occurred before sample collection. The functional complement assay assessed alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which was significantly diminished (6%); complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits, and antifactor H antibodies were not detected.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing extensive medical treatment, the patient is currently in satisfactory overall health, exhibiting sufficient urine output. However, her renal function remains impaired, as evidenced by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she will have ongoing follow-up through the nephrology outpatient service (see figure 4). The patient is continuing maintenance therapy with intravenous eculizumab at a dose of 1200 mg every two weeks, for a total of four months following the induction phase. Comprehensive genetic testing for complement-related abnormalities revealed no pathogenic variants; these results were relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, with the official written report still pending at the time of this report. In light of these findings, complement inhibition is being maintained due to the severe nature of her condition, with plans to reassess the possibility of discontinuation should her kidney function stabilize. Detection of pathogenic complement gene variants would support prolonged therapy and vigilant monitoring for recurrence, but such genetic changes were not present in this individual.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

When adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the primary clinical focus is to rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by a profound deficiency in ADAMTS13 (<10%). Tools such as the PLASMIC score assist in clinical decision-making while awaiting laboratory confirmation, which remains essential for definitive diagnosis. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, reflecting a low likelihood of TTP, and her ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, rendering TTP improbable and informing the direction of subsequent evaluations.

STEC-HUS represents another critical differential. This patient’s PCR testing for Shiga toxin was negative, and lack of an antecedent gastrointestinal illness alongside negative microbiological evidence ruled out STEC-HUS.

The combination of ongoing haemolysis, presence of schistocytes, dependence on dialysis for AKI, and renal biopsy findings consistent with TMA ultimately pointed to a diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). The diagnosis was reinforced by negative STEC PCR results and notable clinical response following initiation of eculizumab. A minimal past medical history diminishes the likelihood of a baseline complement abnormality necessitating a second insult, supporting the interpretation of infection-associated CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is established as a clinical and pathological diagnosis after ruling out other causes, and complement assays (C3/C4) can remain within normal limits throughout the clinical course. Crucially, the presence of normal C3 and C4 values does not rule out CM-HUS, as these measurements may not fall outside reference intervals at any time. In this case, hemolysis was detected only as the patient’s sepsis began to resolve, implying an infection-induced onset of CM-HUS rather than chronic disease.

For our patient, initial C3/C4 levels and ADAMTS13 activity were checked just prior to starting the first plasma exchange (PEX). Broader complement testing was conducted 8 days following the last PEX and 4 days after administration of the initial eculizumab dose. The reduction seen in the alternative pathway predominantly reflects the impact of treatment, rather than an underlying abnormality in complement activation or depletion.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ is a result of dysregulation within the alternative complement pathway and often occurs in individuals with an underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50% to 70% of cases exhibit genetic mutations or autoantibodies against complement regulatory proteins—including factor H, factor I, factor B, and membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nonetheless, the condition demonstrates incomplete penetrance; an external precipitant such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disorder, or specific drugs is usually necessary for clinical disease to develop.

Though uncommonly documented, Capnocytophaga canimorsus is capable of activating complement pathways, especially in hosts with heightened vulnerability. Many C. canimorsus isolates exhibit complement sensitivity, but certain encapsulated strains are partially resistant, highlighting the crucial role of complement in immune defense. This leads to a clinical dilemma: activation of complement is essential for eradicating the bacterium, yet in susceptible individuals, excessive activation may provoke uncontrolled endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The present report contributes to a limited but expanding number of cases linking C. canimorsus to complement-mediated TMAs. For example, Bjørkto et al documented a patient who developed CM-HUS secondary to C. canimorsus infection and carried a variant in complement factor B, achieving remission with eculizumab therapy. Unlike that previously reported individual, our patient lacked any pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene mutations, suggesting that infection alone was sufficient to elicit CM-HUS in the absence of detectable genetic complement abnormalities.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

The management of CM-HUS in the setting of ongoing infection involves particular difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against the terminal complement component 5, constitutes the standard treatment for CM-HUS and leads to significant improvements in both haematological and renal parameters, especially when administered promptly. Initiating therapy late correlates with incomplete renal recovery, thereby increasing the likelihood of developing end-stage kidney disease.

PEX, in contrast, is crucial for survival in TTP but yields only short-term benefits in CM-HUS, primarily by removing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies; however, it does not address the fundamental defect in the complement pathway. In the described patient, diuresis resumed following the last PEX treatments prior to eculizumab administration, demonstrating only brief stability. Lasting haematologic and renal improvement occurred only after complement inhibition was achieved.

A principal treatment challenge involves the use of complement inhibition in the presence of active bacteraemia, as the terminal complement components are essential for host defense against encapsulated microorganisms—including Neisseria and Capnocytophaga—prompting concerns about heightened infectious susceptibility with eculizumab. In this patient, eculizumab therapy commenced once blood cultures were negative, thereby reducing the risk of uncontrolled infection. Vaccination, prophylactic antimicrobials, and vigilant monitoring are imperative when employing complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely diagnosis of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition remain critical factors for positive outcomes. In this case, the patient attained hematologic remission and partial improvement in renal function, in line with existing data that indicate renal status and the interval to starting eculizumab are principal prognostic factors.

Despite the rarity of CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus, this report underscores the need to consider infectious causes—including atypical pathogens—in individuals presenting with new TMA. Enhanced recognition and use of molecular diagnostic methods may facilitate identification of additional cases.
