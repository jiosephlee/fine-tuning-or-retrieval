Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her clinical course was complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to total anuria and dependence on dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus as the causative organism. Although initial treatment with antibiotics and supportive measures led to some clinical stabilization, she subsequently experienced abrupt onset of isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Subsequent laboratory assessment and a renal biopsy revealed findings consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), resulting in a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). After receiving plasma exchange and beginning eculizumab therapy, she achieved haematological remission and experienced partial improvement in renal function. This report emphasizes that C. canimorsus can serve as a rare precipitant of CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults, and describes the patient’s diagnostic process, the difficulties encountered in establishing the diagnosis, and the intricacies of managing TMA in the context of sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow-growing, gram-negative bacillus commonly present in the normal oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Although infections in humans are infrequent, transmission may result from bites, scratches, or merely close interaction with these animals. The range of clinical manifestations is wide and includes isolated meningitis, rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Despite the organism's broad susceptibility to antibiotics, mortality rates may reach up to 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most commonly associated with severe zoonotic disease in humans. The pathogen's demanding growth requirements and slow proliferation in culture frequently lead to delays in identification, contributing to the rising application of molecular diagnostic tools such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the reported case, infection with C. canimorsus initially manifested as septic shock accompanied by marked leukopaenia and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. While sepsis-induced multiorgan dysfunction dominated the early course, subsequent development of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed toward an additional TMA, illustrating how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be concealed in the early stages by overlapping sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department experiencing rapidly deteriorating symptoms. Four days before admission, she sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and received a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed to her on the same day, she did not commence antibiotic therapy as she was unable to obtain her medication. Subsequently, she developed a fever exceeding 40 °C, general malaise, and decreased oral intake. She sought medical evaluation after experiencing numbness and discoloration in her feet. At the primary-care emergency facility, her blood glucose was found to be low and resistant to treatment with oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a minimum of 0.6 mmol/L, which resulted in her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and interactive, yet exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottled and cold lower limbs. She denied the use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of similar symptoms among her family members. Initial laboratory investigations indicated significant systemic disturbance characterized by severe inflammatory response, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated hepatic enzymes, and coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). The patient persisted with hypoglycaemia despite administration of large amounts of intravenous glucose, which was attributed to the underlying sepsis. Upon admission, urinary output was within normal limits; urinalysis was notable for proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

In light of her combination of symptoms—including elevated temperature, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—the clinical team interpreted her findings as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteremia and early sepsis, as indicated by a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. A potential surgical etiology for the mottling of her extremities, such as necrotizing fasciitis, was excluded based on both physical examination and imaging studies. Owing to the seriousness of her condition, empirical intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) therapy was initiated to provide coverage for organisms commonly associated with canine exposure, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was suspected from the beginning and, after 7 days, its presence was confirmed by both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was subsequently moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) to allow for more intensive observation.

Due to the pancytopenia and coagulopathy, hematology was consulted for further assessment. Their differential diagnosis considered acute leukemia and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH). Hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) was also evaluated but appeared less likely due to the presence of leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin levels, and an absence of reticulocyte compensation. Flow cytometry was performed and bone marrow biopsy was initially planned; however, as flow cytometry returned normal—with no evidence of increased CD34+ blasts or abnormal cell populations—the biopsy was subsequently cancelled. Clinically, her symptoms remained most consistent with sepsis. Supportive therapy, including transfusion of platelets and fibrinogen, was provided during the diagnostic workup.

Upon admission to the ICU, the patient developed complete anuria and experienced a rapid decline in renal function. Efforts to manage her condition through intensive fluid administration and simultaneous use of loop diuretics failed to yield improvement. Subsequently, she manifested signs of septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by significant fluid overload, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Evaluation for autoimmune disease with antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) returned negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, she exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding manifested as ecchymoses, along with cyanosis and ischemic changes at the distal ends of multiple toes and fingers; however, pulses and sensory function remained intact. These findings indicated microthrombotic complications in the setting of DIC. Although her illness affected multiple organ systems, she did not require vasopressor support apart from the stress-dose corticosteroids that were given during the initial phase of sepsis. Additionally, she was treated with albumin infusions and other blood products.

Following eight days of hospitalization, she was moved to the nephrology service due to dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and the need for continued intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, she was haemodynamically stable but remained anuric, exhibited generalized edema, and had multiple hematomas; however, she was fully alert and oriented. Her antibiotic regimen was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin, guided by blood culture results.

During her stay on the nephrology unit, laboratory assessments indicated a downward trend in inflammatory markers, a reactive leukocytosis, a recovery in platelet count, and stable hemoglobin levels. Renal parameters continued to demonstrate azotemia, but coagulation studies had normalized, consistent with resolution of the prior coagulopathy. Despite this initial progress, her condition evolved when she experienced an abrupt, isolated decline in hemoglobin concentration (from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days), in the absence of overt bleeding or any haemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts stayed consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin levels became undetectable, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) values increased, collectively indicative of continued haemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, was positive for IgG, which raised the possibility of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was administered with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were performed over a 2-day period. As corticosteroid treatment failed to halt the haemolytic process and platelet counts stayed low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional diagnostic workup was undertaken. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of MAHA and supportive of TMA diagnosis (figure 2). The initial DAT result was likely falsely positive due to penicillin use or earlier transfusions, since subsequent testing was negative.

Complement evaluation and ADAMTS13 activity were sent for assessment. Local standard complement tests (C3/C4) were within reference range, and functional testing of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways was also unremarkable. ADAMTS13 activity returned soon after at 43.5%, essentially excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP generally manifests with levels under 10%. Pending these findings, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as interim management ahead of administering the initial dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and areas of crescent formation. These findings were compatible with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Results from immunofluorescence and electron microscopy are not included, since immunofluorescence did not demonstrate any immune complex deposits, and electron microscopy failed to show signs indicative of primary glomerulopathy.

A stool PCR assay was negative, thereby excluding Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS).

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, the patient’s spontaneous urine output resumed. Eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg IV weekly for four weeks) was started following administration of the initial doses of Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (serogroup B) meningococcal vaccines. The second vaccine doses were scheduled for eight weeks later to be given in the outpatient setting. Because the vaccines had not been provided at least two weeks prior to initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement testing was ordered from Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden, to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Due to practical and safety concerns, the patient underwent three PEX sessions with Octaplas and received one induction dose of eculizumab prior to sample collection. The functional complement assessment revealed severely diminished alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50 at 6%), with normal levels of complement factors H, I, and B, and negative antifactor H antibodies.

Outcome and follow-up:

After receiving extensive medical care, the patient is currently in stable overall health and maintains sufficient urine production. Renal function, however, is still compromised (serum creatinine measured at 175 µmol/L), and she is scheduled for ongoing follow-up in the nephrology outpatient department (figure 4). She remains on maintenance eculizumab therapy (1200 mg intravenously every two weeks), continuing for four months after the initial induction phase. Comprehensive complement genetic analysis did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; the laboratory conveyed the results verbally, with a formal written report awaited at the time of this submission. Given these findings, complement inhibition treatment is being maintained in light of the severity of her condition, with plans to reconsider its discontinuation should her renal function reach a stable state. If a pathogenic complement gene alteration had been found, this would support prolonged therapy and careful monitoring for disease recurrence. In this patient, however, no such mutation was identified.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adults who present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, it is essential to promptly rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Clinical scoring systems such as PLASMIC can assist in stratifying risk while awaiting definitive laboratory assessment, but confirmation relies on laboratory findings. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7 (indicating a low likelihood for TTP) and her ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, making TTP an improbable diagnosis and guiding the subsequent investigative approach.

STEC-HUS must also be considered. However, polymerase chain reaction testing for Shiga toxin was negative, and the lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms combined with negative microbiological results made STEC-HUS unlikely.

Ultimately, persistent hemolysis, ongoing schistocytosis, dependence on dialysis for AKI, and renal biopsy demonstrating TMA were consistent with a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). This was further substantiated by negative PCR for STEC and a rapid clinical response to eculizumab. Her minimal past medical history does not support the presence of a pre-existing complement defect necessitating a ‘second hit,’ and instead favors infection-related CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is diagnosed based on clinical and pathological findings after ruling out other causes, and C3 and C4 complement levels can remain within normal limits throughout the illness. Consequently, normal C3 and C4 values do not rule out CM-HUS, since complement concentrations may stay within reference intervals during the course of the disorder. Additionally, the onset of haemolysis after the improvement of sepsis supports a diagnosis of infection-induced CM-HUS rather than a previously established condition.

For this patient, standard complement assays (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were measured immediately prior to the initial PEX treatment. Extended complement studies were conducted eight days after completing PEX and four days following the first administration of eculizumab. The decrease noted in the alternative complement pathway most likely reflects effects from therapy rather than depletion or activation inherent to the complement system itself.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ results from aberrant activation of the alternative complement pathway, often in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of affected patients possess genetic alterations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulators, including factors H, I, and B, or the membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nonetheless, the expression of disease is variable, with overt clinical symptoms usually necessitating an external precipitating event, such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or certain pharmaceuticals.

While infrequently described, Capnocytophaga canimorsus has been shown to trigger complement activation, particularly in vulnerable populations. Importantly, this organism exhibits diverse responses to serum: many strains are susceptible to complement-mediated killing, whereas some capsule-positive isolates demonstrate partial evasion, signifying that complement function is central to host protection. This duality creates a clinical challenge, as robust complement activation not only helps eradicate the microbe but may also induce unrestrained endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in predisposed hosts.

The present case augments a limited but expanding collection of reports that associate C. canimorsus infection with complement-mediated TMAs. For instance, Bjørkto et al detailed a patient who developed CM-HUS following C. canimorsus infection and carried a complement factor B mutation, ultimately achieving remission with eculizumab therapy. Unlike that instance, our patient was not found to carry any pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement genes, which supports a diagnosis of infection-provoked CM-HUS occurring without detectable genetic complement dysregulation.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS during ongoing infection introduces specific complexities. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against terminal complement component 5, remains the established treatment for CM-HUS, offering significant improvement in both hematologic and renal parameters, especially when started promptly. A delay in starting therapy correlates with partial renal recovery and an increased likelihood of progression to end-stage kidney disease.

PEX, while critical in TTP management, yields only temporary effects in CM-HUS, predominantly by clearing circulating complement fragments, pro-inflammatory molecules, or autoantibodies, but it does not rectify the fundamental complement pathway abnormality. In the case described, the patient's diuresis resumed following completion of PEX sessions and prior to administration of eculizumab, indicative of only short-term stabilization. Persistent improvement in hematologic and renal function was observed exclusively after complement inhibition was achieved.

A central therapeutic concern is the initiation of complement inhibition during active bloodstream infection, since terminal complement components are essential for the immune defense against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, raising the risk of eculizumab-related infections. Here, eculizumab was commenced once blood cultures confirmed clearance, thereby reducing the possibility of worsening infection. When utilizing complement inhibitors, vaccination, prophylactic antimicrobials, and vigilant monitoring are critical safeguards.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition are key factors in achieving a positive prognosis. In this case, the patient reached hematological remission along with partial improvement of renal function, which aligns with prevailing data indicating that both baseline renal status and the interval to eculizumab initiation significantly impact outcomes.

Despite the rarity of C. canimorsus-associated CM-HUS, this report underscores the need to evaluate for infectious etiologies, including uncommon pathogens, in patients exhibiting new TMA. Enhanced recognition and the use of molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate discovery of additional cases of this nature.
