Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after being bitten by a dog. Her clinical course was marked by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete anuria and the need for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus as the causative pathogen. Although she showed initial improvement with antimicrobial therapy and supportive measures, she subsequently developed sudden, isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia. Further laboratory analysis and a renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). After receiving plasma exchange and starting eculizumab therapy, the patient achieved haematological remission and experienced partial renal recovery. This report emphasizes the uncommon role of C. canimorsus infection in triggering CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults and reviews the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the difficulties encountered in distinguishing between potential causes, and the intricate nature of the disease course and treatment strategies in TMA that develops after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a gram-negative, encapsulated rod that grows slowly and constitutes part of the typical oropharyngeal microbiota in dogs and cats. Though uncommon, human infection may be acquired not only through bites and scratches but also through mere proximity or contact with these animals. Manifestations in affected individuals vary widely and may include isolated cases of meningitis, rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organs, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Despite the organism’s sensitivity to many antibiotics, the mortality rate remains as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often associated with invasive zoonotic infections in humans. Identification of the pathogen may be delayed due to its demanding culture requirements and slow growth, leading to the increased adoption of molecular diagnostic approaches, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the case presented, infection with C. canimorsus initially manifested with septic shock, marked leukopaenia, and coagulopathy consistent with DIC. While sepsis-related involvement of multiple organ systems predominated during the early clinical course, subsequent findings of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated the onset of a concomitant TMA. This progression emphasizes how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be concealed during the initial phases dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with two prior uncomplicated pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department due to a rapidly deteriorating clinical state. Four days before admission, her dog bit her upper lip, after which she received a tetanus vaccination for prevention. On the day of the bite, she was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin), but she did not start antibiotic therapy because she was unable to retrieve the medication. Subsequently, she developed a high fever (>40 °C), felt unwell, and had decreased oral intake. She sought medical care after experiencing numbness and discoloration in her feet. In the primary-care emergency setting, she was found to have hypoglycemia that did not respond to oral glucose or glucagon, with her blood glucose reaching a low point of 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and cooperative, though she exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottled and cool skin over the lower limbs. She reported no use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of illness or similar symptoms among her family members. Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated pronounced systemic disturbance characterized by intense inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated liver enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas testing indicated metabolic acidosis with a pH measured at 7.27 (reference: 7.35–7.43), alongside an increased lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference: <2.5 mmol/L). The patient continued to experience hypoglycaemia in spite of substantial intravenous glucose administration, suspected to be secondary to sepsis. On presentation, diuresis was sufficient; urinalysis detected protein (2+) and blood (3+).

Her array of symptoms—fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—was assessed as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3). After clinical assessment and imaging, necrotising fasciitis and other surgical etiologies for her mottled extremities were excluded. Owing to the severity of her clinical status, empirical intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated for coverage of organisms commonly transmitted by dogs, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was an early consideration and was subsequently confirmed by both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures after 7 days. The patient was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for heightened surveillance.

A haematology consult was sought due to her pancytopenia and coagulopathy. The potential diagnoses included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also evaluated but deemed improbable because of leukopaenia, raised inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin levels, and an absence of compensatory reticulocyte response. Flow cytometric analysis was performed and a bone marrow biopsy was scheduled; however, the latter was cancelled when flow cytometry revealed no elevated CD34+ blast population or aberrant cell phenotypes. Clinically, her findings supported a diagnosis of sepsis. Pending additional diagnostic information, supportive transfusions of thrombocytes and fibrinogen were provided.

Following her admission to the ICU, the patient became completely anuric with a marked and rapid decline in renal function. Efforts at aggressive fluid replacement and simultaneous loop diuretic therapy proved ineffective. She went on to develop septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by fluid overload, making continuous renal replacement therapy necessary. Autoimmune screening, including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) assays, returned negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, she experienced widespread skin hemorrhage with the appearance of ecchymoses, as well as distal cyanosis and ischemic changes involving multiple fingers and toes, although peripheral pulses and sensation remained intact. These signs were compatible with microthrombotic processes occurring as part of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Notwithstanding multisystem involvement, vasopressor agents were not required except for stress-dose corticosteroids initiated during the early septic phase. In addition to blood product support, she was administered albumin infusions.

Following an 8-day hospital stay, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit due to sustained dialysis-requiring acute kidney injury (AKI) and the need for intravenous antibiotic management. Upon arrival, she was hemodynamically stable but had no urine output, exhibited widespread edema, and had multiple bruises, though she was conscious and fully oriented. Based on blood culture findings, antibiotics were reduced to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin.

During her time on the nephrology ward, laboratory tests indicated a downward trend in inflammatory markers, a reactive leukocytosis, an upward trend in platelet count, and stable hemoglobin concentration. Renal function tests continued to show elevated nitrogenous waste, while coagulation studies returned to normal values, reflecting improvement in her coagulopathy. Despite initial clinical gains, her condition worsened when she experienced a sudden, isolated decline in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L within 6 days) in the absence of any apparent bleeding or circulatory compromise. Platelet counts stayed consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin levels became undetectable, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) increased, all consistent with active hemolysis (see Figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, returned positive for IgG, which raised the possibility of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was commenced with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a span of 2 days. Because haemolysis did not improve with corticosteroid treatment and the platelet count persisted at 41–46 × 10⁹/L, additional investigations were pursued. Examination of a peripheral blood smear revealed schistocytes in 15%–20% of cells, a finding indicative of MAHA and pointing towards TMA (figure 2). The original positive DAT likely represented a false positive, potentially attributable to penicillin exposure or previous transfusions, as subsequent testing yielded a negative result.

Complement studies and ADAMTS13 activity were requested. Locally performed complement assessments (C3/C4) showed values within normal ranges, and functional evaluation of classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways demonstrated no abnormalities. ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently measured at 43.5%, thereby excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP is characterized by activity less than 10%. While awaiting laboratory results, plasma exchange (PEX) was initiated as interim treatment before administration of the first dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was undertaken, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, findings that are indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The most illustrative diagnostic evidence in this instance was observed in the light microscopy images provided in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy results are not depicted; immunofluorescence did not detect immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy found no indication of primary glomerulopathy.

Infection-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome caused by Shiga toxin-producing E. coli (STEC-HUS) was excluded based on a negative stool PCR test.

After the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), the patient resumed spontaneous urine output. Eculizumab induction therapy was started (900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks) following the initial administration of the meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (covering serogroup B). The second vaccine doses were scheduled for eight weeks later on an outpatient basis. Since the vaccines had not been provided at least two weeks before initiating complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was administered.

Comprehensive complement assays to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) were sent to Skåne Laboratory at Lund University in Sweden. To address logistical challenges and ensure patient safety, three sessions of PEX using Octaplas and one induction dose of eculizumab were completed prior to sample collection. Functional complement evaluations included quantitative alternative pathway activity (AH50), which was significantly decreased at 6%, while levels of complement factors H, I, and B remained within normal limits, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intricate medical management, the patient exhibits overall good health and satisfactory urine production. Renal function, however, remains impaired, as evidenced by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L. She is scheduled for ongoing follow-up in the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). Eculizumab therapy is maintained at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, continuing for four months after induction. Comprehensive complement genetic analysis failed to reveal any pathogenic mutations; the results were delivered verbally by the reference laboratory, although the official written report was not yet available when this case was submitted. In light of these findings, complement inhibition therapy is being sustained due to the seriousness of her clinical presentation, with plans to reevaluate its necessity if kidney function stabilizes. Discovery of pathogenic complement gene alterations would support extended therapy with diligent surveillance for recurrence, but no such variants were detected in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

The primary concern when adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by a marked deficiency of ADAMTS13 (<10%). While clinical scoring tools such as PLASMIC can assist in stratifying patients during the interim, definitive diagnosis relies on laboratory results. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood for TTP, and ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, which further decreases the probability of TTP and informs subsequent diagnostic pathways.

STEC-HUS was also considered in the differential diagnosis. Shiga toxin PCR testing for the patient was negative. The lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and negative laboratory testing do not support STEC-HUS.

The combination of ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, dialysis-requiring AKI, and kidney biopsy findings indicative of TMA pointed towards a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). Excluding STEC infection by PCR and the patient’s prompt improvement following initiation of eculizumab reinforced the diagnosis. Furthermore, the patient's minimal medical history diminishes the likelihood of a pre-existing complement abnormality needing an additional trigger and favors infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is established as a clinicopathological diagnosis once other causes have been excluded, and complement components C3 and C4 may remain within normal limits throughout the clinical course. Consequently, CM-HUS cannot be ruled out solely based on normal C3/C4 values, since these levels may not fall outside standard parameters. In this case, the onset of haemolysis occurred only after the patient's sepsis began to resolve, implicating infection-related CM-HUS as opposed to a pre-existing condition.

For this patient, initial tests for C3, C4, and ADAMTS13 activity were collected immediately prior to the first session of plasma exchange (PEX). Extended complement assays were conducted eight days following completion of the final PEX session and four days subsequent to the initial administration of eculizumab. The decrease observed in alternative pathway activity likely reflects effects of therapy rather than being indicative of an underlying defect or excessive activation of complement.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS (previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS’) results from improper regulation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently affecting individuals with genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50% to 70% of affected patients possess genetic mutations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory proteins—including factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nevertheless, the penetrance of these abnormalities is not absolute, with clinical symptoms generally emerging after exposure to an external trigger, such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or certain drugs.

Although uncommon, C. canimorsus is capable of complement pathway activation, especially in vulnerable patients. C. canimorsus demonstrates varied sensitivity to serum; while most isolates are complement-sensitive, some encapsulated variants exhibit partial resistance, underscoring the critical role of complement in immune defense. This leads to a clinical dilemma: activation of complement is necessary to eradicate the pathogen, but in individuals with underlying susceptibility, this response may also provoke excessive endothelial injury and TMA.

The present case contributes to limited but increasing evidence associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMA. Bjørkto et al documented a representative instance involving CM-HUS precipitated by C. canimorsus in a patient carrying a factor B complement variant, who responded to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that report, our patient did not have any detected pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene alterations, suggesting infection-induced CM-HUS without identifiable inherited complement dysregulation.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection presents distinct difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody against terminal complement component 5, is recognized as first-line treatment for CM-HUS and significantly enhances hematological and renal outcomes, especially when administered promptly. Delays in starting therapy are linked to partial renal recovery and increased likelihood of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

PEX, while vital in TTP, offers only temporary improvement in CM-HUS by removing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies but does not address the fundamental complement dysfunction. In our case, diuresis resumed following the final sessions of PEX but prior to eculizumab administration, indicative of only a short-term stabilization. Lasting hematological and renal recovery was observed only after initiation of complement blockade.

An important therapeutic consideration is implementing complement blockade during the presence of bacteremia, since terminal complement components play a vital role in protection against encapsulated bacteria, such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, highlighting concerns regarding infection risks associated with eculizumab. Here, eculizumab was introduced once blood cultures had cleared, thereby reducing the danger of uncontrolled infection. It remains imperative to provide vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and diligent surveillance when prescribing complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition remain critical factors influencing positive clinical outcomes. In this case, the patient experienced normalization of hematological parameters and partial restoration of kidney function, which aligns with existing literature indicating that renal status and the interval prior to starting eculizumab treatment principally affect prognosis.

While CM-HUS due to C. canimorsus infection is exceptionally uncommon, this report underscores the necessity to consider infectious precipitants—including rare pathogens—in individuals presenting with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and molecular diagnostic methods may facilitate detection of additional cases.
