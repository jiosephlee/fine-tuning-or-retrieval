Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no significant medical history presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She subsequently developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete anuria necessitating dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia. Although initial management with antibiotics and supportive therapy resulted in some clinical improvement, she experienced abrupt, isolated hemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia. Laboratory testing and a kidney biopsy demonstrated features of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and a diagnosis of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) was made. After undergoing plasma exchange and beginning eculizumab therapy, hematological remission was attained, and partial renal recovery ensued. This report illustrates C. canimorsus infection as an unusual precipitant of CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults and describes the patient's diagnostic evaluation, the diagnostic complexities encountered, and the challenges inherent in the management of TMA post-sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative rod that grows slowly and is commonly found as part of the normal oral flora in dogs and cats. Although infections in humans are uncommon, transmission may occur through animal bites, scratches, or mere proximity. The spectrum of clinical manifestations is wide, including presentations from meningitis alone to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Even though this organism is generally susceptible to a broad range of antibiotics, the mortality rate can be as high as 30%.

Among the Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often associated with invasive zoonotic disease in people. Its diagnosis is often delayed because of its demanding growth requirements and slow replication in laboratory cultures, leading to increased use of molecular diagnostic approaches, including real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the case described, the infection with C. canimorsus initially presented as septic shock, pronounced leukopaenia, and coagulopathy characteristic of DIC. While early clinical progression was dominated by organ involvement secondary to sepsis, subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), declining platelet counts, and exacerbation of acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed toward an added TMA. This underscores the possibility that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) might go unrecognized during the initial phase marked by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with two past pregnancies without complications, arrived at the emergency department in the midst of a rapidly deteriorating condition. She had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip four days earlier and had received a tetanus vaccine as preventive measure. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed at the time, but she did not begin antibiotics, as she was unable to retrieve her medication. Subsequently, she experienced a significant fever (>40 °C), general malaise, decreased oral intake, and sought care after developing numbness and discoloration in her feet. In the primary-care emergency setting, she was noted to have hypoglycemia, which was resistant to both oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a lowest recorded value of 0.6 mmol/L. This finding led to her urgent referral to the emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and communicative, but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness of the lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal medications or other substances, and there was no history of similar illnesses among her family members. Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated significant systemic abnormalities, including pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated hepatic transaminases, and impaired coagulation. Arterial blood gas analysis showed metabolic acidosis, with a pH measured at 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, she persisted in a hypoglycaemic state, presumed secondary to sepsis. Upon arrival, urine output was sufficient; urinalysis detected proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Considering the combination of fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy along with acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis, her clinical picture was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis, indicated by a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. A surgical etiology for the mottling of her extremities—including necrotising fasciitis—was excluded following both clinical examination and radiologic assessment. Owing to the severity of her symptoms, empirical intravenous therapy with broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to cover organisms associated with canine exposure, such as Capnocytophaga species. Importantly, C. canimorsus was suspected from the outset, and was subsequently confirmed after seven days through both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive monitoring.

Because of pancytopenia and coagulopathy, haematology was involved for further evaluation. Differential diagnoses included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Although haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also considered, it was deemed unlikely given leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and lack of compensatory reticulocytosis. Flow cytometry was performed and a bone marrow biopsy was planned, but results showed no increase in CD34+ blasts or abnormal phenotypes, resulting in cancellation of the biopsy. The overall clinical scenario was most consistent with sepsis. Pending further diagnostic results, the patient received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Following her admission to the intensive care unit, the patient ceased producing urine entirely and her renal function deteriorated rapidly. Attempts at managing this included vigorous intravenous fluid administration together with loop diuretics, both of which were ineffective. She then developed septic cardiomyopathy complicated by volume overload, necessitating continuous renal replacement therapy. Evaluation for autoimmune disease, including testing for antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, she began exhibiting widespread cutaneous bleeding, ecchymoses, and cyanotic-ischemic changes of the distal phalanges of several toes and fingers. Nevertheless, peripheral pulses and sensation remained intact. These clinical features were consistent with microvascular thrombosis due to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Despite multiple organ systems being affected, she never required vasopressor therapy except for stress-dose steroids administered during the early phase of sepsis. She also received infusions of albumin in addition to other blood components.

Following an 8-day hospital stay, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit due to dialysis-requiring acute kidney injury (AKI) and continued intravenous antibiotics. Upon admission, she was haemodynamically stable but remained anuric, exhibited widespread oedema and multiple bruises, yet was fully conscious and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, later transitioning to oral Apocillin, guided by blood culture findings.

While on the nephrology service, laboratory assessment indicated a reduction in inflammatory markers, reactive leukocytosis, recovery in platelet count, and stable haemoglobin levels. Tests of renal function continued to demonstrate persistent azotaemia, but coagulation studies had normalised, consistent with resolution of coagulopathy. After initial clinical progress, her condition changed with a sudden, isolated decline in haemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L across 6 days, in the absence of overt bleeding or haemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained low at 30–40×10⁹/L; haptoglobin was undetectable, and LDH concentrations increased, all pointing to ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT, also known as the Coombs test) yielded a positive result for IgG, indicating the possibility of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was commenced together with standard prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over a two-day period. Due to persistent haemolysis despite corticosteroid treatment and ongoing thrombocytopenia (platelet counts ranging from 41 to 46 × 10⁹/L), additional investigations were performed. Examination of the peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, which is indicative of MAHA and suggestive of TMA (see figure 2). The initial DAT result was most likely falsely positive, possibly attributed to penicillin exposure or previous transfusions; subsequent repeat testing returned negative results.

Complement assays and ADAMTS13 activity were requested. The routinely available complement tests (C3/C4) fell within normal ranges, and functional tests of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways were also unremarkable. ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently measured at 43.5%, excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP generally occurs with activities less than 10%. While these investigations were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was begun as an interim therapy prior to administration of the initial dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic changes in the glomeruli with the presence of fibrin thrombi, and some areas with crescent formation, all consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The most informative diagnostic evidence was provided by the light microscopy images depicted in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy results are not displayed; immunofluorescence did not identify any immune complex deposits, and electron microscopy did not find features suggestive of a primary glomerular disorder.

Stool PCR testing was negative, thereby excluding the diagnosis of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS).

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, the patient began to pass urine spontaneously once again. Following initial administration of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (serogroup B)—eculizumab was started for induction therapy at a dosage of 900 mg intravenously per week over a four-week period. Plans were made to administer the second vaccine doses as an outpatient after 8 weeks. Since the vaccines had not been given at least two weeks before the initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement assessment to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was requested from the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. Due to logistical factors and to maintain patient safety, three plasma exchange sessions using Octaplas were carried out, and one dose of induction eculizumab was administered prior to obtaining laboratory samples. Functional complement testing incorporated quantitative AH50 to evaluate alternative pathway activity, which was significantly decreased at 6%. Complement factors H, I, and B demonstrated normal concentrations, and antifactor H antibodies were not detected.

Outcome and follow-up:

After intensive medical management, the patient exhibits a stable general status with sufficient urine production. Renal function remains impaired, indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she is scheduled for ongoing care in the nephrology outpatient setting (figure 4). Maintenance therapy with eculizumab (1200 mg IV every 2 weeks) continues for 4 months following induction. Comprehensive complement genetic analysis did not reveal any disease-causing variants; these findings were provided verbally by the reference laboratory, with the official written documentation pending at the time of manuscript submission. Given these findings and the severity of her illness, complement inhibition therapy is being maintained, with future evaluation for possible cessation if kidney function stabilises. Detection of pathogenic complement gene mutations would support a prolonged treatment course and vigilant surveillance for recurrence, but no such variants were identified in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adults who present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, it is essential to first rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Tools such as the PLASMIC score are useful for preliminary assessment while waiting for definitive laboratory results. For this patient, a PLASMIC score of 4/7 indicated a low likelihood of TTP, and the measured ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, making TTP an improbable diagnosis and informing subsequent evaluation.

STEC-HUS must also be considered in the differential. PCR testing for Shiga toxin was negative in this patient. The lack of antecedent gastrointestinal symptoms and absence of microbiological evidence support the exclusion of STEC-HUS.

In summary, ongoing haemolysis, presence of schistocytes, dependence on dialysis due to AKI, and kidney biopsy findings indicative of TMA led to a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). The diagnosis was further substantiated by negative PCR testing for STEC and marked clinical improvement following the initiation of eculizumab. Given her minimal medical history, the possibility of a pre-existing complement abnormality necessitating a ‘second hit’ is unlikely, instead favoring an infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS represents a clinical and pathological diagnosis of exclusion, and complement components C3 and C4 can be within normal ranges throughout the illness. Therefore, normal C3 and C4 levels do not rule out CM-HUS, since these values may remain unchanged during the condition’s progression. In this case, haemolysis became apparent only after the resolution of sepsis, indicating that the CM-HUS was likely precipitated by infection rather than being a pre-existing disorder.

For this patient, initial complement assays (C3 and C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were measured directly prior to beginning plasma exchange (PEX). Additional, more comprehensive complement analysis was conducted eight days after completion of PEX and four days following the first administration of eculizumab. The detected decrease in the alternative complement pathway is best attributed to effects of therapy rather than to underlying complement pathway activation or deficiency.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ is the result of inappropriate activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently occurring in individuals with an underlying genetic susceptibility. Genetic mutations or the presence of autoantibodies that impair complement regulators—including factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are detected in approximately 50–70% of cases. Nonetheless, not all individuals with these risk factors will develop disease, as clinical onset generally requires an inciting factor such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disorder, or exposure to specific drugs.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus, while infrequently documented, is capable of stimulating complement pathways, particularly in vulnerable hosts. The organism exhibits diverse sensitivity to serum: many strains are easily neutralized by complement activity, but those expressing a capsule may evade destruction to some extent, underscoring the role of complement in host defense. This presents a clinical dilemma, as activation of complement can facilitate pathogen elimination yet also precipitate uncontrolled endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in predisposed patients.

This report expands the limited but increasing literature that connects C. canimorsus infection to complement-mediated TMAs. Bjørkto et al previously detailed a case in which CM-HUS, associated with C. canimorsus and a complement factor B mutation, responded favorably to eculizumab therapy. Differing from their findings, our investigation did not reveal any pathogenic or likely pathogenic genetic variants within complement-related genes in the patient, supporting a scenario of infection-associated CM-HUS that arose in the absence of a detectable inherited complement abnormality.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS during an ongoing infection poses distinctive difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against the terminal complement protein C5, represents the primary treatment for CM-HUS and significantly enhances hematological and renal outcomes, especially when administered promptly. Initiation of therapy later in the course has been correlated with partial renal recovery and an increased likelihood of progression to end-stage kidney disease.

Conversely, plasma exchange (PEX) is life-preserving in TTP but yields only transient improvement in CM-HUS by eliminating circulating complement components, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, without addressing the fundamental complement dysfunction. In the present case, urine output resumed following the last PEX session, prior to eculizumab administration, indicating a brief stabilization. Lasting hematological and renal improvement was achieved only after complement inhibition was established.

An important therapeutic challenge is employing complement inhibition during active bacteremia, since terminal complement elements are essential for defense against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, raising concern about infection risks related to eculizumab. In the scenario described, eculizumab was started after blood cultures had cleared, thereby reducing the danger of unchecked infection. When using complement inhibitors, vaccination, prophylactic antimicrobials, and vigilant observation remain imperative.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition represent key factors associated with positive prognosis. In this case, the patient attained haematological remission along with partial restoration of renal function, aligning with existing data which indicate that baseline renal function and the interval before starting eculizumab treatment are primary prognostic factors.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus infection is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of evaluating infectious precipitants, even rare ones, in individuals who develop new TMA. Greater recognition and the application of molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate detection of additional cases of this nature.
