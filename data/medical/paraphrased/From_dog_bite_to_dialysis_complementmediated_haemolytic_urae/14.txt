Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no significant prior medical history presented with rapidly worsening sepsis 4 days after sustaining a dog bite. Her clinical course was complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, eventually resulting in complete anuria and requirement for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she showed initial improvement after receiving antibiotics and supportive therapy, she subsequently experienced abrupt onset of isolated haemolysis and sustained thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory studies and findings on renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Treatment with plasma exchange and eculizumab led to haematological recovery and partial restoration of renal function. This case underscores the unusual occurrence of C. canimorsus infection as a precipitating factor for CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, and describes the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the complexities inherent in distinguishing among potential diagnoses, and the challenging clinical management of TMA following sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow-growing gram-negative bacillus that exists as part of the normal oral flora in both dogs and cats. Infections in humans are uncommon, and the organism may be transmitted through bites, scratches, or close proximity to animals. Clinical manifestations can range widely, from isolated meningitis to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiple organ dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although the organism is generally sensitive to a broad spectrum of antibiotics, case fatality rates may reach up to 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in severe zoonotic disease in humans. The diagnostic process may be prolonged due to its exacting growth requirements and sluggish propagation in culture media, leading to greater reliance on molecular techniques such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In our report, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock, pronounced leukopenia, and a coagulopathy consistent with DIC. While the initial stage was notable for sepsis with multisystem involvement, subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and aggravated acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated an additional TMA process. This progression demonstrates that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be masked by the initial presentation with sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department due to rapidly deteriorating health. She had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip four days before admission and subsequently received a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Although she was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) at the time of the incident, she was unable to pick up the medication, and thus no antibiotics were started. She subsequently experienced a high fever (over 40 °C), general malaise, poor oral intake, and ultimately presented to medical care upon developing numbness and discoloration in her feet. At a primary-care emergency facility, she was noted to have hypoglycemia that was resistant to oral glucose and glucagon, with her lowest recorded blood glucose measured at 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating an urgent referral to our emergency department.

Upon presentation, the patient was conscious and interactive but demonstrated signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness in the lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal supplements or illicit substances, and there was no family history of similar complaints. Initial laboratory tests showed pronounced systemic abnormalities characterized by significant inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas assessment indicated a metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.27 (reference range: 7.35–7.43), and an increased lactate concentration at 5.7 mmol/L (reference <2.5 mmol/L). Persistent hypoglycemia was noted despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, attributed to sepsis. Upon arrival, the patient continued to produce urine in sufficient amounts; urinalysis revealed proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

In light of her clinical features—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—her illness was assessed as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) due to bacteraemia and the early stages of sepsis (qSOFA score 1/3). Following both clinical assessment and imaging studies, a surgical source for her mottled limbs, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded. Owing to the significant severity of her case, empirical treatment with broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated, targeting pathogens associated with dogs, such as Capnocytophaga species. Suspicion for C. canimorsus was raised early, and this was subsequently confirmed on day 7 via both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for intensified observation.

Haematology was brought in due to the observed pancytopenia and coagulopathy. Their differential diagnosis comprised acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also considered, but factors such as leukopaenia, levels of inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and a non-compensatory reticulocyte response made this less likely. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted, and a bone marrow biopsy was initially planned; however, as flow cytometry results revealed no increase in CD34+ blasts or abnormal cellular phenotypes, the biopsy was ultimately deferred. The clinical picture remained in keeping with sepsis. During this period, and while awaiting further diagnostic clarification, the patient received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Upon admission to the intensive care unit, the patient became entirely anuric, with her renal function declining rapidly. Attempts were made to restore kidney function through vigorous fluid resuscitation combined with loop diuretics, but these interventions were ineffective. She went on to develop septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by fluid overload, necessitating the initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy. Testing for autoimmune markers, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, the patient exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding with ecchymosis and the emergence of cyanotic, ischaemic changes at the distal portions of multiple toes and fingers; however, peripheral pulses and tactile sensation remained intact. These clinical signs were indicative of microthrombotic processes associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although she developed involvement of multiple organ systems, she did not require vasopressors apart from stress-dose corticosteroids given during the early phase of sepsis. The patient was also administered albumin in addition to various blood components.

Following eight days of inpatient care, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit due to ongoing dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and the need for intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival, she was haemodynamically stable but exhibited complete absence of urine output, diffuse swelling, and multiple bruises, though she was alert and cognitively intact. In light of blood culture findings, antibiotic treatment was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, which was subsequently switched to oral Apocillin.

During her stay in the nephrology ward, laboratory tests demonstrated a decrease in inflammatory markers, a reactive rise in leukocyte count, recovery of platelet numbers, and stable haemoglobin levels. Renal assessments revealed continued azotaemia, whereas coagulation studies had returned to normal, signifying an improvement in her prior coagulopathy. The patient's course subsequently shifted after this initial progress; she experienced an acute, isolated decline in haemoglobin concentration (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days) without clinical or laboratory evidence of bleeding or circulatory compromise. Platelet counts remained low at 30–40×10⁹/L, with haptoglobin levels becoming undetectable and a concomitant increase in LDH, all consistent with ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) yielded a positive result for IgG, indicating the possibility of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was administered in conjunction with standard prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over a period of 2 days. Because the haemolytic activity persisted despite corticosteroid treatment and platelet counts remained low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional assessment was necessary. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, findings indicative of MAHA and raising suspicion for TMA (see figure 2). The initial positive DAT was probably spurious, caused by penicillin exposure or previous transfusions, as a follow-up DAT returned negative.

Complement panels and ADAMTS13 activity were requested for evaluation. Standard complement assessments available locally (C3 and C4) fell within reference ranges, and function-based evaluations of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways were also normal. Subsequently, the ADAMTS13 activity result was reported as 43.5%, which excludes a diagnosis of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) since TTP is generally associated with activity levels below 10%. In the interim, while these laboratory results were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as an interim treatment before administering the initial eculizumab dose.

Following an increase in the platelet count to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was carried out, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic changes in glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and areas of crescent formation—findings compatible with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The principal diagnostic features in this case were illustrated in the light microscopy images provided in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy findings are not depicted, as immunofluorescence testing showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy did not reveal signs of a primary glomerulopathy.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was ruled out on the basis of a negative stool PCR analysis.

After the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), the patient experienced a return of spontaneous urine output. Eculizumab induction therapy was started (900 mg intravenously every week for four weeks) after the first administrations of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B). The schedule for the second doses of these vaccines was set for eight weeks later in an outpatient setting. Since the vaccines had not been given at least two weeks prior to beginning complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was administered.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement analysis was referred to Skåne Laboratory at Lund University in Sweden. For patient safety and due to logistical factors, three PEX sessions using Octaplas were conducted and an initial dose of eculizumab administered before collecting the samples. Functional assessment of the complement system showed that alternative pathway activity (measured by quantitative AH50) was significantly diminished at 6%, whereas complement components H, I, and B were within normal limits and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical therapy, the patient remains clinically well and is producing an appropriate volume of urine. Renal function is still compromised, with a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she will continue to be monitored in the nephrology outpatient setting (see figure 4). The patient is receiving ongoing maintenance therapy with intravenous eculizumab at 1200 mg every two weeks, sustained for 4 months following induction. Extensive complement genetic analysis did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; these preliminary results were communicated verbally by the reference laboratory, with the official written documentation pending at the time of manuscript preparation. In light of her severe clinical course and current findings, complement inhibition is being maintained, with future evaluation to consider cessation should her kidney function stabilise. Discovery of disease-associated complement gene alterations would have indicated the need for extended therapy with close surveillance for disease recurrence; nonetheless, no such mutations were detected in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

The foremost concern in adults presenting with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI) is to rule out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), characterized by a marked deficiency of ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). While waiting for definitive laboratory results, clinical scoring systems such as PLASMIC are useful for initial stratification; nevertheless, confirmation relies on laboratory analysis. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, further reducing the probability of TTP and shaping subsequent investigative steps.

Another differential diagnosis to consider is STEC-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS). However, the patient’s PCR assay for Shiga toxin was negative. Additionally, there was no history of preceding gastrointestinal illness, and all microbiological studies were unremarkable, making STEC-HUS improbable.

In summary, ongoing hemolysis, the presence of schistocytes, requirement for dialysis due to AKI, and histological evidence of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) on renal biopsy collectively pointed toward a diagnosis of complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS), as illustrated in figure 5. This conclusion was bolstered by negative STEC PCR results and the patient’s swift clinical response to eculizumab therapy. The lack of significant prior health issues diminishes the plausibility of a pre-existing complement abnormality requiring a secondary trigger and instead favors an infectious etiology as the precipitant for CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis that is determined after other causes have been excluded, and C3 and C4 complement levels can remain within normal limits throughout the illness. It is crucial to recognize that normal C3 and C4 values do not rule out CM-HUS, as these markers may not fall outside the reference intervals during the disease. Of particular interest, the onset of haemolysis coincided with improvement in sepsis, which is indicative of CM-HUS precipitated by infection rather than an underlying condition.

For this patient, assessments of basic complement (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were carried out just prior to the initial plasma exchange (PEX). Additional complement analysis was conducted 8 days following the last PEX treatment and 4 days subsequent to the first administration of eculizumab. The diminished activity observed in the alternative complement pathway most likely reflects therapeutic intervention rather than an inherent complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ results from inappropriate activation of the alternative complement pathway, typically in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50%–70% of cases are associated with either genetic alterations or autoantibodies impacting complement regulators such as factor H, factor I, factor B, or the membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nonetheless, expression of the disease is variable, often necessitating an external precipitating event, with infections, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or certain pharmacologic agents acting as the most common triggers.

While C. canimorsus is infrequently documented to cause activation of the complement system, this phenomenon has been observed particularly in hosts with increased vulnerability. Importantly, C. canimorsus demonstrates heterogeneity in its sensitivity to serum; many strains are susceptible to complement-mediated killing, whereas capsule-producing isolates exhibit partial resistance, underscoring the key role of complement in defense mechanisms against this organism. This interaction presents a clinical dilemma: effective complement activity aids in eradicating the organism but, in predisposed individuals, may concurrently induce uncontrolled endothelial injury and the development of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Our case contributes to the limited but expanding series of reports associating C. canimorsus infection with complement-mediated TMA. Notably, Bjørkto et al. presented a case of CM-HUS induced by C. canimorsus in a patient harboring a complement factor B mutation, who responded to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that previous report, we did not detect any pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations in complement genes in our patient, which suggests the possibility of infection-induced CM-HUS arising in the absence of identifiable inherited complement abnormalities.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

The management of CM-HUS in the setting of ongoing infection is particularly complex. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5, represents the established therapy for CM-HUS and has been shown to significantly enhance both hematologic and renal outcomes, especially if started promptly. Delays in administration are linked with partial renal recovery and a subsequently greater likelihood of advancing to end-stage renal disease.

Conversely, plasma exchange (PEX) is critical for survival in cases of TTP, yet it confers only short-term improvement in CM-HUS by clearing complement fragments, pro-inflammatory substances, or autoantibodies from circulation, rather than correcting the fundamental defect in complement regulation. In the present patient, urine output resumed following the concluding PEX treatments but before initiation of eculizumab, indicative of temporary disease stabilization. Complete and lasting improvements in hematologic and kidney function were achieved solely after complement inhibition therapy.

A central treatment challenge involves the administration of complement inhibitors like eculizumab during episodes of active bacteremia, as terminal complement components are essential for host defense against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, heightening concerns of infection risk tied to eculizumab use. Here, eculizumab was given after blood cultures showed no growth, reducing the danger of unchecked infection. Immunization, preventive antibiotics, and diligent surveillance are critical adjuncts when employing complement-blocking agents.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition are key factors associated with better outcomes. In our case, the patient attained hematological remission and partial improvement in kidney function, in line with existing data indicating that renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab are primary prognostic factors.

While CM-HUS associated with C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to consider infectious causes—including rare pathogens—in individuals presenting with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced recognition and advanced molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate the detection of additional cases.
