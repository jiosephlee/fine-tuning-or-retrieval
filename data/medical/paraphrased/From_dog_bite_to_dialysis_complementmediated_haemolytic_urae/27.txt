Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with swiftly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her condition advanced to disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which eventually led to complete anuria and the need for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia. Although she showed initial clinical stabilization with antibiotics and supportive measures, she subsequently experienced abrupt isolated hemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopenia. Laboratory analysis and renal biopsy established a diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), specifically complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS). Hematologic remission was attained after plasma exchange and starting eculizumab, with partial improvement in renal function. This case illustrates C. canimorsus infection as an unusual precipitant of CM-HUS in previously healthy adults, and discusses the patient’s diagnostic process, the complexities involved in distinguishing potential etiologies, and the challenges of managing TMA arising post-sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus characterized by slow growth and commonly residing in the oropharyngeal microbiota of dogs and cats. Human infection is uncommon and can be transmitted through bites, scratches, or even through close exposure. The spectrum of clinical manifestations is broad, from isolated meningitis to severe sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although the organism exhibits wide susceptibility to antibiotics, the mortality rate may reach as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most frequently implicated in invasive zoonotic infections affecting humans. Owing to its fastidious growth requirements and protracted culture times, diagnostic recognition can be delayed, leading to increasing reliance on molecular approaches such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the present case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock, marked leukopaenia, and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. The initial disease trajectory was predominantly characterized by sepsis-driven involvement of multiple organs; however, subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopaenia, and progressive acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed toward an overlapping TMA process. This progression emphasizes that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be masked during the early stages dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant medical history and two prior uncomplicated pregnancies presented to the emergency department due to acute clinical deterioration. Four days earlier, she had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and had been given a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. She was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) at that time, but did not begin antibiotic therapy as she was unable to obtain the medication. Subsequently, she experienced a high fever exceeding 40 °C, generalized malaise, and decreased oral intake. She later sought care after noticing numbness and discoloration of her feet. In the primary-care emergency setting, hypoglycaemia was noted and did not respond to either oral glucose or glucagon, reaching a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating her urgent referral to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was awake and interactive, though she exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness of the lower extremities. She denied the use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of similar illness among her family members. Initial laboratory tests demonstrated profound systemic disturbances, characterized by intense inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas assessment indicated a metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.27 (reference: 7.35–7.43) and a significantly increased lactate concentration at 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). Persistent hypoglycaemia was observed despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, which was attributed to sepsis. Upon arrival, she exhibited adequate urine output; urinalysis was notable for proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her array of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—were assessed as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) resulting from bacteraemia and early sepsis, with a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. Clinical and imaging assessments excluded a surgical etiology for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis. Because of the critical nature of her presentation, empiric intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was administered to address dog-related pathogens, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was suspected from the beginning and later verified on day 7 through both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. The patient was moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for heightened surveillance.

In light of the pancytopenia and coagulopathy, a haematology consultation was obtained. Possible diagnoses included acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was part of the differential but was deemed less likely due to the presence of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and lack of reticulocyte compensation. Flow cytometry was conducted, and a bone marrow biopsy was considered; however, normal findings—absence of increased CD34+ blasts or abnormal phenotypes—led to the decision to forgo the biopsy. The clinical manifestation aligned with sepsis. Supportive therapy with platelet and fibrinogen transfusions was provided while pending additional diagnostic results.

Following admission to the ICU, she exhibited complete absence of urine output, and her renal function deteriorated rapidly. Attempts at vigorous fluid resuscitation combined with loop diuretics failed to produce improvement. She progressed to septic cardiomyopathy associated with fluid overload, leading to the initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune screening (including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) assays) yielded negative results.

By the third ICU day, she manifested widespread cutaneous bleeding characterized by ecchymosis as well as distal cyanosis and ischemia affecting multiple toes and fingers, although pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical features were indicative of microthrombosis consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). In spite of involvement of multiple organs, she did not require vasopressor therapy other than the stress-dose corticosteroids administered during the early septic phase. Additionally, she was treated with albumin infusions and various blood products.

Following 8 days of hospitalization, the patient was moved to the nephrology ward due to continued dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and the need for intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival at the ward, she was hemodynamically stable but remained anuric and displayed generalized edema along with multiple hematomas; she was, however, awake and fully oriented. Based on blood culture findings, her antibiotic regimen was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently transitioned to oral Apocillin.

During her stay in the nephrology unit, laboratory data demonstrated a reduction in inflammatory markers, a reactive leukocytosis, recovery in platelet count, and stable hemoglobin concentrations. Renal function markers revealed ongoing azotemia, while coagulation tests normalized, reflecting the resolution of coagulopathy. Laboratory parameters at this phase are detailed in Table 2.

Despite an initial period of stabilization, her clinical status shifted with the onset of an acute isolated decrease in hemoglobin from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over a 6-day period, occurring in the absence of clinical signs of bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet levels remained consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin became undetectable, and there was a rise in LDH, all of which signified sustained hemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test returned positive for IgG, leading to consideration of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was commenced in addition to routine prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a two-day span. Persistence of haemolysis despite corticosteroids, and continued thrombocytopenia (platelets 41–46 × 10⁹/L), prompted further investigation. Peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, findings consistent with MAHA in keeping with TMA (figure 2). The initial DAT positivity was presumed to be a false positive attributable to penicillin use or preceding transfusions; a subsequent repeat was negative.

Complement testing and ADAMTS13 activity assays were performed. Standard local complement levels (C3/C4) fell within normal reference ranges, and assays of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways showed normal function. ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently found to be 43.5%, thereby excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), in which values are generally less than 10%. During the interval awaiting definitive results, plasma exchange (PEX) therapy was begun as a temporary measure before initiating eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia with the presence of fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, findings indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Immunofluorescence studies and electron microscopy are not presented, as immunofluorescence did not detect immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy showed no signs of an underlying primary glomerular disease.

The diagnosis of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) was excluded based on negative stool PCR testing.

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, the patient began to spontaneously produce urine again. Initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W, along with Bexsero for serogroup B—were administered prior to starting eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg intravenously each week for a total of four weeks). The follow-up doses for both vaccines were scheduled to occur eight weeks later in an outpatient setting. Apocillin prophylaxis was initiated, since vaccination had not occurred at least two weeks before the initiation of complement inhibition.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement analysis was requested from the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University in Sweden. Due to both logistical constraints and concerns for patient safety, three PEX sessions using Octaplas and one induction dose of eculizumab were conducted prior to specimen collection. Functional complement testing showed a substantially decreased alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50 at 6%), whereas levels of complement factors H, I, and B remained within normal ranges, and testing for antifactor H antibodies yielded negative results.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intricate medical management, the patient currently displays stable overall health with satisfactory urine production. Renal function remains impaired, as evidenced by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L; ongoing monitoring will occur through follow-up in the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). She continues to receive maintenance eculizumab therapy (1200 mg IV every 2 weeks), sustained for 4 months following initial induction. Comprehensive complement genetic analysis did not detect any pathogenic mutations; these findings were relayed orally by the reference laboratory, although the formal written report had yet to be received at the time of submission. Given the available data, complement inhibition therapy is being maintained because of the disease's severity, with plans to review its necessity should kidney function stabilize. The discovery of pathogenic complement gene mutations would support prolonged therapy along with vigilant surveillance for relapse, but such variants were absent in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adults who present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, it is essential to first rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Clinical scoring tools such as PLASMIC can assist in initial assessment pending laboratory confirmation, as definitive diagnosis relies on ADAMTS13 testing. The patient's PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood for TTP, and her ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, further reducing suspicion for TTP and informing subsequent investigation.

STEC-HUS must also be considered. The patient’s PCR test for Shiga toxin was negative. Additionally, the lack of antecedent gastrointestinal symptoms and negative microbiology make STEC-HUS improbable.

The combination of ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, AKI requiring dialysis, and renal biopsy indicative of TMA favored a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). The diagnosis was reinforced by PCR-based exclusion of STEC and the patient’s rapid clinical response following eculizumab initiation. The absence of significant prior medical history diminishes the possibility of an underlying complement abnormality necessitating a ‘second hit’, instead suggesting CM-HUS precipitated by infection.

CM-HUS is diagnosed by excluding other causes and may present with normal complement (C3/C4) concentrations throughout its clinical course. Therefore, CM-HUS cannot be ruled out based solely on normal complement values, since these may not deviate from reference intervals during the illness. Additionally, haemolysis was noted only once the sepsis had begun to resolve, indicating that CM-HUS was precipitated by infection instead of being present beforehand.

For this patient, initial complement assays (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were checked right before starting the first PEX treatment. More extensive complement assessment was conducted 8 days following the completion of PEX and 4 days after administration of the first eculizumab dose. The diminished activity seen in the alternative pathway is most likely attributed to the effects of therapy, rather than inherent complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS (previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS’) results from abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with a genetic predisposition. Genetic variants or autoantibodies that impact complement regulatory proteins—such as factors H, I, and B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are present in approximately 50% to 70% of cases. Nevertheless, the condition does not always manifest, as incomplete penetrance requires an external precipitating factor; common triggers include infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, and certain pharmaceuticals.

C. canimorsus has infrequently been reported to initiate complement pathway activation, particularly in hosts with increased vulnerability. Of note, C. canimorsus demonstrates variable susceptibility to serum complement: while most isolates are sensitive, some strains with a capsule exhibit partial resistance, underscoring the role of complement-mediated mechanisms in host defense. This presents a clinical dilemma, since complement activation is necessary for pathogen clearance but may induce uncontrolled endothelial injury and TMA in those with underlying predisposition.

The present case expands a limited but growing literature connecting C. canimorsus to complement-mediated TMAs. For instance, Bjørkto et al documented a patient with CM-HUS attributed to C. canimorsus infection and carrying a complement factor B variant, who responded positively to eculizumab therapy. In contrast to this prior report, our patient did not reveal any pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement genes, providing evidence for infection-associated CM-HUS occurring in the absence of an identifiable genetic predisposition.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Management of CM-HUS in the setting of ongoing infection presents distinct difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against terminal complement component 5, represents the established treatment for CM-HUS and significantly enhances both haematologic and renal outcomes, especially when started promptly. Initiating therapy late correlates with only partial recovery of renal function and thus an increased likelihood of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

Conversely, PEX is crucial for survival in TTP but confers merely short-lived benefits in CM-HUS, primarily through the removal of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, rather than addressing the root cause of complement dysfunction. In the presented patient, urine output improved following the last PEX treatments, but before eculizumab administration, indicating temporary stabilization. Lasting improvement in haematologic and renal function occurred only following complement inhibition.

A central therapeutic challenge involves employing complement inhibition during active bloodstream infection, since terminal complement components are essential in defending against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, leading to concerns over eculizumab-related susceptibility to infection. In this patient, eculizumab therapy commenced only after blood cultures showed no microbial growth, thereby reducing the risk of uncontrolled infection. When utilizing complement inhibitors, immunization, prophylactic antibiotics, and vigilant monitoring remain critical.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition are critical factors associated with a positive prognosis. In this case, the patient attained haematological remission alongside partial restoration of renal function, aligning with literature that identifies renal status and rapid initiation of eculizumab as primary prognostic factors.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus infection is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to consider infectious etiologies—even rare ones—in individuals who develop newly diagnosed TMA. Increased recognition and advanced molecular testing could facilitate detection of additional cases.
