Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after being bitten by a dog. She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, eventually becoming completely anuric and requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although her condition initially improved with antibiotic therapy and supportive measures, she subsequently experienced abrupt isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory assessment and renal biopsy were consistent with a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). After treatment with plasma exchange and eculizumab, haematological remission was attained and her renal function showed partial recovery. This case demonstrates C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon cause of CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults and describes the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the difficulties encountered in distinguishing among possible diagnoses, and the complicated clinical course and management of TMA post-sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is part of the typical oropharyngeal microbiota in dogs and cats. Human infection is uncommon and may result from bites, scratches, or mere proximity to animals. The clinical spectrum is wide, encompassing isolated meningitis, fulminant septicemia, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan failure, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although the organism is generally susceptible to multiple antibiotics, the fatality rate can be as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in serious zoonotic infections in humans. Due to its slow and demanding growth requirements, the diagnosis may be postponed, leading to increased reliance on molecular diagnostics, including real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the described case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested initially as septic shock, profound leukopaenia, and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. While the early phase was characterized by multiorgan dysfunction secondary to sepsis, subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and deteriorating acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed to a concurrent TMA. This progression underscores how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be masked during the initial phase of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical issues and two previous normal pregnancies arrived at the emergency department due to a rapidly deteriorating health status. Four days before admission, she sustained a dog bite on her upper lip and received a tetanus shot for prophylaxis. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed at the time of the injury, but she did not begin antibiotic therapy as she was unable to obtain the medication. Subsequently, she experienced a high fever exceeding 40 °C, malaise, decreased oral intake, and ultimately sought medical care when she developed numbness and discoloration in her feet. At a primary-care emergency facility, she was found to have hypoglycaemia that was unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, with her lowest blood glucose recorded at 0.6 mmol/L. This finding led to her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and cooperative, yet demonstrated signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness of her lower limbs. She reported no use of herbal treatments or other substances, and there was no history of similar symptoms among her family members. Initial laboratory studies indicated profound systemic abnormalities, characterized by significant inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and coagulation dysfunction. Arterial blood gas assessment showed metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range: 7.35–7.43) and an increased lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). Persistent hypoglycaemia was noted, unresponsive to substantial doses of intravenous glucose, which was attributed to sepsis. Upon presentation, she demonstrated sufficient urine output; urinalysis revealed proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her array of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—was considered indicative of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and incipient sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3). After both physical examination and imaging, a surgical etiology for her mottled limbs, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded. Given the seriousness of her condition, intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated for empirical broad-spectrum coverage of pathogens commonly associated with dogs, including Capnocytophaga species. Of particular note, C. canimorsus was suspected early and subsequently identified in both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures seven days later. She was then transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced observation.

In light of her pancytopenia and coagulopathy, haematology input was sought. The differential diagnoses entertained included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was another possibility but was deemed improbable given findings of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and inadequate reticulocyte response. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted and bone marrow biopsy was scheduled; however, normal findings—with neither elevated CD34+ blast population nor aberrant phenotypes—prompted cancellation of the biopsy. The clinical scenario corresponded most closely to sepsis. During the period pending laboratory evaluation, thrombocyte and fibrinogen transfusions were provided for supportive care.

Following admission to the ICU, she developed complete anuria with a marked and rapid decline in renal function. Intensive intravenous fluid administration combined with loop diuretics was undertaken but proved ineffective. She went on to develop septic cardiomyopathy and clinically significant fluid overload, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), were both negative.

By the third ICU day, she manifested widespread cutaneous bleeding accompanied by ecchymoses and the appearance of cyanosis and ischemic changes in the distal phalanges of multiple toes and fingers; however, distal pulses and sensory function remained intact. This presentation was indicative of microthrombotic events likely related to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Throughout this period of multiple organ dysfunction, vasopressor therapy was never necessary aside from stress-dose corticosteroids administered for her initial septic episode. She was also treated with albumin as well as various blood products.

Following eight days in hospital, the patient was moved to the nephrology service due to ongoing dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and the need for continued intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, she was hemodynamically stable but remained anuric, with widespread oedema and several haematomas, though she was fully alert and oriented. Based on the results of blood cultures, her antibiotic regimen was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin.

During her stay on the nephrology unit, laboratory results demonstrated an improving inflammatory response, a reactive leukocytosis, recovering platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin concentrations. Renal function parameters indicated continued azotaemia, whereas coagulation studies returned to normal, signifying resolution of her prior coagulopathy. Her laboratory values during this period are detailed in Table 2.

Despite initial progress, her clinical course deteriorated when she experienced an abrupt and isolated decline in hemoglobin concentration (from 111 to 68 g/L over a six-day span) in the absence of obvious bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently suppressed (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin became undetectable, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) increased, consistent with ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) detected the presence of IgG, which raised concerns for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. The patient was started on high-dose prednisolone in addition to standard prophylactic measures, and received several blood transfusions over a 2-day period. As haemolysis failed to improve with steroid treatment and platelet counts persisted at 41–46 × 10⁹/L, additional investigation was undertaken. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, indicating MAHA consistent with TMA (see figure 2). The initial DAT result was most probably a false positive attributable to penicillin administration or earlier transfusions; subsequent DAT testing yielded a negative result.

Complement levels and ADAMTS13 activity were sent for laboratory evaluation. Routine complement analyses (C3/C4) performed locally were within reference ranges, and functional assays for the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways were similarly unremarkable. ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently found to be 43.5%, thus excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), given TTP is defined by activity below 10%. While results were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced as interim management before the administration of eculizumab.

After the platelet count had risen to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescent formation, findings in keeping with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The diagnosis was primarily supported by the light microscopic findings illustrated in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy images are not included, as immunofluorescence did not show immune complex deposits and electron microscopy failed to demonstrate any signs of primary glomerular disease.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) was excluded, as stool PCR analysis returned negative results.

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) procedures, the patient began to spontaneously produce urine again. Eculizumab induction therapy was started after administration of the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B)—at a regimen of 900 mg intravenous weekly for four weeks. The subsequent vaccine doses were scheduled to be given eight weeks later during outpatient follow-up. Because the vaccines had not been delivered two weeks in advance of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was provided.

Comprehensive complement testing to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was ordered through Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. For logistical and safety reasons, three PEX sessions using Octaplas and a single induction dose of eculizumab were administered prior to obtaining samples. Functional complement assays included quantitative measurement of alternative pathway activity (AH50), which was significantly decreased to 6%. However, complement factors H, I, and B were within normal ranges, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing complex medical treatment, the patient is currently stable overall, demonstrating satisfactory urine production. However, her renal function remains impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L, and she will continue with follow-up in the nephrology outpatient service (see figure 4). She is still receiving maintenance therapy with eculizumab at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, continued for four months after induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of the complement system did not detect any pathogenic variants; these preliminary findings were communicated orally by the reference laboratory, with the formal written report not yet available at the time of submission. Given these results, complement inhibition therapy is being maintained in light of the significant severity of her condition, with plans to re-evaluate the possibility of discontinuing treatment should kidney function reach a plateau. If pathogenic mutations in complement genes were found, this would have supported prolonged therapy accompanied by vigilant monitoring for recurrence. In this case, however, no such genetic abnormalities were present.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

The foremost consideration in adult patients presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI is to rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). While clinical assessment tools such as the PLASMIC score provide interim risk stratification, definitive diagnosis relies on laboratory testing. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, reflecting a low likelihood of TTP, and ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, further diminishing the suspicion for TTP and guiding subsequent investigations.

STEC-HUS also warrants attention. Shiga toxin PCR testing was negative in this patient. Additionally, the absence of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and negative stool testing render STEC-HUS an improbable cause.

Taken together, the ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, dialysis-dependent AKI, and renal biopsy demonstrating TMA point towards a diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). This assessment was bolstered by negative STEC PCR results and notable clinical improvement following the initiation of eculizumab. Her lack of significant prior medical history argues against a pre-existing complement abnormality necessitating a 'second hit' and favors infection-induced CM-HUS as the etiology.

CM-HUS is identified as a clinicopathological diagnosis reached by excluding other possibilities, and levels of complement components (C3/C4) may remain within the normal range throughout the illness. Crucially, the presence of normal complement levels (C3/C4) does not rule out CM-HUS, since these values can persist in the reference range during disease progression. Hemolysis developed only once sepsis started to resolve, indicating that CM-HUS was precipitated by infection rather than being a longstanding condition.

For this patient, initial complement measurements (C3/C4) as well as ADAMTS13 activity were collected prior to the first plasma exchange (PEX) procedure. Additional, extended complement assays were conducted eight days following the last PEX and four days after administration of the initial eculizumab dose. The decrease noted in the alternative pathway is attributable primarily to therapeutic intervention, rather than underlying complement deficiency or ongoing activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS, previously known as ‘atypical HUS’, results from inappropriate activation of the alternative complement pathway and frequently occurs in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of affected patients possess genetic mutations or autoantibodies that impair the function of complement regulators such as factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Despite this, expression of the disease is incomplete, and clinical presentation generally necessitates an additional external precipitant—typically infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or select medications.

Though uncommonly documented, C. canimorsus has been shown to stimulate complement pathways, especially in hosts with underlying vulnerabilities. Importantly, the organism exhibits variable susceptibility to serum, with many strains susceptible to complement while certain encapsulated variants display partial resistance; this underscores complement function as a critical host defense. This presents a clinical dilemma: while complement activity is crucial for bacterial clearance, in patients with predisposing factors, this activation may provoke uncontrolled endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The present case contributes to the limited literature associating C. canimorsus infection with complement-mediated TMAs. Notably, Bjørkto et al previously documented a case of CM-HUS triggered by C. canimorsus in a patient carrying a complement factor B variant, which responded to eculizumab therapy. Differing from this report, genetic analysis in our patient did not reveal any pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene mutations, implicating infection-induced CM-HUS in the absence of a detectable inherited complement disorder.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection poses specific difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5, is considered the primary therapy for CM-HUS and substantially enhances both haematological and renal outcomes, especially if administered promptly. Starting treatment late correlates with partial renal recovery and subsequently increases the likelihood of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

PEX, by comparison, is critical for survival in TTP but confers only temporary improvements in CM-HUS. Its effects are largely attributed to the removal of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory factors, or autoantibodies, rather than rectifying the fundamental complement dysfunction. In this patient, diuresis was restored following the final PEX treatments yet prior to eculizumab administration, indicating only short-term stabilization. Ongoing haematological and renal recovery was observed exclusively after inhibiting complement activity.

A significant therapeutic challenge involves employing complement inhibitors during active bacteraemia, since terminal complement proteins are vital for protecting against encapsulated organisms—including Neisseria and Capnocytophaga—which raises concerns regarding infection risk with eculizumab. In this particular scenario, eculizumab was started once blood cultures were negative, thereby reducing the threat of uncontained infection. Continued vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant surveillance are crucial when using complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition represent the primary factors linked to positive outcomes. In this case, the patient experienced hematologic remission and partial restoration of renal function, aligning with current data that indicate renal status and timing of eculizumab initiation are key prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS related to C. canimorsus is exceptionally uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of accounting for infectious causes—even rare ones—in individuals who present with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced vigilance and advances in molecular diagnostic techniques could facilitate the detection of additional similar cases.
