From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history presented with rapidly escalating sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She subsequently developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, progressing to complete anuria necessitating dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although her initial response to antibiotics and supportive measures was favourable, she experienced abrupt onset of isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopaenia. Further laboratory testing and renal biopsy revealed a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Treatment with plasma exchange and eculizumab resulted in haematological remission and partial recovery of renal function. This report underscores the potential for C. canimorsus infection to act as a rare precipitant of CM-HUS in previously healthy adults and details the diagnostic evaluation, differential diagnosis challenges, and complexities associated with managing TMA after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus, an encapsulated, slow-proliferating gram-negative bacillus, constitutes part of the typical oropharyngeal microbiota in both dogs and cats. While human infection is uncommon, it may be transmitted through bites, scratches, or even non-traumatic close contact. The clinical manifestations are diverse, encompassing conditions from isolated meningitis to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although C. canimorsus tends to be broadly sensitive to antibiotics, the associated mortality rate can be as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most commonly implicated in severe zoonotic disease affecting humans. Owing to its exacting growth requirements and characteristically slow proliferation in standard cultures, identification can be protracted, thereby driving the adoption of advanced molecular diagnostic techniques, including real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the case described here, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock, marked leukopenia, and coagulopathy compatible with DIC. Initially, the disease course was characterized by sepsis-induced multiorgan compromise; however, subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and progressive acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed toward an overlapping TMA process. This progression underscores the diagnostic challenge posed by complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) when it arises amidst, and is initially concealed by, sepsis and DIC in the early stages.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two previously uneventful pregnancies arrived at the emergency department in a state of rapidly deteriorating health. Four days earlier, she had suffered a dog bite to her upper lip and subsequently received a tetanus vaccine as a preventative measure. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed on the day of injury, she was unable to obtain the medication and did not begin antibiotic therapy. She later developed a high-grade fever (exceeding 40 °C), malaise, and decreased oral intake. Medical attention was sought after she noticed numbness and discoloration in her feet. Upon assessment in primary care emergency, she was found to be hypoglycaemic, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon therapy, with a minimum blood glucose of 0.6 mmol/L. This finding necessitated urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and appropriately interactive but displayed clinical signs consistent with sepsis, including mottling and coldness of the lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal medicines or illicit substances, and no family members had experienced similar symptoms. The initial set of laboratory investigations demonstrated profound systemic abnormalities, including significant inflammatory response, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, raised liver enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas analysis showed metabolic acidosis, with a pH measured at 7.27 (normal: 7.35–7.43), as well as a notably increased lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference: <2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, she persisted with hypoglycaemia, presumed secondary to sepsis. At the time of evaluation, the patient produced urine adequately; urinalysis demonstrated proteinuria (2+) and significant haematuria (3+).

Based on her clinical features—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by AKI, and lactic acidosis—her illness was assessed as DIC attributable to bacteraemia and the early stages of sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1/3). After comprehensive clinical and imaging assessments, potential surgical causes for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded. In light of the severity of her initial presentation, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to provide coverage for dog-associated organisms, notably Capnocytophaga species. Importantly, C. canimorsus was considered as a likely pathogen early in her evaluation, and its presence was subsequently confirmed through both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures after 7 days. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for heightened observation.

Given the findings of pancytopenia and coagulopathy, the haematology team was consulted. The working differential diagnosis encompassed acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Although haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was included among potential diagnoses, it was deemed improbable owing to features such as leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and a lack of compensatory reticulocytosis. Flow cytometric analysis was completed, and a bone marrow biopsy was planned; however, in the absence of abnormalities—specifically, normal CD34+ blast counts and no atypical phenotypes—the biopsy was not pursued. The clinical findings pointed towards a diagnosis of sepsis. While diagnostic results were pending, supportive care was provided with transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient exhibited complete anuria and experienced a swift decline in renal function. Efforts to restore fluid balance through intensive resuscitation and the administration of loop diuretics proved ineffective. Subsequently, she manifested signs of septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by fluid overload, necessitating the commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serological assessments, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, she developed widespread cutaneous bleeding characterized by ecchymoses, as well as cyanotic and ischaemic alterations at the distal extremities, affecting several toes and fingers; however, peripheral pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical features were indicative of microthrombotic processes associated with DIC. Although multiple organ systems were implicated, she did not require vasopressor therapy aside from the administration of stress-dose corticosteroids during the initial phase of sepsis. Treatment also included albumin infusions and transfusion of additional blood products.

Following an 8-day hospital stay, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit for continued management of dialysis-dependent AKI and administration of intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, she remained haemodynamically stable but was anuric, exhibited widespread oedema, and presented with multiple haematomas; neurologically, she was alert and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was stepped down to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin according to blood culture findings.

While under nephrology care, laboratory investigations demonstrated a downward trend in markers of inflammation, reactive leukocytosis, recovering platelet counts, and stable haemoglobin concentrations. Despite this, assessments of renal function continued to reveal persistent azotaemia. Coagulation studies returned to normal values, consistent with resolution of the coagulopathy. A summary of her laboratory data from this period is provided in Table 2.

However, following these initial improvements, her condition was complicated by the onset of an abrupt, isolated decline in haemoglobin levels (from 111 to 68 g/L over a 6-day interval), in the absence of bleeding or cardiovascular compromise. Platelet counts remained markedly low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin became undetectable, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels increased, collectively indicative of ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, yielded a positive result for IgG, prompting consideration of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. Consequently, high-dose prednisolone therapy was commenced, accompanied by standard prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions across a two-day period. Persistence of haemolysis despite corticosteroid administration, along with sustained thrombocytopenia (platelet count 41–46 × 10⁹/L), necessitated further diagnostic workup. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, in line with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (see figure 2). It was subsequently considered that the initial DAT may have represented a false positive due to the effects of penicillin or antecedent transfusions; a follow-up DAT was negative.

Complement assessment and measurement of ADAMTS13 activity were performed. The locally accessible standard complement assays (C3/C4) returned results within the reference range, and functional testing of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways also revealed no abnormalities. Shortly thereafter, ADAMTS13 activity was reported at 43.5%, effectively excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), given that TTP typically manifests with activity levels below 10%. While these findings were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was initiated as a temporizing measure prior to administration of the first eculizumab dose.

After the thrombocyte count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and isolated crescent formation—all features indicative of TMA.

The key diagnostic characteristics in this scenario were most clearly displayed in the light microscopy findings presented in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy results are not included here: immunofluorescence testing did not show any immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy failed to identify evidence of an underlying primary glomerulopathy.

Testing for Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) returned negative by stool PCR, excluding this etiology.

After the patient underwent the second and third sessions of PEX, there was a restoration of spontaneous diuresis. Induction therapy with eculizumab (administered at 900 mg intravenously each week for four weeks) commenced after initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (for serogroup B)—were given. Plans for the administration of the second vaccine doses, scheduled for 8 weeks later, were made for follow-up in the outpatient setting. Since the vaccines were not delivered two weeks before initiating complement blockade, prophylactic Apocillin was started.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated TMA, comprehensive complement evaluation was conducted by the Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden). Considering safety and logistical needs, the patient underwent three rounds of PEX with Octaplas and received an induction dose of eculizumab prior to obtaining samples. Functional complement assessments, including quantitative AH50 to measure alternative pathway activity, revealed a significant decrease (6%). Nonetheless, levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within the normal range, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical therapy, the patient is currently stable overall and maintains satisfactory urine production. Despite this, renal function remains impaired, as reflected by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she is scheduled for ongoing monitoring in the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). She is still receiving maintenance eculizumab at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, and this regimen will be maintained for four months following the initial induction phase. Comprehensive genetic analysis of complement-related genes did not detect any disease-causing variants; this outcome was verbally communicated by the reference laboratory, although the formal written documentation was not available at the time of manuscript submission. In light of her clinical severity, complement inhibition therapy is being maintained, with plans to reconsider its cessation if renal function reaches a stable plateau. The detection of pathogenic variants in complement genes would support extended therapy with vigilant monitoring for potential relapses, but such mutations were not identified in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs represent a group of intersecting clinical syndromes.

When adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost step is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by a profound ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Although laboratory assessment is definitive for diagnosis, clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score can help in risk stratification pending test results. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4/7, corresponding to a low likelihood of TTP, and ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, effectively excluding TTP and informing subsequent diagnostic evaluation.

Another differential diagnosis is STEC-HUS. The patient’s Shiga toxin PCR was negative, and the lack of prior gastrointestinal symptoms in conjunction with unremarkable microbiological findings make STEC-HUS improbable.

In this scenario, ongoing haemolysis, presence of schistocytes, dialysis-dependent AKI, and kidney histopathology in line with TMA were indicative of CM-HUS (figure 5). This conclusion was bolstered by both negative Shiga toxin PCR results and prompt clinical response to eculizumab. Furthermore, the absence of notable prior medical issues diminishes the likelihood of an underlying complement abnormality necessitating a 'second hit', lending greater support to an infection-induced CM-HUS diagnosis.

CM-HUS is identified through a clinicopathological process of elimination, and it is important to recognize that complement levels (C3/C4) can be within normal limits throughout the progression of the illness. The presence of normal complement levels (C3/C4) does not preclude a diagnosis of CM-HUS, as these parameters may not deviate from the norm during the disease. Significantly, haemolysis was detected only after the resolution of sepsis had begun, indicating that CM-HUS was likely triggered by infection rather than reflecting an underlying chronic disorder.

For our patient, standard complement measurements (C3/C4) along with ADAMTS13 activity were collected directly before initiating the first session of PEX. More comprehensive complement analyses were subsequently conducted 8 days after the last PEX treatment and 4 days following the initial administration of eculizumab. The noted reduction in activity of the alternative pathway is best explained as a consequence of therapy, instead of being attributable to inherent complement deficiency or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS (previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS’) is characterized by improper regulation of the alternative complement pathway, most frequently observed in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of cases involve genetic mutations or the presence of autoantibodies that impair complement regulatory components, such as factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nevertheless, not all individuals with these risk factors develop the disease; the onset usually necessitates an environmental trigger, such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or exposure to particular drugs.

While uncommon, C. canimorsus has the capacity to stimulate complement activation, especially in hosts with increased vulnerability. C. canimorsus strains vary in their response to serum, with the majority being susceptible to complement-mediated killing, though some encapsulated strains exhibit partial resistance. This underscores the significance of complement activity in host protection. However, this also presents a clinical dilemma: activation of complement is crucial for eliminating the pathogen, but in predisposed individuals, it can also lead to excessive endothelial injury and TMA.

The present case contributes to the limited but expanding evidence connecting C. canimorsus infection with complement-mediated TMAs. An illustrative example is provided by Bjørkto et al, who documented a patient with C. canimorsus-induced CM-HUS possessing a variant in complement factor B who responded favorably to eculizumab therapy. Distinct from this prior report, our patient exhibited no detectable pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations in complement genes, indicating a scenario of infection-associated CM-HUS in the absence of identifiable hereditary complement abnormalities.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS in the presence of ongoing infection poses distinct difficulties. Eculizumab—a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5—remains the primary therapy for CM-HUS, offering substantial improvements in both haematological and renal parameters, especially when administered promptly. Delays in starting eculizumab are linked to suboptimal renal recovery and a heightened likelihood of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

In comparison, plasma exchange (PEX) is critical for survival in TTP but yields only transient benefits in CM-HUS. Its effect is largely limited to removing circulating complement breakdown products, inflammatory substances, or autoantibodies, rather than addressing the core defect in the complement pathway. In the patient described, urine output improved following the final PEX treatments but prior to administration of eculizumab, indicating only short-lived clinical improvement. Durable recovery of blood and renal indices was achieved only after complement inhibition was instituted.

An important clinical challenge is the deployment of complement inhibitors such as eculizumab when bacteraemia is still present, since the terminal complement pathway is vital for host immunity against encapsulated bacteria—including species like Neisseria and Capnocytophaga—which heightens the risk of infections related to eculizumab. In the case discussed, initiation of eculizumab was deferred until blood cultures were negative to reduce the likelihood of exacerbating an ongoing infection. Immunisation, use of prophylactic antimicrobials, and vigilant surveillance are imperative for patients undergoing complement inhibition therapy.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition are key factors associated with improved prognosis. In line with existing literature indicating that both renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab therapy substantially influence outcomes, our patient experienced haematological remission alongside partial restoration of kidney function.

While CM-HUS precipitated by C. canimorsus remains exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of including infectious agents—even atypical ones—as potential precipitants in patients who develop new-onset TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and advances in molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate the recognition of additional similar presentations.
