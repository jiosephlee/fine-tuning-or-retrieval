Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after being bitten by a dog. She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which advanced to complete anuria necessitating dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she initially showed clinical improvement after starting antibiotics and receiving supportive care, she later experienced acute isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory studies and kidney biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Hematological remission was attained following plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab, with partial restoration of renal function. This case demonstrates that C. canimorsus infection can uncommonly precipitate CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults, and details the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the difficulties encountered in distinguishing diagnoses, and the complex clinical and therapeutic challenges associated with TMA after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow-growing, gram-negative bacillus that resides within the typical oral flora of dogs and cats. Human infection occurs infrequently and may arise following bites, scratches, or even close interactions. The clinical spectrum is broad, encompassing presentations from isolated meningitis to severe sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although this organism remains generally sensitive to a wide range of antibiotics, case fatality rates can reach 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most commonly associated with invasive zoonotic disease in humans. Identification is often postponed because of its demanding growth requirements and slow culture development, increasingly leading to reliance on molecular diagnostics such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the present case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock accompanied by profound leukopaenia and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. While the initial presentation was marked by widespread organ involvement related to sepsis, subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and aggravated acute kidney injury (AKI) suggested a concurrent TMA, underscoring the potential for complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) to be concealed during the early stages of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant medical history and two previous uncomplicated pregnancies arrived at the emergency department with acute clinical deterioration. Four days before admission, she sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and was administered a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed on the day of the injury; however, she did not start antibiotic therapy as she was unable to obtain the medication. Subsequently, she developed a high-grade fever exceeding 40 °C, general malaise, decreased oral intake, and later reported numbness and discoloration of her feet, prompting her to seek medical care. In the primary-care emergency setting, she was noted to have hypoglycemia that was unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a minimum blood glucose level of 0.6 mmol/L, which led to an urgent referral to our emergency department.

Upon presentation, the patient was conscious and able to engage but demonstrated signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness in the lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal medicines or other substances, and there was no history of similar illness among family members. Initial laboratory results indicated profound systemic disturbances, including significant inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated hepatic enzymes, and coagulation abnormalities. Table 1 summarises these principal findings.

Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43), accompanied by an elevated lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). The patient persisted in a hypoglycaemic state despite receiving substantial amounts of intravenous glucose, presumed secondary to sepsis. Upon admission, urine output was satisfactory; urinalysis revealed proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her array of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy along with acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—was considered indicative of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis, with a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. A surgical etiology for her mottled limbs, including the possibility of necrotising fasciitis, was excluded following clinical assessment and imaging studies. Owing to the severity of her illness, intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin), a broad-spectrum antibiotic, was initiated to target pathogens associated with dog exposure, including Capnocytophaga species. From the outset, infection with C. canimorsus was suspected and subsequently confirmed seven days later through both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was then admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive surveillance.

Given her pancytopenia and coagulopathy, the hematology service was involved. The possible diagnoses considered were acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also thought of, though deemed less probable due to the presence of leukopaenia, raised inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocyte counts. Flow cytometry was carried out, with a bone marrow biopsy scheduled; however, normal findings—including no elevation in CD34+ blasts and no atypical cell phenotypes—resulted in cancellation of the planned biopsy. Her clinical presentation aligned with sepsis. While awaiting diagnostic results, she received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient became completely anuric, and her renal function deteriorated swiftly. Vigorous intravenous fluid administration in combination with loop diuretics was undertaken, but without effect. She then developed septic cardiomyopathy and exhibited signs of fluid overload, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), were negative.

By ICU day 3, she manifested generalized cutaneous bleeding, ecchymosis, and cyanotic as well as ischemic changes involving several fingers and toes, yet distal pulses and sensory function were preserved. These manifestations indicated microthrombotic events within the setting of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although multiple organ systems were affected, she did not require vasopressors except for stress-dose corticosteroids given early during sepsis management. Albumin infusions were provided in addition to various blood products.

Following eight days of hospitalization, the patient was relocated to the nephrology unit for continued management of dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and ongoing intravenous antibiotic treatment. Upon arrival, she was haemodynamically stable but had complete anuria, widespread edema, and multiple bruises, though she remained awake and oriented. Based on blood culture findings, her antibiotic regimen was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, then subsequently transitioned to oral Apocillin.

During her time on the nephrology service, laboratory evaluation demonstrated a trend toward resolution of inflammation, reactive leukocytosis, improvement in platelet count, and steady hemoglobin levels. Renal function assessments continued to reveal ongoing azotaemia. Coagulation studies normalized, consistent with improvement of her coagulopathy. However, after an initial period of stabilization, her condition evolved with an abrupt, isolated decline in hemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over six days, occurring in the absence of overt hemorrhage or hemodynamic compromise. Her platelet count remained low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin was no longer detectable, and LDH became elevated, findings suggestive of persistent hemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, prompting consideration of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone therapy commenced with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple units of blood were transfused over a span of two days. Since the haemolytic process did not improve with corticosteroid treatment and platelet counts remained low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional investigation was deemed necessary. Examination of a peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of MAHA and raising concern for TMA (see figure 2). The initial DAT result was likely falsely positive due to penicillin use or recent transfusions; a repeat DAT was subsequently negative.

Complement analysis and ADAMTS13 activity assays were ordered. Routine complement markers (C3 and C4) available locally were normal, and functional tests evaluating the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways also yielded normal results. ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently measured at 43.5%, thus excluding a diagnosis of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP typically shows activity less than 10%. In the interim, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced as a temporary measure before administering the initial dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, all indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Results from immunofluorescence and electron microscopy are not included, as immunofluorescence did not demonstrate any immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy showed no features suggestive of primary glomerulopathy.

Stool PCR testing was negative, thereby excluding Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS).

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) procedures, the patient experienced a return of spontaneous urine output. Eculizumab induction was started (900 mg intravenously weekly for 4 weeks) after administering the initial doses of Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (serogroup B) meningococcal vaccines. The second vaccine doses were scheduled to be administered 8 weeks later during outpatient follow-up. Because the vaccines had not been given at least 2 weeks before commencing complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), extended complement evaluation was sent to Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. Due to logistical concerns and safety precautions, the patient underwent three sessions of PEX using Octaplas and received a single induction dose of eculizumab before the samples were collected. Functional complement assessment included quantitative AH50 testing for alternative pathway activity, which revealed a significant reduction (6%), whereas levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits, and antifactor H antibodies were not detected.

Outcome and follow-up:

After intensive medical treatment, the patient remains in overall good health and maintains satisfactory urine output. Renal function is still impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L, and she will undergo ongoing monitoring in the nephrology outpatient setting (figure 4). She is maintained on eculizumab therapy at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, continuing for 4 months after the initial induction period. Comprehensive complement genetic analysis did not detect any disease-causing variants; these preliminary findings were shared verbally by the reference lab, with the official written report pending at the time of submission. Given the seriousness of her illness, complement inhibition therapy is being sustained, with intentions to reconsider cessation if her renal function stabilizes. Discovery of pathogenic complement gene mutations would support continued long-term treatment with vigilant observation for potential relapse. Nevertheless, no such variants were present in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost task is to rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by a pronounced ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). While clinical scoring systems such as PLASMIC may assist in triage pending laboratory confirmation, definitive diagnosis relies on lab testing. This patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood for TTP, and her measured ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, further reducing suspicion for TTP and guiding subsequent investigations.

Another differential diagnosis is STEC-HUS. However, the patient’s PCR test for Shiga toxin was negative. The lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and negative microbiological assays weigh against a diagnosis of STEC-HUS.

In the end, the combination of ongoing hemolysis, schistocytosis, AKI necessitating dialysis, and kidney biopsy showing features of TMA pointed toward a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). The diagnosis was reinforced by PCR exclusion of STEC and her prompt clinical response following the initiation of eculizumab treatment. Her otherwise unremarkable medical history diminishes the likelihood of a pre-existing complement abnormality requiring a 'second hit,' and favors an infection-associated CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is diagnosed based on clinical and pathological findings, typically after other conditions are excluded, and complement concentrations (C3/C4) can remain within normal limits throughout its progression. It is crucial to recognize that normal C3 and C4 values do not rule out CM-HUS, since these may not fluctuate outside reference values during the illness. Importantly, hemolysis manifested only after the sepsis began to resolve, indicating that CM-HUS was likely precipitated by infection rather than being present beforehand.

For this patient, preliminary complement studies (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were assessed immediately before the initial session of plasma exchange (PEX). Broader complement profiling was undertaken 8 days after the last PEX treatment and 4 days following the administration of the first eculizumab dose. The decrease noted in the alternative pathway is best interpreted as an effect of therapy, rather than reflecting innate complement deficiency or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS, previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ stems from abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently observed in individuals with genetic susceptibility. Genetic alterations or autoantibodies affecting complement regulatory elements—including factors H, I, B, and membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are identified in approximately 50% to 70% of affected patients. Despite this, disease penetrance remains incomplete, and external provocations such as infections, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or certain pharmacological agents are typically required for clinical symptoms to arise.

C. canimorsus, although infrequently cited, has the capacity to stimulate complement pathways, especially in vulnerable patients. Of note, C. canimorsus exhibits differing levels of sensitivity to serum complement; while many strains are complement sensitive, those expressing a capsule may demonstrate partial resistance, underscoring complement’s critical role in host protection. This introduces a clinical dilemma: activation of the complement system assists in pathogen clearance but may simultaneously provoke unchecked endothelial injury and TMA in susceptible individuals.

This report contributes to a limited but expanding collection of studies associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. For instance, Bjørkto et al documented a case where CM-HUS precipitated by C. canimorsus in a patient with a complement factor B mutation was successfully managed with eculizumab. Unlike that report, our case revealed no pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene variants, suggesting the development of infection-associated CM-HUS without an identifiable inherited complement defect.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection entails distinct challenges. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5, is considered the primary treatment for CM-HUS and leads to significant improvement in hematologic and renal parameters, especially with prompt administration. Initiating therapy late has been linked to partial renal recovery and an increased likelihood of progression to end-stage kidney disease.

By contrast, plasma exchange (PEX) is vital in treating TTP, but offers only transient benefit in cases of CM-HUS. Its effect is primarily through removing circulating complement products, pro-inflammatory substances, or autoantibodies, without addressing the underlying defect in the complement pathway. In the patient discussed, urine output resumed following the last PEX treatments and prior to administration of eculizumab, suggesting merely temporary stabilization. Durable improvement in hematologic and renal function was observed only subsequent to complement inhibition.

A significant treatment dilemma concerns the application of complement inhibitors during ongoing bacteremia, since terminal complement components are essential for defending against encapsulated bacteria such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, which raises apprehension regarding infectious complications associated with eculizumab use. Here, eculizumab administration was deferred until after blood cultures were negative, reducing the risk of persistent infection. Vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring are mandatory when employing complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition are critical factors in achieving a positive clinical outcome. In this case, the patient experienced haematological remission and partial improvement of renal function, aligning with existing data indicating that both baseline renal status and the interval prior to eculizumab initiation are key prognostic indicators.

While C. canimorsus-associated CM-HUS occurs infrequently, this report emphasizes the need to consider infectious etiologies—including rare pathogens—in individuals with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and the use of molecular diagnostic tools may facilitate the detection of additional cases.
