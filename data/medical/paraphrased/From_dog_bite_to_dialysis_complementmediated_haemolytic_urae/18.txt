Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her condition progressed to disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, eventually resulting in complete anuria and the need for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although her clinical status initially improved with antibiotic treatment and supportive management, she subsequently experienced abrupt onset of isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory evaluations and findings on renal biopsy established the diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), specifically complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Treatment with plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab led to haematological remission and partial restoration of renal function. This case demonstrates that C. canimorsus infection can serve as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults and describes the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, difficulties in differential diagnosis, and the complexities inherent in the clinical trajectory and therapeutic management of TMA post-sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow-growing, gram-negative bacillus commonly present in the oropharyngeal flora of cats and dogs. Infections in humans are infrequent and may be acquired through bites, scratches, or even close proximity to these animals. The spectrum of clinical symptoms is broad, encompassing everything from isolated cases of meningitis to acute, rapidly progressing sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although C. canimorsus generally shows susceptibility to many antibiotics, the mortality rate can reach as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is the one most commonly associated with serious zoonotic infections in humans. Due to the organism’s exacting growth requirements and its slow proliferation in culture, diagnosis is often postponed, leading to a greater reliance on advanced molecular diagnostic techniques such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the presented case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock accompanied by marked leukopaenia and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. While the early stages were primarily characterized by sepsis with multi-organ dysfunction, subsequent development of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and deterioration in renal function indicated a concurrent TMA. This progression illustrates how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be masked by the initial sepsis and DIC manifestations.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant medical history and two previous uncomplicated pregnancies came to the emergency department due to a rapidly deteriorating clinical state. Four days before presentation, she sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and was given a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. She was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) on the day of the bite, but did not begin antibiotic therapy because she was unable to collect the medication. Subsequently, she developed a high-grade fever (>40 °C), general malaise, decreased oral intake, and later noticed numbness and discoloration in her feet, prompting her to seek medical care. In a primary-care emergency setting, she was found to have hypoglycemia that did not respond to oral glucose or glucagon, reaching a low of 0.6 mmol/L, and was thus urgently transferred to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was awake and communicative, but exhibited clinical signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness of the lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no family history of similar illness or symptoms among relatives. Initial laboratory evaluations demonstrated pronounced systemic abnormalities, notably severe inflammatory response, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevations in liver enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas measurement demonstrated metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference <2.5 mmol/L). The patient continued to experience hypoglycaemia despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, attributed to sepsis. On presentation, urine output remained sufficient; urinalysis detected proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

In light of her clinical features—namely, fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—her presentation was considered indicative of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) resulting from bacteraemia and the early stages of sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3). Surgical etiologies for her peripheral mottling, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded following both physical examination and imaging studies. Given the critical nature of her illness, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to ensure coverage of organisms associated with canines, including Capnocytophaga species. Importantly, C. canimorsus infection was suspected from the beginning and subsequently confirmed seven days later on both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. The patient was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for heightened surveillance.

Hematology was engaged for evaluation owing to the presence of pancytopenia and coagulopathy. Considerations included acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was contemplated but deemed improbable given the findings of leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocyte count. Flow cytometry was conducted, and bone marrow biopsy was initially planned; however, as results were within normal limits—without evidence of increased CD34+ blasts or aberrant cell populations—the biopsy was not performed. The clinical picture remained consistent with sepsis. While pending further diagnostic clarification, the patient received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient became entirely anuric and her kidney function deteriorated quickly. Intensive intravenous fluid therapy and simultaneous use of loop diuretics were not effective. She went on to develop septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by fluid overload, necessitating continuous renal replacement therapy. Testing for autoimmune markers, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By the third ICU day, she manifested widespread cutaneous hemorrhage characterized by ecchymoses, as well as distal cyanosis and ischemic changes involving multiple toes and fingers, though peripheral pulses and sensation remained intact. These symptoms were indicative of microvascular thrombosis associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although her illness affected several organ systems, she did not require vasopressors aside from the stress-dose corticosteroids given during the early septic period. Her treatment also included infusions of albumin and additional blood products.

Following 8 days of inpatient care, she was relocated to the nephrology unit for continued management of dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and intravenous antibiotic administration. Upon arrival, the patient was hemodynamically stable but experienced complete absence of urine output, notable widespread edema, and several hematomas, though she remained alert and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was adjusted to intravenous ampicillin, and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin, in accordance with blood culture findings.

During her stay in nephrology, laboratory assessments demonstrated a trend towards reduced inflammation, presence of reactive leukocytosis, platelet count recovery, and stable hemoglobin levels. Renal panels continued to reveal persistent azotemia, while normalization of coagulation studies suggested improvement in the prior coagulopathy. A summary of her laboratory parameters at this stage is presented in Table 2.

Although there was initial clinical progress, her course was complicated by an abrupt isolated decline in hemoglobin (from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over 6 days) with no signs of bleeding or circulatory instability. Platelet counts remained low (30–40×10⁹/L), while undetectable haptoglobin and increased LDH concentrations were noted, consistent with ongoing hemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test returned positive for IgG, which prompted consideration of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. Treatment was initiated with high-dose prednisolone in addition to standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a 48-hour period. Since there was no improvement in the haemolytic process after corticosteroid therapy, and platelet counts persisted at low levels (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional investigations were necessary. Examination of the peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of MAHA and suggestive of TMA (see figure 2). The initial DAT result was probably a false positive attributable either to penicillin exposure or previous transfusions; repeat DAT subsequently returned negative.

Complement assessment and ADAMTS13 activity testing were performed. Both C3 and C4 levels measured using locally available assays were within normal ranges, and functional tests of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways were similarly unremarkable. ADAMTS13 activity results became available soon thereafter, with a value of 43.5%, thereby making a diagnosis of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) unlikely, since TTP is characterized by ADAMTS13 activity below 10%. During the interim, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as temporary treatment before administration of the initial dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count had risen to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, revealing severe acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli featuring fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, findings supportive of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The diagnostic clarity in this case was primarily established by the light microscopy findings depicted in figure 3. Neither immunofluorescence nor electron microscopy images are provided; immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposits, and electron microscopy found no indications of primary glomerular disease.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was ruled out on the basis of a negative stool PCR analysis.

After the patient underwent the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) procedures, spontaneous urine output was restored. Once the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (covering serogroup B)—were administered, induction therapy with eculizumab commenced at 900 mg intravenously each week for a total of four weeks. The scheduling of the vaccines’ second doses was set for eight weeks later in the outpatient setting. Since the vaccines had not been administered at least two weeks prior to the initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement evaluation was sent to the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. Due to logistical constraints and concerns for patient safety, three PEX sessions with Octaplas and one induction dose of eculizumab were carried out before sample collection. Functional complement assays demonstrated that the alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50) was significantly diminished at 6%, whereas complement factors H, I, and B levels remained within normal limits, and antifactor H antibodies tested negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After the intensive medical management, the patient’s overall condition is stable and her urine output is satisfactory. Renal function remains impaired, with a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she will be monitored through follow-up visits in the nephrology outpatient department (see figure 4). She is receiving maintenance therapy with eculizumab at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, maintained for four months after the initial course. Comprehensive genetic testing of the complement pathway did not reveal any pathogenic variants; the preliminary results were communicated verbally by the reference laboratory, though the official written report had not yet been received at submission. Given these findings, complement inhibition therapy is being maintained because of her significant disease severity, with plans to reassess therapy continuation should kidney function stabilize. The identification of pathogenic mutations in complement genes would support a more prolonged treatment duration with vigilant surveillance for recurrence. However, no such mutations were detected in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adult patients presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, the foremost concern is to rule out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which is characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Triage can be facilitated by clinical scoring tools such as PLASMIC while awaiting definitive laboratory confirmation, which remains the diagnostic standard. This patient had a PLASMIC score of 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and her ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, further discounting TTP as the underlying cause and allowing the diagnostic process to focus elsewhere.

Consideration was also given to STEC-HUS; however, PCR testing for Shiga toxin returned negative, and the lack of antecedent gastrointestinal symptoms and unremarkable microbiological findings diminished the probability of STEC-HUS.

The persistent hemolysis, schistocytes on blood film, requirement for dialysis due to AKI, and renal biopsy demonstrating features of thrombotic microangiopathy collectively pointed towards a diagnosis of complement-mediated HUS (CM-HUS) (figure 5). The diagnosis was reinforced by negative PCR testing for STEC and a brisk clinical response to eculizumab therapy. The patient’s otherwise unremarkable medical background argues against an inherent complement abnormality necessitating a secondary trigger, thereby supporting infection-associated CM-HUS as the most plausible etiology.

CM-HUS is diagnosed clinically and pathologically by excluding other causes, and complement concentrations (C3/C4) can remain within normal limits throughout the disease. It is important to recognize that normal C3 and C4 values do not rule out CM-HUS, as these levels may persist in the reference range during the illness. In this case, hemolysis was noted only after improvement in sepsis, which supports a diagnosis of infection-induced CM-HUS as opposed to an underlying condition.

For this patient, baseline complement studies (C3/C4) as well as ADAMTS13 activity were assessed immediately prior to the initial session of plasma exchange (PEX). More comprehensive complement assays were conducted eight days after the last PEX procedure and four days following the first administration of eculizumab. The detected reduction in alternative complement pathway activity is most likely attributable to therapeutic intervention, rather than to endogenous complement depletion or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ is the result of improper activation of the alternative complement pathway, often in individuals with a genetic susceptibility. Genetic alterations or autoantibodies that impact complement regulatory proteins—such as factors H, I, and B or the membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—can be found in approximately 50% to 70% of affected patients. Nonetheless, the condition exhibits incomplete penetrance, and clinical onset typically depends on the presence of external precipitating events, with common triggers including infections, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or specific drugs.

Although cases are infrequent, Capnocytophaga canimorsus is capable of stimulating complement pathways, especially in hosts with increased vulnerability. The organism exhibits variable susceptibility to serum; most strains are complement-sensitive, while some capsule-bearing isolates are partially resistant, underscoring the significance of complement in host defense mechanisms. This situation introduces a therapeutic dilemma: although complement activity is crucial for pathogen clearance, it may also provoke excessive endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in those predisposed.

The presented case contributes to the emerging literature that associates C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. One notable example reported by Bjørkto et al involved a patient with CM-HUS due to C. canimorsus who carried a variant in complement factor B and experienced a favorable response to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that previously documented case, our patient did not exhibit any identifiable pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement-related genes, supporting the notion of infection-induced CM-HUS occurring in the absence of a known hereditary complement defect.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Treating CM-HUS in the presence of ongoing infection involves particular complexities. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against complement component 5, is considered the first-line therapy for CM-HUS and significantly enhances haematological as well as renal outcomes, with greater benefit observed when administered promptly. Postponing eculizumab can result in suboptimal renal recovery and thus an increased chance of progression to end-stage renal disease.

While plasma exchange (PEX) is crucial in TTP, it provides only short-term relief in CM-HUS, largely by eliminating circulating complement proteins, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, but without addressing the fundamental complement abnormality. In the patient described, urine output improved after the last PEX session and prior to starting eculizumab, indicating only fleeting stabilization. Long-term improvement in blood and kidney parameters was observed only following complement inhibition.

An important therapeutic consideration is the use of complement inhibitors during active bacteraemia, as terminal complement activity is vital in protecting against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga. This raises apprehension about heightened infection risk related to eculizumab. In this scenario, eculizumab treatment commenced after the patient’s blood cultures had cleared, thereby reducing the possibility of uncontrolled infection. Ensuring immunization, providing prophylactic antibiotics, and maintaining vigilant surveillance are imperative when complement blockade is employed.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition are key factors influencing positive outcomes. In this case, the patient experienced haematologic remission along with partial improvement in renal function, in line with existing data that underscore renal status and the interval before eculizumab initiation as primary prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS caused by C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to consider infectious precipitants—including rare causes—in patients presenting with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and the use of molecular diagnostic techniques could facilitate detection of additional cases.
