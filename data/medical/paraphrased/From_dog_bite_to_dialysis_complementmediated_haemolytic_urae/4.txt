Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s, with no prior health issues, developed rapidly progressing sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her clinical course was marked by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which eventually led to complete anuria and a reliance on dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she initially responded to antibiotic therapy and supportive management, she subsequently experienced abrupt onset of isolated haemolysis along with ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Subsequent laboratory investigations and a renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Implementation of plasma exchange and eculizumab resulted in haematological remission and partial improvement in renal function. This report illustrates C. canimorsus infection as an unusual precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults and details the diagnostic evaluation, differential diagnostic challenges, and the multifaceted nature of managing TMA in the context of post-septic complications.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a gram-negative, encapsulated bacillus that grows slowly and constitutes part of the normal oropharyngeal microbiota in both dogs and cats. Infections in humans are uncommon and typically result from bites, scratches, or even close proximity to these animals. The clinical spectrum associated with this organism is diverse, encompassing presentations from isolated cases of meningitis to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiple organ dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although this pathogen is broadly responsive to antibiotics, mortality rates may be as high as 30%.

Among the Capnocytophaga genus, C. canimorsus is the species most frequently implicated in severe zoonotic infections in humans. The identification of this bacterium is frequently delayed due to its stringent growth requirements and protracted culture times, resulting in an increasing reliance on molecular diagnostic techniques, including real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the present case, infection with C. canimorsus initially manifested as septic shock with marked leukopaenia and coagulopathy consistent with DIC. Although early clinical findings were characterized by sepsis-induced multiorgan dysfunction, subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and a deterioration in acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated an additional TMA process. This underscores the potential for complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) to remain undetected during the initial stages of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department experiencing a rapid decline in her condition. Four days before hospital admission, she had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and was given a tetanus vaccination as a preventive measure. She was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) at the time of the injury, but did not begin taking the antibiotic because she was unable to pick up the medication. Over the subsequent days, she developed a high fever (exceeding 40 °C), malaise, and decreased oral intake, ultimately seeking medical help after noticing numbness and discoloration of her feet. In a primary-care emergency setting, she was discovered to have hypoglycaemia that was unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a lowest recorded value of 0.6 mmol/L, which led to her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and interactive, but exhibited clinical signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness of the lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of similar illness among her family members. Initial laboratory investigations indicated significant systemic disturbances, characterized by pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, AKI, elevated hepatic enzymes, and coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas analysis identified a metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). The patient exhibited persistent hypoglycaemia despite the administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, attributed to the ongoing septic process. On presentation, diuresis was preserved, and urinalysis was notable for proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Given her array of clinical signs—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by AKI and lactic acidosis—the diagnosis was considered to be DIC resulting from bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1/3). Surgical etiologies for her mottled limbs, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded following both physical examination and imaging studies. Owing to the critical nature of her presentation, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated in order to provide coverage for canine-associated pathogens, specifically targeting Capnocytophaga species. Importantly, suspicion for C. canimorsus was raised from the beginning and subsequently confirmed 7 days later through both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. The patient was then transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced observation and management.

In view of the pancytopenia and coagulopathy, a haematology consultation was sought. The differential diagnosis encompassed acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also entertained, though ultimately deemed unlikely in light of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin levels, and an absence of a compensatory reticulocyte response. Flow cytometry was undertaken and a bone marrow biopsy scheduled; however, findings of normal flow cytometry, with no rise in CD34+ blasts or aberrant cell populations, led to the decision not to proceed with biopsy. The clinical scenario was most consistent with sepsis. In the interim, platelet and fibrinogen transfusions were provided for supportive care while awaiting definitive results.

Upon admission to the ICU, the patient developed complete anuria and her renal function deteriorated rapidly. Efforts to manage her condition with aggressive intravenous fluids and simultaneous use of loop diuretics were ineffective. She progressed to septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by volume overload, necessitating continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), returned negative results.

By the third day of her ICU stay, she manifested generalized cutaneous hemorrhage, ecchymoses, and distal cyanosis and ischemia of multiple toes and fingers, while distal pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical signs indicated microthrombotic processes attributable to DIC. Although her illness involved multiple organ systems, she did not require vasopressors apart from stress-dose corticosteroids provided during the initial septic event. In addition, her treatment included infusions of albumin and various blood products.

Following an 8-day hospital stay, the patient was relocated to the nephrology ward for continued management of dialysis-dependent AKI and intravenous antibiotic treatment. At the time of transfer, she remained clinically stable but was anuric, exhibiting generalized edema and several hematomas, though she was alert and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin in accordance with blood culture findings.

While in the nephrology ward, laboratory investigations revealed a downward trend in inflammatory markers, presence of reactive leukocytosis, platelet count recovery, and stable hemoglobin levels. Renal function tests, however, continued to indicate azotaemia, while coagulation studies returned to within normal limits, suggesting resolution of her coagulopathy. Table 2 provides a summary of her laboratory data during this period.

Despite early signs of clinical improvement, her condition subsequently deteriorated, evidenced by an abrupt decline in haemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days) in the absence of overt bleeding or haemodynamic compromise. Thrombocytopenia persisted, with platelet counts between 30–40×10⁹/L. Concurrently, haptoglobin dropped to undetectable levels and LDH increased, collectively signifying continued haemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, prompting consideration of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia as a diagnosis. High-dose prednisolone therapy commenced together with standard prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions within a 48-hour period. Persistence of haemolysis despite corticosteroid intervention, along with persistently reduced platelet counts (41–46 × 10⁹/L), necessitated further diagnostic investigation. Examination of a peripheral blood smear identified 15%–20% schistocytes, indicating the presence of MAHA consistent with TMA (figure 2). It was determined that the initial positive DAT was probably a false positive result related to prior penicillin administration or transfusions, as a subsequent DAT was negative.

Complement panels and ADAMTS13 activity were submitted for evaluation. Locally conducted complement tests (C3/C4) showed normal findings, and assessments of classical, alternative, and lectin pathway functionality were also unremarkable. The ADAMTS13 activity was soon reported as 43.5%, thereby excluding a diagnosis of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which is typically characterized by activity less than 10%. In the interim, pending laboratory results, plasma exchange (PEX) was initiated as an interim therapy before administration of the initial dose of eculizumab.

After the thrombocyte count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation—findings consistent with TMA.

The most illustrative diagnostic evidence in this scenario was provided by the light microscopy images depicted in figure 3. Neither immunofluorescence nor electron microscopy findings are included; the former identified no immune complex deposition, while the latter revealed no signs indicative of a primary glomerulopathy.

A diagnosis of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was excluded, as stool PCR testing was negative.

After the second and third PEX procedures, the patient’s ability to produce urine without assistance was restored. Eculizumab induction therapy was started (900 mg intravenously per week for 4 weeks) after the initial administration of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B). The follow-up doses of these vaccines were scheduled to be administered as outpatient procedures eight weeks later. Because the vaccines were not given at least two weeks prior to the commencement of complement inhibition, Apocillin was prescribed prophylactically.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated TMA, comprehensive complement assays were conducted at Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden). For reasons related to both practical considerations and the patient’s safety, three PEX treatments with Octaplas and a single eculizumab induction dose were performed prior to obtaining blood samples. Functional complement evaluation demonstrated a significantly decreased alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50 at 6%), while levels of complement components H, I, and B were within normal ranges, and testing for antifactor H antibodies yielded negative results.

Outcome and follow-up:

After an intensive and multifaceted medical treatment, the patient is currently in stable overall health, exhibiting satisfactory urine output. Her renal function, however, remains compromised, as indicated by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L. Ongoing monitoring is planned in the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). She is still receiving maintenance eculizumab at a dosage of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, scheduled to continue for four months following induction therapy. Comprehensive genetic testing for complement pathway abnormalities did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; these findings were relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, although the official written report was not yet available at the time of submission. Given these results, complement inhibition therapy is being maintained due to the significant severity of her presentation, with a future review to consider cessation should kidney function remain stable. The detection of disease-causing complement gene variants would support prolonged therapy and careful monitoring for potential relapse. In this case, however, no such genetic mutations were found.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs include a spectrum of intersecting clinical syndromes.

In adults presenting with a combination of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost step is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is marked by a pronounced deficiency in ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). Scoring systems such as PLASMIC provide a clinical framework for risk assessment while definitive laboratory results are pending. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4/7, indicating a low pretest likelihood for TTP, while an ADAMTS13 activity of 43.5% effectively excluded TTP and informed the direction of further evaluation.

STEC-HUS also constitutes a critical differential. Shiga toxin was not detected in this patient via PCR, and the absence of gastrointestinal prodrome combined with negative microbiological assays argued strongly against a diagnosis of STEC-HUS.

The combination of ongoing haemolysis, schistocytosis, dialysis-requiring AKI, and TMA-consistent findings on kidney biopsy ultimately led to the diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). This conclusion was further bolstered by the exclusion of STEC based on PCR results and the patient’s swift improvement following initiation of eculizumab. Additionally, the lack of notable past medical issues diminishes the likelihood of a latent complement disorder requiring a 'second hit' mechanism, and instead points toward infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is identified based on clinical and pathological criteria after other diagnoses have been excluded, and complement measurements (C3/C4) may remain within normal limits throughout the illness. Significantly, the presence of normal C3/C4 levels does not rule out CM-HUS, as these values may not deviate from the norm at any stage of the disease. In this case, the onset of haemolysis occurred only after the patient’s sepsis began to resolve, pointing toward infection-induced CM-HUS rather than an underlying, chronic condition.

For this patient, standard complement assays (C3/C4) and evaluation of ADAMTS13 activity were conducted just prior to the initiation of the first PEX session. Further, expanded complement analyses took place eight days following the last PEX procedure and four days post-administration of the initial eculizumab dose. The decrease observed in the alternative pathway activity aligns most closely with therapeutic intervention effects, rather than reflecting an inherent deficiency or upregulation of complement activity.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS (previously known as ‘atypical HUS’) results from improper activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently due to underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of affected individuals possess genetic alterations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulators such as factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nevertheless, the condition exhibits incomplete penetrance, and overt clinical symptoms often emerge only after an external precipitant, most commonly infections, pregnancy, autoimmune processes, or exposure to certain drugs.

While uncommon, C. canimorsus has the capacity to stimulate complement pathways, especially in individuals with heightened vulnerability. Importantly, C. canimorsus demonstrates heterogeneity in serum susceptibility: many isolates are sensitive to complement-mediated killing, yet some capsule-bearing strains exhibit partial resistance, underscoring the significance of complement in immune defence. This dynamic generates a clinical dilemma: although complement activation facilitates pathogen clearance, it can simultaneously provoke excessive endothelial injury and TMA in genetically susceptible individuals.

The present case contributes to the expanding literature associating C. canimorsus with complement-driven TMAs. For example, a report by Bjørkto et al detailed a case of CM-HUS triggered by C. canimorsus in a patient harboring a variant in complement factor B, who improved following eculizumab therapy. Unlike that previously described case, our patient did not carry pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene variants, indicating a scenario of infection-driven CM-HUS without apparent genetic complement dysregulation.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection involves distinct complexities. Eculizumab—a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5—remains the primary therapy for CM-HUS, demonstrating significant benefits for both haematological and renal recovery, particularly if administered promptly. Delays in treatment initiation are linked to suboptimal renal recuperation and an increased likelihood of developing end-stage kidney disease.

Conversely, while PEX is essential for patient survival in TTP, its utility in CM-HUS is largely limited to temporary improvements. The primary mechanism is the removal of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory agents, or autoantibodies, but this intervention fails to address the fundamental dysfunction in the complement system. In the described patient, urine output resumed only after the last PEX treatments but prior to starting eculizumab, suggesting short-lived stabilisation. Enduring improvement in blood parameters and kidney function occurred solely after complement inhibition.

A principal clinical challenge is the administration of complement inhibitors during ongoing bacteraemia, since terminal complement components play a vital role in host defence against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga. This raises important safety considerations regarding eculizumab-induced vulnerability to infections. In this scenario, eculizumab was started only after negative blood cultures were confirmed, thereby reducing the potential for worsening infection. Preventive strategies—including vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring—remain crucial when treating patients with complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition are critical factors associated with improved prognosis. In our case, the patient attained haematological remission and demonstrated partial recovery of renal function, in alignment with existing data indicating that both baseline renal status and the interval to eculizumab initiation are central prognostic determinants.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus infection remains exceedingly uncommon, this case underscores the necessity of evaluating infectious etiologies—even rare ones—in individuals presenting with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance, coupled with advances in molecular diagnostics, may facilitate the recognition of additional cases.
