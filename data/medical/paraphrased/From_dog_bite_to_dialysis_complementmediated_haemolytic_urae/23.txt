Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical conditions presented with rapidly advancing sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury that worsened to complete anuria, ultimately requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia. Although she initially responded clinically to antibiotics and supportive measures, she subsequently experienced abrupt isolated hemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopenia. Laboratory analysis and renal biopsy confirmed a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), with a diagnosis of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS). After undergoing plasma exchange and beginning treatment with eculizumab, she achieved hematologic remission and her renal function showed partial improvement. This report illustrates that C. canimorsus infection can act as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in previously healthy adults, and details the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the difficulties encountered in distinguishing between potential diagnoses, and the intricacies of managing TMA subsequent to sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is commonly present in the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Infections in humans are infrequent, with transmission possible through bites, scratches, or even mere close interaction. The spectrum of clinical manifestations is broad, encompassing isolated meningitis as well as fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although this organism is generally susceptible to multiple antibiotics, the mortality rate can reach as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often associated with invasive zoonotic infections in people. Its challenging and slow growth on culture media can delay diagnosis, which has led to an increased reliance on molecular diagnostics, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In our patient, the infection with C. canimorsus was characterized by septic shock, marked leukopaenia, and coagulopathy consistent with DIC. While sepsis-driven involvement of multiple organ systems predominated the initial phase, subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and progressive acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated an overlapping TMA. This underscores the possibility that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may not be apparent during the initial presentation dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, with no prior health issues and a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department due to a swiftly deteriorating clinical state. She had sustained a dog bite on her upper lip four days earlier and received prophylactic tetanus vaccination. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed on the day of the bite, but she did not start antibiotic therapy as she was unable to obtain her prescription. Subsequently, she developed a high fever (exceeding 40 °C), general malaise, decreased oral intake, and later experienced numbness and discoloration in her feet, prompting her to seek further medical evaluation. At a primary-care emergency visit, she was discovered to be hypoglycaemic, with low blood sugar unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a minimum level of 0.6 mmol/L. This led to her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was awake and interactive but demonstrated signs of sepsis, including mottled and cool skin over the lower limbs. She denied taking any herbal supplements or other substances, and there was no history of illness or comparable symptoms among family members. Initial laboratory investigations indicated significant systemic abnormalities, including pronounced inflammatory markers, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas measurement revealed metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.27 (reference range: 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (<2.5 mmol/L). She continued to experience hypoglycemia in spite of administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, attributed to the ongoing sepsis. Upon presentation, diuresis remained sufficient; urinalysis demonstrated proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her array of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis, as indicated by a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. After both clinical examination and radiological assessment, surgical causes for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded. Owing to the serious nature of her presentation, empirical intravenous therapy with broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to target pathogens associated with canine contact, including Capnocytophaga species. Specifically, C. canimorsus was considered from the outset and, seven days later, was confirmed through both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. The patient was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced observation.

Consultation with haematology was sought because of pancytopenia and coagulopathy. Potential diagnoses included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also considered, but was thought less probable due to findings of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and absence of compensatory reticulocytosis. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted and a bone marrow biopsy was scheduled; nevertheless, normal flow cytometry—showing neither increased CD34+ blast population nor abnormal phenotypes—resulted in cancellation of the biopsy. Clinically, her presentation was compatible with sepsis. During the period of diagnostic evaluation, supportive transfusions of thrombocytes and fibrinogen were provided.

Upon admission to the ICU, the patient developed complete anuria with a marked and swift decline in kidney function. Efforts at intensive fluid resuscitation combined with the use of loop diuretics did not yield improvement. Subsequently, she experienced septic cardiomyopathy complicated by volume overload, necessitating commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies for antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA) were both negative.

By the third ICU day, the patient manifested widespread cutaneous bleeding characterized by ecchymoses and distal ischemic as well as cyanotic changes involving multiple toes and fingers, while distal pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical features were indicative of microthrombotic events associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). In spite of involvement of multiple organ systems, she never needed vasopressors except for the stress-dose corticosteroids administered during her early septic course. She was also treated with infusions of albumin and other blood products.

Following eight days in the hospital, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit due to ongoing dialysis-requiring acute kidney injury and continued need for intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, she was hemodynamically stable but remained anuric with generalized edema and several hematomas, though she was awake and oriented. Based on results from blood cultures, her antibiotic regimen was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently transitioned to oral Apocillin.

During her stay on the nephrology service, laboratory findings indicated an improving inflammatory response, with reactive leukocytosis, a trend toward platelet recovery, and stable hemoglobin values. Renal indices still reflected persistent azotemia, although coagulation studies returned to normal, suggesting resolution of the coagulopathy. Laboratory data at this stage are presented in Table 2.

Despite this initial progress, her clinical status shifted with the onset of a sudden, isolated hemoglobin decrease (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days) in the absence of clinical signs of bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), and she developed undetectable haptoglobin with elevated LDH levels, all of which pointed to ongoing hemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT, also known as the Coombs test) yielded a positive result for IgG, prompting consideration of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was initiated together with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over the course of 2 days. Due to lack of response to corticosteroid therapy and persistent thrombocytopenia (platelet counts of 41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional diagnostic work-up was performed. Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified 15%–20% schistocytes, a finding characteristic of MAHA and indicative of TMA (see figure 2). It is possible that the initial DAT positivity resulted from penicillin use or earlier transfusions; subsequent repeat testing returned negative.

Complement testing and ADAMTS13 activity assays were ordered. Locally available standard complement assays (C3 and C4) fell within reference ranges, and assessments of functional activity across classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways were similarly unremarkable. Shortly thereafter, ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, thus excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP is usually associated with ADAMTS13 activity levels below 10%. While awaiting these laboratory findings, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced to bridge the gap before starting eculizumab therapy.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which demonstrated marked acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia with the presence of fibrin thrombi, and some crescents, findings indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The most conclusive diagnostic information was provided by the light microscopy, as illustrated in figure 3. Immunofluorescence findings are not displayed, as this method did not detect any immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy is also not shown because it failed to reveal features suggestive of a primary glomerulopathy.

Stool PCR analysis was negative, thus excluding Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) as a diagnosis.

After the patient underwent the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), spontaneous urine output resumed. Once the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B)—had been administered, induction treatment with eculizumab began (900 mg intravenously once per week for four weeks). The administration of the second vaccine doses was scheduled to occur eight weeks later in an outpatient setting. As vaccination had not been completed at least two weeks prior to initiation of complement blockade, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement assessment was sent to Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. Due to considerations related to logistical factors and patient safety, three PEX sessions with Octaplas and a single induction dose of eculizumab were performed ahead of sample collection. The functional complement tests included quantitative assessment of alternative pathway activity (AH50), which demonstrated a significant reduction (6%), although the levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing extensive medical treatment, the patient is currently stable overall and maintains satisfactory urine output. Renal function remains compromised, as indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and ongoing follow-up will be conducted through the nephrology outpatient service (see figure 4). Eculizumab maintenance therapy (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks) has been continued for 4 months after induction. Comprehensive complement genetic screening did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; this information was conveyed orally by the reference laboratory, while the official written report was still pending at the time of submission. Given these findings and the patient's severe clinical presentation, complement inhibition therapy is ongoing, with reassessment planned to consider discontinuation if kidney function stabilizes. Detection of pathogenic variants in complement genes would support prolonged therapy alongside vigilant monitoring for recurrence, but such variants were not observed in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

The foremost concern when adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by markedly reduced ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). While awaiting laboratory confirmation, clinical scoring systems such as PLASMIC can provide interim risk assessment. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicative of a low likelihood of TTP, and measured ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, further reducing suspicion for TTP and guiding subsequent evaluation.

STEC-HUS was also considered in the differential diagnosis. However, Shiga toxin testing by PCR was negative. The lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and negative microbiological assays made STEC-HUS an unlikely etiology.

The diagnosis of CM-HUS was supported by ongoing haemolysis, schistocytes, dialysis-dependent AKI, and kidney biopsy findings consistent with TMA (figure 5). Additional evidence included negative PCR testing for STEC and her prompt clinical response following initiation of eculizumab treatment. Her largely unremarkable past medical history argues against an underlying complement abnormality requiring an additional trigger, and instead supports infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is diagnosed clinically and pathologically after ruling out other causes, and complement levels (C3 and C4) can remain within normal limits throughout the illness. Therefore, having normal complement values does not rule out CM-HUS, as they may stay within normal ranges during disease progression. Of note, haemolysis developed only once the sepsis started resolving, which is more indicative of an infection-induced CM-HUS instead of an existing condition.

In this case, standard complement assays (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were assessed immediately prior to the initial PEX treatment. Expanded complement analysis occurred eight days following the last PEX procedure and four days after the patient's initial administration of eculizumab. The decrease seen in the alternative pathway is best attributed to therapeutic intervention rather than underlying complement exhaustion or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously known as “atypical HUS,” results from unregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, typically in individuals with a genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50% to 70% of patients possess either genetic variants or autoantibodies that impair complement regulatory proteins such as factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Despite these risk factors, the condition has incomplete penetrance, and onset generally requires an external precipitating factor, most often an infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disorder, or specific medications.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus has only infrequently been implicated in complement pathway activation, a phenomenon mainly observed in vulnerable populations. The bacterium exhibits variable susceptibility to serum complement, with many strains being complement sensitive, while certain encapsulated variants exhibit partial resistance; this underscores the key defensive role of complement in host responses. Consequently, there is a clinical dilemma: complement activation aids in bacterial clearance but may also provoke uncontrolled endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in susceptible patients.

The present case contributes to the limited but expanding literature associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. One instructive case report by Bjørkto et al detailed a patient who developed CM-HUS from C. canimorsus infection and harbored a variant in complement factor B, ultimately responding to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that instance, our patient did not have identifiable pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement genes, which is consistent with a diagnosis of infection-induced CM-HUS in the absence of confirmed hereditary complement dysfunction.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS when concurrent infection is present poses specific difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed at terminal complement component 5, is established as first-line therapy for CM-HUS and significantly enhances both hematological and renal outcomes, especially when administration occurs promptly. Initiating treatment late correlates with partial renal recovery and an increased likelihood of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

PEX, although lifesaving in TTP, offers only short-term improvement in CM-HUS, primarily through the elimination of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, without addressing the root dysfunction in the complement cascade. In this patient, diuresis resumed following the last PEX procedures but before receiving eculizumab, indicating only temporary stabilization. Persistent hematological and renal restoration was observed solely after complement inhibition was achieved.

A significant therapeutic challenge involves deploying complement inhibition in the context of ongoing bacteremia, since terminal complement factors are essential for protection against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, heightening concern for infections related to eculizumab therapy. In the present case, eculizumab was started once blood cultures had cleared, thereby reducing the risk of exacerbating infection. When using complement inhibitors, measures such as immunization, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant surveillance are crucial.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition represent the key factors associated with positive patient outcomes. In this case, the patient experienced hematologic remission and partial restoration of kidney function, aligning with current literature indicating that baseline renal function and the interval prior to eculizumab administration are primary prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this case emphasizes the need to evaluate infectious causes—including atypical pathogens—in individuals with newly diagnosed TMA. Greater clinical vigilance and advances in molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate the recognition of additional similar cases.
