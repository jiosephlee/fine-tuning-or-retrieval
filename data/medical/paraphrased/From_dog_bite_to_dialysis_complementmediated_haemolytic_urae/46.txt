Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical history presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after being bitten by a dog. She experienced disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete loss of urine output requiring dialysis. Blood cultures grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus, confirming bacteremia. Although she initially improved with antibiotics and supportive therapy, she subsequently developed acute isolated hemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopenia. Laboratory data and renal biopsy demonstrated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated hemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Treatment with plasma exchange followed by eculizumab induced hematologic remission and led to partial recovery of renal function. This report underscores Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection as an uncommon cause of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, and details the diagnostic evaluation, challenges encountered in differential diagnosis, and the complex clinical trajectory and management in cases of TMA arising after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and resides in the normal oropharyngeal microbiota of dogs and cats. Human infection with this organism is uncommon and may be acquired through bites, scratches, or close contact with animals. Clinical manifestations are highly diverse, with presentations ranging from isolated cases of meningitis to severe sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although C. canimorsus demonstrates wide susceptibility to antibiotics, mortality rates can reach up to 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in severe zoonotic infections in humans. Its demanding growth requirements and slow culture characteristics can delay identification, leading to greater utilization of molecular diagnostics such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the patient described, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock accompanied by marked leukopaenia and coagulation abnormalities consistent with DIC. The early phase was notable for multiorgan involvement attributable to sepsis, while later emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed toward superimposed TMA. This progression underscores the potential for complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) to be masked during the initial stages of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department exhibiting a rapidly deteriorating clinical status. She had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip four days before presentation and was given a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) had been prescribed on the day of the bite, but she did not begin the antibiotic because she was unable to pick up her prescription. She subsequently experienced a high fever (exceeding 40 °C), malaise, decreased oral intake, and eventually developed numbness and discoloration of her feet, prompting her to seek medical care. In primary care, she was found to have hypoglycaemia unresponsive to oral glucose and glucagon, with her lowest blood glucose measured at 0.6 mmol/L. This led to her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was awake and able to communicate, though she exhibited signs of sepsis with cool, mottled skin on her lower limbs. She denied the use of herbal products or other substances, and there were no reports of similar illness among her family members. Initial laboratory evaluations indicated significant systemic abnormalities, including pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas demonstrated a metabolic acidosis, with a pH measured at 7.27 (normal range: 7.35–7.43), and a raised lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference: <2.5 mmol/L). The patient continued to have hypoglycaemia despite administration of large amounts of intravenous glucose, believed to be secondary to sepsis. At the time of presentation, her urine output was sufficient; urinalysis indicated proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

In light of her clinical picture—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—her condition was considered as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis, as reflected by a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. A potential surgical etiology for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded following both clinical assessment and radiological studies. Owing to the gravity of her presentation, empiric intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics with piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) were initiated to target pathogens associated with dog exposure, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was strongly suspected at initial presentation and subsequently identified in both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures after 7 days. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced surveillance.

The presence of pancytopenia and coagulopathy prompted a consultation with haematology. Differential diagnoses considered encompassed acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Although haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was entertained, it was deemed improbable given leukopaenia, normal inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocyte levels. Flow cytometry was performed, and bone marrow biopsy initially planned; however, these were ultimately abandoned after results showed no elevation in CD34+ blasts or abnormal cellular phenotypes. The clinical scenario aligned most closely with sepsis. Supportive therapy with thrombocyte and fibrinogen transfusions was provided during the interim pending further diagnostic results.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient developed complete anuria accompanied by a swift decline in renal function. Efforts with vigorous intravenous fluids combined with loop diuretics were ineffective. She went on to experience septic cardiomyopathy and fluid overload, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including ANA and ANCA, were negative.

By ICU day 3, she manifested widespread cutaneous bleeding, the appearance of ecchymoses, and distal cyanotic and ischemic changes involving multiple fingers and toes, although distal pulses and neurological function remained intact. These clinical signs were indicative of microvascular thrombotic events in the setting of DIC. Although multiple organs were implicated, she did not require any vasopressor support apart from the stress-dose corticosteroids used early in her septic illness. She was additionally treated with infusions of albumin and other blood product support.

Eight days into her hospital stay, the patient was moved to the nephrology ward to continue treatment with dialysis for AKI requiring renal replacement therapy and to receive intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival, she was clinically stable but anuric, exhibited generalized edema and several hematomas, and remained alert and oriented. Her antibiotics were narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, followed by conversion to oral Apocillin according to blood culture findings.

Laboratory assessments in the nephrology unit revealed improving markers of inflammation, a reactive leukocytosis, platelet recovery, and stable hemoglobin values. Renal chemistry tests continued to show persistent azotemia, whereas coagulation indices had normalized, pointing toward resolution of her prior coagulopathy. Following her initial improvement, there was a shift in her clinical status characterized by an abrupt, isolated decline in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days), occurring in the absence of overt bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently suppressed (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin was no longer detectable, and LDH concentrations increased, signifying ongoing hemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, indicating the possibility of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was commenced, in addition to standard prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over a span of two days. As haemolysis did not improve following corticosteroid therapy and platelet counts persisted at a low level (41–46 × 10⁹/L), further diagnostic workup was considered necessary. Examination of a peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, supporting the diagnosis of MAHA, suggestive of TMA (figure 2). The initial DAT result was probably a false positive, potentially attributable to penicillin administration or previous transfusions; subsequent testing was negative.

Complement levels and ADAMTS13 activity were sent for assessment. Routine complement tests available locally (C3/C4) were found to be within the normal range, and functional evaluations of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways were similarly unremarkable. Shortly thereafter, ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, effectively excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP usually manifests with values below 10%. During the waiting period for these investigations, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as interim treatment prior to initiating eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was carried out, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia with fibrin thrombi, and focal crescent formation, all findings indicative of TMA.

The key diagnostic features were most clearly visualized with light microscopy, as presented in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy images are omitted, given that immunofluorescence did not demonstrate any immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy failed to reveal signs of primary glomerular disease.

A diagnosis of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was excluded based on a negative result from the stool PCR assay.

After the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), the patient began to produce urine spontaneously again. The initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B)—were administered prior to initiating eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks). Plans were made to administer the second vaccine doses as an outpatient, eight weeks later. As the vaccines had not been given two weeks prior to complement inhibition, Apocillin was prescribed for prophylaxis.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement testing was ordered from Skåne Laboratory at Lund University in Sweden. For reasons related to logistics and patient safety, three plasma exchange sessions with Octaplas and one initial eculizumab dose were performed before collecting samples. Functional complement analysis measured alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which was significantly decreased (6%), while levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal ranges, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical therapy, the patient is presently in satisfactory overall health and demonstrates sufficient urine production. Her renal function remains compromised, as reflected by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she is scheduled for ongoing evaluation in the nephrology outpatient department (figure 4). Eculizumab is being administered as maintenance therapy at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, maintained for four months following induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis targeting complement system components did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; these findings were relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, with the official written report not yet available at the time of manuscript submission. Considering the severity of her clinical presentation, complement inhibition therapy is being maintained, with future plans to reevaluate its necessity if kidney function stabilizes. If pathogenic variants in complement-related genes had been found, prolonged treatment accompanied by vigilant surveillance for recurrence would have been advised; however, such mutations were absent in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost task is to rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by pronounced ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Clinical scoring systems such as PLASMIC are helpful for risk stratification while awaiting laboratory confirmation, which remains the gold standard for diagnosis. The PLASMIC score for this patient was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, thereby making TTP improbable and guiding subsequent investigations.

STEC-HUS represents another differential diagnosis. Shiga toxin PCR in this patient was negative. Additionally, the absence of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and negative microbiological results further oppose a diagnosis of STEC-HUS.

Eventually, the combination of ongoing haemolysis, schistocyte presence, dialysis-dependent AKI, and kidney biopsy demonstrating TMA was consistent with CM-HUS (figure 5). Exclusion of STEC via PCR and the patient’s rapid improvement upon initiation of eculizumab therapy reinforced this diagnosis. Her minimal past medical history diminishes the possibility of an underlying complement abnormality requiring a secondary trigger, instead supporting an infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS represents a clinicopathological diagnosis that requires ruling out other conditions, and complement measurements (C3/C4) may stay within normal limits throughout the illness. It is critical to note that normal complement values (C3/C4) do not rule out CM-HUS, as these values can remain in the normal range during the disease course. Of note, hemolysis manifested only after improvement of sepsis, supporting a hypothesis of infection-induced CM-HUS rather than an underlying disorder.

For this patient, standard complement assays (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were assessed immediately prior to the first session of plasma exchange (PEX). Expanded complement testing was carried out eight days following the last PEX session and four days after the initial administration of eculizumab. The detected decrease in alternative pathway activity is most likely attributable to the effects of therapy, rather than representing baseline complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS, previously known as ‘atypical HUS,’ results from abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway, typically in individuals with an underlying genetic susceptibility. Genetic mutations or autoantibodies affecting complement regulatory components—including factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are found in 50–70% of cases. Despite this, not all carriers develop disease, as a secondary stimulus, commonly an infection, pregnancy, autoimmune condition, or particular drugs, is usually necessary for clinical expression.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus has infrequently been documented as a complement pathway activator, especially among at-risk patients. This organism demonstrates varying sensitivity to serum; while many strains are susceptible to complement-mediated lysis, capsule-forming variants exhibit partial resistance. Thus, complement activation is integral to host defense mechanisms. Clinically, this creates a dilemma: although complement helps eradicate the bacteria, it may also provoke unchecked endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in those predisposed.

The present case contributes to a limited but expanding set of reports associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMA. A report by Bjørkto et al described a patient whose CM-HUS induced by C. canimorsus was linked to a complement factor B variant and responded to eculizumab therapy. Differing from that scenario, our patient did not have detectable pathogenic or likely pathogenic genetic alterations in complement-related genes, lending support to the concept of infection-induced CM-HUS in the absence of identified inherited complement dysregulation.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection presents distinct complexities. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against the terminal complement protein C5, is considered the treatment of choice for CM-HUS and has been shown to significantly enhance haematological and renal prognoses, especially when administered promptly. Postponed therapy is linked to partial renal recovery and therefore an increased likelihood of developing end-stage kidney disease.

By comparison, PEX is crucial for survival in TTP, yet in CM-HUS its effects are short-lived and mainly due to elimination of circulating complement byproducts, inflammatory substances, or autoantibodies, without addressing the underlying complement abnormality. In the present patient, urine output resumed following the last PEX procedures but prior to receiving eculizumab, indicating only brief stabilization. Enduring improvements in blood and kidney parameters occurred only following administration of complement inhibition.

A central challenge in therapy involves the administration of complement inhibition during active bacteraemia, since terminal complement proteins are vital in host defence against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, raising specific infectious risk concerns associated with eculizumab. In this scenario, eculizumab was begun once blood cultures were clear, thus reducing the possibility of uncontrolled infection. Continued use of vaccines, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant clinical monitoring are imperative when employing complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition are critical factors influencing positive outcomes. In this case, the patient attained hematological remission with some improvement in kidney function, aligning with existing data indicating that renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab treatment are key prognostic factors.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus infection is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of evaluating infectious causes, even rare ones, in individuals presenting with newly diagnosed TMA. Greater clinical awareness and the use of molecular diagnostic tools may facilitate detection of additional similar cases.
