Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: An otherwise healthy woman in her late 40s presented with rapidly advancing sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She experienced disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, ultimately resulting in complete anuria and a requirement for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she initially improved clinically with antibiotic therapy and supportive measures, she later developed abrupt, isolated haemolysis accompanied by persistent thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory assessments and findings from a renal biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), specifically complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Treatment with plasma exchange and subsequent administration of eculizumab led to haematological remission and partial restoration of renal function. This report illustrates C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults and details the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the complexities associated with differential diagnosis, and the challenges of managing TMA that develops subsequent to sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, slowly proliferating gram-negative bacillus that exists as a normal component of the oral flora in dogs and cats. Although infections in humans are uncommon, they may arise through animal bites, scratches, or even close physical proximity. The clinical spectrum is broad, encompassing isolated cases of meningitis as well as rapid-onset sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multi-organ dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). While the bacterium generally responds to a wide range of antibiotics, reported mortality rates can be as high as 30%.

Among the Capnocytophaga genus, C. canimorsus is the species most often associated with invasive zoonotic infections in people. Its demanding culture requirements and protracted growth can contribute to diagnostic delays, leading to a growing reliance on molecular diagnostic techniques, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the case presented, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock, pronounced leukopaenia, and coagulopathy consistent with DIC. Initial management was complicated by extensive sepsis-related organ dysfunction; however, subsequent findings of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), a drop in platelet count, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) were consistent with an overlaying TMA. This progression illustrates how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may initially be concealed during the sepsis and DIC phase.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department due to a rapidly deteriorating clinical status. Four days before admission, she sustained a dog bite on her upper lip and received a prophylactic tetanus vaccination. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed on the day of the bite, she did not begin the antibiotic regimen because she was unable to collect the medication. Subsequently, she experienced a high fever (exceeding 40 °C), malaise, and decreased oral intake. She later developed numbness and discoloration of her feet, which led her to seek medical care. In the primary-care emergency setting, she was found to be hypoglycemic, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, with a lowest recorded blood glucose of 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating emergency transfer to our facility.

Upon presentation, the patient was awake and cooperative but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottled and cool lower limbs. She denied using herbal products or other substances, and there was no family history of similar illnesses. Initial laboratory studies demonstrated profound systemic abnormalities with pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated liver enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas testing indicated metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a raised lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, she continued to experience hypoglycemia, presumed secondary to sepsis. On admission, urine output was sufficient; urinalysis detected protein at 2+ and blood at 3+.

Owing to her array of symptoms—which included fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—her clinical picture was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [qSOFA] score 1/3). After thorough clinical assessment and imaging, surgical etiologies for the mottling of her extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded. Given the seriousness of her presentation, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to cover pathogens associated with dogs, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was strongly suspected from the outset and subsequently confirmed after seven days on both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for heightened observation.

Because of her pancytopenia and coagulation abnormalities, the haematology team was consulted. The differential diagnoses encompassed acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Although haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also considered, it was deemed improbable given the presence of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and non-compensatory reticulocyte counts. Flow cytometry was conducted, and a bone marrow biopsy was initially planned; however, as results revealed no increase in CD34+ blasts or abnormal immunophenotypes, the biopsy was not pursued. The overall presentation clinically supported sepsis. In the interim while awaiting test results, the patient was managed supportively with transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Following her admission to the ICU, the patient became entirely anuric and exhibited a marked decline in renal function. Attempts at aggressive intravenous fluid administration in conjunction with loop diuretic therapy proved ineffective. Subsequently, she developed septic cardiomyopathy complicated by volume overload, necessitating the commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Immunological screening—including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) tests—was negative.

By the third ICU day, she presented with extensive cutaneous bleeding characterized by ecchymoses, as well as distal cyanosis and ischemia involving multiple toes and fingers, though distal pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical signs were indicative of microvascular thrombosis associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Despite the extent of organ dysfunction, vasopressor therapy was limited to stress-dose corticosteroids given during the early septic episode. She also received albumin infusions and various other blood products.

After eight days of hospitalization, she was moved to the nephrology unit for continued management of dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and ongoing intravenous antibiotic treatment. Upon arrival, the patient was hemodynamically stable but anuric, exhibiting diffuse edema and multiple hematomas, while remaining alert and oriented. In accordance with blood culture findings, her antibiotic regimen was stepped down to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently to oral Apocillin.

Laboratory assessment on the nephrology ward demonstrated an improving inflammatory response, with reactive leukocytosis, platelet recovery, and stable hemoglobin concentration. Renal function tests revealed persistent azotemia. Meanwhile, coagulation studies returned to normal, suggesting the resolution of her coagulopathy. Her laboratory findings at this stage are detailed in Table 2.

Despite initial clinical progress, her course was complicated by an abrupt, isolated decline in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over a six-day period) in the absence of overt hemorrhage or hemodynamic compromise. Thrombocytopenia persisted (30–40 × 10⁹/L), haptoglobin became undetectable, and LDH increased, all consistent with active hemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) or Coombs test returned positive for IgG, indicating a potential diagnosis of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was initiated together with standard prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over a two-day period. Persistent haemolysis despite corticosteroid treatment, in conjunction with thrombocytopenia (platelet count 41–46 × 10⁹/L), necessitated further investigation. Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, in keeping with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and suggestive of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). The original positive DAT was probably due to a false positive caused by either penicillin use or prior transfusions; subsequent testing for DAT was negative.

Complement evaluation, including ADAMTS13 activity assessment, was conducted. Routine complement tests, specifically C3 and C4 levels, fell within normal parameters, and functional assays assessing the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways were similarly unremarkable. The ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43.5%, effectively excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which is generally associated with activity levels under 10%. While these laboratory results were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced as interim management ahead of the initial administration of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was carried out, revealing pronounced acute tubular necrosis, ischemic changes in the glomeruli with the presence of fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, all of which were indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The key diagnostic observations were most clearly illustrated by the light microscopy images in figure 3. Findings from immunofluorescence and electron microscopy were not included; immunofluorescence testing showed no evidence of immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy did not reveal features of primary glomerulopathy.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was ruled out on the basis of a negative stool PCR analysis.

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, the patient began producing urine spontaneously again. Induction therapy with eculizumab (900 mg intravenously each week for four weeks) was started after the initial administrations of the meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (for serogroup B). Plans were made to administer the second vaccine doses eight weeks later in the outpatient setting. Since vaccination had not taken place at least two weeks prior to the initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement analysis, aiming to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), was sent to Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. For logistical and safety reasons, three sessions of PEX utilizing Octaplas, as well as one induction dose of eculizumab, were conducted prior to blood collection. Functional studies of the complement system, including a quantitative AH50 assay (assessing alternative pathway activity), showed a significant reduction (6%), although complement factors H, I, and B were within normal ranges, and testing for antifactor H antibodies returned negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After extensive medical treatment, the patient remains clinically stable with satisfactory urine production. Her renal function is still impaired, as evidenced by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she will be monitored regularly in the nephrology outpatient service (figure 4). She is receiving maintenance therapy with eculizumab at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, which has been ongoing for 4 months following the induction phase. Comprehensive genetic screening of the complement pathway revealed no pathogenic variants; this finding was communicated verbally by the reference laboratory, although the official written report was not yet available at the time of manuscript submission. Given these results, complement inhibition therapy is being sustained in light of the severity of her condition, with plans to reevaluate the potential for discontinuation should renal function reach a plateau. Discovery of disease-causing complement gene mutations would support ongoing therapy with vigilant monitoring for recurrence, but none were identified in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adults who present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the first step is to rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by a marked reduction in ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). Tools such as the PLASMIC score may aid in initial risk assessment while awaiting definitive laboratory results, as the diagnosis relies on laboratory confirmation. In this case, the patient had a PLASMIC score of 4/7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and an ADAMTS13 activity level of 43.5%, which further reduced suspicion for TTP and prompted continued evaluation for alternative etiologies.

STEC-HUS remains an essential differential diagnosis. Polymerase chain reaction testing for Shiga toxin was negative in this patient. Furthermore, the absence of a preceding gastrointestinal illness and the negative microbiological findings made STEC-HUS less probable.

In conclusion, persistent evidence of haemolysis, presence of schistocytes, dependence on dialysis due to AKI, and renal biopsy revealing TMA were compatible with CM-HUS (see figure 5). The diagnosis was supported by the PCR exclusion of STEC and the patient's notable clinical recovery following eculizumab therapy initiation. Her lack of significant past medical conditions argues against an underlying complement abnormality necessitating a secondary insult, and instead favors an infection-induced CM-HUS process.

CM-HUS represents a clinicopathological diagnosis made by exclusion, and complement measurements (C3/C4) can remain within normal limits throughout its progression. It is important to note that CM-HUS cannot be ruled out by normal complement values, as these parameters may stay within the reference interval during the illness. Additionally, in this case, haemolysis became evident only after sepsis started to resolve, indicating that the CM-HUS was likely initiated by infection rather than being present beforehand.

For our patient, initial complement assessments (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were performed directly before initiating the first plasma exchange (PEX) treatment. A more comprehensive complement evaluation was conducted 8 days following the last PEX procedure and 4 days subsequent to administration of the first dose of eculizumab. The decrease observed in the alternative complement pathway most likely reflects effects of therapy, as opposed to primary complement activation or deficiency.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS’, results from inappropriate activation of the alternative complement pathway, typically in individuals with an underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50% to 70% of affected patients possess either genetic mutations or autoantibodies directed against complement regulatory components, including factors H, I, and B, or the membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Despite this, disease expression is variable, as clinical features generally emerge only following exposure to external precipitants such as infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or specific pharmacologic agents.

C. canimorsus has only infrequently been documented to initiate complement activation, predominantly in hosts with heightened vulnerability. This organism demonstrates a spectrum of serum sensitivity; many strains are readily neutralized by complement, whereas others, particularly those with capsular expression, exhibit increased resistance—emphasizing the significance of complement in the immune response against this pathogen. This dynamic presents a clinical challenge: while activation of complement is necessary for pathogen clearance, in susceptible individuals, it may concurrently provoke unregulated endothelial injury and subsequent thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The present case expands upon the limited but increasing number of reports associating C. canimorsus infection with complement-mediated TMAs. For example, Bjørkto et al. reported a case of C. canimorsus–associated CM-HUS in a patient carrying a complement factor B variant, who responded favorably to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that case, genetic analysis in our patient did not identify any pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement genes, reinforcing the concept that infection alone may precipitate CM-HUS even in the absence of an identifiable hereditary complement abnormality.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Treating CM-HUS during ongoing infection entails specific complexities. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody against terminal complement component 5, is the established therapy for CM-HUS, leading to significant improvements in both haematologic parameters and renal function, especially if administered promptly. Late initiation has been linked to suboptimal renal recovery and a greater likelihood of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

PEX is a critical intervention in TTP, yet its role in CM-HUS is mainly transient, offering only short-lived benefits by eliminating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies from circulation, without addressing the primary complement dysregulation. In the present case, the patient's urine output resumed following the final PEX procedures but before eculizumab therapy, signifying temporary improvement only. It was not until complement inhibition was implemented that sustained normalization of haematological markers and renal function was achieved.

One central therapeutic concern involves the administration of complement inhibitors in the setting of ongoing bacteraemia, since terminal complement proteins are essential for host defense against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, thereby posing a risk of infections associated with eculizumab. Here, eculizumab treatment was commenced only after negative blood cultures were confirmed, thereby reducing the risk of uncontrolled infection. Implementation of immunization, ongoing antibiotic prophylaxis, and rigorous monitoring are vital strategies when utilizing complement-blocking agents.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early administration of complement inhibition remain key factors influencing positive outcomes. In this case, the patient reached hematologic remission and experienced partial restoration of renal function, in line with existing data indicating that renal status and rapid commencement of eculizumab therapy are primary prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS associated with C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this case underscores the need to include infectious etiologies—particularly atypical ones—among the differential diagnoses in patients presenting with new-onset TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and advances in molecular diagnostic techniques could facilitate recognition of additional cases.
