Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A previously well woman in her late 40s presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, eventually becoming completely anuric and requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia. Although her condition initially improved with antibiotic treatment and supportive measures, she subsequently experienced abrupt, isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopenia. Laboratory evaluations and a renal biopsy confirmed a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Initiation of plasma exchange and eculizumab therapy resulted in haematologic recovery and partial restoration of renal function. This report demonstrates that C. canimorsus infection can act as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, detailing the patient’s diagnostic process, the complexities of differentiating potential etiologies, and the challenging nature of management and clinical evolution in TMA subsequent to sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is part of the typical oropharyngeal microbiota of dogs and cats. Human infection is uncommon, and transmission may result from bites, scratches, or even proximity to these animals. The spectrum of clinical manifestations ranges from isolated meningitis to severe septicemia, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although this organism generally responds well to a variety of antibiotics, the mortality rate may reach up to 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in invasive zoonotic infections in humans. Its demanding growth requirements and slow culture kinetics often delay detection, thus there is growing reliance on molecular diagnostics, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In our patient, infection with C. canimorsus initially manifested as septic shock accompanied by marked leukopenia and coagulopathy consistent with DIC. While sepsis and multiorgan dysfunction characterized the early phase, subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed toward an overlay of TMA. This underscores the potential for complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) to be masked in the early stages of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two prior uneventful pregnancies arrived at the emergency department due to rapidly deteriorating health. Four days before admission, she sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and was administered a tetanus vaccine as a preventive measure. She was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) at that time; nonetheless, she did not begin antibiotic therapy because she was unable to obtain the medication. She subsequently developed a high-grade fever exceeding 40 °C, felt generally unwell, reduced her oral intake, and later presented to medical care when she noticed numbness and color changes in her feet. During an evaluation at a primary-care emergency facility, she was discovered to be hypoglycemic, resistant to both oral glucose and glucagon administration, with her lowest recorded glucose at 0.6 mmol/L. This finding led to her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and engaged, but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness of both lower limbs. She denied taking herbal preparations or other substances, and there was no history of illness or similar symptoms among family members. Initial laboratory investigations showed significant systemic disturbances, with pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, increased liver transaminases, and impaired coagulation. Arterial blood gas measurements demonstrated metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43), accompanied by an elevated lactate concentration of 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of large amounts of intravenous glucose, hypoglycaemia persisted, which was attributed to the septic process. The patient exhibited sufficient urine output upon presentation; urine analysis indicated proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her clinical findings—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—were interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) resulting from bacteremia and early sepsis, with a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. Surgical causes for her mottled extremities, such as necrotizing fasciitis, were excluded through both physical examination and imaging studies. In light of the gravity of her clinical presentation, intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated as empirical broad-spectrum therapy targeting canine-associated pathogens, including Capnocytophaga species. Capnocytophaga canimorsus was suspected early in the course and subsequently identified 7 days later from both aerobic and anaerobic blood culture specimens. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced surveillance.

Given her pancytopenia and coagulopathy, a hematology consultation was requested. Possible etiologies considered included acute leukemia and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) was also contemplated but deemed less likely based on findings of leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and reticulocyte counts lacking compensatory response. Flow cytometric analysis was performed, and a bone marrow biopsy was scheduled; however, normal flow cytometry findings—including absence of increased CD34+ blasts or aberrant phenotypes—resulted in cancellation of the biopsy. The clinical scenario aligned with a diagnosis of sepsis. During the waiting period for diagnostic results, thrombocyte and fibrinogen transfusions were provided for supportive care.

Following her admission to the ICU, the patient became entirely anuric and experienced a swift decline in renal function. Intensive intravenous fluid administration in combination with loop diuretics was undertaken but proved ineffective. She then progressed to develop septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by volume overload, which led to the commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serology, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By ICU day 3, the patient exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding, ecchymosis, and distal cyanotic and ischemic lesions involving multiple toes and fingers, though peripheral pulses and sensory function remained intact. These manifestations were indicative of microthrombotic activity associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Throughout the period of multisystem dysfunction, she did not require vasopressor agents apart from stress-dose corticosteroids administered during the initial septic period. She additionally received albumin infusions as well as other blood product transfusions.

Following 8 days of hospitalization, she was moved to the nephrology unit due to continued dialysis-dependent AKI and the need for intravenous antibiotic administration. At the time of transfer, the patient remained clinically stable despite being anuric, exhibiting generalized edema and multiple hematomas, but was awake and oriented. Based on blood culture findings, her antibiotics were stepped down to intravenous ampicillin, which was subsequently switched to oral Apocillin.

During her stay in the nephrology ward, laboratory tests revealed a recovering inflammatory profile, reactive leukocytosis, an upward trend in platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin values. Renal parameters continued to reflect azotemia, whereas coagulation studies returned to normal, suggesting coagulopathy resolution. Laboratory data from this period are outlined in Table 2.

Following this initial phase of stabilization, the patient's course was complicated by an abrupt, isolated hemoglobin decrease (from 111 to 68 g/L within 6 days), in the absence of overt hemorrhage or haemodynamic compromise. Thrombocytopenia persisted (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin was undetectable, and LDH became elevated, consistent with ongoing hemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, yielded a positive result for IgG, prompting concerns regarding autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was commenced with routine prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over a 2-day interval. However, persistent haemolysis unresponsive to corticosteroids and sustained thrombocytopenia (platelet count 41–46 × 10⁹/L) necessitated additional investigation. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, which was indicative of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and suggestive of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). The initial DAT result was likely erroneously positive, attributable to either penicillin administration or previous transfusions; repeat DAT testing returned negative.

Complement studies and ADAMTS13 activity were submitted for assessment. Locally accessible routine complement assays (C3/C4) fell within reference ranges, and functional assessments of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways showed no abnormalities. ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently reported as 43.5%, thereby excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP characteristically manifests with activity levels under 10%. While these results were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was undertaken to bridge the patient to their first administration of eculizumab.

After the platelet count reached 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was carried out, which demonstrated pronounced acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescents, all indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy data are omitted, since immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy did not identify features of primary glomerular disease.

Stool PCR testing was negative, excluding Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli-related hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS).

After the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), the patient resumed spontaneous urination. Meningococcal vaccination was started with initial doses of Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B), after which eculizumab induction therapy began (900 mg intravenous weekly for four weeks). Plans for the second doses of these vaccines were scheduled to take place eight weeks later during outpatient follow-up. Since the meningococcal vaccines had not been administered two weeks prior to beginning complement inhibition, Apocillin was provided for prophylaxis.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement testing was sent to the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. For logistical and safety reasons, three plasma exchange sessions with Octaplas and one induction dose of eculizumab were carried out prior to obtaining specimens. Functional complement assays included a quantitative AH50 test assessing alternative pathway activity, which showed a significant reduction (6%). Complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits, and antifactor H antibodies were not detected.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical treatment, the patient presents in overall good health and maintains satisfactory urine output. However, her renal function remains compromised, with a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L. She is scheduled for ongoing follow-up in the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). The current regimen consists of maintenance eculizumab administered intravenously at 1200 mg every two weeks, continued for four months following initial induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of the complement system revealed no pathogenic mutations; this outcome was relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, although the official written report was still pending at the time the manuscript was submitted. In light of the available data, complement inhibition therapy is being maintained due to the serious nature of her condition, with plans to reevaluate discontinuation should renal function stabilize. If pathogenic complement gene alterations had been detected, extended therapy along with vigilant monitoring for recurrence would have been advised. In this case, such variants were absent.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adults who present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost task is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by a marked deficiency of ADAMTS13 (<10%). While clinical assessment tools such as the PLASMIC score can assist in triaging patients during the interim before definitive test results are available, the diagnosis is ultimately laboratory-driven. The patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and her ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, which further made TTP improbable, thereby informing the next steps in evaluation.

STEC-HUS is also an important diagnosis to consider. PCR testing for Shiga toxin in this patient was negative. Coupled with the absence of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and negative microbiological assays, this made STEC-HUS an unlikely diagnosis.

The combination of ongoing haemolysis, schistocytes on peripheral smear, renal failure requiring dialysis, and biopsy findings consistent with TMA pointed towards a diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). Additional support for CM-HUS came from the exclusion of STEC infection via PCR and the patient’s swift clinical response to eculizumab treatment. The lack of notable medical history weakens the possibility of an underlying complement abnormality necessitating a ‘second hit’, instead suggesting an infection-induced onset of CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is diagnosed by excluding other causes based on clinical and pathological findings, and complement concentrations (C3 and C4) may stay within normal limits throughout the illness. It is critical to recognize that normal C3 and C4 values do not rule out CM-HUS, since these may stay within the normal range as the disease progresses. Of note, haemolysis became evident only after sepsis showed signs of resolution, supporting infection-related CM-HUS rather than an underlying, pre-existing disorder.

For this patient, baseline C3, C4, and ADAMTS13 measurements were obtained directly prior to initiating the first PEX session. Broader complement analyses were carried out 8 days following the last PEX session and 4 days subsequent to the initial administration of eculizumab. The decreased activity of the alternative pathway detected is interpreted as more likely attributable to effects of therapy rather than to underlying depletion or activation of complement components.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS, formerly referred to as 'atypical HUS,' results from abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway, typically in individuals with a genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50% to 70% of cases are associated with genetic mutations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory proteins, including factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nevertheless, the disease exhibits incomplete penetrance, and its onset generally requires an additional external precipitating factor, most frequently infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or certain pharmaceuticals.

C. canimorsus is infrequently documented as a complement pathway activator, but it can do so, especially in vulnerable patients. Importantly, C. canimorsus has varying degrees of susceptibility to serum complement, with numerous isolates being sensitive to complement, though some capsule-producing strains possess partial resistance. Consequently, complement function plays a critical role in host defense against this organism. This scenario leads to a clinical dilemma: while activation of complement is necessary to eliminate the bacteria, it may also precipitate unchecked endothelial injury and TMA in genetically predisposed individuals.

The present case expands the limited literature connecting C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. An instructive example by Bjørkto et al involved a patient who developed CM-HUS due to C. canimorsus infection and carried a complement factor B variant, achieving resolution with eculizumab therapy. In comparison to that case, our patient did not demonstrate any pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene alterations, supporting the concept of infection-induced CM-HUS occurring without clear evidence of an underlying hereditary complement disorder.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Treating CM-HUS amid an ongoing infection poses specific difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5, is the primary therapy for CM-HUS and leads to significant improvements in blood and kidney parameters, especially when administered promptly. Starting treatment late correlates with less complete renal recovery and increases the chance of developing end-stage kidney disease.

On the other hand, plasma exchange (PEX) is critical for survival in TTP but offers only temporary relief in CM-HUS, as it removes complement fragments, inflammatory substances, or autoantibodies from circulation without addressing the fundamental complement system abnormality. In this case, the patient's urine output resumed after the last sessions of PEX and prior to eculizumab, indicating a short-lived stabilization. Lasting hematologic and kidney improvement was only achieved following complement inhibition.

A major therapeutic challenge is the use of complement inhibitors during active bacteremia, since terminal complement is vital in host defense against encapsulated bacteria such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, raising concerns about infection risk linked to eculizumab. In this patient, eculizumab therapy was started after blood cultures no longer showed infection, thereby reducing the chance of uncontrolled infection. Continued vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant observation are crucial when employing complement-blocking agents.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition therapy remain key factors associated with positive clinical outcomes. The patient described attained hematological remission along with partial recovery of kidney function, which aligns with published data indicating that renal status and the interval to eculizumab initiation principally influence prognosis.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus is exceptionally uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of evaluating potential infectious precipitants—even rare ones—when encountering individuals with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced recognition and utilization of molecular diagnostic techniques could facilitate the detection of additional similar cases.
