Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which ultimately led to complete cessation of urine output and a need for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. While her condition initially improved with antimicrobial and supportive measures, she subsequently experienced abrupt isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory testing and renal biopsy revealed features consistent with a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Treatment with plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab resulted in haematological recovery and partial improvement of kidney function. This report underscores the unusual role of C. canimorsus infection as an instigator of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, and details the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the difficulties in distinguishing possible causes, as well as the intricate clinical trajectory and management considerations associated with TMA developing after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is commonly present in the normal oral flora of dogs and cats. Infections in humans are uncommon and can result from bites, scratches, or even casual interactions. The disease can manifest in numerous ways, including isolated cases of meningitis, rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although this organism is generally susceptible to a broad range of antibiotics, the associated mortality rate may reach up to 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in invasive zoonotic infections in people. Its demanding growth requirements and slow cultivation in laboratory settings frequently lead to delayed diagnosis, which has led to increased reliance on molecular diagnostic techniques like real-time nanopore sequencing.

In our report, infection with C. canimorsus was characterized by septic shock, pronounced leukopaenia, and coagulopathy in keeping with DIC. While the patient’s early disease progression was marked primarily by multiorgan dysfunction related to sepsis, subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed to superimposed TMA. This scenario illustrates how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be masked in the initial phase dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues and two previous uneventful pregnancies presented to the emergency department due to a rapidly deteriorating condition. She had sustained a dog bite on her upper lip four days earlier and was given a tetanus vaccine for prevention. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed on the day of the injury, but she was unable to start the antibiotic because she did not pick up the prescription. Subsequently, she experienced a high fever (>40 °C), general malaise, decreased oral intake, and eventually sought care when she noticed numbness and discoloration in her feet. During her evaluation at a primary care emergency facility, she was found to be hypoglycemic, which did not respond to oral glucose or glucagon, with her blood glucose dropping to a low of 0.6 mmol/L. This led to her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was awake and able to answer questions, but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness of the lower limbs. She denied having used herbal products or other substances, and reported no family members had experienced similar symptoms or illnesses. Initial laboratory investigations indicated significant systemic disturbances, with pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated liver enzymes, and coagulopathy. Blood gas testing demonstrated a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range: 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal: <2.5 mmol/L). Persistent hypoglycaemia was noted despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, suspected to be secondary to sepsis. On presentation, the patient's urinary output was sufficient; urinalysis detected proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Based on her clinical presentation—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury and lactic acidosis—her condition was assessed as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteremia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1/3). Surgical causes for her mottled extremities, such as necrotizing fasciitis, were excluded following thorough clinical and radiological investigations. Owing to the severity of her symptoms, intravenous broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to empirically target pathogens associated with dog exposure, including Capnocytophaga species. Capnocytophaga canimorsus was considered likely from the beginning and confirmed seven days later through positive aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. The patient was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced observation.

Haematology was consulted due to her pancytopenia and coagulopathy. Acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis were among the possible differential diagnoses. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also contemplated but deemed improbable given the presence of leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and insufficient reticulocyte response. Flow cytometry was conducted and a bone marrow biopsy planned; however, normal findings—with no evidence of increased CD34+ blasts or abnormal phenotypes—resulted in the biopsy being withheld. Overall, her symptoms aligned with a diagnosis of sepsis. During the interim period awaiting diagnostic confirmation, supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were provided.

Upon admission to the ICU, she became entirely anuric, with renal function deteriorating swiftly. Attempts at vigorous intravenous fluid administration combined with loop diuretics did not achieve improvement. Subsequently, she developed septic cardiomyopathy complicated by volume overload, necessitating the commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune panels, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By ICU day 3, she experienced widespread skin hemorrhages manifested as ecchymoses, along with cyanosis and ischemia affecting several distal fingers and toes, though both pulses and sensory function remained intact. These signs were indicative of microthrombotic processes associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although multiple organ systems were involved, she did not require vasopressor agents other than the stress-dose corticosteroids administered during the acute septic episode. Albumin and various blood products were also administered.

Following 8 days of hospitalization, the patient was moved to the nephrology ward due to persistent dialysis-requiring acute kidney injury (AKI) and continued need for intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival, she was hemodynamically stable but had no urine output, displayed generalized edema, and presented with multiple hematomas; she was, however, fully alert and oriented. Her antibiotic regimen was stepped down to intravenous ampicillin, then later switched to oral Apocillin in line with blood culture findings.

During her stay in the nephrology unit, laboratory tests revealed a trend toward improvement of inflammatory markers, the presence of reactive leukocytosis, normalization of platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin values. Renal panels continued to demonstrate azotemia, yet coagulation studies normalized, signifying a resolution of coagulopathy. Laboratory findings at that time are detailed in Table 2.

Despite initial clinical stabilization, her condition changed abruptly as she experienced a sudden, isolated decline in hemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L within 6 days, without any signs of active bleeding or compromise in hemodynamics. Platelet counts remained consistently decreased at 30–40×10⁹/L, haptoglobin levels became undetectable, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) increased, collectively pointing toward continued hemolysis (figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, was positive for IgG, prompting concern for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was commenced in conjunction with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple transfusions were administered across a 2-day period. Due to persistent haemolysis and refractory thrombocytopenia (platelet count 41–46 × 10⁹/L) despite corticosteroid therapy, additional investigation was deemed necessary. Examination of the peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of MAHA in keeping with TMA (see figure 2). It was considered that the initial DAT positivity was likely spurious, attributable to penicillin exposure or earlier transfusions, as follow-up testing was negative.

Complement panels and ADAMTS13 activity were subsequently requested. Routine complement assays (C3/C4) were unremarkable, and functional assessments of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways also yielded normal results. ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43.5%, which effectively excluded thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP is generally associated with activity below 10%. During the interval awaiting these laboratory findings, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as an interim measure before administration of the first eculizumab dose.

After the platelet count reached 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was undertaken, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and isolated crescent formation, all indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The principal diagnostic evidence was observed on light microscopy, as depicted in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy were not included; immunofluorescence identified no immune complex deposits, while electron microscopy showed no signs of underlying primary glomerular pathology.

A diagnosis of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-related hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) was excluded because stool PCR testing was negative.

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, the patient resumed spontaneous urine production. Once initial doses of the meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (serogroup B)—were administered, eculizumab induction therapy was started at a dose of 900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks. The second vaccinations were scheduled for 8 weeks later in the outpatient setting. As the vaccines had not been given at least two weeks prior to initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement studies to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) were submitted to the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. To address logistical factors and ensure patient safety, three PEX sessions with Octaplas and a single induction dose of eculizumab were completed before laboratory samples were drawn. Functional complement assays included a quantitative AH50 test for alternative pathway activity, which was significantly decreased at 6%, whereas levels of complement factors H, I, and B were normal and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After the extensive medical management, the patient maintains stable overall health and satisfactory urine production. Renal function remains impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and ongoing follow-up in the nephrology outpatient service is planned (see figure 4). She is receiving maintenance eculizumab therapy (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks), for a duration of 4 months following induction. Comprehensive complement genetic analysis did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; these findings were communicated orally by the reference laboratory, while the official written report is still pending as of the submission date. Given these findings, complement inhibition therapy will continue in light of the disease severity, with future evaluation for discontinuation if kidney function stabilizes. Should pathogenic complement gene variants have been found, extended therapy with vigilant monitoring for relapse would have been recommended. However, this patient did not harbor any such mutations.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adult patients presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost step is to rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Clinical assessment tools such as PLASMIC can assist in initial stratification pending confirmatory laboratory testing, which remains definitive for diagnosis. This patient’s PLASMIC score was 4/7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, which made TTP improbable and informed the direction for subsequent diagnostic evaluation.

STEC-HUS must also be considered in differential diagnosis. Shiga toxin PCR results were negative in this case. The lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and negative microbiological studies disfavored STEC-HUS.

In summary, the ongoing haemolysis, presence of schistocytes, requirement for dialysis due to AKI, along with kidney biopsy evidence of TMA, led to the diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). The exclusion of STEC through PCR and the patient’s prompt improvement following eculizumab therapy reinforced the diagnosis. Her unremarkable medical history diminishes the possibility of a previously existing complement disorder needing an additional trigger and instead supports the theory of CM-HUS precipitated by infection.

CM-HUS is diagnosed clinically and pathologically by ruling out other causes, and C3/C4 complement concentrations may remain normal throughout the illness. Therefore, normal complement values do not rule out CM-HUS, as they can persist within normal limits during the disease. In this case, haemolysis was not present until sepsis started resolving, pointing toward infection-induced CM-HUS as opposed to a previously existing disorder.

For this patient, C3/C4 complement assays and ADAMTS13 activity were measured just before the initial plasma exchange (PEX) procedure. Further complement evaluation was performed eight days following the last PEX and four days after administration of the first eculizumab dose. The decreased alternative pathway activity observed is most likely attributable to therapeutic intervention, rather than primary complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS, previously known as ‘atypical HUS,’ is characterized by abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibilities. Approximately 50%–70% of cases have detectable genetic alterations or autoantibodies that disrupt regulation of complement proteins, including factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nevertheless, incomplete penetrance is observed, and clinical disease often necessitates an external precipitating event, such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or exposure to specific drugs.

While uncommon, C. canimorsus has the capacity to stimulate complement activation, especially in hosts with increased susceptibility. C. canimorsus exhibits varying sensitivity to serum; many isolates are vulnerable to complement, whereas some capsule-containing strains may partially resist its effects. This underscores the centrality of complement-mediated mechanisms in host defense. However, this protective function poses a dilemma: effective complement-mediated pathogen elimination may, in certain predisposed hosts, precipitate excessive endothelial injury and TMA.

This report contributes to the limited but expanding series of cases associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. Bjørkto et al notably described an individual who developed CM-HUS due to C. canimorsus and who possessed a factor B variant, responding favorably to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that case, our patient did not have identifiable pathogenic or likely pathogenic genetic variants in complement-related genes, providing support for the possibility of infection-provoked CM-HUS in the absence of overt inherited complement abnormalities.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Treating CM-HUS during an ongoing infection involves distinct difficulties. The established treatment for CM-HUS is eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against terminal complement component 5, which significantly enhances hematological and renal outcomes, particularly when administered promptly. Initiating eculizumab late is linked to incomplete recovery of renal function and an increased likelihood of developing end-stage kidney disease.

PEX is crucial in managing TTP and can be life-saving; however, its benefit in CM-HUS is temporary, primarily achieved through elimination of circulating complement components, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, without addressing the underlying defect in the complement pathway. In this patient, diuresis resumed after the last PEX treatments but before the start of eculizumab, indicative of short-term stabilization. Durable hematological and renal improvements were observed only after complement inhibition commenced.

An important challenge is the use of complement inhibitors when bacteraemia is present, because terminal complement elements play a vital role in protection against encapsulated pathogens, such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, leading to concerns regarding the increased risk of infections associated with eculizumab. In this instance, eculizumab therapy was started after blood cultures no longer showed bacteria, reducing the risk of uncontained infection. When complement blockers are used, immunizations, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring are mandatory.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition are critical for achieving positive clinical outcomes. The patient described attained haematological remission and partial improvement in renal function, in alignment with existing data indicating that kidney function status and the interval before starting eculizumab therapy are key prognostic factors.

While CM-HUS associated with C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to recognize infectious precipitants—including rare causes—in individuals presenting with newly diagnosed TMA. Greater awareness and advances in molecular diagnostics could facilitate detection of additional similar cases.
