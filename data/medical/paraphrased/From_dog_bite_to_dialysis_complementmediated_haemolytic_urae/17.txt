Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no previous health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her clinical course included disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury that advanced to complete anuria and reliance on dialysis. Blood cultures revealed Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although her condition initially improved with antibiotic therapy and supportive management, she subsequently experienced abrupt isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory studies and renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), resulting in a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Treatment with plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab led to haematological remission and partial recovery of renal function. This report underscores C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, and describes the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the challenges encountered in differentiating diagnoses, and the complex clinical and therapeutic considerations associated with TMA after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow-growing, gram-negative bacillus that resides in the normal oral flora of dogs and cats. Human disease is uncommon and may result from animal bites, scratches, or even mere close contact. The clinical spectrum is broad, with manifestations ranging from isolated meningitis to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although the organism typically exhibits susceptibility to many antibiotics, the reported mortality rate may reach up to 30%.

Among the Capnocytophaga genus, C. canimorsus is most commonly implicated in invasive zoonoses in humans. Identification may be protracted because of its fastidious growth requirements and slow propagation in culture, thereby increasing reliance on molecular diagnostic techniques, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In this report, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock accompanied by pronounced leukopenia and coagulopathy, in keeping with DIC. While the early phase was characterized by sepsis with involvement of multiple organ systems, subsequent findings of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and progressive acute kidney injury (AKI) were indicative of an additional TMA process. This underscores how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be masked during the initial stages dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two uneventful previous pregnancies arrived at the emergency department due to a swiftly deteriorating health status. Four days before admission, her dog bit her upper lip, for which she received a tetanus vaccine as a preventive measure. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed the same day; nonetheless, she did not begin antibiotic therapy because she was unable to retrieve the medication. Subsequently, she experienced a marked fever exceeding 40 °C, general malaise, and decreased oral intake. She sought medical care upon noticing numbness and discoloration of her feet. In the primary care emergency setting, she was found to be hypoglycemic—resistant to oral glucose and glucagon administration—with her blood glucose reaching a low point of 0.6 mmol/L. This warranted her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and cooperative, yet displayed signs of sepsis, including mottled and cool lower limbs. She denied the use of herbal medicines or any other substances, and there was no history of similar illness among family members. Initial laboratory tests demonstrated significant systemic disruption, featuring pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas assessment indicated metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a raised lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). Persistent hypoglycaemia was noted despite the administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, which was attributed to the septic state. On presentation, urine output was preserved; urinalysis detected proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her array of clinical findings—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—were considered consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1/3). Potential surgical causes of her mottled limbs, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded based on clinical assessment and imaging studies. Due to the seriousness of her clinical state, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to target pathogens commonly associated with dog exposures, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was clinically suspected early in her course and subsequently confirmed via both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures seven days later. She was then admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring.

Owing to the observed pancytopenia and coagulopathy, haematology was consulted. The working differential diagnosis included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also considered; however, this was deemed less likely due to the presence of leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and a lack of compensatory reticulocytosis. Flow cytometry was performed and bone marrow biopsy was initially planned; however, findings showed normal parameters without increased CD34+ blasts or aberrant cellular populations, leading to the decision not to proceed with biopsy. The overall clinical picture remained compatible with sepsis. During this period, supportive care included transfusions of thrombocytes and fibrinogen.

Following admission to the intensive care unit, the patient developed complete anuria with a rapid decline in kidney function. Vigorous intravenous fluid therapy in combination with loop diuretics was tried, but this approach proved ineffective. She later manifested fluid overload and septic cardiomyopathy, necessitating the initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy. Testing for autoimmune markers, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, the patient exhibited widespread cutaneous hemorrhages characterized by ecchymoses, as well as cyanosis and ischemic changes in several fingers and toes; however, both pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical signs were suggestive of microthrombotic events occurring in the context of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although multiple organs were affected, she did not need vasopressor therapy aside from the administration of stress-dose corticosteroids during the early septic period. The patient also received albumin in addition to other transfused blood products.

Following eight days of inpatient care, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit due to persistent dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and the need for intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival, she was hemodynamically stable but anuric, exhibiting widespread edema and several hematomas, while remaining alert and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, later transitioning to oral Apocillin in accordance with blood culture findings.

During her stay on the nephrology service, laboratory assessment demonstrated a trend toward lower inflammatory markers, a reactive leukocytosis, improving platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin concentrations. Renal indices revealed ongoing azotemia, whereas coagulation studies had normalized, suggestive of resolving coagulopathy. Her laboratory data from this period are presented in Table 2.

However, following this period of stabilization, her clinical status changed abruptly when she experienced an isolated decline in hemoglobin concentration (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days), in the absence of overt bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet levels persisted at low values (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin was undetectable, and there was a concomitant increase in LDH, all consistent with active hemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (Coombs test) yielded a positive result for IgG, suggesting a possible diagnosis of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. Treatment was initiated with high-dose prednisolone in addition to standard prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over the course of 2 days. As there was no improvement in the haemolytic activity following corticosteroid therapy and the platelet count persisted at a low level (41–46 × 10⁹/L), further investigations were undertaken. Examination of the peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of MAHA and raising the possibility of TMA (see figure 2). The initial DAT result was suspected to be a false positive, potentially attributable to penicillin use or preceding transfusions; subsequent testing returned a negative result.

Complement investigations along with ADAMTS13 activity testing were requested. Results from local complement assays (C3 and C4) fell within normal parameters, and evaluation of classical, alternative, and lectin pathway functionality was likewise unremarkable. Subsequently, ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43.5%, a value inconsistent with thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which generally requires levels less than 10% for diagnosis. In the interim, prior to administration of the initial eculizumab dose, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced as a provisional treatment.

Upon achieving a thrombocyte count of 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which demonstrated severe acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia with fibrin thrombi, and areas of crescent formation, aligning with a diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The key diagnostic characteristics in this scenario were most clearly represented by the light microscopy images provided in figure 3. Neither immunofluorescence nor electron microscopy results are depicted, as immunofluorescence indicated an absence of immune complex deposition and electron microscopy showed no signs of a primary glomerular disease.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was ruled out on the basis of a negative stool PCR analysis.

After the patient’s second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, spontaneous urine output resumed. Eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg intravenously once weekly for four weeks) was started after administration of the initial doses of the meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (for serogroup B). The second set of vaccinations was scheduled for eight weeks later as an outpatient. Since the vaccines had not been given at least two weeks before initiating complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

For evaluation of potential hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement studies were sent to Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden). Due to logistical issues and to ensure patient safety, three PEX sessions using Octaplas and one loading dose of eculizumab were administered prior to sample collection. Functional complement analysis included assessment of alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which was significantly diminished (6%), whereas complement components H, I, and B remained within the normal range, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After the intensive medical therapy, the patient remains in stable overall health with sufficient urine production. Renal function is still impaired (serum creatinine 175 µmol/L), so ongoing follow-up is planned in the nephrology outpatient setting (see figure 4). She is still receiving maintenance eculizumab (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks), which has continued for 4 months since induction. Comprehensive genetic screening for complement abnormalities did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; these findings were relayed orally by the reference laboratory, with the official written report still pending submission. Given this clinical scenario, complement inhibition therapy is being maintained due to the significant severity of her condition, and periodic evaluation will be performed to consider stopping treatment if kidney function shows sustained improvement. Detection of disease-causing complement gene variants would indicate a preference for extended therapy with vigilant surveillance for recurrence, but such mutations were not found in this patient.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

The foremost concern in adult patients exhibiting MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by a marked reduction in ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). Clinical scoring systems such as the PLASMIC score can be useful to stratify risk during the interim period before laboratory results are available, though final diagnosis requires lab confirmation. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, with an ADAMTS13 activity level of 43.5%, thereby reducing the probability of TTP and informing subsequent diagnostic investigations.

Another critical differential is STEC-HUS. This patient had negative PCR results for Shiga toxin. The lack of prior gastrointestinal symptoms along with negative microbiological assays made STEC-HUS an unlikely diagnosis.

In the end, the combination of ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, persistent AKI requiring dialysis, and renal biopsy showing TMA pointed towards a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). This was further substantiated by negative PCR testing for STEC and the patient’s prompt response to eculizumab treatment. Her minimal past medical history lessens the argument for an underlying complement abnormality requiring an additional trigger and instead favors CM-HUS provoked by an infectious process.

CM-HUS is diagnosed by excluding other causes and is characterized clinically and pathologically; C3 and C4 complement values can remain within normal limits throughout the disease. Crucially, normal C3 and C4 measurements do not eliminate the possibility of CM-HUS, since levels may not deviate from the normal range during illness. In this case, it is noteworthy that hemolysis was observed only after sepsis showed signs of resolution, supporting the hypothesis of sepsis-induced CM-HUS rather than a pre-existing disorder.

For this patient, initial assessments of basic complement components (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity occurred directly prior to the inaugural PEX treatment. Additional, more comprehensive complement studies were conducted eight days after the last PEX and four days following the patient’s initial administration of eculizumab. The diminished function in the alternative complement pathway is most plausibly attributable to therapeutic intervention, as opposed to inherent complement pathway depletion or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS’, results from inappropriate activation of the alternative complement pathway, typically in individuals with an underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50% to 70% of affected patients possess genetic alterations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory proteins—including factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nonetheless, not all individuals with these risk factors develop the disease, as expression often requires an additional precipitating factor such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune processes, or certain drugs.

While infrequently documented, C. canimorsus has demonstrated the capacity to stimulate complement pathways, particularly in at-risk patients. The organism exhibits heterogeneity in serum vulnerability: although many isolates are complement-sensitive, select encapsulated strains demonstrate partial resistance, underscoring the essential role of complement in the host’s immune response. This dynamic presents a clinical dilemma—effective complement activation can eradicate the bacterium but may simultaneously precipitate unregulated endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in those with an existing predisposition.

This report further supports the emerging association between C. canimorsus and complement-mediated TMAs. Bjørkto et al. have previously detailed a case of C. canimorsus-induced CM-HUS in a patient carrying a variant in complement factor B, which responded favorably to eculizumab therapy. Unlike the aforementioned case, our patient did not exhibit pathogenic or likely pathogenic alterations in complement-associated genes, indicating that CM-HUS may arise solely from infectious triggers, even in the absence of an identifiable genetic predisposition affecting the complement system.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection is particularly complex. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5, constitutes the main therapy for CM-HUS and significantly enhances both hematological and renal recovery, especially when administered promptly. Initiating treatment late has been linked to suboptimal renal restoration and a greater likelihood of advancing to end-stage renal disease.

PEX is essential and life-saving for TTP; however, in cases of CM-HUS, it offers only short-term improvement. This benefit is achieved primarily through the removal of complement byproducts, inflammatory substances, or autoantibodies from circulation, but it does not address the root dysfunction within the complement pathway. In this patient’s case, urine output increased following the last PEX sessions but prior to the administration of eculizumab, representing merely a transient stabilization. Definitive and lasting improvements in blood and kidney function were observed only once complement inhibition was established.

A central therapeutic concern involves employing complement inhibition during periods of active bacteremia, as terminal complement components are essential for host defense against encapsulated organisms like Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, thus highlighting the heightened risk of infections related to eculizumab use. In this situation, eculizumab was started only after clearance of bacteremia, as confirmed by negative blood cultures, to reduce the risk of exacerbating infection. Immunization, prophylactic antibiotics, and vigilant clinical surveillance are crucial components when treating with complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition are critical factors influencing a positive prognosis. In this case, the patient experienced hematological remission along with partial improvement in renal function, aligning with existing data that underscore renal status and the interval to eculizumab administration as key prognostic variables.

While CM-HUS associated with C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report emphasizes the necessity of considering infectious etiologies—including rare ones—when evaluating patients with newly developed TMA. Greater clinical vigilance and advancements in molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate recognition of additional similar instances.
