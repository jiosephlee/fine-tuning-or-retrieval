Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health conditions experienced rapidly escalating sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her clinical course was complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, eventually leading to complete loss of urine output and reliance on dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she initially responded to antibiotics and supportive therapies, she subsequently suffered from abrupt-onset isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory investigations and renal biopsy established a diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), specifically complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Implementation of plasma exchange and eculizumab therapy led to haematological remission and partial recovery of renal function. This case underscores the role of C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, while detailing the diagnostic process, the difficulties in differential diagnosis, and the multifaceted nature of managing TMA in the aftermath of sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus characterized by slow growth, which commonly inhabits the oral cavities of dogs and cats as part of their normal flora. Human infections are uncommon, but can result from animal bites, scratches, or even simple close contact with pets. The spectrum of clinical manifestations is broad, including presentations such as isolated meningitis, rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Even though this organism generally exhibits sensitivity to a wide array of antibiotics, reported mortality rates may reach up to 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most frequently implicated in causing invasive zoonoses in humans. Its identification can often be challenging and protracted due to both its demanding growth requirements and prolonged culture periods, which has encouraged the adoption of advanced molecular diagnostics, including real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the clinical case we describe, infection with C. canimorsus led to the development of septic shock, marked leukopaenia, and coagulation disturbances typical of DIC. While the early stage of illness was primarily characterized by sepsis with multiple organ involvement, subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), a drop in platelet count, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated a secondary TMA process. This progression underscores the potential for complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) to remain unrecognized during initial phases dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two previous uneventful pregnancies arrived at the emergency department exhibiting a rapidly deteriorating condition. Four days before hospitalization, she suffered a dog bite to her upper lip and subsequently received a tetanus vaccination as a preventive measure. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed immediately after the injury, she did not start the antibiotic therapy because she was unable to pick up the medication. She subsequently experienced a high-grade fever (>40 °C), general malaise, and decreased oral intake, eventually seeking medical care upon the onset of numbness and discoloration in her feet. During an evaluation at a primary-care emergency facility, she was found to be hypoglycaemic, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, with her blood glucose reaching a low of 0.6 mmol/L. This finding necessitated immediate referral to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and interactive but showed clinical signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness in the lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal preparations or illicit substances, and there was no history of similar illness among family members. Initial laboratory tests demonstrated significant multisystem dysfunction, characterized by pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, AKI, elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas assessment indicated a metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.27 (normal range: 7.35–7.43) and an elevated lactate concentration of 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). Hypoglycaemia persisted despite administration of large amounts of intravenous glucose, which was presumed secondary to sepsis. At presentation, her urine output remained within normal limits; urinalysis was notable for proteinuria (2+) and haematuria (3+).

Based on her array of clinical signs—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy alongside acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—her illness was assessed as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) stemming from bacteraemia and the early stages of sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3). A surgical etiology for her mottled limbs, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded following both physical and imaging assessments. Given the critical nature of her symptoms, empirical intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to target pathogens associated with dogs, including Capnocytophaga species. Importantly, C. canimorsus was considered early and was subsequently identified after seven days through both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was then admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more rigorous monitoring.

Hematology specialists were engaged because of her pancytopenia and coagulopathy. Potential diagnoses considered were acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also evaluated, but this was deemed improbable given the presence of leukopenia, heightened inflammatory indices, normal haptoglobin levels, and absence of compensatory reticulocytosis. Flow cytometric analysis was undertaken, and a bone marrow biopsy was initially scheduled; however, the absence of abnormal findings—including no rise in CD34+ blast cells or aberrant immunophenotypes—resulted in the biopsy being called off. Clinically, her signs continued to align with sepsis. In the interim, while diagnostic results were pending, supportive care including platelet and fibrinogen transfusions was provided.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient experienced complete anuria and a swift decline in renal function. Efforts at aggressive fluid resuscitation, along with simultaneous administration of loop diuretics, did not yield improvement. Her clinical course was further complicated by the onset of septic cardiomyopathy and resultant fluid overload, necessitating continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), were negative.

By the third ICU day, the patient exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding characterized by ecchymoses, as well as distal cyanosis and ischemic changes involving multiple toes and fingers, although peripheral pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical features were indicative of microthrombosis consistent with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Despite multiorgan dysfunction, she did not require vasopressors apart from stress-dose corticosteroids administered during the early septic phase. She was also given albumin infusions in addition to other blood products.

Following eight days of hospitalisation, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit to continue treatment for dialysis-dependent AKI and intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, she remained haemodynamically stable yet anuric, exhibited widespread oedema and several haematomas, but maintained normal alertness and orientation. Antibiotic therapy was subsequently narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, and later transitioned to oral Apocillin, in accordance with blood culture findings.

While on the nephrology service, laboratory results revealed improvement in inflammatory markers, a reactive leukocytosis, recovery in platelet counts, and stable haemoglobin levels. Despite these improvements, renal indices continued to display persistent azotaemia, though normalisation of coagulation studies suggested resolution of the coagulopathy. After initial progress, her clinical status deteriorated, marked by an abrupt, isolated decline in haemoglobin (dropping from 111 to 68 g/L over six days) despite no evidence of active bleeding or haemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained chronically reduced (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin dropped below detectable levels, and LDH increased, all indicative of ongoing haemolytic activity (see figure 1).

A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) for IgG suggested the possibility of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. As a result, high-dose prednisolone was administered in conjunction with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were performed over a span of 2 days. The patient exhibited ongoing haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia (platelet count 41–46 × 10⁹/L) despite corticosteroid treatment, prompting more comprehensive assessment. Examination of a peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of MAHA and raising suspicion for TMA (see figure 2). The initial positive DAT was most likely an artifact attributable to penicillin exposure or prior transfusions, as follow-up testing was negative.

Complement panels and ADAMTS13 activity were subsequently submitted for further analysis. Standard complement measurements (C3/C4) were found to be within reference intervals, and functional assays assessing the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways also yielded normal results. ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently reported at 43.5%, which is not consistent with thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP generally manifests with activity below 10%. While these confirmatory tests were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was started to provide interim treatment before administering the first dose of eculizumab.

Following an increase in thrombocyte count to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was carried out, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, glomeruli displaying ischaemic changes with fibrin thrombi, as well as occasional crescent formations—findings indicative of TMA.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was excluded due to a negative PCR result from the stool sample.

After the second and third PEX treatments, the patient’s spontaneous urine output resumed. Eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg intravenously once per week for four weeks) was started after administering initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B). The administration of the second vaccine doses was scheduled to occur as an outpatient procedure eight weeks later. Since the vaccines were not given two weeks prior to initiating complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement testing, aimed at excluding hereditary complement-mediated TMA, was requested from Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden). For logistical reasons and to ensure patient safety, three Octaplas PEX procedures and one eculizumab induction dose were completed before sample collection. Assessment of functional complement activity showed a substantially decreased alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50 at 6%), whereas the levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits, and antifactor H antibody testing returned negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical intervention, the patient’s overall status is stable, and her urine output remains satisfactory. Nevertheless, renal function is still compromised (serum creatinine measured at 175 µmol/L), necessitating ongoing surveillance through the nephrology outpatient service (figure 4). She is receiving maintenance eculizumab at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, with this regimen maintained for four months following initial treatment. Comprehensive genetic testing of the complement pathway did not detect any disease-causing variants; these preliminary findings were shared verbally by the reference laboratory, while the official report was still forthcoming at the time of manuscript submission. Given the significant severity of her presentation, complement inhibition therapy is being maintained, with future plans to reconsider its continuation if renal function shows stabilization. Discovery of pathogenic complement gene mutations would have supported extended treatment duration with careful surveillance for recurrence; however, such genetic alterations were not present in this patient.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs include a spectrum of intersecting clinical syndromes.

In adults who present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost diagnostic step is to rule out thrombotic TTP, a disorder characterized by a marked reduction in ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). Tools such as the PLASMIC score can aid in risk stratification while awaiting definitive laboratory assessment, though a firm diagnosis relies on biochemical confirmation. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low probability of TTP prior to testing, with measured ADAMTS13 activity at 43.5%, thereby diminishing the likelihood of TTP and prompting further evaluation.

STEC-HUS also warrants consideration as a differential diagnosis. The absence of Shiga toxin by PCR testing, alongside a lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms, and negative microbiological studies makes STEC-HUS improbable in this context.

The aggregate findings of ongoing haemolysis, presence of schistocytes, AKI requiring dialysis, and renal biopsy revealing TMA established the diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). This conclusion was corroborated by negative PCR results for STEC and the patient’s swift clinical response to eculizumab treatment. Furthermore, the lack of any substantial prior medical issues argues against a baseline complement abnormality necessitating a secondary trigger, instead suggesting that infection directly precipitated CM-HUS in this patient.

CM-HUS is identified as a diagnosis of exclusion based on both clinical and pathological criteria, and it is important to note that complement levels (C3/C4) can remain within normal limits throughout the disease. Thus, the presence of normal complement values (C3/C4) does not rule out CM-HUS, as these levels may not fluctuate significantly over the course of the illness. In this case, the onset of haemolysis occurred only after clinical improvement from sepsis was noted, which implies that the CM-HUS was likely precipitated by the infection, rather than being a pre-existing disorder.

For this patient, initial assessments of complement factors (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were performed immediately prior to starting the first session of plasma exchange (PEX). Additional, more comprehensive complement assays were conducted eight days following the last PEX treatment and four days post-administration of the initial eculizumab dose. The decrease observed in the alternative complement pathway most plausibly reflects a response to therapeutic intervention, rather than a manifestation of baseline complement activation or deficiency.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS (previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS’) is characterized by aberrant activation of the alternative complement pathway, a process that frequently occurs in individuals with a genetic predisposition. Approximately 50%–70% of affected individuals possess genetic mutations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory elements, including factors H, I, and B, as well as membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nevertheless, expression of the disease is not universal among carriers, with clinical onset typically necessitating an exogenous trigger such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or specific medications.

C. canimorsus has seldom been implicated in complement pathway activation; however, this organism can indeed stimulate complement responses in vulnerable hosts. Many strains of C. canimorsus are susceptible to complement-mediated lysis, though capsule-bearing variants may partially evade this response, underscoring the significance of complement activity in immune defense. This situation poses a clinical conundrum: while complement activation is essential for pathogen eradication, it can also precipitate unrestrained endothelial injury and TMA in those with underlying susceptibility.

The present case contributes to the expanding body of evidence associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. A salient example by Bjørkto et al outlined a case of CM-HUS induced by C. canimorsus in a patient harboring a variant in complement factor B, which responded well to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that case, our patient did not exhibit any pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement genes, indicating a form of infection-triggered CM-HUS in the absence of identified inherited complement abnormalities.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Treating CM-HUS in the setting of ongoing infection poses specific difficulties. The monoclonal antibody eculizumab, which targets the terminal complement protein 5, represents the preferred therapeutic approach for CM-HUS, leading to significant improvements in both haematological parameters and kidney function, especially when administered promptly. Delays in starting eculizumab have been correlated with suboptimal renal recovery, increasing the likelihood of developing end-stage kidney disease.

PEX, although critical and often lifesaving in the management of TTP, offers only short-term improvement in CM-HUS. This temporary benefit arises from the removal of soluble complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, rather than correcting the fundamental complement pathway abnormality. In our case, the patient’s urine output improved after the last rounds of PEX but prior to eculizumab administration, indicating merely a brief period of disease stabilization. Durable haematologic and renal improvement followed only after inhibiting complement activity.

A central challenge is administering complement inhibitors during an ongoing bloodstream infection, as terminal complement components play a vital role in defending against encapsulated organisms, such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga. This underscores the potential for infection-related complications with eculizumab use. In the presented case, eculizumab therapy commenced only once blood cultures turned negative, thereby reducing the risk of exacerbating infection. Ensuring immunization, providing antibiotic prophylaxis, and maintaining vigilant monitoring are all critical practices when employing complement pathway inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely diagnosis of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition remain the key factors associated with positive clinical outcomes. In this case, the patient attained haematological remission alongside partial improvement in renal function, mirroring established findings that prognosis primarily depends on renal status and the interval to the initiation of eculizumab treatment.

While occurrences of CM-HUS related to C. canimorsus infection are exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of evaluating infectious aetiologies, including atypical pathogens, in individuals who present with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance coupled with advances in molecular diagnostic techniques could facilitate the recognition of additional cases of this nature.
