Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical conditions developed rapidly progressing sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her condition was complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, eventually resulting in complete loss of urine output and the need for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she initially improved with antibiotic and supportive treatment, she later experienced abrupt onset of isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory investigations and kidney biopsy demonstrated the presence of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Hematological remission was achieved after plasma exchange and eculizumab initiation, with partial recovery of kidney function. This case underscores C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon cause of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults and details the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the difficulties in distinguishing among potential causes, and the challenges of managing TMA arising secondary to sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that exhibits slow growth and is commonly found within the oropharyngeal microbiota of dogs and cats. Although human infection is infrequent, it can result from animal bites, scratches, or even close physical proximity. Clinical manifestations span a broad spectrum, including isolated meningitis, rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). While C. canimorsus generally exhibits sensitivity to a wide range of antibiotics, reported mortality rates can be as high as 30%.

Of the Capnocytophaga genus, C. canimorsus is most commonly implicated in severe zoonotic infections in humans. Detection is often challenging and may be delayed, as the organism is difficult to cultivate and grows slowly in standard laboratory cultures. This diagnostic challenge has led to increasing reliance on molecular approaches, including real-time nanopore sequencing, for timely identification.

In the case described, C. canimorsus infection was characterized by the onset of septic shock, marked leukopaenia, and coagulation abnormalities indicative of DIC. While early stages were dominated by sepsis-associated multi-organ dysfunction, subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), significant thrombocytopenia, and progressive acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed towards the additional presence of TMA. This progression underscores that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can initially be masked by the acute features of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department due to a rapidly deteriorating condition. Four days before admission, she had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and received a tetanus vaccine as a preventive measure. Although she was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) on the same day, she did not start antibiotic therapy because she was unable to pick up the medication. Subsequently, she experienced a high fever (exceeding 40 °C), general malaise, decreased oral intake, and ultimately sought medical help when she noticed numbness and discoloration in her feet. At an urgent primary-care facility, she was found to be hypoglycemic, with blood glucose unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a minimum of 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating emergency transfer to our department.

Upon presentation, the patient was conscious and interactive, though she demonstrated signs of sepsis, such as mottled and cool lower limbs. She denied using herbal medications or any other substances, and there was no history of similar symptoms among family members. Initial laboratory evaluation indicated profound systemic disturbances, including pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas analysis indicated metabolic acidosis, with a pH measured at 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43), alongside an increased lactate concentration of 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of large amounts of intravenous glucose, the patient persisted with hypoglycaemia, attributed to her septic state. At the time of admission, she continued to have satisfactory urine output; urinalysis detected 2+ proteinuria and 3+ haematuria.

In light of her array of clinical features—including fever, pancytopenia, a coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—her condition was attributed to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) as a complication of bacteraemia and emerging sepsis (with a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1/3). A surgical origin for the mottling of her limbs, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded after thorough physical examination and imaging studies. Given the gravity of her symptoms, empiric intravenous therapy with broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to target pathogens commonly associated with dogs, notably including Capnocytophaga species. Of note, C. canimorsus infection was suspected from the outset and subsequently verified by both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures after seven days. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for intensified observation and care.

Haematology specialists were consulted because of the concurrent pancytopenia and coagulopathy. The differential diagnoses at that time encompassed acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was considered but regarded as improbable due to findings such as leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and a lack of compensatory reticulocytosis. Flow cytometric analysis was undertaken, and a bone marrow biopsy was initially scheduled; however, as flow cytometry returned within normal limits—showing no increase in CD34+ blasts or aberrant immunophenotypes—the biopsy was not performed. The overall clinical picture was in keeping with a diagnosis of sepsis. Pending further diagnostic clarification, the patient received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Following her admission to the ICU, the patient became entirely anuric, and her renal function deteriorated swiftly. Efforts at robust fluid resuscitation combined with loop diuretic therapy proved ineffective. She subsequently manifested septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by volume overload, necessitating commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serological assessments, specifically antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), returned negative results.

By her third day in the ICU, she developed widespread cutaneous hemorrhage characterized by ecchymoses, along with cyanotic and ischaemic alterations in several toes and fingers; however, peripheral pulses and sensory function were maintained. These clinical features aligned with microthrombotic events in the setting of DIC. Despite the extent of organ system compromise, she did not require vasopressor agents other than the stress-dose corticosteroids provided during the acute septic episode. In addition, she was administered albumin as well as various blood product transfusions.

Following an 8-day hospitalization, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit to continue treatment for dialysis-dependent AKI and receive intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival, she was haemodynamically stable but remained anuric, exhibited widespread oedema and numerous haematomas, yet remained conscious and oriented. Based on blood culture findings, her antibiotic regimen was stepped down to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently transitioned to oral Apocillin.

During her stay on the nephrology ward, laboratory investigations demonstrated a downward trend in inflammatory markers, reactive leukocytosis, recovery in platelet count, and stable haemoglobin levels. Kidney function tests, however, continued to reflect persistent azotaemia, although coagulation parameters returned to normal, pointing to an improvement in her coagulopathy. Her laboratory findings at this time are summarized in Table 2.

Despite her initial progress, her condition took a turn when she experienced a rapid and isolated decline in haemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days), with no overt bleeding or signs of haemodynamic compromise. Her platelet count stayed low (30–40×10⁹/L); haptoglobin dropped below detectable levels, and LDH concentrations increased—consistent with ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, suggesting a possible diagnosis of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was initiated together with standard prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over a two-day period. The lack of improvement in haemolysis despite corticosteroid administration, coupled with persistently low platelet counts (41–46 × 10⁹/L), prompted additional investigations. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, supporting the presence of MAHA consistent with TMA (figure 2). The initial positive DAT result was probably falsely elevated, potentially attributable to penicillin administration or antecedent transfusions; a subsequent DAT was negative.

Further workup included assessment of complement profiles and ADAMTS13 activity. Standard complement assays (C3/C4) available locally were found to be within reference intervals, and tests assessing the functional integrity of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways were similarly unremarkable. The ADAMTS13 activity measurement, reported soon afterward, was 43.5%, which effectively excluded thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) as the cause, given that TTP is generally associated with activity levels below 10%. While these diagnostic results were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced as interim treatment prior to the administration of the first dose of eculizumab.

Following an elevation of the thrombocyte count to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed pronounced acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia characterized by fibrin thrombi, and sporadic crescent formations—findings consistent with TMA.

The diagnostic conclusions were most clearly illustrated by the light microscopy images presented in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy data are not displayed, as immunofluorescence did not indicate any immune complex deposits, and electron microscopy showed no signs indicative of a primary glomerulopathy.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was excluded based on the absence of Shiga toxin gene targets in stool PCR testing.

After the second and third PEX procedures, the patient experienced a restoration of spontaneous urine output. Eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg intravenously per week for four weeks) commenced following administration of the initial doses of the meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B). The subsequent vaccine doses were scheduled for administration as outpatient procedures eight weeks later. Because the vaccines were not given at least two weeks prior to the initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was provided.

Comprehensive complement analyses to exclude hereditary complement-mediated TMA were requested from Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. Taking into account practical constraints and patient safety, three Octaplas-based PEX sessions and one induction dose of eculizumab were completed before samples were obtained. Functional assessment of the complement system included measurement of alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which was found to be substantially decreased (6%), whereas complement factors H, I, and B were within normal ranges, and tests for antifactor H antibodies yielded negative results.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical management, the patient currently exhibits stable overall health and maintains satisfactory urine output. Nevertheless, her renal function remains compromised, as indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and ongoing monitoring will take place at the nephrology outpatient clinic (see figure 4). She is receiving maintenance eculizumab therapy (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks) for a total of four months following initial induction treatment. Comprehensive genetic analysis of the complement pathway did not reveal any disease-causing mutations; this information was delivered orally by the reference laboratory, with the official written results still awaited at the time of manuscript submission. Given these findings and the severity of her clinical presentation, complement inhibition therapy is being maintained, with a scheduled review to consider discontinuation if her renal function stabilizes. The detection of pathogenic complement gene mutations would support prolonged therapy accompanied by vigilant relapse surveillance. However, no such mutations were identified in this patient’s genetic testing.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs represent a spectrum of clinical disorders with overlapping presentations.

When adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost consideration is ruling out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by pronounced ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). To facilitate risk stratification during the interim period before definitive results are available, clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score can be employed, but definitive diagnosis rests on laboratory confirmation. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low pretest likelihood for TTP, while ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5% further diminished the probability of TTP, steering the diagnostic process in another direction.

STEC-HUS represents another key diagnostic possibility. However, Shiga toxin testing by PCR returned negative in this patient. In combination with the absence of a preceding gastrointestinal illness and negative microbiologic evaluations, these factors effectively ruled out STEC-HUS.

The combination of ongoing haemolysis, the presence of schistocytes, AKI necessitating dialysis, and kidney biopsy findings compatible with TMA ultimately indicated a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). This conclusion was bolstered by negative Shiga toxin PCR results and the patient's marked improvement following the initiation of eculizumab. Additionally, her unremarkable past medical history made an underlying complement abnormality with a required ‘second hit’ unlikely, instead supporting an infection-associated etiology for her CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is identified based on clinical and pathological features once other diagnoses have been excluded, and complement components (C3/C4) may remain within normal limits throughout the illness. It is critical to recognize that typical complement values (C3/C4) do not rule out CM-HUS, as these can persist in the normal range during the disease’s progression. Moreover, haemolysis was noted only after there was improvement in sepsis, pointing toward an infection-induced CM-HUS rather than an underlying chronic condition.

For this case, core complement assays (C3/C4) as well as ADAMTS13 activity measurements were taken right before initiating the first session of PEX. Subsequent extended complement profiling took place 8 days after the last PEX session and 4 days subsequent to administering the initial dose of eculizumab. The decrease observed in the alternative pathway activity likely reflects the impact of therapeutic interventions rather than being indicative of inherent complement system consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS (previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS’) results from inappropriate activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with an underlying genetic predisposition. Genetic alterations or autoantibodies that disrupt the function of complement regulatory proteins—such as factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are detected in approximately 50–70% of cases. Nevertheless, not all individuals with such variants will manifest the disease; an external precipitating factor, most often an infection, pregnancy, autoimmune condition, or certain drugs, is usually necessary for clinical symptoms to develop.

Though infrequent in the literature, C. canimorsus has been documented to activate complement pathways, especially in vulnerable individuals. The bacterium demonstrates heterogeneity in its susceptibility to serum-mediated killing: many isolates are sensitive to complement, while a subset—those with a polysaccharide capsule—exhibit partial resistance. This suggests complement activity is vital to controlling the infection. However, this protective mechanism introduces a clinical dilemma: while complement activation contributes to pathogen clearance, it can also provoke excessive endothelial injury and TMA in genetically or otherwise predisposed hosts.

The present case contributes to the limited, but expanding, documentation connecting C. canimorsus to complement-mediated TMAs. For instance, a notable case reported by Bjørkto et al involved a patient who developed C. canimorsus-associated CM-HUS in the context of a complement factor B mutation, and responded well to eculizumab treatment. By contrast, genetic analysis in our patient revealed no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement-associated genes, reinforcing the idea that CM-HUS can be instigated purely by infection in the absence of an identifiable inherited complement abnormality.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS during ongoing infection introduces distinct difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits the terminal complement component 5, is regarded as the first-line therapy for CM-HUS and significantly enhances both hematological and renal outcomes, with the greatest benefit seen when administered promptly. Delays in starting eculizumab are correlated with less complete renal recovery and an increased likelihood of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

PEX, while lifesaving for patients with TTP, generally offers only transient improvement in CM-HUS. This temporary effect results from the removal of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory molecules, or autoantibodies, without addressing the primary complement dysregulation. In the case presented, restoration of diuresis followed the last PEX sessions but preceded administration of eculizumab, signifying only a brief stabilization. Lasting improvements in hematologic and renal function only emerged subsequent to complement inhibition.

An important therapeutic consideration is the employment of complement inhibition in the setting of ongoing bacteraemia. Terminal complement proteins are vital for host defense against encapsulated bacteria such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, thereby heightening concerns over infections associated with eculizumab use. In the current case, initiation of eculizumab occurred only after clearance of blood cultures, thereby reducing the risk of exacerbating active infection. Nonetheless, vaccination, use of antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring are necessary components when employing complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely diagnosis of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition are critical factors influencing positive clinical outcomes. In this case, the patient attained haematological remission alongside partial recovery of renal function, in alignment with existing literature that underscores renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab as key prognostic variables.

While CM-HUS triggered by C. canimorsus remains exceptionally uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of investigating infectious etiologies—including atypical pathogens—in patients who present with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and the application of molecular diagnostic tools may facilitate the detection of additional cases.
