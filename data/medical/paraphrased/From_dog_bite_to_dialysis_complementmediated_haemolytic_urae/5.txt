Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical issues presented with rapidly escalating sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her clinical course was complicated by the development of disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to total anuria, necessitating dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she experienced initial clinical improvement following antibiotic and supportive therapy, she subsequently manifested abrupt isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory assessment and renal biopsy substantiated the diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), with findings consistent with complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Haematological remission was attained after plasma exchange and the commencement of eculizumab, alongside partial restoration of kidney function. This report underscores C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in adults with intact immune systems, detailing the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, challenges in differential diagnosis, and the complexities encountered in both the clinical evolution and management of TMA secondary to sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that exhibits slow growth and resides as part of the typical oropharyngeal flora in both dogs and cats. While infections in humans are uncommon, transmission may result from animal bites, scratches, or even close contact. The clinical manifestations are diverse, spanning from isolated meningitis to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although this organism is generally susceptible to a range of antibiotics, the associated mortality rate may be as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in severe zoonotic infections affecting humans. Identification is often delayed due to its fastidious growth requirements and prolonged culture times, increasingly necessitating molecular diagnostic tools, including real-time nanopore sequencing.

In our reported case, the patient developed septic shock with pronounced leukopaenia and coagulopathy indicative of DIC following C. canimorsus infection. While the initial phase was dominated by multiorgan involvement secondary to sepsis, subsequent findings of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), marked thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed towards an overlapping TMA process. This progression underscores that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may remain unrecognized during the initial presentation of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant medical history and two previously uncomplicated pregnancies arrived at the emergency department experiencing rapid clinical deterioration. Four days before hospitalization, she suffered a dog bite on her upper lip and subsequently received a prophylactic tetanus vaccine. Although she was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) at the time of the injury, she did not start the antibiotic due to inability to retrieve the medication. She subsequently experienced high-grade fever (>40 °C), general malaise, and diminished oral intake. She sought further medical care after developing numbness and discoloration in her feet. In the primary-care emergency setting, she was discovered to be hypoglycaemic, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, with her blood glucose reaching a low point of 0.6 mmol/L. This warranted her immediate transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and oriented but exhibited clinical features consistent with sepsis, including mottling and coolness of the lower limbs. She denied consumption of herbal supplements or other substances, and there was no reported history of similar illnesses among family members. Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated significant multisystem involvement, notable for pronounced inflammatory response, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, transaminitis, and coagulation abnormalities. Arterial blood gas assessment identified metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range: 7.35–7.43) and a raised lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference <2.5 mmol/L). Despite the administration of large amounts of intravenous glucose, persistent hypoglycaemia was observed, likely secondary to sepsis. At presentation, urine output was adequate; urinalysis revealed 2+ proteinuria and 3+ haematuria.

Based on her collection of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy in conjunction with AKI, and lactic acidosis—her clinical state was assessed as DIC resulting from bacteraemia and early sepsis, as indicated by a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1/3. Following thorough clinical and radiological assessment, a surgical etiology for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded. Owing to the seriousness of her initial presentation, empiric intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was commenced to target potential dog-associated microorganisms, specifically Capnocytophaga species. It is noteworthy that C. canimorsus was highly suspected from the beginning, a diagnosis confirmed one week later by positive aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. For enhanced monitoring and management, the patient was then admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU).

Due to the presence of pancytopenia and coagulopathy, haematology input was sought. The working differential included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was evaluated but ultimately deemed less probable, considering the leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and a non-compensatory reticulocyte response. Flow cytometry was performed, and bone marrow biopsy initially scheduled; however, following normal findings—including no increase in CD34+ blasts and no abnormal phenotypes—the biopsy was canceled. Clinically, the features aligned with sepsis. While awaiting further investigations, the patient received supportive transfusions of thrombocytes and fibrinogen.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient developed complete anuria and her renal function deteriorated swiftly. Efforts at vigorous fluid resuscitation in combination with loop diuretics did not yield improvement. Her condition progressed to septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by volume overload, which necessitated the commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune screening, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), returned negative results.

By the third ICU day, she exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding, marked ecchymoses, and cyanotic as well as ischaemic changes at the extremities of multiple toes and fingers. Despite these changes, peripheral pulses and sensory perception remained intact. These manifestations aligned with microthrombotic processes in the setting of DIC. In spite of her multisystem involvement, vasopressors were not required beyond stress-dose corticosteroid therapy provided during the initial septic episode. She was additionally managed with albumin and various blood products.

Following eight days of hospital admission, the patient was relocated to the nephrology ward for continued management of dialysis-dependent AKI and intravenous antibiotic treatment. Upon arrival to the ward, she was hemodynamically stable yet anuric, exhibited widespread edema and multiple bruises, but remained alert and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was subsequently narrowed to intravenous ampicillin and then transitioned to oral Apocillin, guided by blood culture findings.

During her nephrology ward stay, laboratory evaluations demonstrated a downtrend in markers of inflammation, with leukocytosis in the reactive range, improving platelet counts, and stable haemoglobin levels. Renal function tests revealed ongoing azotaemia, whereas coagulation studies normalized, consistent with resolution of her previous coagulopathy. Despite early positive trends, her course was complicated by a sudden, isolated decrease in haemoglobin (from 111 g/L to 68 g/L within six days) in the absence of any clinical bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts continued to be depressed (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin levels dropped below detectable limits, and LDH was elevated, collectively pointing towards ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, was positive for IgG, which raised concern for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was promptly administered in addition to standard prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over a period of 2 days. As the haemolysis persisted despite corticosteroid treatment and platelet counts remained persistently low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), further diagnostic investigation was pursued. Examination of the peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, indicating MAHA and suggestive of TMA (see figure 2). It is probable that the initial positive DAT represented a false positive result, potentially due to penicillin exposure or preceding transfusions; a subsequent DAT returned negative.

Assessment of complement pathways and ADAMTS13 activity was undertaken. Routine complement measurements (C3/C4) available onsite fell within normal parameters, and functional assays of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways similarly showed no abnormalities. The result for ADAMTS13 activity, received soon thereafter, measured 43.5%, effectively excluding a diagnosis of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP is classically associated with activity less than 10%. In the interim, pending definitive test outcomes, plasma exchange (PEX) was initiated as a bridging intervention prior to the first administration of eculizumab.

After the thrombocyte count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and sporadic crescent formation—findings in keeping with TMA.

The most salient diagnostic features were those observed under light microscopy, as depicted in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy data are omitted here; immunofluorescence showed an absence of immune complex deposition, while electron microscopy did not identify features of primary glomerulopathy.

STEC-HUS was excluded following a negative result on stool PCR testing for Shiga toxin-producing E. coli.

After the patient underwent the second and third sessions of PEX, spontaneous diuresis resumed. Eculizumab therapy was started after administration of the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (serogroup B)—using a regimen of 900 mg intravenously each week for a total of four weeks. The follow-up doses for these vaccines were scheduled for eight weeks later as outpatient procedures. Since the required two-week interval prior to initiating complement inhibition with vaccines had not elapsed, Apocillin prophylaxis was provided.

Comprehensive complement testing was ordered from Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden) to exclude hereditary forms of complement-mediated TMA. For reasons related to patient safety and logistical constraints, three sessions of PEX with Octaplas and one induction dose of eculizumab were carried out prior to sample collection. The functional complement assay revealed a substantially decreased alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50) at 6%, while the levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits, and no antifactor H antibodies were detected.

Outcome and follow-up:

After intensive medical intervention, the patient is currently stable overall, exhibiting satisfactory urine output. However, renal function remains impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L. Ongoing care will be provided through the nephrology outpatient service (figure 4). The patient is maintained on eculizumab therapy (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks), which will be continued for a period of 4 months following induction. Comprehensive complement genetic analysis did not detect any pathogenic variants; preliminary findings were relayed verbally from the reference laboratory, while the formal written report was still pending at manuscript submission. In view of the disease’s severity, complement inhibition therapy will be maintained, with plans to evaluate the possibility of cessation if renal function shows sustained improvement. Detection of pathogenic mutations in complement genes would have supported prolonged therapy and vigilant relapse monitoring; however, no such genetic abnormalities were found in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs represent a spectrum of closely related clinical conditions.

In adults who present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost concern is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Although clinical assessment tools such as the PLASMIC score assist with stratification while awaiting laboratory confirmation, the ultimate diagnosis hinges on ADAMTS13 activity testing. For our case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7 (indicating a low pretest probability for TTP), and ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, reducing the likelihood of TTP and redirecting further diagnostic efforts.

STEC-HUS also warrants consideration. However, polymerase chain reaction testing for Shiga toxin was negative. Moreover, the lack of a preceding gastrointestinal illness and negative results from microbiological assays do not support a diagnosis of STEC-HUS.

Collectively, ongoing haemolysis, evidence of schistocytosis, AKI necessitating dialysis, and histopathological findings on renal biopsy compatible with TMA, all indicated a diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). This conclusion was corroborated by the negative Shiga toxin PCR and the patient’s prompt improvement following eculizumab administration. The absence of significant prior medical history makes the possibility of an underlying complement abnormality less likely, favoring the theory of an infection-induced CM-HUS episode.

CM-HUS is identified through a clinicopathological process that primarily involves ruling out other potential causes, and complement concentrations (C3/C4) may stay within normal parameters throughout the illness. Crucially, the presence of normal C3 and C4 levels does not rule out a diagnosis of CM-HUS, as these values can persist within reference intervals during the disease. Of particular note, haemolysis manifested only after there was an improvement in sepsis, indicating that the CM-HUS was likely initiated by infection rather than being pre-existing.

For our patient, measurements of standard complement (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were taken right before the initial PEX session. Extended complement assessment occurred eight days following the last PEX session and four days subsequent to the first eculizumab administration. The reduction noted in the alternative complement pathway is best explained by the effects of therapy, instead of a baseline complement deficiency or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS (formerly referred to as ‘atypical HUS’) is characterized by aberrant activation of the alternative complement pathway, often occurring in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50%–70% of cases present with either genetic variants or autoantibodies that impact complement regulatory components, such as factors H, I, and B, as well as membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nevertheless, not all individuals with these predispositions will develop the disease; typically, an additional external trigger—most frequently infections, pregnancy, autoimmune processes, or specific medications—is necessary for clinical disease to emerge.

Although infrequent, C. canimorsus has been shown to stimulate complement pathways, especially in hosts with increased vulnerability. The bacterium displays heterogeneity in susceptibility to serum-mediated killing: many isolates are sensitive to complement, whereas certain capsule-producing strains can partially evade complement attack. This underscores the pivotal function of complement in the immune response. This situation presents a clinical dilemma: while complement activation contributes to pathogen elimination, it can simultaneously result in unchecked endothelial injury and TMA in predisposed individuals.

The present case further expands the limited literature associating C. canimorsus infection with complement-mediated TMAs. Of note, Bjørkto et al detailed a case involving a patient with C. canimorsus-triggered CM-HUS carrying a variant in complement factor B who responded favorably to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that instance, no pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene variants were detected in our patient, lending support to the concept of infection-driven CM-HUS manifesting in the absence of an identified inherited complement abnormality.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amidst ongoing infection is particularly complex. Eculizumab—a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5—is the preferred therapy for CM-HUS, demonstrating substantial benefits for both haematological and renal outcomes, especially with early administration. Initiating treatment later is linked to partial renal recovery, leading to an increased likelihood of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

In comparison, while PEX is essential in the management of TTP, its effectiveness in CM-HUS is limited and temporary. The benefit primarily stems from removal of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, rather than correction of the intrinsic complement system abnormality. In our case, the patient's urine output resumed following the last PEX sessions but before the initiation of eculizumab, indicating only short-lived improvement. Durable haematological and renal restoration was achieved solely after complement inhibition.

An important therapeutic challenge involves the use of complement inhibitors in the presence of active bacteraemia, since terminal complement components are vital for host immunity against encapsulated pathogens such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga. This raises substantial concerns regarding infection risks associated with eculizumab therapy. In this instance, eculizumab was started only after clearance of blood cultures, thereby reducing the hazard of exacerbating infection. Implementation of vaccination, prophylactic antibiotics, and vigilant follow-up remains crucial during treatment with complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and swift commencement of complement inhibition represent the primary factors associated with positive clinical outcomes. In this case, the patient attained haematological remission alongside partial improvement in renal function, aligning with existing data that underscore renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab therapy as key prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS related to C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of evaluating potential infectious precipitants—even rare pathogens—in patients who present with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and advanced molecular diagnostic tools may facilitate recognition of additional cases in the future.
