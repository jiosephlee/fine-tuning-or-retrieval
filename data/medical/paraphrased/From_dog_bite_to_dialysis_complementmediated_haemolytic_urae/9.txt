Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no significant prior medical history presented with fulminant sepsis occurring 4 days after being bitten by a dog. Her clinical course was complicated by the onset of disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, eventually leading to complete loss of urine output and dependence on dialysis. Blood cultures grew Capnocytophaga canimorsus. Although she initially responded to antibiotics and supportive measures, she subsequently experienced abrupt, isolated haemolysis along with sustained thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory investigations and renal biopsy established the diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), with complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) identified as the underlying etiology. Treatment with plasma exchange and eculizumab resulted in haematological remission and partial restoration of renal function. This case illustrates that C. canimorsus infection, although rare, can precipitate CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults, and it describes the diagnostic evaluation, the intricacies involved in distinguishing between potential causes, and the challenges of management for TMA occurring in the context of sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus commonly present in the oropharyngeal microbiota of dogs and cats. Infections in humans are uncommon, and transmission may result from animal bites, scratches, or even close proximity to animals. The clinical spectrum is broad, encompassing cases of isolated meningitis as well as rapid-onset sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although the bacterium is generally susceptible to a wide range of antibiotics, mortality rates can be as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is the most frequently implicated in invasive zoonotic infections in humans. Because of its demanding growth requirements and prolonged time to culture positivity, diagnosis is often delayed, thereby increasing reliance on molecular diagnostics such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the present report, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock with profound leukopenia and coagulation abnormalities in keeping with DIC. Initial management centered on sepsis-associated multiorgan failure; however, subsequent findings of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), reduced platelet count, and progressive acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated the emergence of TMA. This sequence of events demonstrates that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may remain unrecognized in the initial stages dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously healthy and with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department with an acutely deteriorating condition. Four days earlier, she had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and received a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Although she was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) at the time of injury, she did not begin antibiotic therapy due to her inability to obtain the medication. Subsequently, she developed a high fever (>40 °C), general malaise, and decreased oral intake, later seeking care after experiencing numbness and discoloration in her feet. At a primary-care emergency facility, she was noted to have hypoglycemia that did not respond to oral glucose or glucagon, with the lowest measured value at 0.6 mmol/L, leading to her urgent referral to our emergency department.

Upon presentation, the patient was conscious and interactive but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottled, cool lower limbs. She denied using herbal products or other substances, and no family members had experienced comparable symptoms or illnesses. Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated significant systemic dysfunction, characterized by profound inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated hepatic transaminases, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference <2.5 mmol/L). Hypoglycaemia persisted despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, presumed secondary to sepsis. On admission, urine output was preserved; urinalysis detected proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her array of clinical findings—fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy alongside acute kidney injury and lactic acidosis—was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteremia and the early stages of sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1/3). A surgical etiology for her peripheral mottling, such as necrotizing fasciitis, was excluded following clinical assessment and radiographic imaging. Due to the critical nature of her case, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to target pathogens commonly associated with dogs, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was highly suspected from the outset and confirmed seven days later by growth in both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. Subsequently, she was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring.

Hematology consultation was sought because of the pancytopenia and coagulopathy. Considerations in the differential included acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) was entertained but deemed improbable owing to the presence of leukopenia, increased inflammatory markers, unremarkable haptoglobin, and a non-compensatory reticulocyte count. Flow cytometry was performed, and bone marrow biopsy was planned; however, both normal findings—including the absence of elevated CD34+ blasts or aberrant immunophenotypes—resulted in cancellation of the biopsy. Clinically, the constellation of symptoms was attributed to sepsis. Supportive management involved transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen while awaiting additional diagnostic information.

Upon admission to the ICU, the patient developed complete anuria accompanied by a rapid decline in kidney function. Intensive intravenous fluid administration along with simultaneous use of loop diuretics failed to restore urine output. The patient later exhibited signs of septic cardiomyopathy with worsening fluid overload, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Testing for autoimmunity, including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, she presented with generalized cutaneous bleeding, ecchymoses, and ischemic cyanotic changes involving several fingers and toes, though peripheral pulses and sensation were maintained. These manifestations were attributed to microthrombotic processes related to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although she experienced multiorgan involvement, her hemodynamics remained stable enough to avoid vasopressor therapy except for the stress-dose corticosteroids given during her initial sepsis. The patient was also treated with albumin infusions and various blood products.

Following an 8-day stay in the hospital, she was moved to the nephrology service due to persistent dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and for continued intravenous antibiotic administration. At the time of transfer, the patient was hemodynamically stable though still anuric, exhibited diffuse edema and several hematomas, but remained conscious and oriented. Based on blood culture findings, her antibiotics were sequentially reduced to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin.

Upon arrival to nephrology, laboratory investigations demonstrated a downward trend in inflammatory markers, a reactive leukocytosis, gradual normalization of platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin concentrations. Renal function assessment indicated ongoing azotemia, while coagulation studies had normalized, suggesting improvement in her coagulopathic state. Table 2 provides a summary of her laboratory findings at this point.

Despite some early clinical progress, her status deteriorated with the onset of an abrupt, isolated decrease in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days), with no clinical signs of bleeding or circulatory compromise. Platelet counts persisted at low levels (30–40×10⁹/L); haptoglobin became undetectable, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) values increased, consistent with active hemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, leading to concern for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone therapy was initiated, together with standard prophylaxis, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over a two-day period. As haemolysis did not improve with corticosteroids and platelet counts persisted at low levels (41–46 × 10⁹/L), further assessment was indicated. Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified schistocytes comprising 15%–20% of red blood cells, a finding characteristic of MAHA and indicative of TMA (see figure 2). The initial DAT positivity was probably attributable to penicillin use or recent transfusions; subsequent testing was negative.

Complement testing and ADAMTS13 activity were submitted for evaluation. The routinely available complement measurements, specifically C3 and C4, were within reference ranges, and functional assessments of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways yielded normal results as well. ADAMTS13 activity, reported shortly thereafter, was 43.5%, which effectively excluded a diagnosis of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), given that TTP is characterized by activity levels less than 10%. While pending these laboratory results, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as an interim measure before administration of the initial dose of eculizumab.

Following an increase in the platelet count to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted. The pathology revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, glomeruli demonstrating ischemia with fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, findings indicative of TMA.

The key diagnostic features in this case were most apparent in the light microscopy findings presented in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy images are not included; immunofluorescence showed no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy did not reveal changes consistent with a primary glomerular disease.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was ruled out on the basis of a negative stool PCR analysis.

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, the patient experienced the return of spontaneous urine output. Induction therapy with eculizumab was started (900 mg intravenously per week for 4 weeks) after the first doses of meningococcal vaccinations were administered—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (covering serogroup B). The follow-up doses of these vaccines were scheduled to be given as an outpatient eight weeks later. Since vaccination had not occurred at least two weeks prior to the start of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement analysis to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) was requested from Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden). For patient safety and logistical reasons, three PEX treatments using Octaplas and an initial eculizumab induction dose were administered before sample collection. Functional testing of the complement system, including a quantitative AH50 (alternative pathway activity), revealed a significantly decreased value (6%), while complement components H, I, and B were within normal limits, and tests for antifactor H antibodies were negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical treatment, the patient is currently stable and demonstrates sufficient urine output. Renal function, however, remains impaired, evidenced by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and ongoing care will be provided in the nephrology outpatient setting (figure 4). She continues on maintenance therapy with eculizumab at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, extending for 4 months following induction therapy. Results from comprehensive complement genetic screening did not reveal any disease-causing mutations; this information was relayed verbally from the reference laboratory, with the official report still pending at the time this report was prepared. Given the gravity of her presentation, complement inhibition is being maintained with plans for future evaluation of cessation if kidney function stabilizes. Discovery of a pathogenic complement genetic variant would support prolonged therapy with vigilant monitoring for possible relapse; however, no such mutations were found in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adult patients presenting with microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI), the foremost concern is ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which is characterized by a pronounced reduction in ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). While clinical assessment tools such as the PLASMIC score can aid initial triage prior to laboratory confirmation, definitive diagnosis hinges upon laboratory findings. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and her ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, further reducing suspicion for TTP and allowing the diagnostic focus to shift accordingly.

Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli–associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) was also considered. However, PCR testing for Shiga toxin was negative. The lack of antecedent gastrointestinal symptoms and the absence of microbiological evidence reduced the probability of STEC-HUS.

Consequently, the presentation of ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, dialysis-dependent AKI, and renal biopsy findings indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) pointed toward a diagnosis of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) (see figure 5). This diagnosis was corroborated by the negative Shiga toxin PCR and a marked clinical response to eculizumab. The absence of notable prior medical issues makes a chronic complement deficiency less likely, instead suggesting an infectious trigger as the precipitating factor for CM-HUS in this patient.

CM-HUS is identified through a clinicopathological process of exclusion, and C3 and C4 complement concentrations may be within normal limits throughout the disease progression. Critically, CM-HUS cannot be ruled out by normal C3 or C4 levels, as these can remain unaltered during the illness. In this case, it is notable that hemolysis developed only after the patient's sepsis began to resolve, indicating that CM-HUS was likely provoked by infection rather than representing a pre-existing condition.

For our patient, standard complement assays (C3, C4) and assessment of ADAMTS13 activity were conducted just prior to the initial plasma exchange (PEX) session. Further comprehensive complement analysis was carried out 8 days following the last PEX procedure and 4 days post-administration of the first eculizumab dose. The reduction observed in the alternative complement pathway aligns most closely with therapeutic intervention effects, rather than inherent complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as 'atypical HUS', is attributed to improper activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with a genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of affected patients possess genetic mutations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory proteins, including factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nonetheless, incomplete disease penetrance is observed, as clinical symptoms generally necessitate an external precipitant such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or exposure to specific drugs.

Although cases are uncommon, Capnocytophaga canimorsus is capable of engaging the complement pathways, particularly in vulnerable individuals. C. canimorsus exhibits a spectrum of serum susceptibility, with most isolates being sensitive to complement, but certain strains that produce a capsule demonstrate partial complement resistance. As such, complement function represents a key aspect of immune defense against this organism. This creates a clinical dilemma; while complement activity is essential for pathogen eradication, in those who are susceptible, its activation may precipitate uncontrolled endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

This report contributes to a limited but expanding series of publications correlating C. canimorsus with complement-driven TMAs. Bjørkto et al notably documented a case of CM-HUS instigated by C. canimorsus in a patient found to carry a complement factor B mutation, who experienced a favorable outcome following eculizumab therapy. By contrast, our patient did not possess detectable pathogenic or likely pathogenic alterations in complement-related genes, thereby supporting the possibility of CM-HUS triggered by infection in the absence of identifiable inherited complement abnormalities.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS in the setting of ongoing infection is particularly challenging. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against terminal complement component 5, represents the mainstay therapy for CM-HUS and significantly enhances both hematologic and renal outcomes, especially when administered promptly. Starting treatment late correlates with less complete renal recovery and an increased likelihood of progression to end-stage kidney disease.

Conversely, plasma exchange (PEX) is critical for survival in TTP, but confers only short-term benefit in CM-HUS by removing circulating complement components, pro-inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, without addressing the intrinsic complement pathway abnormality. In our case, the return of diuresis occurred after the last PEX sessions yet prior to the initiation of eculizumab, indicating merely temporary improvement. Lasting hematologic and renal improvement was achieved solely following the initiation of complement inhibition.

A central clinical challenge is administering complement inhibition amidst active bacteremia, as terminal components of the complement pathway are vital for protection against encapsulated organisms—including Neisseria and Capnocytophaga—prompting concerns about heightened infection risk associated with eculizumab. In this situation, eculizumab was started only after confirmation of negative blood cultures, thereby reducing the risk of exacerbating infection. Immunization, prophylactic antibiotics, and careful surveillance remain critical measures when employing complement pathway inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early administration of complement inhibition represent the key factors associated with improved prognosis. In our case, the patient attained hematologic remission and partial renal recovery, aligning with existing data indicating that renal function at presentation and the interval before eculizumab initiation are primary prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS related to C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report emphasizes the need to consider infectious precipitants—including rare pathogens—in individuals presenting with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical awareness and molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate the recognition of additional cases.
