Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history developed rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her condition deteriorated with the onset of disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, ultimately resulting in complete anuria and the need for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia. Although she initially showed clinical improvement with antibiotic therapy and supportive interventions, she subsequently experienced abrupt, isolated hemolysis alongside ongoing thrombocytopenia. Laboratory evaluation and findings from renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), with a diagnosis of complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) established. After treatment with plasma exchange and eculizumab was started, hematologic parameters normalized and there was partial recovery of renal function. This report underscores C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults and details the patient’s diagnostic process, the complexities in distinguishing between possible diagnoses, and the challenging management decisions encountered in cases of TMA after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow-growing, gram-negative bacillus commonly present within the oropharyngeal flora of cats and dogs. Infections in humans are infrequent and may be acquired through animal bites, scratches, or even close interactions. Clinical manifestations exhibit a wide spectrum, including isolated meningitis, fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although this organism is generally susceptible to multiple antibiotics, case fatality rates may be as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most frequently implicated in severe zoonotic infections in humans. Diagnostic delays often result from its fastidious characteristics and slow culture growth, leading to an increased reliance on molecular diagnostics such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the reported patient, infection with C. canimorsus initially manifested as septic shock accompanied by marked leukopaenia and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. While early progression was characterized by sepsis-induced multiorgan involvement, subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and aggravated acute kidney injury (AKI) suggested a secondary TMA process. This underscores that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be concealed during the initial stages of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two previous straightforward pregnancies arrived at the emergency department due to acute clinical deterioration. Four days before presentation, she sustained a dog bite on her upper lip and was given a tetanus vaccine as a preventive measure. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed immediately following the bite; however, she did not start the antibiotics because she was unable to obtain the medication. Subsequently, she experienced high-grade fever (exceeding 40 °C), general malaise, and decreased oral intake. She delayed seeking healthcare until she noticed numbness and discoloration developing in her feet. During an assessment at a primary-care emergency facility, she was noted to have hypoglycemia that did not respond to oral glucose or glucagon, with the lowest measured glucose value of 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating immediate referral to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and appropriately interactive, yet exhibited clinical features of sepsis, including cool and mottled skin on the lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal products or other substances, and there were no reports of illness or similar manifestations among her family members. Initial laboratory assessments demonstrated significant systemic abnormalities characterized by profound inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated hepatic enzymes, and coagulation disturbances. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference <2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of large amounts of intravenous glucose, hypoglycaemia persisted, presumed to be secondary to sepsis. Upon admission, her urine output was adequate; urinalysis indicated proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Owing to the range of her clinical features—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—her presentation was assessed as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) resulting from bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1/3). Clinical and imaging assessments excluded a surgical source for her mottled limbs, such as necrotising fasciitis. Because of the severity at admission, intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics with piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) were initiated to target dog-related organisms, specifically Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was strongly suspected from the beginning and was confirmed one week later by both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for intensified surveillance.

With her pancytopenia and coagulopathy, the haematology team was consulted. Considerations included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis in the differential diagnosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also evaluated but judged improbable due to the presence of leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and absence of reticulocyte response. Flow cytometry was performed and a bone marrow biopsy was scheduled, but the latter was ultimately cancelled after normal flow cytometry results showed no increase in CD34+ blasts or atypical cell populations. The case was clinically compatible with sepsis. During the diagnostic process, she received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Following her admission to the ICU, the patient became completely anuric, accompanied by a rapid decline in kidney function. Intensive fluid resuscitation and the use of loop diuretics were employed concurrently, but these measures proved ineffective. She then developed septic cardiomyopathy complicated by volume overload, leading to the need for continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including ANA and ANCA, were negative.

By the third ICU day, the patient manifested widespread cutaneous bleeding, ecchymoses, and distal cyanosis and ischemia involving several toes and fingers; notably, both pulses and sensation remained intact. These features were indicative of microvascular thrombotic events, consistent with DIC. Although there was multiorgan involvement, vasopressors were not necessary except for stress-dose corticosteroids administered during early sepsis. Additionally, she was given albumin in combination with other blood products.

Following eight days of hospital admission, she was moved to the nephrology unit for continued management of dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and administration of intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, she was haemodynamically stable but remained anuric, exhibited widespread edema and multiple hematomas, and was awake and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was stepped down to intravenous ampicillin, and subsequently to oral Apocillin, guided by blood culture findings.

During her stay in the nephrology ward, laboratory assessments demonstrated a decreasing inflammatory response, reactive leukocytosis, improving platelet counts, and maintained hemoglobin levels. Renal panels revealed ongoing azotemia, while coagulation studies returned to normal values, suggesting resolution of her coagulopathy. Laboratory data at this juncture are provided in Table 2.

Initially, her condition showed improvement, but subsequently the clinical course shifted when she experienced a sudden and isolated reduction in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over a six-day period), in the absence of clinical bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts persisted at low levels (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin was no longer detectable, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) concentrations increased, all pointing toward ongoing hemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, was positive for IgG, suggesting the possibility of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was initiated together with standard prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over a two-day period. As there was no improvement in haemolysis with corticosteroids and platelet counts persisted at low levels (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional investigations were undertaken. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and raising suspicion for thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). The initial DAT result was suspected to be a false positive, potentially attributed to penicillin use or previous transfusions; subsequent repeat testing returned negative results.

Complement profiles and ADAMTS13 activity were ordered. Locally performed complement components (C3/C4) were within reference ranges, and functional assessments of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways were also unremarkable. The result for ADAMTS13 activity was received soon thereafter, showing a value of 43.5%, thus excluding a diagnosis of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP is typically characterized by activity levels below 10%. While awaiting these findings, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as interim management prior to administration of the initial dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and sporadic crescent formation, all indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy images are not presented; immunofluorescence testing did not demonstrate immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy failed to show signs of primary glomerular disease.

Infection with Shiga toxin-producing E. coli leading to hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) was excluded based on a negative stool PCR result.

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) treatments, the patient began to have spontaneous diuresis again. Initiation of eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg intravenously weekly for 4 weeks) followed the administration of the first doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B). Plans were made for the second doses of these vaccines to be given on an outpatient basis, 8 weeks later. Because the vaccines had not been provided at least 2 weeks prior to the start of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was administered.

Comprehensive complement evaluation was ordered to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), with samples sent to Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. For logistical reasons and to maintain patient safety, the patient underwent three sessions of PEX with Octaplas and received one eculizumab induction dose before laboratory sampling. Functional complement tests included quantitative AH50 for alternative pathway activity, which showed a significantly decreased value (6%), while concentrations of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits, and antifactor H antibody testing was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intricate medical treatment, the patient demonstrates good overall health status and satisfactory urine production. Her renal function remains impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L, and she is scheduled for ongoing monitoring in the nephrology outpatient setting (figure 4). The patient is continuing maintenance therapy with eculizumab (1200 mg intravenous administration every 2 weeks), for a duration of 4 months following induction. Comprehensive genetic testing of the complement system revealed no pathogenic mutations; these findings were relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, though the official written documentation was still pending at the time of submission. In light of this information, complement inhibition therapy is maintained because of the seriousness of her condition, with plans for reassessment and potential discontinuation if her renal function stabilizes. Discovery of pathogenic complement gene mutations would support the case for prolonged therapy along with vigilant surveillance for recurrence, but such mutations were absent in this patient.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adult patients presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, it is imperative to first rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score are valuable for stratification while awaiting laboratory confirmation, which remains essential for diagnosis. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, with ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, thereby decreasing the suspicion for TTP and facilitating additional evaluation.

Consideration was also given to STEC-HUS. Shiga toxin PCR testing was negative. The lack of preceding gastrointestinal illness and negative microbiological assays mitigates the likelihood of STEC-HUS.

The diagnosis was ultimately supported by ongoing hemolysis, schistocyte presence, dialysis-dependent AKI, and histologic evidence of TMA on renal biopsy, suggesting CM-HUS (figure 5). The absence of STEC was corroborated by negative PCR results and prompt clinical response to eculizumab. Furthermore, the patient’s minimal medical background diminishes the possibility of an underlying complement abnormality that would necessitate a ‘second hit’, reinforcing the diagnosis of infection-associated CM-HUS.

CM-HUS represents a clinicopathological diagnosis that requires excluding other potential causes, and complement assays (C3 and C4) may remain within normal limits throughout the clinical course. Crucially, the presence of normal C3 and C4 concentrations does not rule out CM-HUS, since these values can persist in the reference interval during the illness. Of note, evidence of haemolysis became apparent only as the patient’s sepsis started to resolve, which is indicative of infection-induced CM-HUS as opposed to a pre-existing disorder.

For this patient, standard complement measurements (C3 and C4) along with ADAMTS13 activity were checked just prior to the first session of plasma exchange (PEX). Additional comprehensive complement analysis was conducted 8 days after the last PEX treatment and 4 days subsequent to initial administration of eculizumab. The identified suppression of the alternative complement pathway is more suggestive of treatment-related effects than of baseline complement exhaustion or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), formerly referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ results from improper activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with an underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of cases involve genetic alterations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory proteins such as factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Despite this association, penetrance remains partial, and clinical symptoms generally emerge only in the presence of an external precipitant, such as infections, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or certain drugs.

Although infrequently documented, Capnocytophaga canimorsus is capable of triggering complement activation, especially in vulnerable hosts. The organism exhibits variable susceptibility to serum complement; while many isolates are sensitive, some strains expressing capsules demonstrate partial resistance. Thus, effective complement function is crucial for host defense. This produces a clinical dilemma: whereas complement activation can help eradicate the organism, it may simultaneously lead to excessive endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in predisposed individuals.

Our report contributes to the limited, yet expanding literature connecting C. canimorsus to complement-mediated TMAs. One notable example by Bjørkto et al detailed a patient who developed CM-HUS triggered by C. canimorsus, with a detected factor B variant, and who responded favorably to eculizumab therapy. In contrast, our patient showed no pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene mutations, lending support to the concept of infection-provoked CM-HUS in the absence of a detectable hereditary complement abnormality.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection involves distinct complexities. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5, constitutes the primary therapy for CM-HUS and significantly enhances haematological and renal outcomes, particularly when administered promptly. Postponed treatment correlates with incomplete renal recovery and thereby an elevated likelihood of developing end-stage kidney disease.

By contrast, plasma exchange (PEX) is a critical intervention in TTP, whereas in CM-HUS it only offers short-term improvement. This effect largely results from clearing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies rather than addressing the fundamental defect in the complement pathway. In this case, the patient exhibited return of diuresis following the last PEX sessions but preceding eculizumab administration, indicating only brief stabilization. Lasting haematological and renal restoration was achieved only with complement inhibition.

A major therapeutic challenge arises when considering complement blockade during active bacteraemia, as terminal complement components are essential for protection against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, thus generating apprehension regarding the risk of infections related to eculizumab. In this situation, eculizumab was started only after blood cultures had cleared, thus reducing the risk of uncontrolled infection. When using complement inhibitors, immunisation, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring remain crucial.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition represent the key factors associated with positive clinical outcomes. In this case, the patient reached haematological remission and experienced partial improvement in renal function, aligning with existing data indicating that prognosis is primarily influenced by baseline renal status and the interval to eculizumab initiation.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus infection is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to consider infectious etiologies—including atypical pathogens—in individuals who present with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinician vigilance and advances in molecular testing may facilitate recognition of additional cases.
