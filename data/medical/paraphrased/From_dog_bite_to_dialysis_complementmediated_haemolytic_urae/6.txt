From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s, with no prior health issues, developed rapidly escalating sepsis four days subsequent to being bitten by a dog. Her condition was complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, eventually leading to complete anuria and dependence on dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she initially responded to antibiotics and supportive management, she experienced abrupt onset of isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Subsequent laboratory assessments and a renal biopsy demonstrated evidence of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Introduction of plasma exchange and eculizumab therapy resulted in haematological remission and partial improvement of renal function. This report demonstrates that C. canimorsus infection can serve as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in adults with competent immune systems, and it details the diagnostic evaluation, differential diagnostic difficulties, and the complexities inherent to both the clinical progression and management strategies in TMA occurring post-sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and commonly resides in the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Although infections in humans are uncommon, they may arise following bites, scratches, or even from close interactions with animals. The clinical spectrum is broad, encompassing cases from isolated meningitis to severe manifestations such as fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Mortality remains significant, with rates reported up to 30%, notwithstanding the organism’s generally wide-ranging antibiotic sensitivity.

Among the Capnocytophaga genus, C. canimorsus is most often associated with severe zoonotic infections in humans. Diagnosis is frequently protracted owing to the organism’s fastidious growth requirements and delayed detection in culture systems, leading to a greater reliance on molecular diagnostic modalities, including real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the present case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock, pronounced leukopenia, and coagulopathy characteristic of DIC. Although early disease was dominated by multiorgan dysfunction secondary to sepsis, subsequent findings of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and deteriorating acute kidney injury (AKI) were indicative of an overlapping TMA process. This clinical evolution underscores that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may initially be masked in the context of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department due to a rapidly deteriorating health status. She had been bitten on the upper lip by her dog four days before presentation and was given a tetanus vaccine as a preventive measure. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed at the time of the incident, she was unable to begin antibiotic therapy because she did not pick up her medication. Over the following days, she developed a high fever exceeding 40 °C, experienced malaise, and her oral intake decreased. She ultimately sought medical help after noting numbness and discoloration in her feet. At the primary-care emergency facility, she was found to be hypoglycaemic, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a minimum blood glucose of 0.6 mmol/L, which necessitated her immediate transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and able to engage, yet exhibited signs consistent with sepsis, such as mottled and cold lower limbs. She reported no consumption of herbal medicines or other substances, and there was no history of similar symptoms among family members. Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated profound systemic disturbance, including significant inflammatory response, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas assessment indicated a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range: 7.35–7.43), alongside a lactate concentration of 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). Persistent hypoglycaemia was observed despite the administration of large quantities of intravenous glucose, attributed to underlying sepsis. Upon evaluation, her urine output was within normal limits; urinalysis detected proteinuria (2+) and significant haematuria (3+).

In light of her array of clinical findings—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—her presentation was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) resulting from bacteraemia and incipient sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1/3). Surgical etiologies for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, were excluded based on comprehensive clinical and imaging assessment. Given the severity of her illness, empirical broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to provide coverage for dog-associated pathogens, notably Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was suspected from the beginning, and its presence was confirmed a week later by both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. Subsequently, she was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced surveillance.

Due to the observed pancytopenia and coagulopathy, a haematology consult was obtained. Considerations in the differential diagnosis included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Although haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was entertained, it was considered unlikely in view of the presence of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and a lack of compensatory reticulocytosis. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted, with a bone marrow biopsy initially planned; however, as flow cytometry revealed no elevation in CD34+ blasts or aberrant phenotypes, the biopsy was ultimately not performed. The clinical scenario remained compatible with sepsis. While diagnostic investigations were ongoing, supportive management included transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient experienced complete cessation of urine output and her renal function deteriorated swiftly. Intensive fluid administration combined with loop diuretics yielded no improvement. Subsequently, she developed septic cardiomyopathy complicated by fluid overload, which necessitated commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Evaluation for autoimmunity, including tests for antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), returned negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, the patient presented with widespread cutaneous hemorrhage manifesting as ecchymoses, accompanied by cyanosis and ischaemic changes at the distal extremities, involving multiple toes and fingers; however, peripheral pulses and sensation were intact. These clinical manifestations were interpreted as evidence of microthrombotic events within the setting of DIC. Despite the involvement of multiple organ systems, she never required vasopressors, except for stress-dose corticosteroids given during the acute septic period. Treatment also included albumin infusions and various blood product transfusions.

Eight days following her admission, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit due to persistent dialysis-dependent AKI and the need for continued intravenous antibiotic administration. Upon arrival, she was hemodynamically stable but remained anuric, exhibited generalized swelling, and had multiple bruises, though she was awake and cognitively intact. In response to blood culture findings, her antibiotic regimen was stepped down first to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently to oral Apocillin.

During her stay in the nephrology ward, laboratory investigations revealed a downward trend in inflammatory markers, reactive leukocytosis, improvement in platelet counts, and stable haemoglobin levels. Despite these signs of recovery, her renal function remained compromised with ongoing azotaemia. Coagulation studies, however, returned to within normal limits, suggesting that coagulopathy was resolving. Table 2 provides an overview of her laboratory data during this period.

Following these initial gains, her clinical course took a turn as she experienced an abrupt, marked decrease in haemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days) in the absence of overt bleeding or changes in hemodynamic status. Her platelet count stayed low (30–40×10⁹/L), serum haptoglobin was no longer detectable, and LDH concentrations increased, all consistent with active haemolysis (see figure 1).

A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) for IgG indicated a possible diagnosis of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone treatment was commenced in conjunction with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a period of two days. Despite this intervention, haemolysis persisted and thrombocytopenia continued (with platelets at 41–46 × 10⁹/L), prompting the need for further diagnostic workup. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, a finding that supported the diagnosis of MAHA associated with TMA (figure 2). The initial DAT positivity was probably attributable to penicillin exposure or recent transfusions; subsequent testing returned negative results.

Complement evaluation and ADAMTS13 activity assays were requested. Conventional complement measurements (C3/C4) were found to be within reference ranges, and functional assays assessing the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways also showed normal activity. Shortly thereafter, the ADAMTS13 activity was reported at 43.5%, thereby excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which is typically characterized by levels below 10%. While awaiting these findings, plasma exchange (PEX) was employed as an interim treatment prior to administration of the initial eculizumab dose.

After the thrombocyte count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed pronounced acute tubular necrosis, glomeruli with ischaemic changes containing fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation—findings characteristic of TMA.

Light microscopy, as illustrated in figure 3, most clearly captured the diagnostic hallmarks in this patient. Neither immunofluorescence nor electron microscopy images are included; immunofluorescence did not show any immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy failed to identify signs of primary glomerulopathy.

A diagnosis of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was excluded, given a negative stool PCR result.

After the patient underwent the second and third PEX sessions, spontaneous diuresis resumed. Eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg intravenously, administered weekly for four weeks) was started following the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B). Plans for the administration of the vaccines’ second doses were made for eight weeks later, to occur on an outpatient basis. Since vaccination had not occurred at least two weeks prior to starting complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was administered.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated TMA, comprehensive complement analysis was ordered from Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden). For logistical and safety reasons, three PEX sessions using Octaplas, as well as one eculizumab induction dose, were completed prior to obtaining the sample. Functional complement evaluation included measurement of alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which revealed a pronounced reduction (6%), while levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical management, the patient currently exhibits stable overall health and maintains satisfactory urine output. Nonetheless, renal function is still compromised, as indicated by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L, and she is scheduled for follow-up visits in the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). Maintenance therapy with eculizumab continues at 1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks, and this regimen has been ongoing for four months after induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of the complement system did not detect any pathogenic variants; these preliminary findings were relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, with the official written report still forthcoming at the time of manuscript submission. Given these circumstances, ongoing complement inhibition therapy is being maintained due to the serious nature of her illness, with plans to reconsider discontinuation contingent on the stabilization of kidney function. The discovery of disease-associated mutations in complement genes would support an extended treatment plan and careful monitoring for relapse. In this case, however, no such genetic variants were identified.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs represent a spectrum of closely related clinical syndromes.

In adults presenting with a combination of MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost concern is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by profound ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). While laboratory confirmation is necessary for diagnosis, tools such as the PLASMIC score can facilitate initial risk stratification during the waiting period. In this case, the patient had a PLASMIC score of 4/7, indicating a low pretest likelihood for TTP, and ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, rendering TTP an unlikely diagnosis and prompting further exploration.

STEC-HUS must also be taken into account. However, the patient's PCR test for Shiga toxin was negative. The lack of a preceding gastrointestinal illness, along with negative microbiologic results, militates against a diagnosis of STEC-HUS.

Ultimately, the presence of ongoing hemolysis, schistocytosis, dialysis-dependent AKI, and renal biopsy findings indicative of TMA collectively pointed to a diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). This diagnosis was bolstered by negative PCR findings for STEC and the patient’s marked improvement following initiation of eculizumab therapy. The patient’s otherwise unremarkable medical history argues against an underlying complement abnormality necessitating a 'second hit,' instead favoring infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS represents a diagnosis of exclusion based on clinical and pathological findings, with complement levels (C3/C4) often remaining within normal limits throughout the course of illness. Critically, normal C3 and C4 concentrations do not rule out CM-HUS, as these values can persist within the normal range while the disease is present. Furthermore, in this case, haemolysis was observed only after clinical improvement of sepsis, implicating infection-induced CM-HUS rather than an underlying chronic process.

For this patient, standard complement assays (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were measured just prior to initiating the first PEX treatment. Extended complement testing was subsequently carried out eight days following the last PEX session and four days post initial administration of eculizumab. The detected decrease in alternative pathway activity most likely reflects therapeutic intervention effects, rather than primary complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS, previously known as ‘atypical HUS’, results from inappropriate activation of the alternative complement pathway, a process often observed in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50% to 70% of affected individuals possess genetic alterations or develop autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory molecules—such as factors H, I, and B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nonetheless, the presence of these risk factors does not guarantee disease development, as clinical expression generally necessitates an environmental stimulus, with triggers including infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or the use of specific pharmaceuticals.

Although infrequently documented, C. canimorsus is capable of instigating complement pathway activation, especially in vulnerable populations. This organism exhibits heterogeneous resistance to serum killing: whereas many strains are susceptible to complement, some variants with a capsule exhibit partial resistance, highlighting the pivotal role of complement in host protection. This dynamic presents a clinical dilemma; while effective complement activity is essential for eradicating the microbe, excessive activation in genetically or otherwise susceptible individuals may provoke unchecked endothelial injury and TMA.

The present case contributes to the limited but expanding literature associating C. canimorsus infection with complement-mediated TMAs. For instance, Bjørkto et al detailed a case where CM-HUS was precipitated by C. canimorsus in a patient harboring a factor B variant, who was successfully managed with eculizumab. In contrast to that report, no pathogenic or probably pathogenic complement gene variants were found in our patient, thereby underscoring a model where infection alone can precipitate CM-HUS even in the absence of evident genetic predisposition related to complement.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS in the setting of ongoing infection entails specific difficulties. The primary therapeutic agent for CM-HUS is eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5; its timely use significantly enhances both haematological and renal outcomes. Early administration is critical, as delays can result in suboptimal renal recovery and elevate the likelihood of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

PEX, on the other hand, is a vital intervention in TTP, but confers only transient improvements in CM-HUS by removing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory factors, or autoantibodies. However, it does not resolve the fundamental complement pathway defect. In our case, the patient’s urine output improved after the last PEX treatments but prior to receiving eculizumab, indicating merely short-lived stabilisation. Definitive and sustained improvement in both blood and kidney parameters was observed only following initiation of complement inhibition.

A central challenge in therapy arises when considering complement inhibition in the presence of active bacteraemia. Terminal complement components play a crucial role in defending against encapsulated organisms, such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, leading to heightened concern for infectious complications associated with eculizumab. To reduce this infection risk, eculizumab was commenced only after blood cultures had cleared. Implementation of vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring remain critical elements of care when using complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition represent the key factors associated with improved outcomes. In this case, the patient attained haematological remission alongside partial renal recovery, aligning with existing literature that identifies both renal status and the interval before initiation of eculizumab treatment as central to predicting prognosis.

Despite the rarity of CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus, this report underscores the necessity of evaluating infectious triggers—including atypical pathogens—in patients presenting with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and advances in molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate the recognition of additional cases.
