Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which advanced to complete anuria and a requirement for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although her condition initially stabilized with antimicrobial therapy and supportive measures, she subsequently experienced sudden isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory evaluation and findings from a renal biopsy were consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Treatment with plasma exchange and eculizumab resulted in haematological remission and a partial recovery of renal function. This case demonstrates that C. canimorsus infection can rarely precipitate CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, and it details the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the challenges encountered with the differential diagnosis, and the complexities inherent to both the disease course and therapeutic management of TMA after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is commonly present in the oropharyngeal microbiota of dogs and cats. Infections in humans are uncommon, with transmission possible not only through animal bites or scratches but also through close exposure. The clinical spectrum is broad and can include presentations from isolated meningitis to rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Although C. canimorsus typically responds to a wide array of antibiotics, reported mortality rates can be as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often associated with invasive zoonotic disease in humans. Diagnosis is often challenging due to its slow and demanding growth requirements in traditional cultures, leading to increased reliance on molecular diagnostics such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the described patient, C. canimorsus infection manifested initially as septic shock, profound leukopenia, and a coagulopathy in keeping with DIC. While sepsis and multiorgan involvement predominated in the early clinical picture, subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and aggravated acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed toward the development of a concurrent TMA. This illustrates that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be masked during the early stages dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A previously well woman in her late forties, with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department experiencing a swiftly deteriorating condition. Four days before admission, she sustained a bite to her upper lip from her dog and was given a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed the day of the dog bite, but the patient was unable to start antibiotics due to not collecting the medication. Subsequently, she developed a high-grade fever (over 40 °C), general malaise, and decreased oral intake. Medical attention was sought after she noticed numbness and discoloration in her feet. At a primary-care emergency setting, she was found to be hypoglycaemic, resistant to both oral glucose and glucagon therapy, with a lowest measured glucose of 0.6 mmol/L, which necessitated her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and responsive but demonstrated clinical signs of sepsis, including mottled, cool lower limbs. She denied the use of herbal medications or other substances, and there was no history of similar symptoms among family members. Initial laboratory investigations indicated profound multisystem involvement characterized by pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic transaminases, and coagulopathy. Table 1 outlines these salient findings.

Arterial blood gas testing identified metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). Persistent hypoglycemia was noted despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, attributed to sepsis. On admission, her urine output was preserved; urinalysis detected proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

In view of her array of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy associated with acute kidney injury and lactic acidosis—her clinical status was assessed as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and incipient sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment [qSOFA] score of 1/3). After thorough clinical and imaging assessments, a surgical etiology for her mottled limbs, such as necrotizing fasciitis, was excluded. Considering the seriousness of her initial findings, empiric intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to target potential dog-related organisms, including Capnocytophaga species. Importantly, C. canimorsus was an early suspected pathogen and this was later confirmed after 7 days through both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced surveillance.

Owing to the presence of pancytopenia and coagulopathy, hematology specialists were consulted. The differential diagnosis encompassed acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Although hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) was also considered, this was regarded as unlikely given her leukopenia, the profile of inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and low non-compensatory reticulocyte counts. Flow cytometry was conducted and a bone marrow biopsy scheduled, but the biopsy was cancelled after flow cytometry demonstrated normal findings without elevated CD34+ blasts or aberrant immunophenotypes. Her symptoms were consistent with a septic process. In the interim, before definitive results, she received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Following her admission to the ICU, the patient became completely anuric and her renal function deteriorated rapidly. Efforts at vigorous fluid resuscitation in combination with loop diuretics were unsuccessful. She progressed to develop septic cardiomyopathy and volume overload, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), returned negative.

By the third day in the ICU, she exhibited generalized cutaneous bleeding with ecchymosis, as well as cyanosis and ischemia involving multiple distal toes and fingers; however, distal pulses and sensory function were maintained. These clinical signs were suggestive of microthrombi secondary to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although several organ systems were affected, she never required vasopressors apart from stress-dose corticosteroids given during early sepsis management. Additionally, she received infusions of albumin and other blood products.

Following eight days of hospitalization, she was moved to the nephrology unit to continue management for dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury and intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, she remained hemodynamically stable but was anuric, exhibited generalized edema, and had multiple hematomas, though she was alert and oriented. Based on blood culture findings, antimicrobial therapy was stepped down to intravenous ampicillin, subsequently switched to oral Apocillin.

During her stay in the nephrology ward, laboratory evaluations revealed a downward trend in inflammatory markers, presence of reactive leukocytosis, recovering platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin concentrations. Renal panel demonstrated ongoing azotemia, while coagulation studies normalized, suggesting resolution of prior coagulopathy. Laboratory values from this period are outlined in Table 2.

While initial progress was observed, her condition changed with an abrupt isolated decline in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days), in the absence of clinical signs of bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained low (30–40 × 10⁹/L). Additionally, haptoglobin levels fell to undetectable, and LDH increased, pointing to continued hemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, yielded a positive result for IgG, prompting concerns for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was initiated along with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a 2-day period. As the haemolysis persisted despite corticosteroid treatment and platelet counts remained low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional investigation became necessary. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, indicating microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) in keeping with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). The initial positive DAT was most likely attributable to penicillin exposure or previous transfusions; a subsequent DAT was negative.

Complement assessments and ADAMTS13 activity testing were submitted for evaluation. Local standard complement levels, including C3 and C4, fell within normal ranges, and functional testing of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways did not reveal abnormalities. The ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43.5%, which effectively excludes thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP is generally defined by an activity level less than 10%. During the interval before these results became available, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as a temporizing measure before administration of the initial eculizumab dose.

When the platelet count reached 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was undertaken, which demonstrated pronounced acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli with the presence of fibrin thrombi, and focal crescent formation, all of which were indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy are not included here; the former detected no deposition of immune complexes, while the latter revealed no characteristics suggestive of a primary glomerular disorder.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was ruled out on the basis of a negative stool PCR analysis.

After the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), the patient’s spontaneous urine output resumed. Once initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B)—were administered, induction therapy with eculizumab was started at 900 mg intravenously weekly for a total of 4 weeks. The follow-up, or second, vaccine doses were scheduled to occur as an outpatient eight weeks later. Since the vaccinations had not been given at least two weeks before the initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was provided.

Comprehensive complement analysis, aimed at excluding hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), was forwarded to Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden). Due to logistical and safety concerns, three Octaplas PEX sessions were completed and one induction dose of eculizumab was administered prior to sample collection. Functional complement assessment encompassed measurement of alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which showed a significantly decreased value (6%), while levels of complement factors H, I, and B remained within normal limits and antibodies to factor H were not detected.

Outcome and follow-up:

After the intensive medical treatment, the patient is clinically stable and demonstrates satisfactory urine production. Her renal function is still impaired, with a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she will have ongoing follow-up in the nephrology outpatient setting (see figure 4). She remains on maintenance eculizumab at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks, now 4 months after induction. Comprehensive genetic testing of the complement pathway did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; this was relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, although the official written report was not yet available at the time of writing. Given the severity of her presentation, complement inhibition is being continued, with plans to reconsider discontinuation if her renal function stabilizes. The presence of pathogenic complement gene variants would support the rationale for long-term therapy with close monitoring for relapse, but none were detected in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adult patients presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury, the foremost step is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by a marked deficiency of ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). Clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score can be utilized for stratification while definitive laboratory testing is pending; however, confirmation of diagnosis relies on lab analysis. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, which indicates a low likelihood of TTP prior to testing, and her ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, thereby making TTP an improbable diagnosis and informing further investigative steps.

Consideration must also be given to STEC-HUS. The patient's polymerase chain reaction testing for Shiga toxin returned negative. Coupled with a lack of antecedent gastrointestinal symptoms and negative microbiological studies, this evidence does not support a diagnosis of STEC-HUS.

In the end, the combination of ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, AKI requiring dialysis, and renal biopsy findings consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy pointed towards a diagnosis of complement-mediated HUS (see figure 5). This conclusion was further validated by negative Shiga toxin PCR testing and her marked improvement following the initiation of eculizumab. The absence of a notable medical history argues against an underlying complement abnormality requiring a “second hit,” instead supporting CM-HUS precipitated by infection.

CM-HUS represents a diagnosis made by excluding other causes and may present with normal complement (C3/C4) concentrations throughout its progression. Crucially, CM-HUS cannot be ruled out solely based on normal C3 or C4 values, as these parameters can stay within standard limits during the disease. Of interest, hemolysis only developed after the patient’s sepsis began to resolve, which supports the interpretation of sepsis-precipitated CM-HUS as opposed to a pre-existing condition.

For this case, complement (C3 and C4) levels and ADAMTS13 activity were measured just prior to initiating the first plasma exchange (PEX) session. Further complement analysis was conducted eight days after completing PEX and four days following the initial administration of eculizumab. The reduction observed in the alternative complement pathway is most likely attributable to therapeutic intervention rather than inherent activation or deficiency of the complement system.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ results from abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway, most frequently occurring in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of patients possess genetic mutations or autoantibodies that disrupt complement regulatory proteins, including factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Despite this, penetrance of the condition is not absolute; clinical onset generally necessitates an external precipitant, with infections, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or specific pharmacologic agents being the most frequent precipitants.

Although infrequently documented, C. canimorsus is capable of activating the complement system, a phenomenon observed predominantly in individuals who are particularly susceptible. It is noteworthy that the serum susceptibility of C. canimorsus is heterogeneous—while many bacterial isolates are vulnerable to complement-mediated lysis, some encapsulated strains exhibit partial resistance—emphasizing the central role of complement in the host's immune response. This results in a clinical dilemma: although complement activation facilitates eradication of the microorganism, in certain predisposed hosts it can simultaneously instigate unrestrained endothelial injury and subsequent thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The present case further contributes to the limited but expanding evidence associating C. canimorsus infection with complement-mediated TMAs. Notably, Bjørkto et al described a case where a patient developed CM-HUS induced by C. canimorsus infection and carried a variant in complement factor B, achieving clinical remission following eculizumab therapy. Unlike that report, our patient exhibited no pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations in complement-related genes, indicating the development of infection-associated CM-HUS in the absence of a detectable heritable complement abnormality.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS during ongoing infection poses distinct difficulties. Eculizumab—a monoclonal antibody directed against terminal complement component 5—is considered the mainstay treatment for CM-HUS, leading to significant improvements in hematologic and renal parameters, especially if started promptly. Initiation at a later stage is linked to incomplete recovery of kidney function and a correspondingly greater likelihood of developing end-stage renal disease.

PEX, by contrast, is a life-saving intervention in TTP but confers only a short-term benefit in CM-HUS, primarily by eliminating circulating complement fragments, inflammatory molecules, or autoantibodies, without addressing the fundamental complement dysregulation. In the case described, the patient's urine output resumed following the last PEX treatments and prior to eculizumab administration, indicating merely temporary stabilization. Long-term hematologic and renal improvement was observed only after initiation of complement blockade.

A significant therapeutic challenge is the use of complement inhibition amidst active bacteremia, as terminal complement activity is vital for defending against encapsulated organisms, such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga. This raises infection risk concerns when eculizumab is used. In this report, eculizumab was administered after negative blood culture results, thereby reducing the potential for uncontrolled infection. Administration of vaccines, prophylactic antibiotics, and vigilant monitoring are essential components of care when employing complement inhibitors.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition are critical factors linked to positive outcomes. In this case, the patient experienced haematological remission and partial improvement in renal function, aligning with existing evidence that emphasizes renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab as key prognostic factors.

While CM-HUS associated with C. canimorsus is exceptionally uncommon, this report underscores the need to consider infectious precipitants, even rare ones, in patients with newly diagnosed TMA. Greater clinical awareness and advances in molecular diagnostics may facilitate detection of additional similar cases.
