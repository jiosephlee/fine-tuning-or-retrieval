Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A previously well woman in her late 40s developed rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She experienced disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete anuria and a requirement for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although her condition initially improved with antibiotics and supportive management, she subsequently experienced abrupt isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory evaluation and findings from renal biopsy confirmed a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Hematological remission was attained after plasma exchange and eculizumab treatment, with partial recovery of renal function. This report emphasizes C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, detailing the patient’s diagnostic process, the differential diagnosis challenges, and the complex nature of TMA that develops following sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is commonly present in the oropharyngeal flora of cats and dogs. Although infections in humans are uncommon, transmission can take place through bites, scratches, or even close physical proximity. The spectrum of clinical manifestations is wide, from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). While it generally exhibits sensitivity to a range of antibiotics, mortality rates may reach up to 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most commonly associated with invasive zoonotic infections in humans. Identification may be delayed given its fastidious growth characteristics and slow culture results, leading to greater reliance on molecular diagnostic approaches, including real-time nanopore sequencing.

In this patient, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock accompanied by pronounced leukopaenia and coagulopathy consistent with DIC. Although the initial presentation was predominantly characterized by sepsis-related multiorgan involvement, subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and progressive acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated concomitant TMA. This underscores how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be masked during the initial phases of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, with no previous health issues and two straightforward pregnancies in her history, arrived at the emergency department in a rapidly deteriorating state. Four days before her admission, she sustained a bite to her upper lip from her dog and received a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed on the day of the injury, but she did not begin the antibiotic regimen, as she was unable to obtain the medication. Subsequently, she experienced a high fever (>40 °C), general malaise, decreased oral intake, and later sought care when she noticed numbness and discoloration in her feet. At the primary-care emergency facility, she was diagnosed with hypoglycemia that did not respond to oral glucose or glucagon, with her lowest recorded blood glucose at 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and interactive, yet displayed signs of sepsis, including mottling and decreased temperature in the lower limbs. She denied using herbal supplements or other substances, and no family members had reported illness or experienced similar manifestations. Initial laboratory assessments indicated substantial multisystem disturbance, highlighted by intense inflammatory response, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated hepatic enzymes, and coagulopathy. Table 1 summarizes these critical laboratory results.

Arterial blood gas demonstrated a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (normal range: 7.35–7.43) and an increased lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference: <2.5 mmol/L). Despite receiving substantial intravenous glucose therapy, hypoglycaemia persisted, attributed to sepsis. At the time of evaluation, the patient maintained sufficient urine output; urinalysis identified proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her array of symptoms—fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury and lactic acidosis—led clinicians to interpret her condition as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) resulting from bacteraemia and incipient sepsis, reflected by a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. After clinical examination and imaging, surgical causes for her mottled limbs, including necrotising fasciitis, were excluded. Given the grave nature of her presentation, she was initiated on broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) to target pathogens associated with dogs, such as Capnocytophaga species. Importantly, C. canimorsus was suspected from the outset and subsequently confirmed in both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures seven days later. She was transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring.

Hematology consultation was sought because of her pancytopenia and coagulopathy. Acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis were considered in the differential, and haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also contemplated, but was deemed unlikely given the presence of leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin levels, and non-compensatory reticulocytes. Flow cytometry was conducted, with a bone marrow biopsy initially planned; however, normal findings—including the absence of elevated CD34+ blasts or aberrant phenotypes—led to cancellation of the biopsy. Clinically, the scenario was judged consistent with sepsis. Supportive transfusions of thrombocytes and fibrinogen were provided while awaiting diagnostic results.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient experienced complete cessation of urine output and rapid deterioration of renal function. Intensive fluid administration along with simultaneous use of loop diuretics failed to produce improvement. She then developed septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by volume overload, prompting initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy. Evaluation for autoimmune disease, including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing, yielded negative results.

By the third ICU day, the patient exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding manifesting as ecchymoses, in addition to distal cyanosis and ischemic changes in multiple fingers and toes; however, peripheral pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical signs were consistent with microthrombosis due to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Despite involvement of several organ systems, vasopressor therapy was not needed apart from administration of stress-dose corticosteroids during the initial septic episode. The patient was also given albumin infusions and various blood product transfusions.

Following eight days of inpatient care, she was moved to the nephrology service for further management of dialysis-requiring acute kidney injury (AKI) and continuation of intravenous antibiotic treatment. At the time of transfer, the patient was haemodynamically stable but remained anuric, exhibited generalized edema, and had multiple hematomas, though she was alert and fully oriented. Antibiotic therapy was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, and subsequently transitioned to oral Apocillin as guided by blood culture findings.

During her stay in the nephrology ward, laboratory studies demonstrated a trend towards improved inflammatory markers, a reactive leukocytosis, platelet counts trending upward, and hemoglobin levels remaining steady. Renal function assays continued to show elevated nitrogenous waste products, while coagulation tests normalized, suggesting resolution of her prior coagulopathy. Table 2 details her laboratory parameters at this point in her course.

Despite these initial gains, her condition evolved as she experienced a precipitous decline in hemoglobin (from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over six days) in the absence of clinical bleeding or signs of haemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts stayed consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin was found to be undetectable, and LDH rose, all consistent with ongoing hemolytic activity (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, returned positive for IgG, indicating a possible diagnosis of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was commenced with routine prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered within a 2-day period. Due to persistent haemolysis unresponsive to corticosteroids and continued thrombocytopaenia (platelet count: 41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional investigation became necessary. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, consistent with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and suggestive of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). The initial DAT result was probably falsely positive as a consequence of penicillin exposure or prior transfusion; subsequent testing yielded a negative result.

Complement assays and ADAMTS13 activity were requested for further analysis. Conventional complement studies (C3 and C4) available locally were within normal ranges, and functional testing of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways showed normal findings. Shortly thereafter, ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, thereby excluding thrombotic thrombocytopaenic purpura (TTP), which characteristically presents with levels below 10%. During the interval awaiting test results, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as interim treatment ahead of the initial administration of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, glomeruli exhibiting ischemic changes with fibrin thrombi, and focal crescent formation, findings consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The most illustrative diagnostic evidence was provided by the light microscopy images presented in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy data are not displayed, as immunofluorescence detected no immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy showed no features indicative of a primary glomerular disorder.

A diagnosis of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) was excluded due to a negative stool PCR test.

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) procedures, the patient resumed spontaneous urine output. Initial immunization with meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B)—was completed before starting eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg intravenously weekly for 4 weeks). Follow-up doses of these vaccines were scheduled to occur eight weeks later in the outpatient setting. As the vaccinations had not been performed at least two weeks prior to complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Advanced complement analysis was requested from Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden) to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Due to both practical and safety concerns, three PEX sessions using Octaplas and a single induction dose of eculizumab were completed prior to collecting specimens. Functional complement assays assessed the alternative pathway (quantitative AH50), revealing a marked decrease (6%), whereas levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal range, and antifactor H antibodies were not detected.

Outcome and follow-up:

After extensive medical treatment, the patient is presently stable and demonstrating satisfactory urine output. Renal function remains impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L, and ongoing follow-up will occur at the nephrology outpatient service (figure 4). She is receiving maintenance therapy with intravenous eculizumab at a dose of 1200 mg every two weeks, sustained for four months following induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis targeting complement-related genes failed to detect any pathogenic mutations; these findings were relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, while the official written report was not available at the time of manuscript submission. Taking this into account, complement inhibition is being sustained due to the severity of her clinical presentation, with a planned future reassessment for possible cessation if kidney function stabilizes. Discovery of pathogenic complement gene alterations would support prolonged therapy with vigilant surveillance for recurrence. In this instance, no such genetic abnormalities were found.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost concern is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Clinical scoring tools such as PLASMIC can assist in patient assessment while waiting for definitive laboratory results, though diagnosis is ultimately laboratory-confirmed. The patient exhibited a PLASMIC score of 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, with an ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, which further diminished the probability of TTP, thus directing subsequent investigations.

STEC-HUS was also considered in the differential diagnosis. Shiga toxin PCR testing was negative for the patient. Additionally, the lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and absence of positive microbial findings made STEC-HUS an unlikely cause.

In summary, the combination of ongoing hemolysis, schistocyte presence, AKI requiring dialysis, and renal biopsy findings indicative of TMA favored a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). Diagnosis was reinforced by negative Shiga toxin PCR results and rapid improvement following eculizumab administration. The patient’s minimal past medical history reduces the likelihood of an underlying complement abnormality necessitating a ‘second hit’, favoring infection-induced CM-HUS as the etiology.

CM-HUS is diagnosed clinically and pathologically after excluding other causes, and complement components C3 and C4 may remain within normal limits throughout its course. It is critical to recognize that CM-HUS cannot be ruled out based on normal C3 and C4 values, as these may not be altered during the illness. In this case, haemolysis manifested only as sepsis was resolving, indicating that CM-HUS was triggered by infection rather than being previously present.

For this patient, measurements of C3/C4 and ADAMTS13 activity were taken immediately prior to the first plasma exchange (PEX) session. More comprehensive complement analysis was done 8 days following the last PEX session and 4 days after the initial eculizumab administration. The decrease observed in the alternative complement pathway likely reflects a response to therapy, rather than being due to inherent complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS, previously known as ‘atypical HUS’, is caused by abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway, commonly affecting individuals with genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of cases carry genetic alterations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulators such as factor H, factor I, factor B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Despite this, the condition does not consistently develop in all carriers, as its clinical onset generally necessitates an external precipitating factor, frequently infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or specific medications.

While uncommon in clinical literature, C. canimorsus is capable of complement pathway activation, especially in hosts with underlying vulnerabilities. The organism demonstrates heterogeneous serum sensitivity: whereas numerous isolates are readily neutralized by complement, some capsule-producing strains possess partial resistance. This underscores the significance of complement activity in host protection. Clinically, this presents a dilemma: complement activation is integral to pathogen elimination but may also provoke excessive endothelial injury and TMA in at-risk patients.

This report contributes to the limited but expanding literature correlating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. Bjørkto et al notably documented a case of CM-HUS provoked by C. canimorsus infection in a patient with a complement factor B variant, who responded favorably to eculizumab. Unlike their case, our patient showed no pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene mutations, implying that CM-HUS may develop after infection even in the absence of identified genetic complement abnormalities.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amidst ongoing infection poses distinct difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5, represents the standard therapy for CM-HUS and significantly enhances hematologic and renal outcomes, with the greatest benefit observed when administered promptly. Postponing treatment is linked to suboptimal renal recovery, thereby increasing the likelihood of progression to end-stage kidney disease.

Conversely, plasma exchange (PEX) is critical for survival in TTP, but in CM-HUS it yields only temporary improvement primarily through the clearance of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, without addressing the fundamental complement dysregulation. In the patient described, diuresis was restored following the last PEX treatments, prior to eculizumab administration, indicating only brief stabilization. Persistent improvement in hematologic and renal parameters was achieved solely after inhibition of the complement pathway.

An important clinical decision involves employing complement inhibitors during active bacteremia, since terminal complement elements play a pivotal role in protection against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga. This raises concerns regarding the increased susceptibility to infections associated with eculizumab. In this instance, eculizumab treatment was started after negative blood cultures had been documented, thereby reducing the risk of uncontrolled infection. When complement inhibitors are prescribed, vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring remain indispensable.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early administration of complement inhibition are critical factors associated with a positive prognosis. The patient described attained hematologic remission and partial renal recovery, aligning with available data indicating that renal status and the interval to commencement of eculizumab are primary prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS associated with C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to consider infectious etiologies, even rare ones, in patients who develop new-onset TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and advances in molecular diagnostic techniques could facilitate detection of additional similar cases.
