Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She subsequently developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, eventually becoming completely anuric and requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia. While she initially responded to antibiotics and supportive measures, she experienced abrupt isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory tests and a renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). After starting plasma exchange and eculizumab, haematological remission was achieved and kidney function partially improved. This case illustrates C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults, detailing the patient's diagnostic evaluation, differential diagnosis challenges, and the complex clinical trajectory and management of TMA following sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus commonly residing in the oropharynx of dogs and cats. In humans, infections are uncommon, and transmission may occur through bites, scratches, or close proximity. The clinical spectrum is broad, encompassing presentations from isolated meningitis to fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Despite susceptibility to numerous antibiotic classes, the reported mortality rate can be as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in invasive zoonotic infections in people. Due to its fastidious growth requirements and delayed culture positivity, diagnostic confirmation is frequently postponed, leading to greater reliance on molecular techniques such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the current case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock, pronounced leukopaenia, and a coagulopathy indicative of DIC. While sepsis-associated multiorgan impairment dominated the initial clinical progression, the subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopaenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed toward an overlay of TMA. This scenario underscores the potential for complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) to be masked during the early stages of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department due to a rapidly deteriorating clinical status. Four days before admission, she sustained a bite from her dog on her upper lip and received a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed at the time, she was unable to begin antibiotic therapy as she did not collect her prescription. Subsequently, she developed a high-grade fever (>40 °C), general malaise, and decreased oral intake. Medical care was sought after she experienced numbness and discoloration of her feet. During her evaluation at a primary-care emergency facility, she was noted to be hypoglycemic, unresponsive to oral glucose and glucagon, with the lowest recorded glucose level being 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon arrival, the patient was conscious and engaged, but showed signs of sepsis with mottling and reduced warmth in her lower limbs. She denied the use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of illness or similar symptoms among her family members. The initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated profound systemic disruption, characterized by intense inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas assessment indicated metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.27 (reference range: 7.35–7.43), and a raised lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, she persisted with hypoglycemia, which was considered secondary to sepsis. Upon admission, diuresis was preserved, and urinalysis revealed proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her combination of clinical findings—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) caused by bacteraemia and evolving sepsis, with a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. Clinical and radiologic assessment excluded a surgical etiology for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis. Owing to the severity of her symptoms, empirical treatment with broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to provide coverage for pathogens associated with dog bites, including Capnocytophaga species. Of particular note, C. canimorsus was considered as a probable causative organism from the beginning, and seven days later this was substantiated when both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures yielded positive results. She was thereafter admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for closer observation.

In response to her pancytopenia and coagulopathic findings, haematology specialists were involved. The differential diagnosis was expanded to include acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was another possibility but appeared less likely due to the presence of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin levels, and reticulocyte counts that did not indicate compensatory response. Flow cytometry was conducted and a bone marrow biopsy was initially scheduled; however, since flow cytometry revealed no increase in CD34+ blasts or any abnormal phenotypes, the biopsy was ultimately not performed. Clinically, the picture aligned with sepsis. In the interim, supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were administered while awaiting diagnostic results.

Upon admission to the ICU, the patient exhibited complete anuria accompanied by rapidly deteriorating kidney function. Efforts to reverse this through vigorous intravenous fluids and simultaneous use of loop diuretics failed. She then progressed to septic cardiomyopathy and developed significant volume overload, prompting the commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune screening, including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), returned negative results.

By ICU day 3, she experienced widespread skin hemorrhages manifested as ecchymoses, as well as distal cyanotic and ischemic lesions involving multiple toes and fingers, though peripheral pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical features were interpreted as microthrombotic processes associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Despite the presence of dysfunction across several organ systems, she did not require vasopressor therapy, other than the stress-dose corticosteroids administered early during sepsis. She was also treated with albumin infusions and additional blood products.

Following eight days of hospital admission, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit due to persistent dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and for continued intravenous antibiotic treatment. Upon arrival in the nephrology ward, she remained hemodynamically stable but exhibited anuria, widespread edema, and several hematomas, though she was alert and oriented. Based on the results of blood cultures, her antibiotics were stepped down to intravenous ampicillin, and subsequently to oral Apocillin.

During her stay in the nephrology ward, laboratory findings demonstrated a decrease in inflammatory markers, a reactive leukocytosis, recovery in platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin concentrations. Renal function assessments revealed ongoing azotemia, while her coagulation profile normalized, suggestive of resolution of her earlier coagulopathy. Her laboratory data at this juncture are detailed in Table 2.

After an initial period of improvement, her condition shifted when she experienced an acute, isolated decline in hemoglobin (dropping from 111 to 68 g/L within six days) in the absence of signs of bleeding or circulatory compromise. Platelet counts remained low (30–40×10⁹/L). Haptoglobin was no longer detectable, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels increased, all consistent with continued hemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, suggesting possible autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was commenced in addition to standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over the course of 2 days. Since corticosteroid therapy did not mitigate the haemolytic activity and platelet counts remained persistently low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), further diagnostic investigation was necessary. Examination of the peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of MAHA and raising concerns for TMA (figure 2). The initial DAT result was probably a false positive attributed to either penicillin or earlier transfusions; subsequent testing was negative.

Complement levels and ADAMTS13 activity were ordered. Routine local complement assays (C3/C4) were normal, and functional assays assessing the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways also yielded normal results. ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently measured at 43.5%, which excludes thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP is typically associated with values below 10%. While pending these findings, plasma exchange (PEX) was begun as interim treatment prior to initiating eculizumab.

After the platelet count had risen to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, findings indicative of TMA.

The most conclusive diagnostic evidence in this case was provided by light microscopy, as illustrated in figure 3. Immunofluorescence results are not presented here because there was an absence of immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy found no signs suggestive of primary glomerular disease.

Stool PCR testing was negative, thereby excluding Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS).

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) procedures, the patient resumed spontaneous urine output. Once the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B)—were administered, eculizumab therapy was started (900 mg intravenously every week for four weeks as induction). The subsequent vaccine doses were scheduled to be given as outpatient treatment 8 weeks later. Since the vaccines had not been provided two weeks prior to complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement studies to exclude hereditary forms of complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) were sent to the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University in Sweden. Given both logistical constraints and safety concerns, the patient underwent three sessions of plasma exchange using Octaplas and received one induction dose of eculizumab before complement samples were drawn. Functional assays included alternative pathway activity measured by quantitative AH50, which was significantly decreased (6%), whereas complement factors H, I, and B were within normal range, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical therapy, the patient’s overall health is good and she maintains sufficient urine production. Renal function is still compromised, evidenced by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she is scheduled for continued care at the nephrology outpatient department (figure 4). Eculizumab is being administered as maintenance therapy at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks, continuing for four months following induction. Broad genetic analysis of the complement pathway did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; at submission, this information was relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, with the official written report yet to be received. In light of these findings, complement inhibition treatment is ongoing due to the severity of the condition, with plans to reevaluate therapy discontinuation if kidney function stabilizes. If pathogenic complement gene variants had been discovered, ongoing treatment with close monitoring for recurrence would be recommended. In this patient, however, no such genetic anomalies were found.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adult patients presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost step is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by pronounced ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Tools such as the PLASMIC score can facilitate risk stratification while laboratory confirmation is pending; however, definitive diagnosis relies on laboratory assessment. The PLASMIC score calculated for this patient was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP. Additionally, the ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5% further diminished the possibility of TTP, directing the subsequent diagnostic evaluation.

STEC-HUS was also considered as a differential diagnosis. The patient’s Shiga toxin PCR result was negative, and the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms prior to presentation, along with negative microbiological tests, reduced the probability of STEC-HUS.

In conclusion, persistent hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, dialysis-requiring AKI, and renal biopsy demonstrating TMA established a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). Further support for CM-HUS was provided by negative STEC PCR and marked clinical improvement after commencement of eculizumab treatment. The lack of significant medical history detracts from the possibility of an underlying complement abnormality necessitating a 'second hit,' and favors infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is identified as a clinicopathologic diagnosis reached by ruling out other conditions, and complement assays (C3/C4) may stay within normal ranges throughout its progression. It is critical to recognize that normal C3 and C4 results do not rule out CM-HUS, as these values can remain normal during the illness. Of note, hemolysis was detected only as sepsis began to resolve, which is indicative of CM-HUS precipitated by infection rather than being a chronic underlying disorder.

In this case, initial complement assessments (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were conducted just prior to the first therapeutic plasma exchange (PEX). Comprehensive complement analyses were subsequently carried out eight days following the last PEX and four days post-initiation of eculizumab therapy. The diminished alternative complement pathway observed is most likely attributable to the effects of administered therapy rather than reflecting primary complement depletion or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously known as 'atypical HUS,' results from abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with genetic susceptibility. Genetic mutations or autoantibodies that affect complement regulatory proteins—including factor H, factor I, factor B, and membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are found in approximately 50%–70% of cases. Despite this, the condition shows incomplete penetrance, and onset of symptoms generally requires an external inciting factor such as infections, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or certain medications.

Capnocytophaga canimorsus has infrequently been documented to initiate complement activation, especially in vulnerable patients. Of note, C. canimorsus demonstrates heterogeneity in its sensitivity to serum complement, with many strains being susceptible to complement-mediated lysis, whereas some that express a capsule possess partial resistance. Thus, complement activity plays a significant role in host protection. This creates a clinical dilemma: complement activation facilitates pathogen clearance but may, in at-risk individuals, precipitate uncontrolled endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The present case contributes to a limited yet expanding body of evidence associating C. canimorsus with complement-driven TMAs. For example, Bjørkto et al reported a case in which CM-HUS triggered by C. canimorsus occurred in a patient carrying a complement factor B variant and was successfully managed with eculizumab. Unlike that prior report, our patient did not harbor any pathogenic or probably pathogenic complement gene alterations, supporting a diagnosis of infection-induced CM-HUS without evidence of a hereditary complement abnormality.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Treating CM-HUS in the setting of ongoing infection involves particular difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody against the terminal complement component C5, constitutes the first-line therapy for CM-HUS and significantly enhances both hematologic and renal recovery, especially with early administration. Initiation of therapy after delay is linked to partial renal improvement and a subsequent elevated likelihood of end-stage kidney disease.

By comparison, plasma exchange (PEX) is crucial for survival in TTP but offers only short-lived improvement in CM-HUS. PEX may eliminate complement fragments, inflammatory molecules, or autoantibodies from circulation, yet does not address the underlying complement dysregulation. In this case, the patient's diuresis resumed following completion of PEX, but before eculizumab treatment, indicative of only brief stabilization. Definitive and durable hematological and renal improvement only followed initiation of complement inhibition.

An important treatment challenge arises with complement inhibition during active bacteremia, since terminal complement activity is essential for host protection against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga. This situation introduces concerns related to the heightened risk of eculizumab-associated infection. For this patient, eculizumab therapy commenced only after clearance of blood cultures, thereby reducing the likelihood of exacerbating infection. Prophylactic vaccination, antimicrobial agents, and vigilant surveillance are critical components when complement inhibitors are used.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition therapy are critical factors linked to improved outcomes. The patient described attained haematological remission and partial recovery of renal function, in agreement with existing literature that identifies renal status and the interval between disease onset and eculizumab initiation as primary prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS associated with C. canimorsus is exceptionally uncommon, this report underscores the need to consider infectious precipitants—including rare pathogens—in patients who develop new-onset TMA. Greater awareness and advances in molecular diagnostics could facilitate recognition of additional similar cases.
