Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues developed rapidly worsening sepsis 4 days after sustaining a dog bite. Her clinical course was complicated by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, advancing to complete absence of urine output and subsequent need for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus as the causative organism. Although she initially responded favorably to antibiotic and supportive treatments, she later exhibited abrupt, isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Findings from laboratory testing and kidney biopsy established a diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), specifically complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Following treatment with plasma exchange and the initiation of eculizumab, she achieved haematological remission, and her renal function showed partial improvement. This report presents C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon cause of CM-HUS in adults with normal immune function, and discusses the diagnostic evaluation, complexities in differential diagnosis, and the intricate nature of managing TMA in the context of sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, slow-growing gram-negative rod commonly present in the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Although infections in humans are uncommon, transmission may result from bites, scratches, or close proximity to these animals. The range of clinical manifestations is broad, including isolated cases of meningitis, rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Even though this pathogen is susceptible to many antibiotics, mortality rates can reach as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in severe zoonotic infections in humans. Because it is difficult to culture and grows slowly in laboratory conditions, diagnosis is frequently delayed, leading to increased use of molecular techniques such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In this report, the infection by C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock, pronounced leukopaenia, and coagulation abnormalities consistent with DIC. While sepsis-driven multiorgan dysfunction predominated in the early phase of illness, subsequent development of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and progression of acute kidney injury (AKI) were indicative of an underlying TMA process. This underscores that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be overlooked during the initial sepsis and DIC presentation.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two previous uneventful pregnancies arrived at the emergency department due to rapidly deteriorating health. Four days before hospitalization, her dog had bitten her upper lip; at that time, she received a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed immediately following the bite, the patient did not start antibiotic therapy as she was unable to pick up the medication. Subsequently, she developed a high-grade fever (>40 °C), general malaise, and decreased oral intake. She later presented for medical care after experiencing numbness and discoloration of her feet. Upon initial evaluation at an urgent care facility, she was found to have hypoglycemia that was resistant to oral glucose and glucagon administration, with the lowest measured glucose level at 0.6 mmol/L, necessitating her emergent transfer to our department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and engaged, though she demonstrated signs of sepsis, including mottled and cool lower limbs. She denied using herbal products or any other substances, and none of her family members exhibited similar symptoms or illnesses. Initial laboratory evaluations indicated significant multisystem abnormalities, featuring pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated liver enzymes, and coagulation disturbances. Table 1 summarizes these principal findings.

Arterial blood gas measurements displayed metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.27 (normal range 7.35–7.43) and an increased lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference <2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, her hypoglycemia persisted and was attributed to sepsis. Upon admission, she maintained sufficient urine output, and urinalysis detected proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her array of symptoms—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—was assessed as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis, with a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. A possible surgical etiology for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded through clinical examination and radiological studies. In light of her critical presentation, empirical intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to cover pathogens commonly associated with dog bites, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was considered a likely pathogen early on, and was ultimately confirmed seven days later by both aerobic and anaerobic blood culture results. The patient was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for more intensive monitoring.

Considering her pancytopenia and coagulopathy, the haematology service was involved. The list of differential diagnoses included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also discussed but was deemed improbable because of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin levels, and non-compensatory reticulocyte count. Flow cytometry was carried out, and a bone marrow biopsy was considered; however, the procedure was not pursued after flow cytometry showed no elevation in CD34+ blasts or abnormal cellular phenotypes. Clinically, her findings were compatible with sepsis. During the diagnostic process, she received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient progressed to complete anuria with a swift decline in renal function. Attempts at vigorous intravenous fluid administration, combined with loop diuretics, were unsuccessful. She went on to develop septic cardiomyopathy complicated by volume overload, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune screening, including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) tests, yielded negative results.

By the third ICU day, the patient manifested widespread cutaneous bleeding with ecchymoses as well as cyanotic, ischemic changes involving multiple distal toes and fingers, although peripheral pulses and sensory function were maintained. These findings were indicative of microthrombotic processes within the framework of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although several organ systems were involved, she never needed vasopressor therapy apart from stress-dose corticosteroids given early in the septic episode. The management regimen also included albumin infusions and various blood products.

Following eight days of hospitalization, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit due to sustained dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and to continue receiving intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival, she remained haemodynamically stable but was not producing urine, exhibited generalized swelling and numerous hematomas, though she was alert and oriented. Antibiotic therapy was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently changed to oral Apocillin in accordance with blood culture findings.

During her stay in the nephrology ward, laboratory investigations revealed improving markers of inflammation, reactive leukocytosis, rising platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin concentrations. Despite normalization of coagulation studies—indicating resolution of coagulopathy—kidney function tests continued to show persistent azotaemia. Her laboratory data from this period are outlined in Table 2.

While the initial trend was toward recovery, her clinical course shifted when she experienced an abrupt and isolated decline in haemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days) with no obvious bleeding source or signs of circulatory compromise. Platelet counts remained persistently low (30–40×10⁹/L). In addition, haptoglobin levels became undetectable and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) increased, consistent with ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) yielded a positive result for IgG, suggesting the possibility of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone therapy was initiated along with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple transfusions were administered within a 2-day period. Due to a lack of response to corticosteroids and persistently low platelet counts (41–46 × 10⁹/L), further investigation was conducted. Examination of the peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, a finding consistent with microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). The original DAT result was likely falsely positive, possibly due to penicillin use or previous transfusions; subsequent testing was negative.

Complement panels and ADAMTS13 activity were requested for additional assessment. Locally performed C3 and C4 complement levels were normal, as were the functional evaluations of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways. ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently reported at 43.5%, effectively excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP is characterized by activity under 10%. In the interim, while these results were pending, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced as an interim measure ahead of initiating eculizumab therapy.

After the platelet count had risen to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia with fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescents, findings in line with TMA.

The principal diagnostic evidence in this scenario was provided by the light microscopy findings depicted in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy results are not illustrated; immunofluorescence testing was negative for immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy did not show primary glomerular disease.

Polymerase chain reaction analysis of stool was negative, thus excluding Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS).

After the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) procedures, the patient began producing urine spontaneously again. Eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks) was started after the initial administration of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B). The second doses of these vaccines were scheduled for eight weeks later in the outpatient setting. Since vaccination had not occurred two weeks prior to initiation of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement testing was performed at Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden) to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Due to logistical issues and safety concerns, three rounds of PEX with Octaplas and one initial dose of eculizumab were administered prior to sampling. Assessment of complement function included quantification of alternative pathway activity (AH50), which was found to be substantially decreased at 6%. Levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing comprehensive medical treatment, the patient exhibits good overall health and maintains sufficient urine production. Renal function remains impaired, as evidenced by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she is scheduled for continued follow-up in the nephrology outpatient setting (figure 4). She is currently receiving maintenance eculizumab (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks), for a period of 4 months following induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of the complement system did not detect any pathogenic mutations; these findings were relayed verbally from the reference laboratory, and the written report had not been issued at the time of manuscript submission. Taking this information into account, complement inhibition therapy is being sustained owing to the seriousness of her presentation, with plans for possible withdrawal if renal function becomes stable. Detection of disease-causing complement gene variants would support a prolonged therapeutic strategy along with vigilant monitoring for recurrence; however, no such genetic alterations were found in this particular patient.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

For adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost task is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by marked deficiency of ADAMTS13 (<10%). Tools such as the PLASMIC score assist in stratifying patients while awaiting laboratory confirmation, which remains essential for definitive diagnosis. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and the measured ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, diminishing suspicion for TTP and directing the subsequent diagnostic pathway.

Another key differential is STEC-HUS. However, Shiga toxin testing by PCR was negative. The patient lacked preceding gastrointestinal symptoms, and microbiological assays failed to detect Shiga toxin, diminishing the probability of STEC-HUS.

In the end, persistent hemolysis, the presence of schistocytes, severe AKI necessitating dialysis, and renal biopsy findings typical of TMA favored a diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). The diagnosis was bolstered by negative PCR testing for STEC and the prompt clinical response observed after starting eculizumab. A negligible past medical history makes the presence of an underlying complement defect less likely and instead points toward infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is diagnosed based on clinicopathological criteria after other causes have been excluded, and complement components (C3/C4) may remain within normal ranges throughout the illness. It is critical to recognize that normal complement values (C3/C4) do not rule out CM-HUS, since these may not fluctuate outside reference limits during the disease. Of note, haemolytic activity was detected only after sepsis started to resolve, indicating that CM-HUS was precipitated by infection rather than being an underlying condition.

For this patient, baseline complement assessments (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 enzyme levels were measured just prior to initiating the first plasma exchange (PEX) treatment. Additional complement evaluations were carried out eight days following the last PEX session and four days after the initial administration of eculizumab. The reduced function of the alternative complement pathway observed is most likely attributable to therapeutic intervention instead of inherent complement exhaustion or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), formerly referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ results from abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with a genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50% to 70% of cases present with genetic alterations or autoantibodies targeting regulatory proteins in the complement system, including factor H, factor I, factor B, and membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Despite this, the disorder does not exhibit full penetrance, and overt disease typically arises following an exogenous insult—most often infections, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or select pharmaceuticals.

Though infrequently documented, Capnocytophaga canimorsus possesses the capability to trigger complement pathways, especially in vulnerable patients. The organism exhibits differential sensitivity to serum complement: numerous isolates are susceptible, while those expressing a polysaccharide capsule can display a degree of resistance. This suggests that complement function is crucial for host protection against this pathogen. Accordingly, a clinical dilemma arises; while complement activation aids in eradicating C. canimorsus, it can simultaneously provoke unrestrained endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy in genetically prone individuals.

Our report contributes to a limited but expanding collection of cases connecting C. canimorsus to complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathies (TMAs). An illustrative case by Bjørkto et al. described a patient who developed CM-HUS attributed to C. canimorsus and carried a variant in the complement factor B gene, responding to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that prior case, our patient did not have identifiable pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement-related genes, pointing towards a scenario of infection-driven CM-HUS absent a clear hereditary complement system vulnerability.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection entails distinct obstacles. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against terminal complement component 5, represents the mainstay treatment for CM-HUS, significantly enhancing both haematologic and renal outcomes, especially with prompt administration. Late commencement of eculizumab is linked to suboptimal renal recovery and an increased likelihood of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

In comparison, plasma exchange (PEX) is critical for survival in TTP but confers only transient improvement in CM-HUS. Its primary benefit stems from removal of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory molecules, or autoantibodies, rather than addressing the fundamental defect in complement regulation. For this patient, urine output resumed following the concluding PEX sessions and prior to eculizumab initiation, indicating temporary stabilization. Long-term haematological and renal improvement was achieved solely after implementation of complement inhibition.

A major clinical decision involves the use of complement inhibition during active bacteremia, since terminal complement components are vital in defending against encapsulated pathogens—such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga—highlighting risks related to infections with eculizumab. Here, eculizumab was started only after blood cultures had cleared, thereby reducing the chance of uncontrolled infection. Immunisation, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring remain crucial when complement inhibitors are utilized.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition represent the key factors associated with positive clinical outcomes. In this case, the patient attained haematological remission along with partial recovery of renal function, aligning with existing data indicating that baseline renal function and the interval before initiating eculizumab are major prognostic determinants.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus is exceptionally uncommon, this report underscores the need to consider infectious causes, even atypical ones, when evaluating patients with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and advancements in molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate recognition of additional similar cases.
