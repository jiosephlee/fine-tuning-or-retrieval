Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health conditions presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, progressing to complete anuria requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia. Although there was initial clinical improvement with antibiotic therapy and supportive measures, she later experienced abrupt onset of isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopenia. Laboratory evaluation and renal biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). Treatment with plasma exchange and eculizumab resulted in haematological remission and partial restoration of renal function. This report illustrates that C. canimorsus can serve as an unusual precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, and describes the diagnostic process, the difficulties encountered in establishing the differential diagnosis, and the intricate clinical course and management strategies when TMA develops following sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is commonly present in the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Although human infections are uncommon, transmission can result from bites, scratches, or even close proximity to animals. The spectrum of clinical manifestations ranges from isolated meningitis to severe presentations including fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). While the organism is generally sensitive to a wide range of antibiotics, mortality rates may reach as high as 30%.

Among the Capnocytophaga genus, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in zoonotic invasive disease in humans. Identification of the organism can be delayed because of its demanding growth requirements and slow culture propagation, which has led to increased utilization of molecular techniques, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In our patient, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock accompanied by profound leukopaenia and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. The initial phase was dominated by sepsis-associated involvement of multiple organ systems. Subsequently, the patient developed microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopaenia, and progression of acute kidney injury (AKI), raising concern for an overlapping TMA. This underscores that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be masked during the initial period of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues and a history of two uneventful pregnancies arrived at the emergency department with a rapidly deteriorating condition. Four days earlier, she sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and was given a tetanus vaccine as a preventive measure. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed on the same day, the patient was unable to obtain the medication, and consequently did not start antibiotics. She subsequently developed a high-grade fever (exceeding 40 °C), generalized malaise, poor oral intake, and eventually presented for medical evaluation due to the onset of numbness and color changes in her feet. During assessment at a primary-care emergency facility, she was found to be hypoglycaemic, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon therapy, with her blood glucose dropping to 0.6 mmol/L at its lowest, leading to urgent referral to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was awake and cooperative, but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottled and cool skin on her lower limbs. She denied using herbal supplements or any other substances, and no family members had experienced comparable symptoms. Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated profound systemic abnormalities, consisting of pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated liver enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas revealed metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range: 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal: <2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, she continued to be hypoglycaemic, believed to be related to sepsis. Upon arrival, urine output was sufficient; urinalysis detected protein at 2+ and blood at 3+.

Considering her range of clinical manifestations—fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—her presentation was determined to reflect disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) due to bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score 1 out of 3). Clinical examination and imaging excluded a surgical etiology for her mottled limbs, such as necrotising fasciitis. In view of the severity of her illness, empirical intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to provide coverage for pathogens associated with canine exposure, including Capnocytophaga species. Of particular importance, C. canimorsus was suspected at the outset and was subsequently identified seven days later through both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was then admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for intensive observation.

Hematology consultation was obtained in response to her pancytopenia and coagulopathy. Differential considerations comprised acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also entertained but considered improbable in light of leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin levels, and reticulocyte counts lacking compensation. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted, with plans for a bone marrow biopsy; however, absence of findings such as elevated CD34+ blasts or abnormal cell phenotypes prompted cancellation of the biopsy. Overall, the clinical picture remained compatible with sepsis. In the interim, supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were provided while awaiting diagnostic confirmation.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient developed complete anuria and a marked decline in renal function. Attempts at vigorous fluid replacement together with loop diuretics did not yield improvement. She then progressed to septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by volume overload, necessitating commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Serological workup for autoimmunity, including ANA and ANCA, returned negative results.

By the third ICU day, the patient exhibited generalized cutaneous bleeding with ecchymoses, in addition to distal cyanosis and ischemic lesions on multiple fingers and toes, although peripheral pulses and sensory function remained intact. These manifestations were suggestive of microvascular thrombosis in the setting of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Notably, despite involvement of multiple organ systems, she did not require vasopressor therapy except for stress-dose corticosteroids given during her initial period of sepsis. She was administered albumin in conjunction with other transfused blood products.

Following eight days in the hospital, the patient was moved to the nephrology ward to continue management of dialysis-dependent AKI and intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, she remained hemodynamically stable but was anuric, exhibited widespread edema, and had multiple hematomas, although she was awake and fully oriented. Based on blood culture findings, her antibiotic regimen was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, and subsequently to oral Apocillin.

In the nephrology unit, laboratory testing revealed a decreasing inflammatory response, leukocytosis consistent with recovery, rising platelet count, and stable hemoglobin levels. Renal parameters continued to show azotemia, though coagulation studies normalized, suggesting resolution of coagulopathy. Laboratory findings at this juncture are detailed in Table 2.

Despite early improvement, her clinical status shifted after a sudden, isolated decrease in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days) occurred without signs of bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained chronically reduced (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin fell below detectable levels, and LDH rose, consistent with persistent hemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) yielded a positive result for IgG, prompting consideration of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. High-dose prednisolone was initiated together with standard prophylactic measures, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over a two-day period. Despite these interventions, haemolysis persisted and platelet counts remained reduced (41–46 × 10⁹/L), prompting further investigation. Examination of a peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) suggestive of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (see figure 2). The initial DAT positivity was likely artifactual, potentially attributable to penicillin administration or preceding transfusions; on retesting, DAT was negative.

Complement panels and assessment of ADAMTS13 activity were submitted for laboratory analysis. Locally performed complement studies (C3 and C4) were normal, and activity across the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways was also within reference ranges. ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43.5%, which essentially excluded thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP is characterized by activity less than 10%. While pending these confirmatory results, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as an interim measure prior to administration of the first dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was undertaken, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and scattered crescents, findings consistent with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The most definitive diagnostic features in this case were identified on light microscopy as depicted in figure 3. Findings from immunofluorescence and electron microscopy were not included; immunofluorescence did not detect immune complex deposits, and electron microscopy showed no indications of a primary glomerulopathy.

Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli-related hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) was excluded due to negative results on stool PCR testing.

After the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), the patient experienced a resurgence of spontaneous urine output. Eculizumab induction therapy was started following administration of the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W, and Bexsero targeting serogroup B—at a dosage of 900 mg intravenously per week for four weeks. The subsequent doses of these vaccines were scheduled for eight weeks later, to be administered in an outpatient setting. Since the vaccines had not been delivered two weeks prior to the commencement of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was provided.

Comprehensive complement evaluation was ordered from Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden) to exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Due to logistical constraints and patient safety, three rounds of PEX using Octaplas and one induction dose of eculizumab were administered before the specimens were collected. Functional complement analysis comprised quantitative AH50 to assess alternative pathway activity, which was significantly diminished at 6%. Levels of complement factors H, I, and B remained within the normal range, and antifactor H antibodies were not detected.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intricate medical treatment, the patient currently presents with stable overall health and sufficient urine production. Renal function is still impaired, indicated by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L, and ongoing follow-up will take place in the nephrology outpatient department (figure 4). She remains on maintenance eculizumab therapy (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks), continuing for 4 months after induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of the complement system did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; preliminary results were provided verbally by the reference laboratory, while the official written report was still pending at submission. With these findings, complement inhibition therapy is being sustained because of the serious nature of her condition, with future evaluation for potential discontinuation contingent upon stabilization of renal function. The detection of pathogenic complement gene variants would support extended therapy with vigilant monitoring for recurrence, but no such mutations were found in this patient.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

When adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the primary concern is to rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by markedly reduced ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). While waiting for laboratory confirmation, tools like the PLASMIC score offer guidance in risk stratification, but a definitive diagnosis depends on ADAMTS13 assay results. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4/7, suggesting a low likelihood of TTP, and the ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, reducing suspicion for TTP and directing further investigative steps.

Another differential diagnosis is STEC-HUS. Shiga toxin PCR testing returned negative for this patient. Moreover, the lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and absence of microbiological evidence diminishes the probability of STEC-HUS.

The collective findings of ongoing haemolysis, schistocytosis, dialysis-requiring AKI, and renal biopsy results indicative of TMA were consistent with CM-HUS (see figure 5). The diagnosis was reinforced by the negative Shiga toxin PCR and marked clinical recovery after the initiation of eculizumab. Furthermore, her minimal medical history does not support a pre-existing complement abnormality necessitating a ‘second hit,’ which instead favors an infection-driven episode of CM-HUS.

CM-HUS represents a clinicopathological diagnosis made by exclusion, with complement concentrations (C3 and C4) potentially remaining within normal limits throughout its progression. Therefore, normal complement values (C3/C4) do not rule out CM-HUS, as these markers can stay within the reference range during the illness. Of particular note, hemolysis only developed after the patient’s sepsis began to resolve, indicating that CM-HUS was likely triggered by the infection rather than being pre-existing.

For this case, standard complement assays (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were assessed just before the initiation of plasma exchange (PEX). More comprehensive complement analyses occurred eight days following the last PEX procedure and four days after the patient’s initial administration of eculizumab. The reduction observed in the alternative complement pathway was most attributable to therapeutic interventions, rather than to inherent complement deficiency or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), formerly referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ results from inappropriate activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently occurring in patients with underlying genetic susceptibility. Genetic abnormalities or autoantibodies directed against complement regulatory proteins—including factor H, factor I, factor B, and membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are found in 50%–70% of affected individuals. Nevertheless, incomplete penetrance is observed, with clinical disease often emerging only after exposure to an external precipitant, such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or certain drugs.

Although rarely documented, Capnocytophaga canimorsus is capable of stimulating complement pathways, predominantly in vulnerable individuals. Importantly, serum susceptibility varies among C. canimorsus isolates: while many strains are susceptible to complement-mediated killing, some encapsulated forms demonstrate partial resistance. This highlights the significance of complement activity in host defense but also introduces a clinical dilemma—effective complement activation eradicates the pathogen yet may also provoke excessive endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in those predisposed.

This report contributes to the limited but expanding literature associating C. canimorsus infection with complement-mediated TMAs. For example, Bjørkto et al described a case involving a patient with C. canimorsus-induced CM-HUS carrying a complement factor B mutation, who responded favorably to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that report, our case did not identify any pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement-related genes, which supports the notion of infection-triggered CM-HUS occurring in the absence of a detectable inherited complement defect.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Treating CM-HUS during an ongoing infection is particularly complex. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5, remains the preferred therapy for CM-HUS and leads to significant improvements in blood and kidney function, especially when started promptly. Postponing treatment may result in suboptimal renal recovery, increasing the likelihood of progression to end-stage renal disease.

While plasma exchange (PEX) is crucial and lifesaving for TTP, its effect in CM-HUS is short-lived, primarily through removal of circulating complement fragments, pro-inflammatory substances, or autoantibodies, but it does not resolve the underlying complement dysregulation. In this patient, urine output improved following the final PEX cycles and prior to eculizumab administration, indicating only a brief period of stabilization. Ongoing improvement in hematologic and renal parameters occurred exclusively after initiation of complement inhibition.

An important therapeutic challenge is the use of complement inhibition while bacteraemia is still present, since terminal complement components are vital for protection against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, raising the possibility of increased risk of infection with eculizumab. For this individual, eculizumab was started only after blood cultures had cleared, thereby reducing the risk of exacerbating an existing infection. When utilizing complement inhibitors, vaccination, antibiotic prophylaxis, and vigilant surveillance continue to be necessary components of care.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition represent the key prognostic factors for optimal outcomes. The patient described here attained hematological remission and partial improvement in renal function, aligning with prevailing data indicating that both renal status and time elapsed before starting eculizumab are critical prognostic variables.

Despite the rarity of CM-HUS associated with C. canimorsus, this case emphasizes the necessity to consider infectious precipitants—including atypical ones—when evaluating patients with newly developed TMA. Greater clinician awareness alongside advanced molecular diagnostic methods could facilitate the detection of additional similar cases.
