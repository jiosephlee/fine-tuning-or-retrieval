Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior medical conditions presented with sepsis of rapid onset, occurring four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her clinical course was marked by the development of disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which advanced to complete anuria requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia. Although her condition initially improved with antimicrobial therapy and supportive measures, she subsequently experienced abrupt isolated hemolysis and sustained thrombocytopenia. Laboratory assessment and renal biopsy established a diagnosis of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS) was identified. Treatment with plasma exchange followed by initiation of eculizumab resulted in hematologic remission and partial restoration of renal function. This report demonstrates that C. canimorsus infection can serve as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in immunocompetent individuals, and describes the patient’s diagnostic evaluation, the challenges in distinguishing among differential diagnoses, as well as the intricate clinical trajectory and management considerations in TMA complicating sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is a slow-growing, encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus commonly present in the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Infections in humans are uncommon and may result from bites, scratches, or close interaction with these animals. The clinical spectrum is broad, encompassing isolated meningitis, rapid-onset sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organs, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Despite susceptibility to a wide range of antibiotics, mortality rates can reach as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in severe zoonotic infections in people. Due to its demanding growth requirements and slow proliferation in laboratory cultures, diagnosis is frequently delayed, leading to increased reliance on molecular diagnostics such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the case described, C. canimorsus infection manifested with septic shock, profound leukopaenia, and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. Initial management was complicated by sepsis-associated multiorgan dysfunction, and subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and deteriorating acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed to a concomitant TMA. This evolution underscores how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may be concealed during the initial stages dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two straightforward pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department experiencing rapidly deteriorating health. She had sustained a dog bite to her upper lip four days before admission and was administered a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed at the time of the bite, she did not begin antibiotic therapy as she was unable to obtain the medication. Subsequently, she developed a high-grade fever (>40 °C), general malaise, and decreased oral intake. She sought medical care after experiencing numbness and discoloration in her feet. At a primary-care emergency facility, she was noted to have hypoglycemia, which did not respond to oral glucose or glucagon and reached a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L. This finding led to her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was awake and able to communicate, but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottling and coolness of the lower limbs. She denied using any herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of similar illness among her family members. Initial laboratory investigations indicated pronounced systemic dysfunction, with significant inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated liver transaminases, and coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas evaluation demonstrated metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). Despite administration of high doses of intravenous glucose, her hypoglycaemia persisted and was attributed to sepsis. On presentation, her urine output was sufficient; urinalysis identified proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Her combination of clinical findings—fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteremia and early sepsis, with a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. Clinical and radiological assessments excluded a surgical etiology for the mottling of her extremities, including necrotising fasciitis. Given the severity of the illness, intravenous broad-spectrum piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) therapy was initiated to provide coverage against pathogens associated with dogs, such as Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was suspected from the outset, and subsequently, after 7 days, both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures confirmed its presence. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for heightened surveillance.

In light of the pancytopenia and coagulopathy, hematology input was sought. Differential considerations encompassed acute leukemia and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Hemolytic uremic syndrome (HUS) was also contemplated but was deemed less probable due to the presence of leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin levels, and lack of compensatory reticulocytosis. Flow cytometry was completed, and a bone marrow biopsy was scheduled; however, following normal findings—without increased CD34+ blasts or abnormal immunophenotypes—the biopsy was not carried out. The clinical scenario remained compatible with sepsis. While diagnostic results were pending, supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were provided.

Upon admission to the ICU, the patient experienced complete cessation of urine output and a rapid decline in renal function. Intensive fluid administration together with loop diuretic therapy was unsuccessful. She went on to develop septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by fluid accumulation, necessitating the commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), returned negative.

By ICU day 3, she manifested widespread cutaneous bleeding characterized by ecchymoses and cyanotic-ischemic changes of multiple distal toes and fingers; however, distal pulses and neurological function were maintained. These clinical signs suggested microvascular thrombosis in the setting of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Despite multi-organ involvement, vasopressors were not needed except for stress-dose corticosteroids provided during the early septic phase. Albumin along with other blood product transfusions was administered.

Following 8 days of hospital admission, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit due to continued dialysis-requiring AKI and ongoing intravenous antibiotic treatment. Upon arrival, she remained hemodynamically stable but continued to have anuria, exhibited widespread edema, and multiple areas of bruising, though she was awake and fully conscious. Based on blood culture findings, her antibiotic regimen was narrowed to intravenous ampicillin, and later transitioned to oral Apocillin.

During her stay in the nephrology ward, laboratory assessments demonstrated a reduction in markers of inflammation, reactive leukocytosis, a rebound in platelet count, and stable hemoglobin levels. However, renal studies revealed persistent azotemia, while coagulation markers normalized, suggesting an improvement in coagulopathy. Laboratory findings from this period are summarized in Table 2.

After this period of improvement, her clinical status changed when she experienced an acute, isolated decline in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over 6 days) in the absence of apparent bleeding or changes in circulatory stability. Platelet counts remained low at 30–40 × 10⁹/L, haptoglobin levels dropped to undetectable levels, and LDH increased, all indicative of ongoing hemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT; Coombs test) returned positive for IgG, indicating possible autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. The patient was started on high-dose prednisolone with routine prophylaxis, and received multiple blood transfusions over a period of two days. As corticosteroids failed to halt the haemolytic process and platelet counts remained persistently low (41–46 × 10⁹/L), further investigation was deemed necessary. Examination of a peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, indicating microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and pointing towards thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). The initially positive DAT likely represented a false positive, possibly due to penicillin use or previous transfusions; subsequent testing was negative.

Complement levels and ADAMTS13 enzyme activity were analyzed. Results from standard local complement testing (C3 and C4) and functional assessment of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways all returned within normal parameters. Shortly thereafter, ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, which essentially excluded thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP is typically characterized by activity less than 10%. In the interim, while pending these outcomes, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as a bridge before administration of the initial dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was carried out, which demonstrated marked acute tubular necrosis, ischemic-appearing glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, findings in line with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The light microscopic characteristics, depicted in figure 3, most clearly illustrated the diagnostic features observed in this patient. Immunofluorescence testing, which did not show immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy, which revealed no signs indicative of primary glomerulopathy, are not depicted.

A negative stool PCR excluded the possibility of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS).

After the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), the patient resumed spontaneous urine output. Following administration of the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo, targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W, and Bexsero for serogroup B—eculizumab induction was started (900 mg intravenously each week for four weeks). The second doses of these vaccines were scheduled for eight weeks later, as part of outpatient care. As vaccination had not occurred two weeks prior to beginning complement inhibition, Apocillin was administered prophylactically.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement testing was sent to Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. For both logistical reasons and to ensure patient safety, three plasma exchange sessions using Octaplas and one induction dose of eculizumab were completed prior to specimen collection. Functional complement assessment included quantitative AH50 analysis of alternative pathway activity, which was significantly diminished (6%). Levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits, and antifactor H antibody tests returned negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical therapy, the patient is currently stable, exhibiting satisfactory urine output. Renal function is still impaired, with a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and ongoing follow-up will take place in the nephrology outpatient clinic (see figure 4). She remains on maintenance eculizumab (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks), continuing this regimen for four months after induction. Comprehensive complement genetic analysis did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; the findings were relayed orally by the reference laboratory, while the official written report was not yet available at submission. In light of these details, complement inhibition therapy is maintained because of the disease’s severity, with future plans to reconsider discontinuation if kidney function improves. The detection of causative complement gene mutations would support prolonged therapy combined with vigilant monitoring for recurrence. In this particular case, no such genetic abnormalities were found.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

The foremost concern in adults exhibiting MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Triage can be facilitated by clinical scoring systems such as PLASMIC while awaiting laboratory confirmation, which remains the definitive method for diagnosis. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and the ADAMTS13 activity measured at 43.5%, further reducing suspicion for TTP and informing subsequent investigation.

STEC-HUS must also be considered. PCR testing for Shiga toxin was negative in this patient. Moreover, the absence of prior enteric symptoms and negative microbiological assays decreased the probability of STEC-HUS.

In conclusion, the findings of ongoing hemolysis, schistocytes on blood smear, AKI requiring dialysis, and kidney histology compatible with TMA supported the diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). The diagnosis was reinforced by the PCR exclusion of STEC and the patient’s marked clinical response to eculizumab therapy. A largely unremarkable medical history provides less evidence for a pre-existing complement abnormality needing a ‘second hit,’ favoring an infection-induced CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is diagnosed based on clinicopathologic features after other possibilities have been excluded, and complement measurements (C3/C4) may remain within normal ranges throughout the illness. Crucially, normal C3 and C4 levels do not rule out CM-HUS, as these values may stay inside normal limits during disease progression. Of particular note, haemolysis was observed only after there was improvement in sepsis, indicating infection-associated CM-HUS rather than a chronic underlying condition.

For this patient, core complement analyses (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity assessments were conducted just prior to the initial plasma exchange (PEX) procedure. Extended complement evaluations were completed 8 days after the last PEX and 4 days following administration of the first dose of eculizumab. The decrease detected in the alternative pathway likely reflects the effects of therapy, as opposed to inherent complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS’, results from unregulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with genetic susceptibility. Genetic variants or autoantibodies directed against complement regulatory proteins—including factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are identified in 50% to 70% of cases. Nonetheless, not all individuals with these variants will develop disease, as clinical expression often necessitates an external inciting factor such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune processes, or exposure to specific drugs.

Though it is infrequently documented, Capnocytophaga canimorsus is capable of activating complement pathways, especially in vulnerable populations. Interestingly, this organism demonstrates varying degrees of susceptibility to serum-mediated killing; while many strains are readily targeted by complement, some encapsulated isolates display partial resistance. Consequently, complement activation is a crucial part of host defense. This dynamic introduces a clinical conundrum: although complement activation helps eradicate the bacterium, in at-risk individuals it can precipitate unchecked endothelial injury and subsequent thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Our report contributes to the limited but expanding literature associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. For instance, a notable case by Bjørkto et al detailed a patient with CM-HUS related to C. canimorsus who harbored a variant in complement factor B and responded to eculizumab therapy. Unlike the aforementioned case, genetic analysis in our patient did not reveal any pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations in complement-associated genes, lending support to the concept of infection-induced CM-HUS occurring even in the absence of an identifiable hereditary complement defect.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

The management of CM-HUS during ongoing infection is particularly challenging. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against terminal complement component 5, is considered the treatment of choice for CM-HUS and significantly enhances both haematological and renal outcomes, especially when administered promptly. Delays in starting eculizumab are linked with suboptimal renal recovery and increased likelihood of developing end-stage kidney disease.

PEX, by contrast, is vital in treating TTP but yields only short-term improvement in CM-HUS. Its main effect is the removal of circulating complement fragments, inflammatory substances, or autoantibodies, rather than addressing the core defect in the complement pathway. In the reported patient, urine output resumed following the last PEX courses but prior to eculizumab therapy, indicating temporary stabilization alone. Persistent improvements in blood and kidney parameters were achieved solely with complement inhibition.

A major challenge is administering complement inhibitors amidst active bacteraemia, since terminal complement components play an essential role in host defence against encapsulated organisms—including Neisseria and Capnocytophaga—raising the possibility of heightened infection risk associated with eculizumab. Here, eculizumab was started only after blood cultures cleared, reducing the chance of persistent infection. Prophylactic vaccination, use of antimicrobial agents, and diligent surveillance are vital when complement blockade is employed.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition represent the key factors associated with positive clinical outcomes. The patient described reached haematological remission and exhibited partial improvement of renal function, aligning with published data that identifies renal status and the interval to eculizumab initiation as principal prognostic variables.

Despite the rarity of CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus, this report underscores the necessity of evaluating infectious precipitants, even uncommon ones, in individuals who present with newly developed TMA. Increased clinical awareness and advances in molecular diagnostic techniques could facilitate the recognition of additional cases of this nature.
