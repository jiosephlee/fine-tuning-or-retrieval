Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete anuria requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although her condition initially improved with antibiotics and supportive therapy, she subsequently experienced sudden isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory investigations and renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) was made. Haematological remission occurred after plasma exchange and initiation of eculizumab, with partial recovery of kidney function. This case demonstrates that C. canimorsus infection can rarely precipitate CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults and describes the patient's diagnostic evaluation, the challenges inherent to differential diagnosis, as well as the complexity of clinical management and therapeutic strategies in TMA secondary to sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is commonly present in the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Human infection is uncommon and may be transmitted through bites, scratches, or even close contact with animals. The clinical spectrum is broad, including isolated meningitis, rapidly progressing sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Mortality rates may reach up to 30% despite the organism’s overall susceptibility to antibiotics.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most often associated with severe zoonotic infections in humans. Its fastidious growth requirements and slow cultivation can delay diagnosis, leading to greater reliance on molecular techniques such as real-time nanopore sequencing for rapid identification.

In our report, infection with C. canimorsus initially manifested as septic shock accompanied by profound leukopenia and a coagulopathy pattern indicating DIC. While sepsis with multiorgan involvement was predominant early on, the subsequent appearance of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) pointed to a secondary TMA. This underscores that complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can remain concealed during the initial phase of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two previous uneventful pregnancies arrived at the emergency department due to a rapidly deteriorating condition. Four days before admission, her dog bit her upper lip, after which she received a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. She was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) at the time of the injury, but she did not start antibiotic therapy as she was unable to pick up the medication. Over the following days, she experienced a high-grade fever (>40 °C), general malaise, and a decreased ability to eat. Medical care was sought when she noticed numbness and a change in color of her feet. Upon evaluation at an urgent primary care service, she was discovered to be hypoglycaemic with a minimum measured blood glucose of 0.6 mmol/L, resistant to both oral glucose and glucagon administration, which led to her emergent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and appropriately interactive, though she exhibited signs of sepsis, including cool and mottled lower limbs. She denied any use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no family history of similar complaints or illness. Initial laboratory testing indicated significant multisystem involvement: pronounced inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury, elevated hepatic transaminases, and coagulation abnormalities. Arterial blood gas evaluation demonstrated a metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (normal range: 7.35–7.43) and an increased lactate concentration at 5.7 mmol/L (reference: <2.5 mmol/L). Persistent hypoglycaemia was noted in spite of administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, attributed to the ongoing sepsis. At presentation, urinary output was adequate; urinalysis detected proteinuria (2+) and hematuria (3+).

Given the range of her clinical signs—fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—the diagnosis was made of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis (with a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3). Surgical causes for her mottled extremities, including necrotising fasciitis, were excluded following physical and imaging assessments. Owing to the gravity of her condition, empirical therapy with intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to provide coverage for canine-associated organisms, particularly Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was immediately suspected, and confirmation was achieved 7 days later by positive results from both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was then admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced monitoring.

Haematology input was sought due to findings of pancytopenia and coagulopathy. The working diagnoses considered acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Although haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was part of the differential, it was regarded as less likely given the presence of leukopaenia, raised inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and insufficient reticulocyte response. Flow cytometric analysis was conducted and a bone marrow biopsy was scheduled; however, with unremarkable findings (no CD34+ blast increase or aberrant phenotypes), the biopsy was ultimately not pursued. The patient's clinical manifestation aligned with sepsis. While diagnostic results were pending, she received supportive transfusions of thrombocytes and fibrinogen.

Following admission to the intensive care unit, the patient became completely anuric, and her renal function deteriorated rapidly. Intensive intravenous fluid therapy combined with loop diuretics was tried, but these measures were ineffective. She progressed to septic cardiomyopathy with subsequent fluid accumulation, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), returned negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, she exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding manifested by ecchymoses, alongside cyanosis and ischemic alterations at the distal aspects of several toes and fingers. Despite these changes, distal pulses and neurological sensation remained intact. These clinical signs indicated microthrombotic events associated with disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Although multiple organ systems were affected, vasopressor therapy was not needed apart from stress-dose corticosteroids provided early in her septic course. She was also given albumin infusions and additional blood product support.

Following eight days in the hospital, she was moved to the nephrology unit due to persistent dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and to continue intravenous antibiotic therapy. Upon arrival, the patient was clinically stable but remained anuric, exhibited generalized edema and multiple hematomas, and was alert and oriented. Based on the blood culture findings, the antibiotics were reduced to intravenous ampicillin, which was subsequently switched to oral Apocillin.

In the nephrology unit, laboratory assessment revealed a trend towards resolution of inflammation, demonstrated by decreasing markers and reactive leukocytosis, improving platelet count, and stable hemoglobin. Renal function studies continued to show azotemia, but coagulation studies returned to normal values, pointing to improvement in the previous coagulopathy. Despite these improvements, her clinical condition shifted when she experienced an acute, isolated decline in hemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over six days), in the absence of any bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts stayed low (30–40×10⁹/L), while haptoglobin was undetectable and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels increased, suggesting active hemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin (Coombs) test returned positive for IgG, indicating possible autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. Consequently, high-dose prednisolone was administered along with standard prophylaxis, and the patient received multiple blood transfusions over a 2-day period. Due to the lack of improvement in the haemolytic process after corticosteroid treatment and persistently low platelet counts (41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional investigations were performed. Examination of a peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, indicating microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), which is compatible with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (see figure 2). The initial positive DAT was most likely a false positive attributable to penicillin exposure or recent transfusions; a repeat DAT subsequently was negative.

Complement evaluation and ADAMTS13 activity testing were conducted. Routine complement assays, including C3 and C4, returned values within normal ranges, and functional testing of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways was also unremarkable. Results for ADAMTS13 activity became available soon after, with a measured activity of 43.5%, effectively excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP is typically associated with activity levels below 10%. While awaiting these outcomes, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced as interim therapy before the initial administration of eculizumab.

Following an increase in platelet count to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was performed, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia accompanied by fibrin thrombi, and sporadic crescent formation—findings indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Key diagnostic features were most evident on light microscopy, as depicted in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy findings are omitted here; immunofluorescence did not detect immune complex deposits, and electron microscopy showed no signs of a primary glomerular pathology.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was ruled out on the basis of a negative stool PCR analysis.

After the patient underwent the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) sessions, spontaneous urine output resumed. Eculizumab induction therapy was begun—administered at 900 mg intravenously each week for four weeks—subsequent to administering initial doses of the meningococcal vaccines Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B). Plans were made to administer the second vaccine doses 8 weeks later in an outpatient setting. Because the vaccines had not been given at least 2 weeks prior to beginning complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was started.

To exclude inherited complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), advanced complement assays were submitted to the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. Taking into account both patient safety and logistical issues, three PEX sessions with Octaplas as well as one loading dose of eculizumab were performed before sample collection. Functional complement assessment comprised a quantitative measurement of alternative pathway activity (AH50), which was notably decreased at 6%. Complement factors H, I, and B were found to be within normal limits, and testing for antifactor H antibodies was negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intricate medical management, the patient currently exhibits stable overall health and satisfactory urine output. Renal function remains compromised, as indicated by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L, and continued follow-up will occur at the nephrology outpatient clinic (see figure 4). She is receiving maintenance eculizumab at a dose of 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, sustained for four months following induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of the complement system revealed no pathogenic variants; these findings were relayed orally by the reference laboratory, and the official written report was still awaited when this report was submitted. Given the severity of her presentation, therapy with complement blockade is being maintained, with a plan to reassess the need for continuation if her kidney function becomes stable. The discovery of pathogenic complement gene mutations would have supported an extended treatment duration with vigilant surveillance for relapse, but no such genetic abnormalities were found in this patient.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

In adult patients presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, it is crucial to first rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by a marked reduction in ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). Tools such as the PLASMIC score can assist with initial risk stratification while awaiting laboratory confirmation. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, which corresponds to a low likelihood for TTP, and her ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, further reducing the probability of TTP and guiding alternative diagnostic approaches.

Consideration was also given to STEC-HUS. The patient's PCR assay for Shiga toxin returned negative. Additionally, the lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms and negative microbial studies argue against a diagnosis of STEC-HUS.

In summary, ongoing hemolysis, schistocytes, AKI requiring dialysis, and renal biopsy features indicative of TMA all supported the diagnosis of CM-HUS (see figure 5). This diagnosis was bolstered by negative Shiga toxin PCR results and a prompt response to eculizumab. The absence of significant past medical history lessens the likelihood of an underlying complement abnormality and favors an infection-induced trigger for CM-HUS.

CM-HUS represents a diagnosis made by excluding other clinicopathological entities, and complement component levels (C3/C4) may remain within normal limits throughout the illness. It is critical to note that CM-HUS cannot be ruled out solely based on normal C3 and C4 values, as these levels may persist within reference intervals during the disease. Of note, hemolysis was observed only after there was improvement in the patient’s sepsis, indicating the development of CM-HUS precipitated by infection rather than the presence of underlying disease.

For this patient, baseline complement measurements (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were assessed immediately prior to the initiation of the first plasma exchange (PEX). Additional comprehensive complement analyses were conducted 8 days following the last PEX session and 4 days subsequent to the initial administration of eculizumab. The decrease observed in the alternative pathway is most indicative of treatment-related effects rather than primary complement consumption or activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS,’ is the result of dysregulation within the alternative complement pathway, typically in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of affected patients possess genetic alterations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory proteins—including factor H, factor I, factor B, and membrane cofactor protein (CD46). However, expression of the disease is variable, as external factors such as infections, pregnancy, autoimmune processes, or specific drug exposures are generally necessary to precipitate clinical symptoms.

Though infrequently documented, Capnocytophaga canimorsus is capable of initiating complement activation, especially in hosts with increased vulnerability. This pathogen demonstrates differing degrees of sensitivity to serum complement: while numerous strains are readily neutralized by complement, some capsule-producing isolates retain partial resistance. Consequently, effective complement function is essential for defending against the organism. This interplay introduces a clinical dilemma: activation of complement is critical for pathogen eradication, yet in genetically susceptible individuals, excessive complement activity may also initiate unregulated endothelial injury and the development of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

This report contributes to a limited, though expanding, group of cases correlating C. canimorsus infection with complement-mediated TMAs. Notably, Bjørkto et al recounted a patient with CM-HUS secondary to C. canimorsus, found to have a complement factor B mutation, and who responded favorably to eculizumab therapy. By contrast, in the current case, sequencing failed to reveal any pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement genes, indicating that infection may induce CM-HUS in the absence of an identifiable hereditary complement defect.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amidst an ongoing infection entails distinct difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits complement component 5, represents the standard therapy for CM-HUS and significantly enhances both haematological and renal outcomes, especially if given promptly. Initiating treatment later correlates with suboptimal renal recovery and increased likelihood of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

Alternatively, plasma exchange (PEX) is vital in the treatment of TTP but yields only short-lived benefit in cases of CM-HUS by removing soluble complement factors, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies from the circulation, yet fails to address the fundamental defect in the complement pathway. In the presented patient, urine output returned following the last PEX treatments and prior to receiving eculizumab, indicating temporary stabilisation only. Long-term recovery of haematological parameters and renal function was achieved only after complement inhibition.

An important management challenge involves employing complement inhibition during ongoing bacteraemia, since terminal components of the complement pathway are essential for host defense against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, raising potential infectious risks with eculizumab administration. In this situation, eculizumab was started only once blood cultures turned negative, thereby reducing the likelihood of uncontrolled infection. The use of vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant monitoring is crucial when prescribing complement blockers.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely diagnosis of CM-HUS and early commencement of complement inhibition are critical factors influencing a positive prognosis. In this case, the patient reached hematologic remission and experienced partial recovery of renal function, aligning with existing data indicating that both renal status and the interval before starting eculizumab are key prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS associated with C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of evaluating infectious precipitants—including atypical pathogens—in patients with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical awareness alongside advancements in molecular testing may facilitate recognition of additional cases.
