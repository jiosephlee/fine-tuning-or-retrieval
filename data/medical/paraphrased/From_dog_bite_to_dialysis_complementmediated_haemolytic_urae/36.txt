Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She subsequently developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which progressed to complete anuria requiring dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she initially improved with antibiotic therapy and supportive measures, she later experienced acute isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Laboratory studies and a renal biopsy confirmed thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), leading to a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). After undergoing plasma exchange and receiving eculizumab, the patient achieved haematological remission and experienced partial recovery of renal function. This report demonstrates that C. canimorsus infection, though rare, can precipitate CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults, and discusses the diagnostic evaluation, differential diagnostic difficulties, and the complex nature of clinical management and treatment strategies for TMA following sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus characterized by slow growth, commonly present in the normal oropharyngeal microbiota of dogs and cats. Infections in humans are uncommon and can be acquired not only through bites and scratches but also via close exposure to these animals. The clinical spectrum is broad, encompassing isolated cases of meningitis, rapidly progressive sepsis, DIC, failure of multiple organ systems, and TMA. Even though the organism exhibits broad antibiotic sensitivity, the case fatality rate can be as high as 30%.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is the one most often implicated in severe zoonotic infections in humans. Due to its slow growth and fastidious behavior, traditional culture methods may result in delayed identification, thereby increasing the reliance on molecular diagnostics including real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the described case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock accompanied by marked leukopaenia and coagulopathy indicative of DIC. Initially, the predominant clinical manifestations reflected sepsis with involvement of multiple organs, but the subsequent emergence of MAHA, a drop in platelet count, and escalating AKI pointed toward a concurrent TMA process. This underscores that CM-HUS can be masked by the features of early sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, previously in good health and with a history of two uneventful pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department experiencing a rapidly deteriorating condition. Four days before admission, she sustained a dog bite on her upper lip and was given a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed on the day of the injury, she did not start the antibiotic because she was unable to pick up her medication. Subsequently, she developed a high fever (>40 °C), general malaise, and decreased oral intake. She later sought care after noting numbness and discoloration in her feet. During her evaluation at the primary-care emergency service, she was found to have hypoglycemia that was unresponsive to oral glucose and glucagon, reaching a low point of 0.6 mmol/L. This led to her urgent referral to our emergency department.

Upon presentation, the patient was awake and oriented but exhibited signs of sepsis, including mottled and cool lower limbs. She reported no use of herbal medications or other agents, and there was no history of similar illness among her family members. The initial laboratory assessment demonstrated significant systemic abnormalities, with pronounced inflammatory response, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic transaminases, and coagulation disturbances. Arterial blood gas analysis identified metabolic acidosis, evidenced by a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43), and raised lactate at 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). Persistent hypoglycaemia was observed, despite administration of large quantities of intravenous glucose, believed to be associated with the septic state. On admission, diuretic function was preserved; urine analysis revealed proteinuria (2+) and haematuria (3+).

Considering her array of clinical manifestations—including elevated temperature, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—her status was assessed as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) in the setting of bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3). Following thorough clinical and radiologic assessment, a surgical etiology for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded. Owing to the gravity of her presentation, she was commenced on broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) in order to provide coverage for canine-associated pathogens, including Capnocytophaga species. Of note, C. canimorsus was suspected initially and was confirmed after 7 days by both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was then moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced surveillance.

Because of the pancytopenia and coagulopathy, hematology was consulted. The differential diagnosis encompassed acute leukemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was also considered, but was thought less probable due to leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin levels, and non-compensatory reticulocyte counts. Flow cytometry was undertaken, and a bone marrow biopsy was scheduled; however, the absence of increased CD34+ blasts or atypical phenotypes on flow cytometry resulted in the biopsy being cancelled. The clinical scenario was most consistent with sepsis. Pending further investigations, she was administered thrombocyte and fibrinogen transfusions for supportive care.

Following her admission to the intensive care unit, the patient became completely anuric with a swift deterioration in renal function. Attempts at vigorous intravenous fluid replacement combined with the use of loop diuretics were unsuccessful. She later developed septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by volume overload, necessitating the initiation of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune serologies, including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), yielded negative results.

By the third day in the ICU, the patient experienced widespread cutaneous bleeding manifested as ecchymoses and cyanotic, ischemic changes at the distal portions of multiple fingers and toes, although both pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical manifestations indicated microthrombotic processes occurring in the setting of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). Despite involvement of multiple organ systems, vasopressor support was never required apart from stress-dose corticosteroids given early during her septic episode. She was also administered albumin infusions in addition to other blood products.

Following eight days in hospital, she was moved to the nephrology unit to continue dialysis for AKI and receive intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, the patient was hemodynamically stable but remained anuric, exhibited generalized edema, and had multiple hematomas, although she was alert and fully oriented. Her antibiotics were subsequently downgraded to intravenous ampicillin, and then to oral Apocillin according to blood culture findings.

Upon admission to nephrology, laboratory tests revealed an improving inflammatory state, reactive leukocytosis, rising platelet counts, and stable hemoglobin levels. Renal function tests still showed ongoing azotemia. Meanwhile, her coagulation studies normalized, indicating resolution of the coagulopathy. Despite early progress, her clinical status shifted when she experienced an abrupt drop in hemoglobin (from 111 g/L to 68 g/L over a six-day span), with no clinical evidence of bleeding or hemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained low (30–40×10⁹/L), while haptoglobin levels became undetectable and LDH was elevated, consistent with continued haemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) was positive for IgG, leading to concern for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. The patient was started on high-dose prednisolone together with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered during a 2-day period. Because the haemolysis did not improve with corticosteroid treatment and platelet counts persisted at a low level (41–46 × 10⁹/L), further investigation was deemed necessary. Examination of a peripheral blood smear revealed schistocytes accounting for 15%–20% of red blood cells, indicative of MAHA and raising the possibility of TMA (figure 2). The initial DAT result was probably a false positive attributable to penicillin exposure or preceding transfusions; subsequent testing showed a negative result.

Complement assays and ADAMTS13 activity were ordered for further assessment. Standard local complement tests (C3 and C4) were found to be normal, and functional evaluations of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways also yielded normal results. The ADAMTS13 activity came back at 43.5%, which was sufficient to exclude thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), since TTP is generally associated with activity below 10%. During the interval before these findings were available, plasma exchange (PEX) was commenced as interim treatment before administration of the initial dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was undertaken, which revealed marked acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli containing fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation, all of which were indicative of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

The primary diagnostic evidence in this patient was most apparent on the light microscopic findings depicted in figure 3. Immunofluorescence studies and electron microscopy were not included, as immunofluorescence testing showed no deposition of immune complexes, and electron microscopy excluded the presence of a primary glomerulopathy.

Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) was excluded due to a negative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) result for stool specimens.

After the patient underwent the second and third plasma exchange (PEX) sessions, spontaneous urine output resumed. Eculizumab induction therapy was started (900 mg intravenously weekly for four weeks) after administration of the initial meningococcal vaccinations—Menveo (targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (for serogroup B). The second vaccine doses were scheduled for eight weeks later in the outpatient setting. Because the immunizations had not occurred two weeks prior to initiating complement inhibition, Apocillin prophylaxis was provided.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement assays were sent to the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. In light of logistical constraints and safety concerns, three PEX procedures with Octaplas and one eculizumab loading dose were carried out prior to sample collection. Functional assessment of the complement system, including alternative pathway activity measured via quantitative AH50, demonstrated a significant reduction (6%). However, levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits, and antifactor H antibodies were absent.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical treatment, the patient exhibits overall stable health and maintains sufficient urine output. Her renal function remains impaired, with a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, and she is scheduled for ongoing monitoring in the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). She is receiving maintenance therapy with eculizumab, dosed at 1200 mg intravenously every two weeks, continuing for four months after induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of complement-related genes yielded no pathogenic variants; the results were relayed verbally by the reference laboratory, while the official written report was still pending at the time of submission. Given these findings, complement inhibition therapy is being sustained due to the severity of her condition, with plans to reconsider discontinuation if kidney function improves. The presence of pathogenic mutations in complement genes would support a longer duration of treatment and close surveillance for possible relapse; however, none were detected in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

The foremost consideration in adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury (AKI) is to rule out thrombotic TTP, characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). Clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score can be utilized for preliminary assessment during the interim period before laboratory confirmation. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and her measured ADAMTS13 activity was 43.5%, thereby making TTP an improbable diagnosis and informing subsequent investigations.

STEC-HUS must also be evaluated. However, the patient's PCR testing for Shiga toxin was negative, and the absence of preceding gastrointestinal illness and negative microbiological findings rendered STEC-HUS unlikely.

In summary, the persistence of haemolysis, the presence of schistocytes, need for dialysis due to AKI, and kidney biopsy findings demonstrating TMA were all indicative of CM-HUS (figure 5). The diagnosis was reinforced by negative PCR testing for STEC and the patient’s swift recovery upon initiation of eculizumab therapy. Her unremarkable prior medical history does not support a pre-existing complement disorder necessitating a 'second hit,' thus favoring infection-related CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is a clinicopathological diagnosis made by exclusion, and patients may exhibit normal complement (C3/C4) concentrations at various points during illness. It is crucial to recognize that CM-HUS cannot be ruled out by normal C3 or C4 values, since these may stay within normal limits throughout the disease. In this case, the onset of haemolysis only occurred as the patient’s sepsis improved, indicating a likely infection-induced CM-HUS rather than a chronic or pre-existing disorder.

For this patient, initial measurements of complement (C3/C4) and ADAMTS13 activity were taken directly prior to initiating the first PEX session. Further, an extended complement panel was collected eight days following the last PEX and four days subsequent to administering the first eculizumab dose. The detected decrease in alternative pathway activity aligns most strongly with the impact of therapies, instead of reflecting inherent complement activation or depletion.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

Complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS), previously known as 'atypical HUS,' is associated with uncontrolled activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently occurring in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50%–70% of affected patients possess genetic alterations or autoantibodies that impair complement regulatory proteins, including factors H, I, and B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nonetheless, the penetrance of these genetic changes is incomplete; clinical disease usually appears only following an external trigger, such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or specific drugs.

While uncommon, C. canimorsus can activate complement pathways, especially in vulnerable populations. Of note, C. canimorsus strains vary in their susceptibility to serum complement; most are sensitive, but certain encapsulated strains demonstrate partial resistance, highlighting the role of complement activity in host defense. This presents a clinical dilemma: activation of complement helps eradicate the infection but may also provoke unregulated endothelial injury and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) in susceptible individuals.

The present case adds to the limited, but growing, evidence associating C. canimorsus with complement-mediated TMAs. Bjørkto et al reported a particularly instructive case in which a patient with CM-HUS triggered by C. canimorsus carried a complement factor B mutation and responded favorably to eculizumab. Differing from this prior report, our patient did not exhibit any pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement-related genes, lending support to infection-provoked CM-HUS in the absence of an identifiable inherited complement defect.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amidst ongoing infection entails distinct difficulties. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody directed against terminal complement component 5, is established as the preferred treatment for CM-HUS, significantly enhancing haematological and renal prognoses, particularly when administered promptly. Initiating therapy late correlates with suboptimal renal restoration and, as a result, increased likelihood of progression to end-stage kidney disease.

By comparison, plasma exchange (PEX) is crucial for survival in TTP, yet only offers short-term advantages for CM-HUS by eliminating circulating complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies, without addressing the fundamental complement system abnormality. For our patient, diuresis was observed following the concluding PEX sessions but preceding eculizumab administration, indicating only brief clinical improvement. Long-term haematological and renal recovery was achieved exclusively after complement inhibition.

A prominent therapeutic concern involves implementing complement blockade in the setting of ongoing bacteraemia, as terminal complement elements are vital for protection against encapsulated bacteria—such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga—thus raising the possibility of increased infection risk linked to eculizumab. Here, eculizumab therapy was initiated subsequent to the clearance of blood cultures, thereby reducing the danger of persistent infection. Preventive measures, including vaccination, antimicrobial prophylaxis, and vigilant observation, are imperative when complement inhibitors are employed.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely detection of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition represent key factors associated with better outcomes. The patient discussed here attained haematological remission and partial recovery of kidney function, aligning with available data that indicate renal status and interval to eculizumab initiation are primary prognostic indicators.

While cases of CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus are exceptionally uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of evaluating infectious causes—including atypical pathogens—in individuals presenting with new-onset TMA. Increased clinical vigilance and advances in molecular diagnostic techniques may facilitate recognition of additional cases.
