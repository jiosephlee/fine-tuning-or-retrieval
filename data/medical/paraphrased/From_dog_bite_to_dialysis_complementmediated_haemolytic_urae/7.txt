From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health conditions presented with swiftly advancing sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. Her illness was marked by disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, eventually leading to complete anuria and dependence on dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although she experienced temporary clinical improvement with antibiotics and supportive interventions, she subsequently developed acute isolated haemolysis and ongoing thrombocytopaenia. Investigations, including laboratory tests and a renal biopsy, revealed a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), resulting in a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). With plasma exchange and the administration of eculizumab, she attained haematological remission and her renal function partially recovered. This report underscores the rarity of C. canimorsus as an inciting factor for CM-HUS in immunocompetent adults and details the patient’s diagnostic process, the complexities inherent in distinguishing between differential diagnoses, and the challenges presented by the management and therapeutic strategies required for TMA secondary to sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and constitutes part of the normal oropharyngeal microbiota in dogs and cats. Although infections in humans are uncommon, transmission can result from animal bites, scratches, or even close physical proximity. The disease spectrum is broad, including isolated cases of meningitis as well as rapidly progressing sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiorgan dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Mortality rates may be as high as 30% despite general sensitivity to antibiotics.

Among Capnocytophaga species, C. canimorsus is most commonly implicated in serious zoonotic infections affecting humans. Owing to its demanding growth requirements and slow propagation in standard cultures, identification is frequently delayed, increasingly necessitating the use of molecular diagnostic approaches like real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the case described, infection with C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock, marked leukopaenia, and a coagulopathic profile indicative of DIC. The initial phase was dominated by sepsis-associated multiorgan failure; however, subsequent onset of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and deteriorating acute kidney injury (AKI) indicated the development of a concurrent TMA. This progression underscores how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can remain undetected during the acute stages characterized by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s, who had no significant past medical history and had previously experienced two uncomplicated pregnancies, arrived at the emergency department due to a rapidly deteriorating health status. Four days before her hospitalization, she sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and subsequently received a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Although she was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) on the day of the bite, she was unable to obtain her medication and thus did not begin antibiotic therapy. She soon developed a high fever (exceeding 40 °C), general malaise, decreased oral intake, and only sought care after experiencing numbness and discoloration in her feet. At the initial evaluation in primary-care emergency services, she was discovered to be hypoglycaemic, unresponsive to both oral glucose and glucagon therapy, with her lowest recorded blood glucose at 0.6 mmol/L. This clinical scenario led to her urgent referral to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and able to engage appropriately, but exhibited clinical features of sepsis, including mottled and cool lower limbs. There was no history of herbal remedy or substance use, and there were no reports of similar symptoms among family members. The initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated significant multisystem involvement, characterized by profound inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Analysis of arterial blood gases indicated a metabolic acidosis, with a measured pH of 7.27 (reference range: 7.35–7.43) and a raised lactate concentration at 5.7 mmol/L (normal <2.5 mmol/L). The patient experienced persistent hypoglycaemia, refractory to substantial intravenous glucose administration, presumed secondary to sepsis. On presentation, urine output was preserved; urinalysis detected 2+ proteinuria and 3+ hematuria.

In light of her clinical features—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy accompanied by acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—her presentation was attributed to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) arising from bacteraemia and the initial stages of sepsis, as indicated by a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. A surgical etiology for the mottled appearance of her extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded following comprehensive clinical and imaging assessment. Due to the critical nature of her presentation, empirical broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to address possible canine-related pathogens, including Capnocytophaga species. Importantly, C. canimorsus infection was suspected early, and subsequent blood cultures, both aerobic and anaerobic, confirmed its presence seven days later. She was then admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced observation.

Hematology specialists were involved due to her pancytopenia and coagulopathy. The working differential diagnosis included acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, with haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) also contemplated but deemed improbable owing to leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and a lack of compensatory reticulocytosis. Flow cytometry was undertaken and bone marrow biopsy was considered; however, unremarkable results, showing no increase in CD34+ blasts or atypical phenotypes, obviated the need for biopsy. Clinically, her symptomatology aligned with a diagnosis of sepsis. While diagnostic evaluations were pending, she received supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen.

Upon admission to the ICU, the patient experienced complete cessation of urine output and a marked decline in renal function. Repeated efforts at aggressive fluid resuscitation combined with loop diuretics yielded no improvement. Subsequently, she progressed to septic cardiomyopathy accompanied by volume overload, necessitating the commencement of continuous renal replacement therapy. Screening for autoimmune markers, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), was negative.

By the third day of ICU admission, the patient manifested widespread cutaneous bleeding characterized by ecchymoses, along with cyanotic and ischaemic changes of the distal extremities, most notably affecting several toes and fingers. Despite these changes, peripheral pulses and sensation remained intact. These manifestations were indicative of microthrombosis in the setting of DIC. Although she experienced dysfunction of multiple organ systems, her hemodynamic status did not necessitate vasopressor support apart from the initial administration of stress-dose corticosteroids during septic shock. Management also included the infusion of albumin and transfusion of other blood components.

Following 8 days of inpatient care, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit for continued management of dialysis-dependent AKI and administration of intravenous antibiotics. At the time of transfer, she was hemodynamically stable but remained anuric, displayed widespread oedema and multiple haematomas, though she was alert and fully oriented. Antimicrobial therapy was adjusted to intravenous ampicillin, and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin in accordance with blood culture findings.

During her stay in the nephrology unit, laboratory evaluation revealed a downward trend in inflammatory markers, a reactive leukocytosis, recovery of platelet counts, and stable haemoglobin concentrations. Renal function tests, however, continued to show elevated azotaemic markers. Coagulation studies returned to normal, pointing towards resolution of her coagulopathy. While initial progress was promising, her clinical status shifted as she experienced an acute, isolated decline in haemoglobin (from 111 to 68 g/L over a 6-day interval) without clinical or laboratory evidence of bleeding or haemodynamic compromise. Her platelet count remained significantly depressed (30–40×10⁹/L); haptoglobin was undetectable and LDH became elevated, all consistent with ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

The direct antiglobulin test (DAT), also known as the Coombs test, returned positive for IgG, prompting consideration of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. The patient was commenced on high-dose prednisolone, in addition to standard prophylactic measures, and received multiple blood transfusions over a two-day period. Ongoing haemolysis unresponsive to corticosteroid therapy, combined with persistent thrombocytopenia (platelet counts 41–46 × 10⁹/L), necessitated further diagnostic investigation. Examination of a peripheral blood smear showed 15%–20% schistocytes, indicating MAHA in line with a diagnosis of TMA (see figure 2). The initial positive DAT result was subsequently attributed to possible interference from penicillin therapy or previous transfusions, as a repeat DAT was negative.

Complement pathway evaluation and ADAMTS13 activity measurements were performed. Conventional complement assays (C3/C4) available locally were within normal parameters, and functional assessments of the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways also yielded normal results. ADAMTS13 activity was subsequently measured at 43.5%, which is not consistent with thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as TTP is generally defined by activity below 10%. While awaiting confirmation of these findings, plasma exchange (PEX) was started as interim management before administration of the initial eculizumab dose.

After the thrombocyte count rose to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed pronounced acute tubular necrosis, glomerular ischemia marked by fibrin thrombi, and focal crescent formation—all indicative of TMA.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was excluded based on a negative PCR analysis from the stool sample.

After the patient underwent the second and third PEX treatments, spontaneous diuresis resumed. Subsequent to administration of initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B)—induction therapy with eculizumab was started at 900 mg intravenously once weekly for a total of four weeks. The follow-up doses of the vaccines were scheduled for 8 weeks later in the outpatient setting. Because the vaccines were not given at least 2 weeks prior to beginning complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was administered.

Comprehensive complement analysis was requested from the Skåne Laboratory (Lund University, Sweden) to exclude hereditary complement-mediated TMA. To accommodate logistical factors and ensure patient safety, three courses of PEX with Octaplas and a single induction dose of eculizumab were completed before laboratory samples were drawn. The functional complement evaluation included measurement of alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which revealed a significant reduction (6%), while levels of complement factors H, I, and B were within normal limits, and tests for antifactor H antibodies were negative.

Outcome and follow-up:

After undergoing intensive medical treatment, the patient currently exhibits stable overall health and satisfactory urine production. Despite this, renal function remains compromised, with a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L, necessitating ongoing follow-up in the nephrology outpatient service (see figure 4). Maintenance therapy with eculizumab (1200 mg intravenously every two weeks) has been sustained for 4 months following induction. Comprehensive genetic analysis of the complement pathway revealed no disease-causing mutations; this outcome was relayed orally by the reference laboratory, with a formal written report still awaited at the time of writing. In light of the disease’s severity, complement inhibition therapy will continue, with future consideration for cessation if kidney function stabilizes. Discovery of pathogenic complement gene variants would typically indicate the need for extended treatment duration and vigilant relapse monitoring; however, no such mutations were present in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs represent a group of closely related clinical conditions.

When adults present with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost task is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is specifically characterized by a marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). While laboratory confirmation is required for diagnosis, clinical tools such as the PLASMIC score can assist with initial assessment. In this case, the patient’s PLASMIC score was 4/7, suggesting a low likelihood of TTP, and her ADAMTS13 activity was measured at 43.5%, further decreasing suspicion for TTP and informing subsequent diagnostic decisions.

Another major differential is STEC-HUS. Since the patient’s PCR for Shiga toxin was negative and there were no antecedent gastrointestinal symptoms or positive microbiological findings, the evidence was against a diagnosis of STEC-HUS.

In conclusion, ongoing hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, dialysis-requiring AKI, and TMA-consistent kidney biopsy findings indicated CM-HUS (figure 5). This diagnosis was corroborated both by PCR exclusion of STEC and by marked improvement after starting eculizumab. The lack of significant prior health issues reduces the probability of an underlying complement abnormality and instead suggests that an infection may have precipitated her CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is identified as a clinicopathological diagnosis of exclusion, and the complement proteins C3 and C4 can remain within normal limits throughout the disease progression. Significantly, the presence of normal C3/C4 levels does not rule out CM-HUS, as these markers may not deviate from the reference range at any point during the illness. Moreover, in this case, hemolysis became evident only after sepsis began to resolve, supporting the hypothesis that the CM-HUS was triggered by infection rather than being a pre-existing condition.

For our patient, initial assessment of complement components (C3/C4) and measurement of ADAMTS13 activity were carried out just prior to administering the first PEX treatment. Additional complement analyses were conducted eight days following the final PEX procedure and four days post-initiation of eculizumab therapy. The decline observed in the activity of the alternative complement pathway most likely reflects the effects of therapy, rather than indicating an underlying deficiency or activation of complement.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS (previously referred to as ‘atypical HUS’) results from aberrant activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50–70% of affected patients carry either genetic alterations or autoantibodies that interfere with the regulation of complement proteins such as factors H, I, and B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nevertheless, the development of clinical symptoms generally requires an additional environmental insult, with infections, pregnancy, autoimmune conditions, or certain pharmacologic agents representing the most frequent precipitants, owing to incomplete disease penetrance.

C. canimorsus, although only sporadically documented, possesses the capability to stimulate complement pathways, particularly in immunologically vulnerable individuals. Interestingly, the degree of complement sensitivity among C. canimorsus strains varies: while most isolates are complement sensitive, a subset that expresses a capsule demonstrates partial resistance. This underlines the crucial role of complement-mediated mechanisms in the host’s defense against the organism. However, this also presents a clinical dilemma—while the activation of the complement system facilitates bacterial clearance, in predisposed persons it may also precipitate unchecked endothelial injury and TMA.

The present case contributes further evidence to the association between C. canimorsus infection and complement-mediated TMAs, which remains infrequently described in the literature. A particularly notable case by Bjørkto et al. detailed an individual who developed CM-HUS after C. canimorsus exposure, who was found to carry a complement factor B mutation and responded to eculizumab therapy. Unlike that report, our patient exhibited no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in complement-related genes, thereby supporting the possibility of infection-driven CM-HUS even in the absence of an identifiable inherited defect in complement regulation.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS in the setting of ongoing infection is particularly complex. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5, represents the standard therapeutic approach for CM-HUS and significantly enhances both haematological and renal outcomes, especially with prompt administration. When initiation of eculizumab is delayed, there is a greater likelihood of suboptimal renal recovery and increased risk of advancement to end-stage kidney disease.

While plasma exchange (PEX) is a crucial and often lifesaving intervention in TTP, it offers only short-term improvement in CM-HUS. PEX primarily acts by removing soluble complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies from the circulation, but does not address the primary defect in the complement cascade. In the case discussed, restoration of diuresis followed the final sessions of PEX but preceded the start of eculizumab, indicating only a brief period of stabilization. Durable recovery of both haematological and renal function was achieved only following initiation of complement blockade.

An important clinical challenge is the decision to introduce complement inhibition in the presence of active bacteraemia, given the essential role of terminal complement proteins in host defense against encapsulated pathogens such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga. This increases the concern regarding infection-related complications associated with eculizumab. Here, eculizumab treatment commenced after confirmation of negative blood cultures, thereby reducing the likelihood of unrestrained infection. The use of complement inhibitors necessitates ongoing preventive strategies, including appropriate vaccination, prophylactic antimicrobials, and vigilant patient monitoring.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely diagnosis of CM-HUS and rapid commencement of complement inhibition represent the key factors associated with positive patient outcomes. In this case, the patient attained haematological remission along with partial restoration of kidney function, aligning with existing literature that identifies baseline renal status and the interval before initiation of eculizumab as critical prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to account for infectious precipitants—including rare etiologies—when evaluating patients with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance combined with advanced molecular diagnostic approaches may facilitate the recognition of additional instances of this disease association.
