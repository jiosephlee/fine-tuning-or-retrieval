From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: An otherwise healthy woman in her late 40s experienced rapidly escalating sepsis four days subsequent to sustaining a dog bite. Her clinical condition worsened with the onset of disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, leading ultimately to total anuria and the need for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteraemia. Although initial treatment with antibiotics and supportive measures resulted in transient clinical stabilization, she soon developed acute isolated haemolysis and sustained thrombocytopaenia. Subsequent laboratory analyses and a renal biopsy demonstrated the presence of thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), establishing a diagnosis of complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS). The patient achieved haematological remission and partial restoration of renal function after receiving plasma exchange and eculizumab therapy. This case underscores the potential for C. canimorsus infection to precipitate CM-HUS even in immunocompetent adults, and it discusses the patient’s diagnostic journey, challenges associated with differentiating similar conditions, and the intricacies involved in managing TMA arising in the context of sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus characterized by slow growth, commonly residing as part of the normal oropharyngeal microbiota of dogs and cats. Although human infection is uncommon, it can be transmitted through animal bites, scratches, or even close physical contact. The clinical spectrum is broad, including presentations such as isolated meningitis, fulminant sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), multiple organ dysfunction, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Even with general sensitivity to antibiotics, the associated mortality rate can be as high as 30%.

Among the Capnocytophaga genus, C. canimorsus is most commonly implicated in serious zoonotic infections affecting humans. Diagnosis is often hampered by its fastidious growth requirements and slow culture kinetics, leading to an increasing reliance on molecular diagnostics, particularly real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the case described, the infection by C. canimorsus manifested as septic shock, pronounced leukopaenia, and coagulopathy compatible with DIC. While sepsis and subsequent multiorgan impairment initially dominated the clinical course, progression to microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), thrombocytopenia, and worsening acute kidney injury (AKI) was later observed. This constellation of findings points to the superimposition of TMA, illustrating how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) may initially be masked during the early stages of sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two previous uneventful pregnancies presented to the emergency department following a rapid deterioration in her health status. Four days before hospital admission, she sustained a dog bite to her upper lip and subsequently received a tetanus vaccine for prophylaxis. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed on the day of the injury, she did not start the antibiotic, as she was unable to retrieve the medication. She subsequently developed a high fever (>40 °C), general malaise, and poor oral intake. Her condition escalated, prompting her to seek medical help when she noticed numbness and discoloration in her feet. During assessment at the primary-care emergency facility, she was found to be hypoglycemic, with levels unresponsive to oral glucose or glucagon, reaching a nadir of 0.6 mmol/L. This led to her emergent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon admission, the patient was conscious and oriented, though demonstrated clinical signs of sepsis, such as mottled and cool lower limbs. She denied the use of herbal medicines or other substances, and there was no history of similar illness among family members. The initial set of laboratory tests indicated significant multisystem involvement, including marked inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated hepatic enzymes, and coagulopathy. Arterial blood gas assessment showed metabolic acidosis, with a pH of 7.27 (reference: 7.35–7.43), accompanied by a raised lactate concentration of 5.7 (<2.5 mmol/L). She persisted with hypoglycaemia despite administration of high-dose intravenous glucose, attributed to underlying sepsis. On admission, urine output was preserved; urinalysis revealed 2+ proteinuria and 3+ haematuria.

Given her range of clinical findings—namely fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy alongside acute kidney injury (AKI) and lactic acidosis—her case was considered to represent disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and the early stages of sepsis, as indicated by a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1/3. Surgical etiologies for her mottled limbs, including necrotising fasciitis, were excluded following a comprehensive clinical examination and imaging studies. Because of the acuity of her illness, empiric broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was promptly initiated to target pathogens commonly associated with dog exposure, such as Capnocytophaga species. Capnocytophaga canimorsus was considered a probable pathogen from the beginning and its presence was confirmed a week later by both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was then transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) for intensified monitoring.

In light of her pancytopenia and coagulopathic features, haematology input was sought. The working differential included possibilities such as acute leukaemia and haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Although haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was contemplated, it was deemed improbable due to the presence of leukopenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin, and reticulocyte counts that did not suggest compensatory activity. Flow cytometry was conducted and a bone marrow biopsy was initially scheduled; however, following the finding of normal flow cytometry results—specifically, an absence of increased CD34+ blasts or atypical cell populations—the biopsy was not pursued. The clinical impression supported a diagnosis of sepsis. Pending further diagnostic clarification, supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were administered.

Following her admission to the ICU, the patient experienced complete anuria accompanied by a swift decline in renal function. Efforts at vigorous fluid administration combined with loop diuretics failed to yield improvement. Soon thereafter, she manifested signs of septic cardiomyopathy along with significant fluid overload, necessitating continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune screening, including antinuclear antibodies (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), returned negative results.

By the third ICU day, she exhibited widespread cutaneous bleeding, ecchymoses, and distal cyanotic as well as ischaemic alterations involving several fingers and toes; however, peripheral pulses and sensory function remained intact. These clinical features suggested the presence of microvascular thromboses in association with DIC. Although her illness affected multiple organ systems, vasopressor therapy was never needed apart from the stress-dose corticosteroids administered during her initial septic period. Additionally, she was treated with albumin and other blood component transfusions.

Eight days following admission, the patient was moved to the nephrology unit for continued management of dialysis-dependent AKI and administration of intravenous antibiotics. Upon arrival at the ward, she was haemodynamically stable but remained anuric, presented with widespread oedema and numerous haematomas, and was fully conscious and oriented. Based on results from blood cultures, the antibiotic regimen was switched to intravenous ampicillin, and subsequently transitioned to oral Apocillin.

Within the nephrology service, laboratory assessments indicated a downtrending inflammatory response, presence of reactive leukocytosis, platelet count recovery, and stable levels of haemoglobin. Renal function parameters demonstrated ongoing azotaemia, while coagulation studies normalized, suggestive of resolving coagulopathy. However, following initial clinical progress, she experienced a sudden, isolated decline in haemoglobin concentration (from 111 to 68 g/L over a six-day period) despite the absence of overt bleeding or haemodynamic compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently reduced (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin became undetectable, and there was a rise in LDH, all of which pointed toward persistent haemolysis (see figure 1).

A positive direct antiglobulin test (DAT) (Coombs test) for IgG suggested a diagnosis of autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. The patient was started on high-dose prednisolone and standard prophylactic measures, and received multiple blood transfusions over a span of 2 days. Due to persistent haemolysis despite corticosteroid administration and continued thrombocytopenia (platelet count 41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional diagnostic steps were undertaken. Examination of the peripheral blood smear identified 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of MAHA and raising suspicion for TMA (figure 2). The initial DAT result was later deemed a probable false positive, potentially attributable to penicillin exposure or recent transfusions; subsequent DAT was negative.

Complement assessments and ADAMTS13 activity measurements were requested. Results from standard complement testing (C3/C4) and functional assays for the classical, alternative, and lectin pathways were unremarkable. The ADAMTS13 activity returned at 43.5%, which essentially excluded thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), as this disorder typically demonstrates activity under 10%. Pending the completion of these investigations, plasma exchange (PEX) was initiated as interim management prior to administration of the first eculizumab dose.

Following an increase in the thrombocyte count to 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, revealing marked acute tubular necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi, and occasional crescent formation—findings indicative of TMA.

The most illustrative diagnostic evidence in this instance was provided by the light microscopy appearance depicted in figure 3. Immunofluorescence and electron microscopy data are not included here, as immunofluorescence did not detect any immune complex deposition, and electron microscopy failed to show features suggestive of a primary glomerulopathy.

A diagnosis of Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated HUS (STEC-HUS) was excluded due to a negative stool PCR test.

After the second and third sessions of PEX, the patient began to produce urine spontaneously once more. Eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg administered intravenously weekly for four weeks) was started following administration of the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (covering serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (targeting serogroup B). Plans were made to give the subsequent vaccine doses eight weeks later in an outpatient setting. As the vaccines were not given two weeks prior to the onset of complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

Comprehensive complement analyses to exclude hereditary complement-mediated TMA were submitted to the Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. To ensure both logistical efficiency and patient safety, the patient underwent three PEX treatments with Octaplas and received a single induction dose of eculizumab before samples were obtained. Functional testing assessed alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50), which was found to be significantly decreased (6%), although levels of complement factors H, I, and B remained within normal ranges, and no antifactor H antibodies were detected.

Outcome and follow-up:

After the intensive medical intervention, the patient remains clinically stable and displays satisfactory urine output. Her renal function is still impaired, as indicated by a serum creatinine of 175 µmol/L, necessitating ongoing surveillance in the nephrology outpatient service (see figure 4). She has been receiving maintenance therapy with eculizumab (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks) for four months following initial treatment. Comprehensive genetic analysis of complement pathway genes did not reveal any disease-causing mutations; these preliminary results were shared verbally by the reference laboratory, with the official report not yet available at the time of writing. Given these findings, and considering the considerable severity of her presentation, complement inhibition therapy is being sustained, with plans to reevaluate cessation should renal function stabilize. In general, detection of pathogenic complement gene mutations would support prolonged therapy with diligent monitoring for recurrence. In this case, however, no such variants were found.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs represent a spectrum of clinical syndromes with overlapping features.

When an adult presents with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost concern is ruling out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by marked ADAMTS13 deficiency (<10%). To aid decision-making before laboratory confirmation, scoring systems such as PLASMIC are utilized; however, definitive diagnosis depends on lab findings. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7 (indicating low pretest probability for TTP) and ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, rendering TTP improbable and directing clinicians to pursue alternative causes.

Another critical differential is STEC-HUS. The patient's PCR was negative for Shiga toxin. Along with the lack of gastrointestinal symptoms, these results make STEC-HUS unlikely.

Given the combination of ongoing haemolysis, presence of schistocytes, AKI necessitating dialysis, and renal biopsy findings indicative of TMA, the diagnosis of CM-HUS was established (figure 5). This conclusion was further substantiated by the negative Shiga toxin PCR and marked improvement with eculizumab therapy. Additionally, the patient's previously unremarkable health history diminishes the likelihood of a congenital complement defect requiring an additional insult, thereby supporting the interpretation of infection-associated CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is established as a clinicopathological diagnosis by excluding other potential causes, and it is important to note that complement levels (C3/C4) can remain within normal limits throughout the course of the disease. Crucially, normal values for C3 and C4 do not rule out CM-HUS, as these markers may persist in the normal range during illness. In this case, the onset of haemolysis coincided with the improvement of sepsis, which supports a diagnosis of infection-induced CM-HUS, rather than an underlying, previously existing disorder.

For our patient, standard complement measurements (C3/C4) along with ADAMTS13 activity were assessed immediately prior to the initiation of the first PEX session. More comprehensive complement profiling was conducted 8 days after the final PEX procedure and 4 days subsequent to the administration of the initial dose of eculizumab. The observed decrease in alternative pathway activity aligns most closely with the impact of therapeutic interventions, rather than being attributed to innate complement deficiency or ongoing activation.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS, formerly known as ‘atypical HUS’, is associated with improper regulation of the alternative complement pathway, typically in individuals with a genetic susceptibility. Approximately 50% to 70% of patients exhibit genetic mutations or possess autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory proteins such as factors H, I, and B, or the membrane cofactor protein (CD46). Nonetheless, disease expression remains incomplete, as the clinical phenotype often necessitates an environmental trigger, including infections, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or exposure to specific medications.

While infrequently documented, *C. canimorsus* has the capability to stimulate complement pathways, especially in vulnerable individuals. It is notable that *C. canimorsus* demonstrates varying degrees of sensitivity to serum complement; although most strains are complement-sensitive, some encapsulated variants exhibit partial resistance, highlighting the significance of complement-mediated immunity in combating the bacterium. This scenario presents a clinical dilemma: while activation of the complement system is vital for eradicating the pathogen, it can concurrently precipitate unrestrained endothelial injury and TMA in patients who are predisposed.

The present case adds to the limited but increasing number of reports implicating *C. canimorsus* in the pathogenesis of complement-mediated TMAs. An illustrative example by Bjørkto et al. involved a patient who developed CM-HUS due to *C. canimorsus*, with an underlying complement factor B mutation and successful therapeutic response to eculizumab. In contrast to this previously described case, our patient did not possess any identified pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene mutations, thereby supporting the occurrence of infection-induced CM-HUS even in the absence of recognizable inherited complement abnormalities.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS amid ongoing infection is particularly complex. Eculizumab—a monoclonal antibody that inhibits terminal complement component 5—constitutes the gold standard for CM-HUS treatment and substantially enhances both haematological and renal outcomes, especially when administered promptly. Initiation of therapy after a delay correlates with suboptimal renal recovery, elevating the probability of advancing to end-stage kidney disease.

By contrast, while PEX is vital for survival in TTP, it affords only limited, short-term improvement in CM-HUS. This benefit results from clearing circulating complement fragments, inflammatory factors, or autoantibodies, but does not resolve the core complement dysregulation. In the presented case, the return of diuresis followed the completion of PEX but preceded eculizumab therapy, indicative of only fleeting stabilisation. Prolonged improvement in haematological parameters and renal function was achieved solely after initiation of complement inhibition.

A significant therapeutic challenge arises regarding the deployment of complement blockade in the setting of concurrent bacteraemia, since terminal complement components play an essential role in host defence against encapsulated pathogens such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga. This heightens apprehension concerning infection risks associated with eculizumab. In this instance, eculizumab therapy commenced only after confirmation of negative blood cultures, thereby reducing the likelihood of uncontained infection. Nonetheless, strategies such as immunisation, use of prophylactic antimicrobials, and vigilant follow-up are mandatory when complement inhibitors are employed.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and swift commencement of complement inhibition therapy are critical factors associated with better patient outcomes. In this case, the patient attained haematological remission alongside partial restoration of kidney function, reflecting existing data that underscore renal status and the speed of eculizumab administration as principal prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus infection is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the necessity of accounting for infectious etiologies—including rare pathogens—when evaluating patients with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced clinical vigilance and advances in molecular diagnostic tools may facilitate the recognition of additional similar cases.
