Title: From dog bite to dialysis: complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome

Tobias Evason Fredriksen, Burhan-Ud-Din Mian, Szilveszter Dolgos

Abstract:

Summary: A woman in her late 40s with no prior health issues presented with rapidly worsening sepsis four days after sustaining a dog bite. She subsequently developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, pancytopenia, and acute kidney injury, which led to total anuria and the need for dialysis. Blood cultures identified Capnocytophaga canimorsus bacteremia. Although she initially showed signs of improvement with antibiotic therapy and supportive management, she later experienced abrupt onset of isolated hemolysis along with persistent thrombocytopenia. Laboratory evaluation and findings on renal biopsy substantiated the diagnosis of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), specifically complement-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome (CM-HUS). Hematologic remission was achieved following plasma exchange and treatment with eculizumab, with partial recovery of renal function. This report emphasizes C. canimorsus infection as an uncommon precipitant of CM-HUS in otherwise healthy adults and details the diagnostic evaluation, difficulties in differentiating causes, and the intricacies of managing TMA after sepsis.

Background:

Capnocytophaga canimorsus is an encapsulated, gram-negative bacillus that grows slowly and is commonly present in the oropharyngeal flora of dogs and cats. Although infections in humans are uncommon, transmission may happen through bites, scratches, or even close interaction with animals. The spectrum of clinical manifestations is wide, encompassing isolated meningitis, rapidly progressive sepsis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), failure of multiple organ systems, and thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA). Mortality rates may reach up to 30%, even though the organism is susceptible to a broad range of antibiotics.

Among the Capnocytophaga genus, C. canimorsus is most often implicated in severe zoonotic infections in humans. Due to its demanding growth requirements and slow culture development, diagnosis is frequently delayed, leading to greater reliance on molecular diagnostic techniques, such as real-time nanopore sequencing.

In the present case, infection with C. canimorsus manifested initially with septic shock, profound leukopaenia, and coagulopathy in keeping with DIC. The early phase of illness was marked by extensive organ involvement related to sepsis, but subsequent emergence of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA), reduced platelet count, and deterioration in renal function indicated concurrent TMA. This underscores how complement-mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM-HUS) can be concealed during the initial period dominated by sepsis and DIC.

Case presentation:

A woman in her late 40s with no significant past medical history and two prior normal pregnancies arrived at the emergency department with an acutely deteriorating clinical status. Four days before her admission, her dog had bitten her upper lip, for which she received a tetanus vaccine as preventative care. Although phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) was prescribed the same day, she did not start the antibiotics because she was unable to pick up the medication. Subsequently, she developed a high fever (>40 °C), generalized malaise, decreased oral intake, and later developed numbness and discoloration of her feet, prompting her to seek healthcare. In primary care emergency, she was found to have hypoglycemia that did not respond to oral glucose or glucagon, with her lowest recorded glucose being 0.6 mmol/L, leading to her urgent transfer to our emergency department.

Upon arrival, the patient was conscious and interacted appropriately, though she exhibited signs consistent with sepsis, including mottled and cool skin on both lower limbs. She denied the use of herbal products or other substances, and there was no history of illness or similar symptoms among family members. Initial laboratory evaluations showed profound systemic disruption, characterized by intense inflammation, pancytopenia, acute kidney injury (AKI), elevated liver enzymes, and evidence of coagulopathy. Table 1 provides a summary of notable laboratory findings.

Arterial blood gases demonstrated metabolic acidosis with a pH of 7.27 (reference range 7.35–7.43) and a lactate concentration of 5.7 mmol/L (reference <2.5 mmol/L). Despite receiving substantial intravenous glucose, the patient continued to experience hypoglycemia, attributed to her septic state. At presentation, urine output was adequate, with urinalysis revealing proteinuria at 2+ and hematuria at 3+.

Her collection of clinical findings—including fever, pancytopenia, coagulopathy, acute kidney injury (AKI), and lactic acidosis—was interpreted as disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis, as reflected by a quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (qSOFA) score of 1 out of 3. A potential surgical etiology for her mottled extremities, such as necrotising fasciitis, was excluded following thorough clinical assessment and imaging studies. In light of the seriousness of her symptoms, broad-spectrum intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (Tazocin) was initiated to provide coverage for dog-related pathogens, including Capnocytophaga species. C. canimorsus was suspected early in the clinical course and was confirmed seven days later through both aerobic and anaerobic blood cultures. She was subsequently moved to the intensive care unit (ICU) for enhanced observation.

Due to the presence of pancytopenia and coagulopathy, haematology was consulted for further evaluation. The differential diagnosis encompassed acute leukaemia as well as haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis. Haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS) was considered; however, it was deemed less likely given the patient's leukopaenia, elevated inflammatory markers, normal haptoglobin levels, and a lack of compensatory reticulocyte response. Flow cytometric analysis was performed, with plans for a bone marrow biopsy; however, findings were within normal limits—including no increase in CD34+ blasts or abnormal immunophenotypes—prompting cancellation of the biopsy. The clinical scenario aligned with sepsis. Supportive transfusions of platelets and fibrinogen were administered while awaiting definitive diagnostic results.

Following admission to the ICU, the patient became entirely anuric, with her renal function deteriorating swiftly. Despite vigorous intravenous fluid administration and the use of loop diuretics, there was no improvement. She later experienced fluid overload secondary to septic cardiomyopathy, necessitating the start of continuous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune investigations, including antinuclear antibody (ANA) and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA) testing, yielded negative results.

By the third day of her ICU stay, the patient manifested widespread cutaneous bleeding with ecchymoses, along with distal cyanosis and ischemic changes involving multiple digits on both hands and feet, but peripheral pulses and sensation remained intact. These clinical signs were interpreted as evidence of microvascular thrombosis associated with DIC. Despite the extent of organ dysfunction, she never needed vasopressors aside from the stress-dose steroids given in the early stage of sepsis. Additionally, she was treated with albumin infusions and received other blood product support.

Following an 8-day hospital stay, the patient was moved to the nephrology service to continue management of dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) and intravenous antibiotic therapy. Upon arrival, she was haemodynamically stable but remained anuric, with diffuse oedema and several haematomas, although she was awake and fully oriented. Based on blood culture results, her antibiotic regimen was stepped down to intravenous ampicillin and subsequently switched to oral Apocillin.

In the nephrology unit, laboratory analyses revealed a trend toward decreased inflammatory markers, persistent yet reactive leukocytosis, platelet recovery, and stable haemoglobin concentrations. Renal function tests showed ongoing azotaemia. Coagulation studies normalised, suggesting resolution of coagulopathy. Her laboratory findings at this stage are detailed in Table 2.

Despite initial progress, her condition evolved as she experienced an abrupt and isolated reduction in haemoglobin from 111 to 68 g/L over a period of 6 days, without any signs of active bleeding or circulatory compromise. Platelet counts remained consistently low (30–40×10⁹/L), haptoglobin became undetectable, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) increased, all consistent with ongoing haemolysis (see figure 1).

A direct antiglobulin test (DAT), or Coombs test, yielded a positive result for IgG, leading to concern for autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. The patient was started on high-dose prednisolone with standard prophylactic measures, and multiple blood transfusions were administered over a 2-day period. Due to continued haemolysis despite corticosteroids and persistent thrombocytopenia (platelet count 41–46 × 10⁹/L), additional work-up was necessary. Review of the peripheral blood smear demonstrated 15%–20% schistocytes, indicative of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia (MAHA) and raising suspicion for thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) (figure 2). The initial DAT was probably a false positive caused by exposure to penicillin or previous transfusions; a repeat DAT returned negative.

Complement assays and ADAMTS13 activity levels were requested. Standard complement measurements available at the facility (C3 and C4) were unremarkable, and functional assessments of the classical, alternative, and lectin complement pathways were also within normal parameters. The ADAMTS13 activity result became available soon after, measuring 43.5%, thus excluding thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), which is typically characterized by levels below 10%. During the interim while awaiting these diagnostics, plasma exchange (PEX) was begun as a temporizing measure before administration of the initial dose of eculizumab.

After the platelet count reached 77×10⁹/L, a renal biopsy was conducted, which revealed pronounced acute tubular necrosis, glomeruli exhibiting ischaemia with the presence of fibrin thrombi, and areas of crescent formation, findings compatible with thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA).

Light microscopy provided the most illustrative diagnostic features in this patient, as depicted in figure 3. Immunofluorescence images and electron microscopy data are not presented; immunofluorescence detected no immune complex deposition, while electron microscopy showed no indication of an underlying primary glomerular disease.

Shiga toxin-producing E. coli-associated hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS) was excluded due to a negative stool PCR test.

After the second and third sessions of plasma exchange (PEX), the patient's spontaneous urine output resumed. Eculizumab induction therapy (900 mg IV weekly for 4 weeks) was begun following administration of the initial doses of meningococcal vaccines—Menveo (targeting serogroups A, C, Y, and W) and Bexsero (for serogroup B). Plans were made for the second vaccine doses to be given eight weeks later in the outpatient setting. As the vaccines had not been provided at least two weeks prior to initiating complement inhibition, prophylactic Apocillin was prescribed.

To exclude hereditary complement-mediated thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), comprehensive complement testing was ordered from Skåne Laboratory at Lund University, Sweden. In consideration of patient safety and logistical factors, three Octaplas-based PEX sessions were completed and a single induction dose of eculizumab was administered prior to sample collection. Functional assessment of the complement system showed a significantly decreased alternative pathway activity (quantitative AH50 of 6%), with normal levels of complement factors H, I, and B, and negative antifactor H antibodies.

Outcome and follow-up:

After receiving intricate medical treatment, the patient is stable overall with sufficient urine production. Her renal function remains impaired, indicated by a serum creatinine level of 175 µmol/L, and she is scheduled for ongoing follow-up in the nephrology outpatient clinic (figure 4). She is still on maintenance eculizumab therapy (1200 mg intravenously every 2 weeks), continuing for 4 months following initial induction. Comprehensive complement genetic analysis did not reveal any pathogenic mutations; these findings were conveyed verbally by the reference laboratory, with the official written report still awaited at the time of manuscript submission. Given the gravity of her illness, complement inhibition will be maintained, and re-evaluation for possible cessation will occur if kidney function stabilizes. Discovery of pathological complement gene variants would have supported prolonged treatment with vigilant observation for relapse, but no such mutations were detected in this case.

Differential diagnosis of TMA

TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.

For adults presenting with MAHA, thrombocytopenia, and AKI, the foremost step is to rule out thrombotic TTP, which is characterized by markedly reduced ADAMTS13 activity (<10%). Clinical scoring systems such as PLASMIC are useful for stratifying patients while awaiting laboratory confirmation, as the definitive diagnosis relies on laboratory findings. In this case, the patient's PLASMIC score was 4 out of 7, indicating a low likelihood of TTP, and ADAMTS13 activity measured 43.5%, thereby decreasing the suspicion for TTP and informing the subsequent investigative pathway.

STEC-HUS was also considered in the differential diagnosis. PCR testing for Shiga toxin in the patient returned negative results. The lack of preceding gastrointestinal symptoms combined with negative microbiological studies suggests that STEC-HUS is improbable.

In summary, the persistence of hemolysis, presence of schistocytes, requirement for dialysis due to AKI, and histological evidence of TMA on kidney biopsy collectively favored a diagnosis of CM-HUS (figure 5). This assessment was further substantiated by negative STEC PCR results and significant clinical improvement following treatment with eculizumab. The patient’s unremarkable past medical history diminishes the likelihood of an underlying complement abnormality that would need a secondary trigger, favoring an infection-mediated onset of CM-HUS.

CM-HUS is diagnosed clinically and pathologically as a process of exclusion, and complement values (C3 and C4) can remain within normal limits throughout the disease. Crucially, the presence of normal C3 and C4 does not rule out CM-HUS, since these parameters may not fall outside reference intervals at any point. Of note, hemolysis was evident only after sepsis began resolving, indicating that CM-HUS was triggered by infection rather than being an underlying condition.

For this patient, standard complement measurements (C3 and C4) as well as ADAMTS13 activity were collected just prior to initiating the first PEX session. Expanded complement analysis was carried out eight days following the last PEX treatment and four days post-initiation of eculizumab. The decrease observed in the alternative pathway likely reflects therapeutic intervention, rather than innate complement activation or depletion.

Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and infectious triggers

CM-HUS, previously referred to as 'atypical HUS,' develops due to abnormal activation of the alternative complement pathway, frequently in individuals with underlying genetic susceptibility. Genetic alterations or autoantibodies targeting complement regulatory proteins—including factors H, I, B, or membrane cofactor protein (CD46)—are detected in 50% to 70% of cases. Nonetheless, not all individuals with these abnormalities exhibit the disease, and clinical presentation generally necessitates a precipitating factor such as infection, pregnancy, autoimmune disorders, or certain drugs.

C. canimorsus, although infrequently described in this context, can stimulate complement pathways, particularly in hosts with heightened vulnerability. The bacterium demonstrates varying sensitivity to serum complement: numerous isolates are complement susceptible, yet some capsule-containing strains exhibit partial resistance, underscoring the pivotal role of complement in defense mechanisms. This gives rise to a clinical dilemma, as complement activation facilitates pathogen elimination but may concurrently provoke unregulated endothelial injury and TMA in individuals predisposed to such complications.

This report contributes to a limited but expanding collection of cases linking C. canimorsus infection with complement-mediated TMAs. Bjørkto et al documented a patient with CM-HUS induced by C. canimorsus who carried a complement factor B variant and responded well to eculizumab therapy. Differing from that previous case, our patient did not possess pathogenic or likely pathogenic complement gene variants, reinforcing the notion of CM-HUS triggered by infection in the absence of established hereditary complement dysfunction.

Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma

Managing CM-HUS in the setting of ongoing infection is particularly complex. Eculizumab—a monoclonal antibody directed at terminal complement component 5—represents the established treatment for CM-HUS and significantly enhances hematologic and renal outcomes, especially with prompt administration. Initiating therapy late correlates with incomplete renal recovery, increasing the likelihood of progression to end-stage renal disease.

Conversely, plasma exchange (PEX) is essential for survival in TTP but confers only a transient advantage in CM-HUS by removing complement fragments, inflammatory mediators, or autoantibodies from circulation, while failing to address the fundamental complement pathway abnormality. In our patient, diuresis resumed following the last PEX treatments, yet before receiving eculizumab, indicating short-term stabilization only. Lasting improvement in hematologic and renal parameters was achieved solely after complement inhibition was instituted.

A significant clinical challenge involves the use of complement inhibitors during active bacteremia, since terminal complement components are necessary for host defense against encapsulated organisms such as Neisseria and Capnocytophaga, raising concerns about the risk of infections associated with eculizumab. In this instance, eculizumab therapy commenced after blood cultures were cleared, thereby reducing the hazard of uncontrolled infection. Ensuring vaccination, prophylactic antimicrobials, and vigilant monitoring are vital components when employing complement blockade.

Conclusion and broader implications

Timely identification of CM-HUS and swift commencement of complement inhibition represent the key factors influencing positive outcomes. The individual described attained hematologic remission and partial improvement in renal function, aligning with existing data that identify renal status and timing of eculizumab initiation as primary prognostic indicators.

While CM-HUS linked to C. canimorsus is exceedingly uncommon, this report underscores the need to include infectious causes—especially atypical ones—when evaluating patients with newly diagnosed TMA. Enhanced recognition and advancements in molecular diagnostic methods could facilitate the detection of additional similar cases.
