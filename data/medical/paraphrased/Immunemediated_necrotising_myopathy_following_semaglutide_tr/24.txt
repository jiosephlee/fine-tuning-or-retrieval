Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a span of 4 months, she exhibited increasing dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels. The onset of these symptoms occurred after a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltration on muscle biopsy. The patient experienced clinical improvement with immunosuppressive therapy, including corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Eventually, she returned to her baseline level of functioning after several months off immunosuppression and continued avoidance of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first documented instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, with significant recovery following brief immunosuppressive and IVIG therapy.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. One of the noted side effects linked to this class of drugs is the reduction of skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report detailed an occurrence of rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics associated with tirzepatide, another widely utilized GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this instance, the patient experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels into the thousands while undergoing treatment, and CK levels began to decrease within several days following cessation of the medication, indicative of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, defined by severe proximal muscle weakness and the presence of necrotic muscle fibers, with minimal lymphocytic infiltration observed on histological examination. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) show strong associations with IMNM. Notably, statins, widely prescribed for metabolic conditions, are closely linked to HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Prior to the recognition of the connection between statins and HMGCR autoantibodies, numerous individuals who manifested necrotising myopathy features were frequently misclassified as having either toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Many such cases demonstrated ongoing myopathy and persistently elevated CK levels even after discontinuation of statins. Subsequent clinical improvement following immunosuppressive treatment indicated an underlying autoimmune process, ultimately contributing to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibody as a key marker of this condition. Importantly, it is estimated that approximately one-third of IMNM patients lack detectable autoantibodies. This seronegative subset presents further diagnostic difficulties and implies the existence of unidentified associated autoantibodies or triggering factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a past medical history including obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism presented with worsening proximal muscle weakness, along with difficulty swallowing and speaking.

She had previously been in overall good health and was able to carry out all activities of daily living independently. Three years before her symptoms progressed, she was evaluated in the outpatient setting for elevated liver enzymes. At that assessment, both aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were modestly increased to roughly double the upper normal limit, with ALT higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet levels remained within reference ranges. The patient did not consume alcohol and was not using any medications known to cause liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed an enlarged liver with diffusely heightened echogenicity, most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Tests for viral hepatitis as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies were negative. Liver biopsy indicated steatohepatitis in keeping with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8, stage 0. The patient received counseling to maintain a low-calorie diet and to engage in regular physical activity.

Given the ongoing presence of hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years post NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was commenced. After receiving her initial dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg administered subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting within hours. The symptoms were unrelenting, prompting hospital admission two days later due to persistent nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal dysfunction, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate administration, and anti-emetic medications. She required a short stay in the intensive care unit where she received non-invasive ventilatory support. Subsequently, her renal parameters improved with hydration alone, negating the requirement for haemofiltration or dialysis. Admission laboratory results revealed creatine kinase (CK) elevation at 337 U/L. No other related manifestations—such as rash, myalgia, or dark-colored urine—were noted. During her hospital course, she developed episodes of atrial fibrillation, but troponin levels remained within normal limits. She was discharged after a five-day hospitalization. At discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had resolved, although she was notably deconditioned. In the ensuing four months, she experienced gradually worsening weakness. She reported no history of statin use, consumption of supplements linked to IMNM like mushrooms or red yeast rice, or other nutritional supplements. She also denied a family history of myopathy, recent substantial or prolonged physical exertion, as well as use of alcohol or illicit drugs.

Four months following her initial and sole administration of semaglutide, her condition further deteriorated. She became unable to stand or sit upright without support, and began experiencing dysphagia to both liquids and solids over several days. Consequently, she sought care at an external hospital where laboratory assessment revealed a creatine kinase level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Management included daily intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg for 5 days, delivered under neurology oversight through telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary medical center for evaluation by rheumatology. On arrival, she was found to be afebrile, with normal blood pressure, and showed no signs of distress. Examination noted pronounced muscle weakness predominantly affecting proximal muscle groups (see table 1). No rash, dermatologic findings, crackles, joint inflammation, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurologic deficits were detected.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she was started on oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg daily. Because of ongoing weakness and significant disease manifestations, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg divided across 4 days. Her creatine kinase (CK) levels showed substantial improvement within 2 weeks (figure 1). She was discharged following nearly 4 weeks of inpatient care. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK levels remained within the normal range.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, showing ongoing progress in her functional abilities and general well-being, with CK levels remaining within normal range. Consequently, her prednisone was tapered quickly and discontinued without difficulty. Owing both to financial factors and her maintained improvement following cessation of semaglutide, she did not undergo further IVIG therapy beyond the initial treatment administered during her hospital stay.

At her most recent outpatient visit, 10 months after starting treatment, she had returned to her pre-illness level of function and had remained off immunosuppressive therapy for the past 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory findings revealed a creatine kinase (CK) of 1237 U/L upon assessment, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels were within the normal range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured at 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 165 U/L, observed in the context of increased CK and a history of hepatic steatosis. The autoantibody panel showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement values were within normal limits; renal function was unremarkable, and she did not present with cytopenias. The myositis antibody profile was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. She did, however, demonstrate a low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein (CRP) was elevated at 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin was increased to 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL) and proBNP was markedly raised at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies included MRI of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI scans of the femurs demonstrated mild and symmetric muscular edema bilaterally, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous edema on the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70%, with a right ventricular systolic pressure measuring 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI indicated mild hypokinesis affecting the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall, accompanied by myocardial edema and absence of definite late gadolinium enhancement; these findings suggest myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, and could alternatively reflect focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No evidence was found of significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or major valvular abnormalities. Doppler ultrasound of the liver indicated hepatosteatosis. The high-resolution CT of the chest was unremarkable for interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed steatotic hepatomegaly but was otherwise without notable findings, including absence of lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced diagnostic evaluations included repeating the liver biopsy and performing a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, which was conducted during her hospitalization, revealed several cores of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis that occupied roughly 70% of the examined liver parenchyma. There was no notable portal inflammatory activity or interface hepatitis. This histological picture was aligned with her previously established diagnosis of hepatosteatosis and indicated mild advancement of her liver pathology, scoring 5/8 for the NAFLD Activity Score and corresponding to stage 1 disease. 

Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle identified scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical staining showed reactivity for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a macrophage marker, confirming myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, necrotising and non-necrotising myofibers stained positively for membrane attack complex (figure 4C), indicating an inflammatory process. There were occasional CD3-positive cells, identifying T-cells (figure 4A), as well as perivascular infiltrates (figure 3C). The collective muscle biopsy findings were diagnostic of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited an immediate and pronounced adverse response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. Initially, she developed gastrointestinal symptoms along with renal insufficiency thought to be due to hypovolemia. At presentation, her creatine kinase (CK) was only slightly increased, which was not compatible with rhabdomyolysis. However, ongoing deterioration despite discontinuing semaglutide, the pattern of muscle weakness, and a 12-fold elevation in CK measured over 4 months following semaglutide exposure suggest immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) potentially linked to semaglutide. In addition, biopsy findings in keeping with drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical indicators for an underlying connective tissue disorder, and maintained clinical improvement after withdrawal of semaglutide and initiation of immunosuppression all further reinforce this association.

It is important to highlight that elevated transaminases were present before the patient began taking semaglutide, a feature observed in myopathies. Nevertheless, hepatosteatosis confirmed by biopsy was documented three years before semaglutide initiation, and her mildly elevated AST/ALT continued even after normalization of CK levels. Therefore, the persistent increase in transaminases is most probably attributed to a chronic liver source rather than muscle injury. Furthermore, her cardiac assessment did not clearly demonstrate evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Considering the clinical context and her swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy remains a plausible diagnosis.

The presence of a positive SS-B antibody, despite negative ANA and SS-A results, was unusual. To date, no literature documents IMNM arising from isolated SS-B positivity in the absence of ANA and SS-A antibodies. Regrettably, we did not obtain a creatine kinase (CK) level prior to initiating semaglutide, so we cannot rule out a pre-existing, slow-progressing autoimmune IMNM. Nonetheless, the patient experienced a 12-fold increase in CK and developed progressive proximal muscle weakness after starting semaglutide, supporting a medication-related etiology for the IMNM. Furthermore, her severe IMNM responded to a short regimen of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), in addition to discontinuation of semaglutide. This clinical trajectory is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which typically improves after temporary immunosuppression and cessation of the causative medication, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM that often necessitates extended, aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, after which she was started on oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Given her ongoing weakness and severity of symptoms, she was administered IVIG at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over four days. Multiple outpatient follow-up visits demonstrated sustained improvements in her functional capabilities and general health, with consistently normal creatine kinase values. Owing to this progress, prednisone was successfully tapered and discontinued over a two-month period. In light of both financial constraints and her stable condition without semaglutide, no further IVIG therapy was administered after the initial inpatient course.

Outcome and follow-up:

She returned for follow-up in the outpatient clinic 10 months after her first assessment and commencement of therapy, having recovered her pre-illness functional capacity, and had been without immunosuppressive medication for 7 months. Her creatine kinase levels stayed within normal limits post-hospitalisation and immunosuppressive treatment. Repeat echocardiogram and cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated resolution of the earlier abnormalities. Additionally, she had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions, such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. These agents most frequently cause gastrointestinal side effects, yet they have also been linked to reduced muscle mass and possibly rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the patient's progression aligns more with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. After a single semaglutide dose, CK rose by 12-fold over a period of 4 months; however, if a direct toxic effect from semaglutide was responsible, as observed in drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, we would anticipate CK levels to return to baseline within several weeks. It is worth noting that a previously documented rhabdomyolysis case associated with tirzepatide adhered to the typical clinical trajectory: the individual presented with a substantial acute CK elevation (CK=57 014 at initial evaluation) and experienced prompt improvement after discontinuing the drug and receiving hydration within a few days, all without immunosuppressive therapy.

In accordance with the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, semaglutide appears to have a probable causal link to IMNM in this patient, given the appropriate temporal association, low likelihood of alternative etiologies or medication-induced effects, and an improvement noted after discontinuation. Re-exposure would not be ethical due to the severe nature of the adverse reaction attributed to semaglutide. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this case. With the rapid increase in semaglutide usage, the purpose of this report is to alert clinicians to this possible adverse event and encourage vigilant pharmacovigilance. Further investigation is needed to determine whether this observed relationship is truly causal or simply coincidental.

The underlying factors contributing to our patient's pronounced response to semaglutide remain unidentified. As GLP-1 receptor agonists gain widespread use, research aimed at identifying risk factors will become increasingly crucial should further cases with temporally close onset following semaglutide administration emerge.
