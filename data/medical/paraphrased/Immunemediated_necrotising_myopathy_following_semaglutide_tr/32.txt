Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. The patient experienced a 4-month progression of dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase, all beginning after a single administration of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated necrotizing myopathy with associated inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) resulted in clinical improvement. She ultimately returned to her premorbid level of function after several months without immunosuppression, while continuing to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This represents the first documented case of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, which showed favorable response to brief immunosuppressive therapy and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized for the management of metabolic disorders, such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the adverse events linked to this drug class, reduction of skeletal muscle mass has been reported. A recent case study detailed a patient who experienced rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics associated with tirzepatide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist frequently prescribed. The individual exhibited a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching values in the thousands during ongoing therapy, and saw improvement in CK concentrations within several days after discontinuing the medication, indicating drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, defined by rapidly progressive proximal muscle weakness, necrosis of muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration observed on histopathology. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) are strongly linked to IMNM. Notably, statin therapy, which is frequently prescribed for metabolic disorders, has a significant association with the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to recognizing this connection, numerous patients exposed to statins developed necrotising myopathy symptoms, and were frequently misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. These individuals often exhibited ongoing muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase levels even after statin withdrawal. The persistence of these findings and subsequent response to immunosuppressive treatment supported the existence of an autoimmune myopathy and ultimately facilitated the identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining marker of IMNM. Nevertheless, it is estimated that approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not express detectable autoantibodies. This seronegative group poses further diagnostic difficulties and indicates that additional autoantibodies or environmental triggers related to IMNM remain uncharacterized.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, whose past medical history included obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep venous thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, presented with worsening proximal muscle weakness, as well as difficulty swallowing and speaking.

Previously, the patient maintained good health and managed all daily activities independently. Three years prior to her current symptoms, she underwent outpatient assessment due to elevated transaminases. At that point, she demonstrated mildly increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) values, both roughly twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. Levels of alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelets were all within normal boundaries. She reported no alcohol intake and was not using medications with hepatic toxicity. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed an enlarged liver with uniformly increased echotexture, most consistent with fatty infiltration. Serological tests for viral hepatitis, as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, all returned negative results. Liver biopsy identified steatohepatitis in line with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with an NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and stage 0. She was counseled to follow a reduced-calorie diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Owing to ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was commenced. Shortly after the initial administration of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, and she was admitted to hospital two days later with nausea, vomiting, insufficient oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal failure, and profound metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetics. She underwent a brief stay in the intensive care unit where non-invasive ventilation was required. Her kidney function subsequently recovered with hydration alone, obviating the need for haemofiltration or dialysis. Creatine kinase upon admission measured 337 U/L, indicating an elevation. She did not report accompanying symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine. During her inpatient stay, she also developed episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though serum troponin remained within normal limits. She was discharged after a 5-day hospitalisation. By discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had abated, but she remained deconditioned. Over the next 4 months, she experienced progressive muscular weakness. She denied previous statin use, ingestion of supplements commonly linked to IMNM such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, as well as any other nutritional supplements. There was no reported family history of muscle disease, no recent intense or extended physical activity, and no use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition progressively worsened. She was unable to maintain standing or sitting posture without support and began to experience difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids over the preceding several days. These symptoms led her to seek care at an external hospital, where laboratory evaluation demonstrated a creatine kinase level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Management included intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, administered under the direction of neurology through telemedicine. Subsequently, she was moved to a tertiary care facility for further rheumatological assessment. On arrival, her vital signs were within normal limits; she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and showed no acute distress. The physical examination was notable for marked muscular weakness, most prominent in proximal muscle groups (see table 1). There were no findings of rash, skin changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcerations, or other neurological abnormalities.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, the patient was placed on oral prednisone at a daily dose of 1 mg/kg. Due to ongoing muscle weakness and significant clinical severity, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, distributed across 4 days. Over a span of 2 weeks, her CK levels showed significant reduction (see figure 1). Her initial hospitalization lasted close to 4 weeks before she was discharged. Approximately 12 days following discharge, she required rehospitalization for 10 days due to septic shock resulting from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK values were found to be within the normal range.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up appointments, during which her physical capabilities and general well-being continued to improve, and her CK remained within normal limits. As a result, she was able to discontinue prednisone quickly. Owing to both financial considerations and her sustained progress after stopping semaglutide, she did not undergo any further IVIG therapy beyond the initial treatment administered during her hospitalization.

Her most recent outpatient visit occurred 10 months following the initial assessment and commencement of therapy, at which time she had returned to her previous functional level and had been without immunosuppressive medications for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a previous peak of 3992 U/L reported one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within reference values. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was measured at 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 165 U/L, against the background of increased serum CK and a known history of hepatic steatosis. Autoimmune screening showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (RNP) antibodies, anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement studies and creatinine were within normal limits, and there was no evidence of cytopenia. The myositis antibody panel was negative for SRP and HMGCR, as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. However, she did show a low titre positivity for SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein measured 22.8 mg/L, both above normal. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL), while pro B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was raised at 4358 pg/mL (normal: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies included MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution CT of the chest, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs showed mild, symmetrical edema in the muscles bilaterally, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, with subcutaneous edema present laterally on both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiogram findings included a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated between 65% and 70%, and right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis affecting the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, accompanied by myocardial edema without clear late gadolinium enhancement, raising the possibility of myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or a localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of considerable myocardial scarring, infarct, or significant valvular disease. Doppler liver ultrasound indicated hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT did not demonstrate interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed steatotic hepatomegaly, but was otherwise normal and lacked lymphadenopathy.

Further diagnostic evaluation included a repeat liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. During her hospitalization, a liver biopsy revealed several cores of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, affecting approximately 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, with absence of notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her previous diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, showing mild advancement of her chronic liver disease to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8, stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analyses demonstrated acid phosphatase (figure 3D) as well as Cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a macrophage marker, indicative of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Membrane attack complex staining was positive on both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), indicating an inflammatory process. Occasional CD3-positive cells, representing T lymphocytes (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were also observed. The muscle biopsy findings supported the diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed a pronounced and immediate adverse response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating an intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal adverse effects and acute renal failure likely related to hypovolemia. At presentation, creatine kinase (CK) was only slightly raised, not meeting criteria for rhabdomyolysis. However, her condition worsened after discontinuation of semaglutide, accompanied by weakness in a distribution characteristic of inflammatory myopathy, and a 12-fold escalation in CK over four months following semaglutide exposure, indicating an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) potentially linked to semaglutide. Additionally, histopathological findings compatible with drug-induced IMNM, absence of features pointing toward an underlying connective tissue disorder, and sustained recovery after initiation of immunosuppressive therapy together with withdrawal of semaglutide, further corroborate this connection.

It is significant that elevated transaminases were observed before she began treatment with semaglutide, a finding that can occur in certain myopathies. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to semaglutide initiation confirmed hepatosteatosis, and mild elevations in AST and ALT remained even after normalization of her CK levels. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevation is most likely attributable to liver pathology rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not definitively demonstrate ongoing myocarditis. In light of her clinical context and swift improvement, stress-related myopathy is also a plausible consideration.

The detection of SS-B antibody in isolation, without concomitant ANA or SS-A positivity, was surprising. To date, there are no documented cases in the literature of isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A serving as a precursor to IMNM. Regrettably, we could not locate a creatine kinase value from prior to semaglutide administration, which precludes ruling out the presence of a pre-existing autoimmune IMNM before starting semaglutide. Nonetheless, a twelve-fold increase in CK levels and worsening proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide exposure suggest that this IMNM episode was most likely induced by the medication. Furthermore, the patient’s marked IMNM resolved following a brief treatment regimen of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin, together with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern of clinical improvement is consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which often responds to short-term immunosuppression after cessation of the causative drug, rather than idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates prolonged and aggressive immunosuppressive intervention.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days in response to her ongoing weakness and pronounced clinical presentation. She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, demonstrating ongoing progress in her functional abilities and general well-being, with creatine kinase levels consistently within the normal range. As a result, her prednisone was successfully tapered over a 2-month period. Owing to the combination of financial factors and continued improvement after discontinuing semaglutide, she did not require further IVIG treatments beyond her initial inpatient course.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months after starting evaluation and therapy, she had returned to her original level of function and had been free from immunosuppressive medications for 7 months. Her creatine kinase levels stayed within normal limits after her hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Repeat assessments with echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previous abnormal findings. Additionally, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation had not recurred.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly utilized and have proven effective in treating metabolic conditions, such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Gastrointestinal adverse effects are most frequently reported, but these agents may also be linked to reduced muscle mass and, in some instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this particular case, the patient's presentation aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. After a single dose of semaglutide, CK levels rose twelve-fold over four months, which contrasts with the expected normalization of CK within weeks if semaglutide’s direct toxicity were responsible, as is typical in drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Importantly, the prior rhabdomyolysis case associated with tirzepatide followed the usual clinical pattern: the patient presented with a significantly elevated CK (CK=57 014), and symptoms rapidly resolved with cessation of the medication and hydration over a few days, with no immunosuppressive therapy required.

Based on the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the connection between semaglutide administration and IMNM in this instance is considered probable, as there was an appropriate temporal association, IMNM was not likely attributable to other medications or conditions, and clinical improvement was noted upon discontinuation of semaglutide. Moreover, attempting a rechallenge would be unethical due to the severity of the adverse event linked to semaglutide. Nonetheless, we recognize that causality cannot be definitively established. Given the rapidly increasing prescription of semaglutide, this case report seeks to inform healthcare providers about this possible side effect and strengthen pharmacovigilance efforts. Additional research is needed to determine if the observed association is causal or coincidental.

Furthermore, what factors may have contributed to the pronounced response our patient experienced following semaglutide exposure remain uncertain. As GLP-1 receptor agonists become more widely used, investigations into individual susceptibility will be crucial, particularly if more cases showing a close temporal association with semaglutide emerge.
