Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report presents a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a 4-month period, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and increased creatine kinase levels. Her clinical manifestations began after a single dose of semaglutide. Investigations demonstrated necrotising myopathy with an accompanying inflammatory infiltrate on muscle biopsy, and she showed improvement with immunosuppressive treatment using corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). She eventually recovered and returned to her prior level of functioning, remaining off immunosuppressive therapy for several months and continuing to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report documents the first known instance of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, which responded to a brief regimen of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. One of the reported side effects for this class of drugs is the reduction of skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report detailed the occurrence of rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics linked to tirzepatide, a frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In the presented case, the patient experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels—reaching into the thousands—while undergoing active therapy, and CK concentrations began to decrease within days of discontinuing the medication, which supports a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) is a distinct form of myopathy distinguished by severe weakness of proximal muscles, necrosis of muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration observed on histological examination. Two autoantibodies, anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP), are strongly linked to IMNM. Notably, statin therapy—which is widely prescribed for metabolic conditions—has a pronounced association with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Previously, several patients exposed to statins developed clinical and pathological signs of necrotising myopathy; these cases were frequently classified as toxic myopathy or polymyositis before the correlation with HMGCR autoantibodies was recognized. In such instances, affected individuals often continued to have elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels and ongoing muscle symptoms even after discontinuation of statin treatment. Subsequent improvement following immunosuppressive therapy suggested an autoimmune etiology and eventually led to the identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of the disorder. It is important to note that approximately one-third of IMNM cases do not exhibit detectable autoantibodies. The absence of autoantibody markers in this subgroup poses added diagnostic difficulty and indicates there are other, as-yet-unidentified autoantibodies and triggers associated with IMNM.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a medical background that included obesity, fatty liver disease, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote history of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening weakness in proximal muscles, as well as difficulties with swallowing and voice changes.

Prior to this presentation, she was in overall good health and managed her daily activities without assistance. Three years before her current decline, outpatient workup had been performed due to elevated transaminases. Laboratory studies at that time revealed mildly increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels—both roughly twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. Results for alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelets were normal. The patient stated she did not consume alcohol and was not taking any liver-toxic medications. Abdominal ultrasonography showed an enlarged liver and diffusely increased echogenicity, most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Screening for viral hepatitis and testing for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies were all negative. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and stage 0 fibrosis. She was counseled to maintain a low-calorie diet and exercise regularly.

Despite ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years after her initial NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Shortly after her first administration of semaglutide (0.25 mg given subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms persisted, resulting in hospital admission 2 days later with complaints of persistent nausea, vomiting, limited oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal failure, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate therapy, and anti-emetic agents. She required temporary transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. Renal function improved following rehydration, and renal replacement therapy (haemofiltration or haemodialysis) was not necessary. Admission labs revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 337 U/L. She exhibited no additional signs such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine indicative of myoglobinuria. During her hospital stay, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation occurred, though troponin assays remained within normal limits. After a 5-day inpatient course, she was discharged; by then, gastrointestinal symptoms had resolved, though she remained physically deconditioned. Over the subsequent 4 months, she noticed increasing weakness. She denied previous statin use, ingestion of supplements linked to IMNM (including mushrooms or red yeast rice), or use of any other nutritional supplements. Additionally, there was no relevant family history of myopathy, nor recent history of strenuous or extended exercise, nor consumption of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months after her initial and sole administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She was unable to stand or sit upright without help and reported a several-day history of difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids. Consequently, she sought care at another hospital, where her creatine kinase was found to be elevated at 3992 U/L (normal range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Neurology consulted via telemedicine, and she received 1000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone each day for 5 days. Following this, she was transferred to a tertiary center for further rheumatologic assessment. On evaluation, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable. Physical examination demonstrated marked weakness, especially in her proximal muscle groups (see table 1). No rash, skin abnormalities, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurologic deficits were observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day was initiated. Due to ongoing significant weakness and severe clinical presentation, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg over the course of 4 days. Her creatine kinase (CK) levels showed substantial improvement within two weeks (see figure 1). She was discharged following an almost 4-week hospital stay. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for a duration of 10 days with septic shock resulting from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; her CK level was within normal limits during this subsequent hospitalization.

She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which she showed ongoing enhancement in both her functional abilities and general wellbeing, along with consistently normal CK results. Consequently, she was able to taper off prednisone quickly. Given both financial considerations and her marked clinical improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered beyond the original course received during her hospitalization.

At her most recent outpatient visit, 10 months post initial assessment and start of therapy, she had restored her baseline functional capacity and had remained off immunosuppressive medication for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory findings revealed a CK of 1237 U/L at the time of evaluation, with a previous peak value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring facility. TSH levels were found to be normal. AST measured at 191 U/L and ALT at 165 U/L, in the context of increased serum CK and a background of fatty liver. Autoantibody screening demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were within normal range, and there were no indications of cytopenias. The myositis-specific antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. She was positive for SS-B autoantibody at a low titre of 2.4 (reference value: <1.0). ESR was measured at 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein at 22.8 mg/L, both elevated. Additionally, HS-Troponin was raised at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was 4358 pg/mL (normal: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies performed included MRI of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler evaluation, high-resolution CT of the chest, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs demonstrated mild, symmetrical edema within the muscles bilaterally, predominantly in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, along with subcutaneous edema observed laterally in both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI findings included mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall segments, in association with myocardial edema and the absence of definite late gadolinium enhancement; these findings were felt to potentially represent myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no indication of notable myocardial infarction, scarring, or significant valvular disease. Liver ultrasound with Doppler indicated hepatosteatosis. The high-resolution chest CT did not demonstrate evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI identified steatotic hepatomegaly but was otherwise normal, without evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Further advanced testing included a repeat liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospitalization, showed several fragments of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving roughly 70% of the sampled liver parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. This was in line with her established hepatosteatosis, indicating mild worsening of her liver condition to a Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease Activity Score of 5 out of 8 and stage 1. Biopsy from the right vastus lateralis muscle revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis identified acid phosphatase staining (figure 3D) and staining for Cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68)—a macrophage indicator—with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, positive staining for the membrane attack complex was observed on both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), supporting the presence of an inflammatory reaction. Occasional cells positive for CD3, a T-cell marker (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C) were also identified. Altogether, the findings from the muscle biopsy were compatible with a diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed an abrupt and notable response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of intolerance. Her early presentation was characterized by gastrointestinal adverse effects and renal failure presumed to be due to hypovolaemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated at initial assessment, which did not support rhabdomyolysis, her ongoing deterioration after discontinuing semaglutide, coupled with the pattern of weakness and a twelvefold rise in CK over the subsequent four months after taking semaglutide, point toward an IMNM likely linked to semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings compatible with drug-related IMNM, absence of other signs of connective tissue disease, and continued improvement following both immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide strengthen this proposed relationship.

It is important to observe that she exhibited transaminitis before beginning semaglutide treatment, a finding often associated with myopathic conditions. Nevertheless, three years before initiating semaglutide, she was diagnosed via biopsy with hepatosteatosis, and her mildly raised AST/ALT remained after normalization of CK levels. Therefore, it appears her chronic transaminase elevation stems from hepatic sources rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Considering the circumstances and her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy would also be plausible.

The finding of isolated SS-B antibody positivity in the context of negative ANA and SS-A was surprising. To date, literature does not document cases of IMNM arising from sole SS-B antibody positivity with both ANA and SS-A negative. A creatine kinase (CK) value prior to the initiation of semaglutide was regrettably unavailable, so a pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM cannot be conclusively ruled out. Nonetheless, the 12-fold elevation in CK and worsening proximal muscle weakness following the introduction of semaglutide suggest that this occurrence of IMNM was likely induced by the medication. Furthermore, the patient’s pronounced IMNM remitted with a brief regimen of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), in combination with discontinuation of semaglutide. This sequence of events is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM—typically responsive to short-term immunosuppression after cessation of the causative agent—than with idiopathic IMNM, which frequently necessitates extended, aggressive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

She received 1000 mg of intravenous methylprednisolone daily for 5 days, after which she was started on oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days, in light of her ongoing weakness and severe clinical presentation. Subsequent outpatient follow-up visits demonstrated continued progress in her functional abilities and general health, along with consistently normal CK values. Based on these findings, she was able to taper off prednisone successfully within a 2-month period. Given both financial factors and stable improvement without semaglutide, she did not require further IVIG treatment apart from the initial hospitalization course.

Outcome and follow-up:

She was most recently evaluated in an outpatient clinic 10 months after starting treatment, at which point she had returned to her previous level of function and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for 7 months. Her CK values stayed normal after hospitalization and immunosuppressive therapy. Follow-up echocardiography and cardiac MRI showed resolution of the abnormalities noted earlier. Additionally, she did not have any further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly prescribed and have proven efficacy in managing metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are common, these agents have also been linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in some instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the patient's progression aligns more with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. Creatine kinase (CK) values rose twelvefold over four months after a single administration of semaglutide, whereas, with a direct toxic effect, CK levels would typically be expected to return to normal within a few weeks, as is observed in other drug-induced rhabdomyolysis scenarios. Importantly, a prior case involving tirzepatide-related rhabdomyolysis displayed the characteristic pattern: the patient presented with a pronounced acute CK rise (CK=57 014) and demonstrated rapid clinical improvement following cessation of the medication and hydration over several days, with no requirement for immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case demonstrates a probable association between semaglutide administration and the onset of IMNM, supported by an appropriate temporal relationship, lack of evidence implicating alternative diseases or medications, and a clinically meaningful improvement following discontinuation. Performing a rechallenge would not be ethical due to the severity of the adverse reaction induced by semaglutide. Nevertheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. Given the widespread adoption of semaglutide, the purpose of this report is to alert healthcare providers to this possible adverse event and promote increased pharmacovigilance. Further research will help determine if this relationship is causal or coincidental.

The factors contributing to our patient’s marked response to semaglutide remain uncertain. As GLP-1 receptor agonists are increasingly utilized, investigations into risk factors predisposing individuals to such adverse events will be essential, particularly if additional cases emerge with a similarly close temporal association to semaglutide treatment.
