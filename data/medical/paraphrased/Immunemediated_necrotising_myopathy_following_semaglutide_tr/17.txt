Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case concerns a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she experienced progressively worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels. These symptoms began after a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings indicative of necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrates identified on muscle biopsy. She was treated with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), leading to clinical improvement. Ultimately, she regained her baseline level of function and remained well for several months after discontinuation of immunosuppressive therapy and ongoing avoidance of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This case represents the first reported instance of IMNM likely linked to use of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, with notable improvement following a limited course of immunosuppressive agents and IVIG.

Background:

In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been widely prescribed for the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Adverse effects for this drug class have included reports of skeletal muscle mass reduction. Notably, a recent case report detailed the development of necrotising rhabdomyolysis linked to tirzepatide, another frequently used GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that case, the patient experienced a substantial increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels into the thousands during ongoing therapy; CK levels began to decline within days of stopping the medication, supporting a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a specific form of muscle disease, marked by rapidly progressing weakness of proximal muscles, histopathologically defined by muscle fibre necrosis with minimal lymphocytic infiltrate. Among autoantibodies, anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) show a strong association with IMNM. Statins, widely prescribed for metabolic conditions, have a significant link to the development of HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Historically, multiple patients who developed necrotising myopathy after statin use were categorized as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis before the connection to HMGCR autoantibody became apparent. In these situations, muscle symptoms and elevated creatine kinase levels frequently persisted after discontinuation of the statin, and subsequent responsiveness to immunosuppressive therapy pointed to an underlying autoimmune etiology. This observation was central in identifying the HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of IMNM. Nevertheless, it is estimated that approximately one-third of individuals with IMNM test negative for these known autoantibodies. This seronegativity not only presents further diagnostic complexity but also implies the existence of yet unidentified autoantibodies or triggers associated with the disorder.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose past medical history included obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, as well as difficulty swallowing and hoarseness.

Prior to this decline, she had enjoyed good health and maintained full independence in daily activities. Three years before her symptoms progressed, she underwent outpatient assessment for elevated liver enzymes. At that time, laboratory tests revealed mildly increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), with values approximately twice the upper normal limit and ALT exceeding AST. Her alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet levels were all within reference ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not taking any potentially hepatotoxic drugs. An abdominal ultrasound indicated enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, which was most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Serologic testing for viral hepatitis, as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, all returned negative results. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis consistent with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and fibrosis stage 0. She was counseled to adhere to a reduced-calorie diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Owing to ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years following her diagnosis of NAFLD, semaglutide therapy was initiated. Soon after administration of her initial subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, and she was admitted to the hospital two days later presenting with persistent nausea, vomiting, insufficient oral intake, dyspnea, acute kidney injury, and marked metabolic acidosis. Her management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and antiemetic agents. She required a brief stay in the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Renal function subsequently recovered with hydration alone, and renal replacement therapy was not needed. Upon admission, her creatine kinase was found to be elevated at 337 U/L. She reported no other symptoms such as rash, myalgias, or dark urine. During the admission, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation developed, though troponin levels remained within normal range. After a five-day hospital stay, she was discharged, with resolution of her gastrointestinal symptoms but ongoing deconditioning. In the ensuing four months, she noted gradually worsening weakness. She reported no previous use of statins or significant intake of supplements linked to IMNM, including mushrooms or red yeast rice, nor any other dietary supplements. She also denied any notable family history of myopathy, engagement in excessive or prolonged physical exercise, and use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition progressively worsened. She became unable to stand or maintain an upright seated position without support, and for several days experienced dysphagia to both liquids and solids. As a result, she presented to an external hospital, where laboratory tests revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Neurology consultants, engaged via telemedicine, recommended a regimen of intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for 5 days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for rheumatological assessment. Upon arrival, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable otherwise. Examination was notable for marked proximal muscle weakness (see table 1), without evidence of rashes, dermatologic findings, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or any additional neurological abnormalities.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she was transitioned to oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Because her profound weakness and severe manifestations persisted, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Significant reduction in her CK levels occurred within a two-week period (see figure 1). She was discharged after an inpatient stay of nearly four weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she required rehospitalization for 10 days because of septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK remained within the normal range.

During several outpatient follow-up visits, she showed ongoing improvement in both her physical function and general health, with CK levels remaining normal. Owing to these improvements, prednisone was tapered off rapidly and successfully. Considering the financial implications as well as her continued recovery following cessation of semaglutide, she did not receive further IVIG beyond the initial hospitalization.

She was last seen in the outpatient setting 10 months after her initial evaluation and treatment initiation and had regained her baseline functional status, off immunosuppression for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level elevated to 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a maximum recorded value of 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was found to be within normal parameters. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, findings interpreted in the context of increased serum CK and known fatty liver disease. Autoimmune serology demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), anti-Sjögren’s syndrome antibody type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement components, as well as creatinine, were within normal limits and no cytopenias were present. Myositis-specific panel was negative for signal recognition particle (SRP), 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR), Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-gamma (TIF1-y), NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. A low-titer SS-B autoantibody was detected at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein (CRP) was also elevated at 22.8 mg/L. Of particular note, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was measured at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL), and pro B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was substantially elevated at 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies comprised bilateral femoral MRI, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver Doppler ultrasound, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of both femurs demonstrated mild, symmetrical muscular edema, predominantly seen in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, as well as subcutaneous edema along the outer thighs bilaterally (figure 2). Echocardiographic evaluation revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, accompanied by myocardial edema and absence of distinct late gadolinium enhancement, findings suggestive of either early myocarditis or focal stress cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or major valvular pathology. Liver ultrasound with Doppler findings included hepatic steatosis. The high-resolution chest CT did not reveal interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI demonstrated fatty enlargement of the liver but was otherwise unremarkable, with no lymphadenopathy detected.

Additional advanced diagnostic testing included a second liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during hospitalization, showed several fragments of liver tissue with both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis affecting roughly 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, but without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her known history of hepatic steatosis, and indicated mild worsening of her chronic liver disease, yielding a NAFLD Activity Score of 5/8, stage 1. The right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated positivity for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a macrophage-associated marker, with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Membrane attack complex staining was also identified on both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), pointing to an inflammatory process. In addition, a few cells positive for CD3, a marker of T cells (figure 4A), as well as perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C), were observed. The overall muscle biopsy findings were indicative of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed a pronounced and rapid adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal adverse effects and renal insufficiency, which was presumed to be secondary to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only slightly raised and did not align with rhabdomyolysis at initial assessment, her ongoing deterioration despite discontinuation of semaglutide, the pattern of her muscle weakness, and a twelvefold rise in CK over the subsequent four months following semaglutide exposure point toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings compatible with drug-induced IMNM, the absence of other clinical signs suggesting a primary connective tissue disorder, and sustained clinical improvement with immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide further reinforce this correlation.

It is worth noting that elevated transaminase levels were present before she began semaglutide treatment, a finding that may occur in myopathies. Nonetheless, a liver biopsy performed three years before starting semaglutide confirmed hepatosteatosis, and the slight increases in AST and ALT remained after her creatine kinase values returned to normal. Therefore, it is probable that her chronically raised transaminases stem from hepatic causes rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not yield definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. In the context of her presentation and swift clinical improvement, a stress-related myopathy could also be a plausible explanation.

The isolated presence of SS-B antibodies, with negative ANA and SS-A, was an unusual finding. To date, there are no documented cases of IMNM arising in the context of SS-B positivity alone alongside negative ANA and SS-A. Regrettably, no creatine kinase measurement was available from before the patient began semaglutide, so we cannot fully rule out the possibility of a pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM. Nonetheless, the marked 12-fold increase in CK levels and the progressive weakness in proximal muscles that followed semaglutide administration suggest that the IMNM episode was more likely induced by the medication. Moreover, the patient’s significant muscle inflammation resolved after a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG combined with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern is more typical of drug-induced IMNM, which often responds to short-term immunosuppression once the triggering drug is withdrawn, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM that generally necessitates ongoing and more aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days, in response to ongoing significant weakness and other severe manifestations. Multiple outpatient follow-up visits documented continued gains in her functional capacity and general wellbeing, along with consistently normal creatine kinase (CK) levels. Consequently, prednisone was able to be tapered quickly and discontinued within 2 months. No further IVIG therapy was given after her initial hospitalization, owing to both financial constraints and her maintained clinical improvement off semaglutide.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent follow-up in the outpatient clinic, which occurred 10 months after her first assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her previous level of function and had been off immunosuppressive medication for 7 months. Her creatine kinase values stayed within normal limits since her hospitalization and immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of earlier cardiac abnormalities. Additionally, she had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy for conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. While gastrointestinal side effects are most frequently observed, these agents have also been linked to reductions in muscle mass and, less commonly, to rhabdomyolysis. In this case, however, the progression of the patient’s illness aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. Following a single dose of semaglutide, creatine kinase levels rose twelvefold over a period of four months, whereas a typical drug-induced rhabdomyolysis due to direct toxic effects would usually present with normalization of creatine kinase within a few weeks. For reference, a previously documented instance of tirzepatide-related rhabdomyolysis followed an expected pattern: the individual developed a sharp, acute increase in creatine kinase (CK=57,014 at the time of presentation) but experienced rapid improvement with cessation of the medication and supportive hydration over the course of several days, requiring no immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there appears to be a probable association between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM in this patient, as evidenced by an appropriate temporal association, a low likelihood that other conditions or medications are responsible, and notable clinical improvement upon discontinuation. A re-exposure test would not be justified given the severity of the adverse effect observed with semaglutide. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. Given the widespread and increasing use of semaglutide, this case report seeks to alert clinicians to this possible adverse event and highlight the need for enhanced pharmacovigilance. Ongoing and future research will be necessary to determine if the relationship is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

Additionally, it remains uncertain what specific factors may have rendered our patient susceptible to such a profound reaction to semaglutide. As GLP-1 receptor agonists become more widely used, investigations into potential predisposing factors will be increasingly important, especially if more cases with a clear temporal link to semaglutide are identified.
