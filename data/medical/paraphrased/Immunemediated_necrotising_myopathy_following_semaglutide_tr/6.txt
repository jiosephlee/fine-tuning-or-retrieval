Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report discusses a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and increased creatine kinase levels. These symptoms began shortly after a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltration on muscle biopsy. She was treated with immunosuppressive therapy using corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulting in clinical improvement. She was able to regain her baseline functionality after discontinuing immunosuppression for several months and refraining from glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first documented instance of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist administration, with noted benefit from a brief period of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past several years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have seen widespread use for the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the known side effects associated with these agents, reduction in skeletal muscle mass has been reported. Recently, a case report documented rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotising characteristics linked to tirzepatide, a GLP-1 receptor agonist frequently prescribed. In that instance, the patient developed markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels—measured in the thousands—during ongoing treatment, and the CK concentrations began to decline just days following cessation of the drug, a pattern that is consistent with medication-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy marked by rapidly progressive weakness predominantly affecting proximal muscles, accompanied by necrosis of myofibres and minimal lymphocytic infiltration on tissue examination. Two autoantibodies, anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP), have a strong association with IMNM. Notably, HMGCR autoantibody positivity correlates significantly with statin exposure, as statins are frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions. Historically, a number of individuals who developed necrotising myopathy following statin therapy were categorized as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis prior to recognition of the relationship with HMGCR autoantibodies. In these cases, patients often exhibited persistent muscle pathology and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels despite discontinuation of statins. Subsequent improvement after immunosuppressive treatment indicated the presence of an autoimmune myopathy, ultimately facilitating identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of the disease. However, it should be noted that approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative. The absence of detectable autoantibodies in this group introduces further diagnostic complexity and implies the existence of undiscovered autoantibodies or contributing factors.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose medical history includes obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening weakness of the proximal muscles, along with difficulty swallowing and speaking.

She had previously maintained good health and was fully capable of performing daily activities independently. Three years before her current decline, she underwent outpatient workup for elevated transaminases. At that time, both aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were mildly increased, reaching up to twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT values higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts remained within standard ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using medications known to cause liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with uniformly increased echogenicity, suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Serologic testing for viral hepatitis, as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, were all negative. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis compatible with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), corresponding to a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and stage 0. Recommendations included adopting a low-calorie diet and engaging in regular physical activity.

Because her NAFLD and obesity persisted 2.5 years post-diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Shortly after the initial subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. The symptoms continued, resulting in hospital admission two days later with nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal failure, and marked metabolic acidosis. She was managed with intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic medications. A brief stay in the intensive care unit was required for non-invasive ventilatory support. Hydration led to restoration of her renal function, and neither haemofiltration nor haemodialysis was necessary. Creatine kinase at presentation measured 337 U/L. She did not report other complaints such as rash, myalgias, or dark urine suggestive of myoglobinuria. During her hospital stay, she developed episodes of atrial fibrillation, though serum troponin remained within normal range. Discharge occurred after five days; at that point, her gastrointestinal symptoms had resolved, although she remained physically deconditioned. Over the following four months, she experienced progressive muscle weakness. She stated that she had not previously taken statins or consumed mushrooms, red yeast rice, or other supplements linked with IMNM, nor did she take any other nutritional supplements. She also denied any significant family history of muscle disorders, recent vigorous or extended exercise, or alcohol or illicit substance use.

Four months following her initial and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or sit upright without help and, for several days, reported trouble swallowing both liquids and solids. As a result, she sought care at a different hospital, where laboratory evaluation showed her creatine kinase (CK) was elevated at 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Neurology was consulted via telemedicine, and she received daily intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg for 5 days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for rheumatology assessment. On arrival, her vital signs indicated that she was afebrile, maintained normal blood pressure, and was not experiencing any acute distress. Examination noted pronounced muscle weakness, particularly in the proximal groups (see table 1). No rashes, skin changes, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca, oral ulcers, or other neurological abnormalities were observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone therapy, she was started on oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg daily. Because her weakness persisted and her symptoms remained severe, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg over 4 days. Over the following 2 weeks, her creatine kinase (CK) levels showed significant improvement (see figure 1). She was discharged following a nearly 4-week hospital stay. Twelve days post-discharge, she was re-admitted for 10 days due to septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK was found to be within normal range.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional capacity and general well-being continued to improve, and her CK levels remained within normal limits. As a result, her prednisone was tapered and discontinued quickly without complications. Given her improved condition after stopping semaglutide and considering the financial constraints, she did not undergo any further IVIG therapy aside from the initial course administered during her hospitalization.

At her most recent outpatient evaluation, 10 months after starting treatment, she had recovered her pre-illness functional status and had been free from immunosuppressive therapy for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a CK of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a maximum recorded value of 3992 U/L documented one week earlier at the facility from which she was transferred. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels were within the reference range. Aminotransferases were increased, with AST at 191 U/L and ALT at 165 U/L, noted in the context of raised serum CK and a known history of hepatic steatosis. Her autoimmune serology demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement measurements and creatinine were normal, and there was no evidence of cytopenia. A comprehensive myositis antibody screen was also negative, including antibodies against SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A. She did, however, have a low-level positive SS-B antibody at 2.4 (with normal defined as <1.0). Inflammatory markers were raised, with an ESR of 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein at 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, cardiac biomarkers were notable for a HS-Troponin of 62 pg/mL (reference range ≤53 pg/mL) and a proBNP value of 4358 pg/mL (reference interval: 0–125 pg/mL).

Imaging studies performed included MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution CT of the chest, and abdominal MRI. MRI of both femurs showed mild, symmetric muscular edema in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, with additional subcutaneous edema on the lateral thighs (figure 2). The echocardiogram estimated the left ventricular ejection fraction at 65%–70%, and the right ventricular systolic pressure measured 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI detected mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall, accompanied by myocardial edema, without definite late gadolinium enhancement. These findings may be consistent with myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No significant myocardial scar, infarction, or notable valvular dysfunction was seen. Liver ultrasound with Doppler indicated hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT found no evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed a steatotic, enlarged liver, with no other remarkable features and no lymphadenopathy.

Further diagnostic evaluations included both a repeat liver biopsy and a biopsy of thigh muscle. During her hospital stay, the liver biopsy yielded several cores of hepatic tissue showing extensive macrovesicular steatosis, comprising both small and large lipid droplets, which accounted for roughly 70% of the analyzed liver parenchyma; there was an absence of significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. The histological findings correlated with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, demonstrating mild worsening, classified as a NAFLD Activity Score of 5/8, stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis revealed scattered necrotic as well as regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B); immunohistochemical analysis showed positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and CD68, a macrophage marker, indicating myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, membrane attack complex staining was noted on both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), pointing toward an ongoing inflammatory process. Occasional CD3-positive cells, identifying T-cell infiltration (figure 4A), along with perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C), were also observed. Collectively, these muscle biopsy findings were indicative of an IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient demonstrated an immediate and pronounced adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating intolerance to the medication. She initially developed gastrointestinal symptoms and renal failure, which was thought to be secondary to hypovolemia. At presentation, her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly raised, not compatible with rhabdomyolysis. However, she continued to deteriorate despite discontinuation of semaglutide, exhibiting progressive weakness and a twelve-fold rise in CK over a four-month period after semaglutide exposure, findings that point to an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely triggered by semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings consistent with drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical signs of an underlying connective tissue disorder, and persistent recovery following both withdrawal of semaglutide and initial immunosuppressive therapy further strengthen the association.

It is significant that the patient demonstrated elevated transaminases before initiating semaglutide, a finding occasionally associated with myopathies. Nevertheless, she was diagnosed with hepatosteatosis based on liver biopsy three years prior to semaglutide therapy, and her mild AST/ALT elevation continued even after creatine kinase (CK) levels returned to normal. Therefore, the persistent transaminase increase is most likely attributable to a hepatic cause rather than muscular injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not establish a diagnosis of active myocarditis. In this context, stress-related myopathy remains a plausible consideration, especially in light of her swift clinical improvement.

The detection of SS-B antibodies in the absence of ANA and SS-A was unanticipated. To date, there are no documented cases describing isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A resulting in IMNM. Regrettably, we were not able to locate a CK value preceding semaglutide administration, which prevents us from fully ruling out underlying autoimmune IMNM prior to initiating semaglutide. Nevertheless, the twelve-fold increase in CK and the gradual onset of proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide exposure suggest that this IMNM episode is more likely linked to the medication. Furthermore, her significant IMNM resolved after a brief period of corticosteroids and IVIG therapy, combined with discontinuation of semaglutide. This progression is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which typically responds to short-term immunosuppression following removal of the causative drug, rather than idiopathic IMNM that often necessitates extended and aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Owing to ongoing weakness and significant symptoms, IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. She attended multiple outpatient appointments, demonstrating ongoing recovery in both functional capacity and general health, as well as persistently normal creatine kinase levels. On this basis, prednisone was tapered off successfully and rapidly over a 2-month period. Taking into account financial constraints and her ongoing improvement after discontinuation of semaglutide, no further IVIG was given beyond the initial treatment during hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient visit, which took place 10 months after her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had recovered her baseline functional abilities and had been without immunosuppressive medications for 7 months. Her creatine kinase levels stayed within normal limits after her hospitalization and treatment with immunosuppressants. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previous abnormal findings. She additionally remained free from further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in managing metabolic conditions like obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal adverse effects are frequently observed, there is also potential for these agents to cause reduced muscle mass and, in rare cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this instance, the patient’s progression aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. After a single administration of semaglutide, CK values rose 12-fold over a span of 4 months, rather than the rapid normalization of CK within weeks typically expected if semaglutide’s toxic effect were responsible, as is often reported in cases of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. For example, a prior rhabdomyolysis case related to tirzepatide conformed to the conventional course: the patient developed an acute, significant elevation in CK (CK=57,014 at presentation) and showed swift clinical recovery upon cessation of the drug and rehydration within days, without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the association between semaglutide administration and IMNM in this instance is classified as probable, due to the presence of a plausible temporal correlation, the improbability of other diseases or medications as the cause, and the observed improvement upon cessation of semaglutide. Re-administration to confirm causality would not be ethical because of the severity of the adverse event related to semaglutide. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this case. Given the increasing prescription of semaglutide, this report is intended to alert clinicians to this possible adverse reaction and to reinforce the importance of pharmacovigilance. Additional research will help determine if this observed relationship is causal or merely coincidental.

The specific factors that may have rendered our patient susceptible to this serious response to semaglutide remain undetermined. As GLP-1 receptor agonists continue to be widely adopted, investigations aimed at identifying risk factors will be crucial, particularly if further cases demonstrating a similar temporal association with semaglutide use emerge.
