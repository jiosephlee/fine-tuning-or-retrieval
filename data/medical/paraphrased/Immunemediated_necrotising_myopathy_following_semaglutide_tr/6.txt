Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy after semaglutide therapy: a potential contributing role?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report presents a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she exhibited worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels. These clinical manifestations emerged after receiving a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings indicative of necrotising myopathy, including the presence of inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. The patient showed clinical improvement in response to immunosuppressive therapy with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Over time, she achieved a gradual recovery, returning to her premorbid level of functioning, remaining off immunosuppression for several months, and continuing to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report documents the first known instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, with an apparent favorable response to a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized in the management of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Although these agents offer significant therapeutic benefits, they have also been linked to adverse outcomes, notably a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. Notably, a recent case report documented the occurrence of rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotizing characteristics in a patient taking tirzepatide, another frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this report, the patient experienced a marked elevation in creatine kinase (CK) levels into the thousands during active drug administration; importantly, CK concentrations began to decline within a few days after cessation of the medication, a temporal relationship that supports the diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique category within the spectrum of myopathies, marked by pronounced proximal muscle weakness and histopathological evidence of myofibre necrosis with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. IMNM is strongly linked to two autoantibodies: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Notably, anti-HMGCR antibodies are frequently found in individuals treated with statins, drugs routinely prescribed for metabolic conditions. Historically, a subset of statin-treated patients who developed necrotising myopathy were mistakenly labelled as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis before the relationship with HMGCR autoantibodies was established. In these instances, affected individuals continued to experience muscle weakness and elevated CK levels even after discontinuing statin therapy. Clinical improvement upon initiation of immunosuppressive therapy supported the hypothesis of an underlying autoimmune process and ultimately contributed to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibodies as a diagnostic hallmark of IMNM. Nevertheless, approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative for known autoantibodies. The absence of detectable autoantibodies in these individuals complicates diagnosis and indicates that other autoantibodies and precipitating factors remain unidentified.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade, whose past medical history included obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a previous episode of deep venous thrombosis with pulmonary embolism, came to medical attention due to worsening proximal muscle weakness as well as new-onset dysphagia and dysphonia.

Prior to the onset of her clinical decline, the patient enjoyed good overall health and maintained complete independence in daily activities. Approximately three years before her current deterioration, she began outpatient assessment due to elevated transaminase levels. During this period, laboratory results revealed mild increases in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), both nearly double the upper limit of normal, with ALT levels surpassing those of AST. Other biochemical parameters, including alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts, were all within normal reference ranges. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any medications known to be hepatotoxic. An abdominal ultrasound identified hepatomegaly accompanied by diffusely increased echotexture, findings most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Serologic testing for viral hepatitis, as well as assays for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, yielded negative results. Liver biopsy confirmed steatohepatitis, aligning with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 4/8, stage 0. She received recommendations to adopt a calorie-restricted diet and to engage in regular physical activity.

Owing to ongoing fatty liver disease and obesity 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was initiated. After administration of her initial subcutaneous semaglutide dose (0.25 mg), she rapidly developed symptoms of nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued unabated, prompting her hospital admission 2 days later due to persistent nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal insufficiency, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate therapy, and anti-emetic medications. She was temporarily placed in the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal function recovered with intravenous hydration alone, obviating the need for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. Upon admission, her creatine kinase (CK) level was found to be elevated at 337 U/L; notably, she exhibited no rash, myalgia, or dark-coloured urine. While hospitalized, she experienced an episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though her serum troponin remained within normal limits. She was discharged after 5 days, at which point her gastrointestinal complaints had resolved, though she remained physically debilitated. In the following 4 months, she experienced a gradual increase in weakness. She reported no previous statin use, no substantial intake of supplements linked to IMNM such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, and no use of any other nutritional supplements. Additionally, she did not report a family history of myopathy, recent intense or prolonged exercise, or the consumption of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition progressively worsened. She became unable to maintain an upright posture independently, whether standing or sitting, and developed new difficulties swallowing both liquids and solids that persisted for several days. As a result, she sought care at an external hospital, where initial labs showed her creatine kinase (CK) level was markedly elevated at 3992 U/L (normal: 12–191 U/L). Neurology was consulted remotely, and she received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary facility for further assessment by rheumatology. On evaluation, her vital signs were stable—afebrile and normotensive—and she appeared comfortable. Neuromuscular examination demonstrated notable proximal muscle weakness (see table 1). No skin manifestations such as rash or changes, crackles on lung auscultation, synovitis, oedema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological impairments were detected.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone therapy, the patient was transitioned to oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Owing to ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced clinical manifestations, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg over the course of 4 days. Over a 2-week period, there was a significant reduction in her CK levels (see figure 1). She was discharged following a hospital stay of almost 4 weeks. However, 12 days post-discharge, she required readmission for 10 days due to septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this episode, her CK remained within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which she maintained improvements in both her functional abilities and general health, along with consistently normal CK values. Consequently, her prednisone was tapered and discontinued without difficulty. Given her sustained progress after discontinuing semaglutide and financial considerations, no further IVIG was administered beyond the initial inpatient treatment.

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months following her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her baseline level of functioning and had been free from immunosuppressive medication for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory investigations revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a maximum recorded value of 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) remained within normal parameters. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, both in the context of raised serum CK and a known diagnosis of hepatic steatosis. Autoimmune serology was significant for the absence of anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement concentrations and renal function were within normal ranges, and no cytopenias were observed. Myositis antibody testing yielded negative results for SRP and HMGCR, in addition to Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. However, a mildly elevated SS-B autoantibody was detected at a titre of 2.4 (reference: <1.0). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was increased at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein was 22.8 mg/L. It is notable that she also demonstrated an elevated high-sensitivity Troponin (HS-Troponin) of 62 pg/mL (normal range ≤53 pg/mL) and a pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) of 4358 pg/mL (normal range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging investigations comprised MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of the bilateral femurs demonstrated mild and symmetrical edema in both legs, with the greatest involvement in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles; subcutaneous edema was also identified along the lateral regions of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiographic evaluation revealed a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70%, alongside a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI indicated mild hypokinesis within the mid-anteroseptal and anterior myocardial segments, accompanied by myocardial edema and absence of late gadolinium enhancement, suggestive of early myocarditis or possible localized stress cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of substantial myocardial scarring, infarction, or relevant valvular disease. Liver ultrasound with Doppler demonstrated the presence of hepatic steatosis. High-resolution CT imaging of the chest ruled out interstitial lung disease and lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI confirmed steatotic enlargement of the liver, but otherwise showed no abnormal findings or lymphadenopathy.

Further advanced diagnostic evaluation involved a repeat liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospitalization, revealed multiple cores of liver tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, affecting roughly 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, without evidence of significant portal inflammatory infiltrates or interface hepatitis. These histopathological features were aligned with her pre-existing diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, reflecting mild advancement to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5/8, stage 1. Examination of the right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy showed scattered necrotic and regenerating myofibres (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical staining identified acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), indicative of macrophage presence, with observed myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, a positive stain for the membrane attack complex was noted on both necrotic and viable myofibres (figure 4C), supporting the presence of an inflammatory process. Occasional CD3-positive (a marker of T cells) cells (figure 4A), along with perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C), were also identified. Collectively, the muscle biopsy findings were in keeping with a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited a rapid and pronounced adverse response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. Initially, she manifested gastrointestinal disturbances and renal failure likely attributed to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) levels were only mildly elevated—an increase not characteristic of rhabdomyolysis—during the first assessment, her clinical deterioration persisted after discontinuation of semaglutide. The pattern of muscle weakness and a twelvefold rise in CK levels over four months post-semaglutide exposure are consistent with the development of an IMNM potentially triggered by semaglutide. Additional evidence supporting this link includes histopathological findings in line with drug-induced IMNM, an absence of clinical indicators pointing to an underlying connective tissue disorder, and sustained clinical improvement following the initiation of immunosuppressive therapy combined with withdrawal of semaglutide.

It is worth mentioning that she exhibited elevated transaminase levels before initiating semaglutide therapy, a phenomenon that can occur in the context of myopathies. Nonetheless, she had a liver biopsy-confirmed diagnosis of hepatosteatosis three years before beginning semaglutide, and her marginally raised AST/ALT values persisted even after creatine kinase concentrations returned to normal. Therefore, it is most probable that her chronically increased transaminases are attributable to liver pathology rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not yield definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. In light of her clinical scenario and swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy would also constitute a plausible explanation.

The detection of positive SS-B antibodies despite negative ANA and SS-A findings was unanticipated. Currently, there is no literature documenting cases of isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A resulting in IMNM. It is noteworthy that we lacked access to CK levels prior to the commencement of semaglutide, precluding us from definitively ruling out a pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM. Nevertheless, the occurrence of a twelvefold increase in CK levels and the onset of progressive proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide administration suggest that this episode of IMNM was likely triggered by the medication. Furthermore, the patient’s severe IMNM responded favorably to a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG, combined with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern of rapid clinical resolution is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which often improves with short-term immunosuppression after removal of the causative agent, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which generally necessitates prolonged and intensive immunosuppressive therapy.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, distributed over 4 days because of ongoing significant weakness and the presence of severe symptoms. Subsequent outpatient follow-up visits demonstrated continuous gains in her physical abilities and general health, with CK levels remaining within normal range throughout. These improvements permitted a swift tapering and discontinuation of prednisone within 2 months. Owing to a combination of financial factors and her maintained progress after discontinuation of semaglutide, no further IVIG treatments were administered beyond the initial hospitalization course.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, occurring 10 months after her first assessment and the commencement of treatment, she had returned to her previous level of functioning and had been off immunosuppressive therapy for 7 months. Her CK values consistently remained within normal limits subsequent to her hospital discharge and immunosuppressive therapy. Follow-up echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the previously observed abnormalities. Furthermore, there was no recurrence of her paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists—including semaglutide—are increasingly utilized and have shown proven efficacy in treating metabolic diseases like type 2 diabetes and obesity. Although gastrointestinal symptoms remain the most frequently encountered adverse reactions, these agents have also been linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in rare cases, rhabdomyolysis. In the case presented here, however, the patient’s trajectory aligns more closely with IMNM rather than with rhabdomyolysis. Over the course of 4 months after a single administration of semaglutide, CK levels rose 12-fold. This pattern diverges from that observed in instances of direct semaglutide-induced rhabdomyolysis, where CK values typically normalize within several weeks. In contrast, a previous report involving tirzepatide-induced rhabdomyolysis detailed a classic clinical pattern: the patient exhibited a pronounced acute surge in CK (CK=57 014 at initial evaluation) that swiftly resolved within days after discontinuing the drug and providing hydration—without necessitating immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the causality assessment guidelines established by the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC), the association between semaglutide administration and the onset of IMNM in this instance can be classified as probable. This is supported by the appropriate temporal association, the lack of alternative explanations involving other drugs or underlying diseases, and the notable improvement following drug discontinuation. Given the severity of the adverse reaction, conducting a rechallenge would not be ethically permissible. Nevertheless, it should be emphasized that definitive causation cannot be conclusively established in this case. With the increasing prevalence of semaglutide use, this case is intended to alert clinicians to a possible adverse event and to underscore the importance of ongoing pharmacovigilance. Further research will be required to determine whether this observed association is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

The specific factors that may have made our patient susceptible to this pronounced response to semaglutide are still undetermined. As the utilization of GLP-1 receptor agonists continues to rise, investigating potential predisposing factors will be essential, especially if additional instances with a strong temporal association to semaglutide administration are observed.
