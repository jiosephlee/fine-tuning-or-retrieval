Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. She experienced progressive dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels over a four-month period. These symptoms emerged after a single administration of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated necrotising myopathy with evidence of inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. The patient was treated with immunosuppressive therapy, including corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulting in clinical improvement. She made a gradual recovery, ultimately regaining her premorbid level of function, and remained off immunosuppression for several months, while consistently avoiding glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This case represents the first documented instance of IMNM likely related to the use of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, showing apparent resolution following a brief course of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

In the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized to treat metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the reported adverse reactions to these agents is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report documented rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing changes linked to tirzepatide, another frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that instance, the patient experienced a marked rise in creatine kinase (CK) levels into the thousands during ongoing therapy; CK levels began to decline within several days after cessation of the drug, a pattern indicative of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy defined by rapid-onset proximal muscle weakness, presence of necrotic myofibres, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration on histopathology. Two autoantibodies closely linked with IMNM are anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Notably, exposure to statins—commonly prescribed for metabolic conditions—has a strong correlation with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Prior to the recognition of the connection with HMGCR autoantibodies, multiple cases of statin users presenting with necrotising myopathy were often mistakenly labeled as toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In many of these individuals, muscular symptoms and elevated creatine kinase levels persisted even after discontinuation of statin therapy. These persistent manifestations, combined with improvement following immunosuppressive treatment, pointed toward an underlying autoimmune myopathy, which subsequently led to the identification of the HMGCR autoantibody as a defining marker of this disorder. Importantly, approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not test positive for known autoantibodies. This seronegative status introduces further complexity in diagnosis and implies that additional autoantibodies or unidentified triggering factors may exist.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, whose medical background included obesity, fatty liver (hepatosteatosis), hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, along with difficulties swallowing and changes in her voice.

Before these symptoms developed, she was in overall good health and could independently manage all her daily activities. Three years prior to the onset of her muscle symptoms, she was assessed as an outpatient due to elevated liver enzymes. At that evaluation, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly raised, each to about double the upper normal limit, with ALT higher than AST. Her alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count were all within normal values. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any medications known to cause liver injury. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with uniformly increased echogenicity, findings suggestive of fatty infiltration. Testing for viral hepatitis, as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, returned negative results. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis consistent with a diagnosis of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and fibrosis at stage 0. She was advised to adhere to a reduced-calorie diet and to incorporate regular exercise into her routine.

Ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity persisted 2.5 years following her NAFLD diagnosis, prompting initiation of semaglutide therapy. Shortly after receiving her first subcutaneous injection of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, leading to hospital admission two days later for refractory nausea, vomiting, poor oral intake, dyspnoea, acute kidney injury, and severe metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and antiemetics. She required a brief stay in the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. With intravenous hydration, her renal function recovered, and there was no requirement for haemofiltration or dialysis. On admission, her creatine kinase was elevated at 337 U/L. She reported no additional symptoms, such as rash, myalgia, or dark-coloured urine. During this admission, she experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, but her serum troponin remained within normal limits. She was discharged after a 5-day hospitalization. At discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had resolved, although she remained physically deconditioned. Over the subsequent four months, she developed gradually worsening weakness. She denied previous statin therapy, notable intake of IMNM-related supplements like mushrooms or red yeast rice, or any other nutritional supplements. She also denied a significant family history of myopathy, recent excessive or sustained exercise, and did not report alcohol or illicit substance use.

Four months after receiving her initial and only dose of semaglutide, the patient's condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or sit upright without support and developed progressive dysphagia affecting both liquids and solids for several days. As a result, she sought care at an outside hospital, where laboratory testing revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Management included a 5-day course of intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily, administered while she was under neurology consultation via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for further rheumatological assessment. On arrival, she was noted to be afebrile, normotensive, and appeared comfortable otherwise. Examination identified pronounced proximal muscle weakness (see table 1), without evidence of cutaneous eruptions, dermatological changes, pulmonary crackles, joint inflammation, swelling, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological abnormalities.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she was started on oral prednisone at a daily dose of 1 mg/kg. Due to ongoing significant weakness and severe symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a dose of 2 g/kg, divided over four days. Over a two-week period, her CK levels showed a substantial decline (see figure 1). Her total hospital stay lasted nearly four weeks before she was discharged. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for ten days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK levels remained within normal limits.

During subsequent outpatient visits, she exhibited steady recovery in both functional ability and general well-being, with CK levels continuing to be normal. As a result, she was rapidly tapered off prednisone without difficulty. Because of both financial considerations and sustained clinical improvement after stopping semaglutide, no further courses of IVIG were administered following her initial treatment during hospitalization.

She was last seen in the outpatient setting 10 months after her initial evaluation and treatment initiation and had regained her baseline functional status, off immunosuppression for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L upon assessment, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded a week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within the reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were measured at 191 U/L and 165 U/L, respectively, against a background of elevated CK and a known history of fatty liver disease. Autoantibody testing showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and serum creatinine were within normal limits, and cytopenias were absent. Myositis-specific panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. However, she did demonstrate a low-titre positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein (CRP) was 22.8 mg/L. High-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was raised at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was markedly elevated at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies included MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI scans of both femurs identified mild, symmetric edema within the muscles of both thighs, predominantly at the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps regions, along with subcutaneous edema at the outer sides of both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography estimated a left ventricular ejection fraction between 65% and 70%, with a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall with evidence of myocardial edema, and no clear late gadolinium enhancement; this was thought to reflect myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly a focal, stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There were no findings of significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or major valvular dysfunction. Liver ultrasound with Doppler demonstrated hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest did not reveal any interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. MRI of the abdomen showed steatotic hepatomegaly but was otherwise normal, without evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced diagnostic procedures included a repeat biopsy of the liver and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, carried out during her hospitalization, revealed several fragments of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, involving about 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results correlated with her previous history of hepatosteatosis, showing slight progression to an NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8 and classified as stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered areas of necrotic and regenerating myofibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and for cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a marker for macrophages, associated with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, membrane attack complex staining was observed on both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), indicative of an inflammatory reaction. Rare CD3-positive (T-cell marker) cells (figure 4A) as well as perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were also seen. Collectively, these muscle biopsy findings were compatible with a diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient had an acute and marked reaction following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating possible intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal complaints and developed renal failure thought to be due to hypovolemia. At the outset, creatine kinase (CK) levels were only mildly raised, making rhabdomyolysis unlikely; however, the patient’s persistent deterioration despite stopping semaglutide, in addition to the distribution of muscle weakness and a twelvefold CK increase within four months post-semanglutide exposure, are indicative of an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings were compatible with a drug-induced IMNM, the absence of clinical features characteristic of a primary connective tissue disorder, and sustained clinical and laboratory improvement following immunosuppression and discontinuation of semaglutide, all further reinforce this proposed association.

It is important to highlight that the patient exhibited elevated transaminase levels before starting semaglutide, a finding that may occur in cases of myopathy. Nonetheless, three years before semaglutide initiation, she underwent a liver biopsy that confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mildly raised AST/ALT continued even after normalization of CK values. This suggests that the chronic transaminase elevation most likely stems from liver rather than muscle involvement. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not provide clear evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Given the clinical scenario and her prompt improvement, stress-induced myopathy remains a plausible consideration.

The detection of SS-B antibody alone, in the absence of ANA and SS-A positivity, was an unexpected finding. To date, there are no documented cases in the literature of IMNM presenting with isolated SS-B positivity and negative ANA and SS-A results. We were unable to retrieve any CK levels measured prior to the patient's initiation of semaglutide. As a result, we cannot conclusively rule out the presence of subclinical autoimmune IMNM before semaglutide therapy began. Nonetheless, the observation of a twelve-fold CK elevation and the onset of progressive proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide administration suggest a greater probability that this IMNM episode was triggered by the medication. Moreover, the patient’s severe IMNM responded favorably to a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG, coupled with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM—which often remits after short-term immunosuppression and removal of the causative drug—than with idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates sustained, aggressive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Because of her ongoing significant weakness and severe symptoms, IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg over 4 days. Outpatient follow-up visits demonstrated steady gains in her functional abilities and general health, with CK levels remaining within the normal range. As a result, prednisone was tapered off quickly and discontinued within 2 months. Owing to both financial factors and her maintained improvement without semaglutide, she did not undergo any further IVIG infusions beyond the initial inpatient treatment.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months after her initial assessment and the start of therapy, her most recent outpatient visit demonstrated recovery to her prior level of functioning, having discontinued immunosuppressive medications for the previous seven months. Her creatine kinase values stayed within the normal limits following hospitalization and immunosuppressive therapy. Follow-up echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of earlier abnormal findings. Additionally, she had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are frequent, these agents may also contribute to reduced muscle mass and, in some instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the patient's symptoms and laboratory findings better align with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. After a single administration of semaglutide, CK values rose by a factor of 12 over a 4-month period, whereas we would typically expect CK normalization within a few weeks if semaglutide-induced toxicity were responsible, as observed in other reports of drug-related rhabdomyolysis. Significantly, the previously documented rhabdomyolysis event involving tirzepatide displayed the classic pattern: there was a pronounced acute spike in CK (CK=57,014 at presentation) that resolved quickly following cessation of the medication and hydration, all without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there is a probable association between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM in our patient, as the temporal association is plausible, alternative causes such as other conditions or medications are unlikely, and withdrawal of semaglutide resulted in a clinically justifiable improvement. A rechallenge was deemed inappropriate due to the severity of the reaction attributed to semaglutide. Nevertheless, we recognize that a definitive causal link cannot be established in this instance. In light of the rapid increase in semaglutide usage, this case report serves to alert clinicians to this possible side effect and to promote active pharmacovigilance. Additional research is required to determine whether the observed relationship is causal or merely coincidental.

Furthermore, the factors that may have made our patient susceptible to this severe reaction with semaglutide remain unidentified. As the utilization of GLP-1 receptor agonists continues to rise, it will be critical for future research to investigate potential risk factors, particularly if similar temporally related cases are identified.
