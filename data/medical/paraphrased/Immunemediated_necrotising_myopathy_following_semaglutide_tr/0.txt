Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case concerns a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over four months, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase. These manifestations appeared after she received a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings of necrotising myopathy with inflammatory cells on muscle biopsy, and her condition improved with immunosuppressive therapy, including corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). She experienced gradual recovery and was able to return to her previous level of function, remaining off immunosuppression for several months and abstaining from glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This represents the first documented instance of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, showing resolution following a brief period of immunosuppressive treatment and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past several years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely prescribed for metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. One reported adverse effect for these agents is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. Recently, a case report highlighted an instance of rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics linked to tirzepatide, another widely utilized GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this report, the patient developed creatine kinase (CK) levels in the thousands during ongoing therapy, and CK values began to decrease within a few days of stopping the medication, supporting a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a specific form of myopathy defined by rapidly progressive weakness of proximal muscles, histopathological evidence of necrotic myofibres, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) demonstrate a strong association with IMNM. Notably, statins—widely used to treat metabolic conditions—are closely linked with HMGCR autoantibody presence. Prior to the recognition of this association, several patients treated with statins developed signs of necrotising myopathy and were frequently diagnosed with toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these individuals, muscle symptoms and elevated CK levels often persisted even after statin therapy was discontinued. Resolution following immunosuppressive treatment indicated an autoimmune etiology, ultimately leading to identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a characteristic marker for the disorder. It should be emphasized, however, that approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not display detectable autoantibodies. The absence of autoantibody in this population not only creates further diagnostic difficulties but also implies the existence of other related autoantibodies and triggers yet to be recognized.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a past medical history significant for obesity, fatty liver disease, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and changes in her voice.

Prior to her current decline, she maintained good health and independence in daily functioning. Three years before symptom onset, she underwent outpatient workup due to elevated liver enzymes. At that point, laboratory studies revealed mildly increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), both roughly two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. Other liver-associated tests, including alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count, were all within normal ranges. The patient reported no alcohol consumption and had not been exposed to hepatotoxic drugs. Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated hepatomegaly along with diffusely increased echogenicity, findings interpreted as most compatible with hepatic steatosis. Investigations for viral hepatitis as well as testing for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies were negative. Liver biopsy confirmed steatohepatitis in line with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), reporting an NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and fibrosis stage 0. She was counseled on the importance of a low-calorie diet and regular physical activity.

Given ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide was started. Shortly after administration of her first subcutaneous dose (0.25 mg), she developed nausea and vomiting. These complaints persisted, leading to hospital admission two days later for continued nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, shortness of breath, acute kidney injury, and profound metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic therapy. A brief stay in the intensive care unit was required for non-invasive ventilation support. Renal function improved following hydration, without necessitating haemodialysis or haemofiltration. Upon admission, creatine kinase was noted to be elevated at 337 U/L. She exhibited no additional findings such as rash, myalgia, or dark-colored urine. During this admission, she developed episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though serum troponins remained within normal limits. After five days of inpatient care, she was discharged with resolved gastrointestinal complaints but persistent deconditioning. In the four months post-discharge, progressive weakness developed. She reported no prior statin use, significant ingestion of other agents linked with IMNM such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, nor any use of nutritional supplements. There was no family history of myopathies, nor any recent history of excessive physical exertion or consumption of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her initial and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to deteriorate. She became unable to stand or remain seated without support and developed several days of difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids. As a result, she sought care at an external hospital, where laboratory assessment revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference: 12–191 U/L) at the time of admission. Management included intravenous methylprednisolone at 1000 mg daily for 5 days, administered under neurology guidance via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary medical center for rheumatological assessment, where she was noted to be afebrile, with normal blood pressure, and otherwise without distress. Examination demonstrated pronounced proximal muscle weakness (see table 1), though no evidence of rash, skin changes, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcerations, or other neurological abnormalities was observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she commenced oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg daily. Due to ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Her CK levels demonstrated significant reduction over a period of 2 weeks (figure 1). She was discharged following an inpatient stay that lasted nearly 4 weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she required rehospitalisation for 10 days because of septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK was found to be within normal limits.

She was monitored on multiple occasions in the outpatient setting, demonstrating ongoing enhancements in both functional capacity and general health, along with consistently normal CK measurements. This favorable response allowed for a rapid taper of prednisone. Because of cost considerations and her sustained progress following cessation of semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered beyond the initial series during her hospitalization.

At her most recent outpatient visit, which occurred 10 months after first presentation and the commencement of therapy, she had returned to her baseline level of function and had been without immunosuppressive treatment for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory assessment revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at presentation, with the highest value recorded as 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels were found to be within normal parameters. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was measured at 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 165 U/L, both in the context of increased CK and a prior diagnosis of fatty liver. Autoantibody testing showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibody A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement component levels, as well as creatinine, were within normal limits, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. A myositis-specific antibody panel returned negative for antibodies against signal recognition particle (SRP), HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-gamma, NXP-2, and cN-1A. A low titer SS-B autoantibody was detected at 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised to 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein was increased to 22.8 mg/L. High-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was also elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference: ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

Imaging studies included MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. Bilateral femur MRI showed mild, symmetric muscular edema primarily in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps regions, accompanied by subcutaneous edema on the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). The echocardiogram indicated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall areas, with associated myocardial edema and absence of late gadolinium enhancement, findings possibly consistent with myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or potentially focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or valvular dysfunction was identified. Liver ultrasound with Doppler revealed hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT showed no evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI demonstrated steatotic hepatomegaly, with no other remarkable findings and no lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced diagnostic evaluations included a repeat liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, obtained during her hospital stay, showed several pieces of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, involving around 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, with no significant portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These results correlated with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis and suggested a mild progression of her liver pathology, classified as NAFLD Activity Score grade 5 out of 8, stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B); immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated acid phosphatase staining (figure 3D) and positivity for cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a macrophage marker, with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Staining for membrane attack complex was observed in both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), indicative of an inflammatory reaction. Additionally, infrequent CD3-positive cells, marking T cells (figure 4A), and inflammatory cells situated perivascularly (figure 3C) were present. The combination of findings on muscle biopsy supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited an abrupt and pronounced response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of intolerance. Early manifestations consisted of gastrointestinal disturbances and renal failure, which were thought to be due to hypovolaemia. Although creatine kinase (CK) was only slightly elevated on initial assessment, not aligning with rhabdomyolysis, her ongoing clinical deterioration off semaglutide, the pattern of muscle weakness, and a 12-fold increase in CK levels over the subsequent 4 months following semaglutide exposure point to an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to semaglutide. Additionally, histopathology demonstrating characteristics of drug-induced IMNM, absence of other clinical evidence suggesting underlying connective tissue disease, and sustained clinical improvement upon discontinuation of semaglutide and immunosuppressive therapy further reinforce this association.

It is significant that transaminase elevation was present before she began taking semaglutide, a finding sometimes associated with myopathies. Nevertheless, she was diagnosed with hepatosteatosis by liver biopsy three years prior to semaglutide initiation, and her mildly increased AST and ALT levels remained even after her CK concentrations returned to normal. Therefore, the persistent elevation of her transaminases is most likely attributable to liver pathology rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not yield definitive evidence of active myocarditis. Given her swift improvement, a stress-related myopathy is also plausible in this context.

The detection of SS-B antibody alone, with negative results for ANA and SS-A, was surprising in this case. To date, there are no documented instances of isolated SS-B positivity, without ANA or SS-A, that have been linked to IMNM. We did not have access to CK values prior to the start of semaglutide, so it is not possible to definitively rule out an underlying, mild autoimmune IMNM predating semaglutide administration. Nevertheless, the twelve-fold increase in CK levels and the gradual worsening of proximal muscle strength following semaglutide suggest a higher probability that this occurrence of IMNM was triggered by the medication. Furthermore, the patient’s severe IMNM responded quickly to a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG, combined with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern of recovery is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM—which may respond rapidly to short-term immunosuppression after cessation of the causative drug—compared with idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates more sustained and aggressive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was started on oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Owing to ongoing weakness and significant symptoms, IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. She was monitored as an outpatient multiple times, demonstrating progressive enhancement in her functional abilities and general well-being, with creatine kinase levels remaining within the normal range. As a result, her prednisone was able to be tapered off successfully over 2 months. Because of financial factors and her stable improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide, no further IVIG was given beyond the initial inpatient regimen.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months following her first assessment and commencement of therapy, her most recent outpatient follow-up indicated restoration of baseline functional abilities, with immunosuppressive agents discontinued for the preceding seven months. Since her hospital stay and immunosuppressive treatment, her creatine kinase values persisted within normal limits. Repeat cardiac imaging—including echocardiogram and MRI—demonstrated resolution of earlier abnormal findings. Additionally, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation had not recurred.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal adverse effects are most frequently reported, these agents may also be linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in rare instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, however, the patient’s presentation aligns more with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. The patient's CK levels rose 12-fold over a 4-month period following a single dose of semaglutide; if the drug had directly caused toxic rhabdomyolysis, it would be anticipated that CK values would return to normal within weeks, as observed in other cases of medication-related rhabdomyolysis. Of particular note, a previously documented instance of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide followed the expected course: the patient experienced a significant acute increase in CK (CK=57 014 at admission) and demonstrated prompt clinical improvement after stopping the drug and receiving hydration over several days, without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case demonstrates a probable association between semaglutide administration and IMNM, as the temporal sequence is appropriate, IMNM is unlikely to be attributable to other conditions or medications, and clinical improvement was observed following discontinuation. Furthermore, given the severity of the adverse event linked to semaglutide, a rechallenge would not be ethically permissible. Nonetheless, we recognize that causality cannot be confirmed in this instance. With the widespread increase in semaglutide use, this report seeks to inform clinicians about this potential side effect and promote pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be necessary to determine whether this relationship is causal or merely coincidental.

The factors that might have predisposed our patient to develop such a pronounced reaction to semaglutide remain undefined. As GLP-1 receptor agonists gain in prevalence, studies investigating underlying susceptibility factors will be essential if other cases with a close temporal association to semaglutide use emerge.
