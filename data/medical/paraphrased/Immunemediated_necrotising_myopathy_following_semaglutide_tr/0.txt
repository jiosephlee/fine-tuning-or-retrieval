Semaglutide therapy and subsequent onset of immune-mediated necrotising myopathy: a possible association?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This paper presents the case of a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. The patient experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels over a four-month period, with symptoms beginning after just one semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation indicated necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrates observed on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) led to clinical improvement. She ultimately regained her baseline functional abilities after several months off immunosuppressive therapy, provided she continued to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. To our knowledge, this is the first documented instance of IMNM likely related to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, with notable improvement following a limited duration of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

In recent times, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely prescribed for the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. However, these agents have also been linked to certain adverse outcomes, including the reduction of skeletal muscle mass. Notably, a recent case report detailed an instance of rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics linked to tirzepatide, which is another GLP-1 receptor agonist in common clinical use. In the documented case, the patient experienced a significant elevation in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching several thousand units, during active therapy; importantly, the CK levels began to decrease within a few days following cessation of the medication, strongly suggesting a causal relationship between the drug and the onset of rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique subtype of myopathy, marked by rapid onset proximal muscle weakness, the presence of necrotic muscle fibres, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration as observed in histopathological examinations. Two autoantibodies, anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP), show a strong correlation with IMNM. Notably, anti-HMGCR autoantibody has a significant association with statin therapy, which is widely prescribed for metabolic conditions. Before recognizing the link between HMGCR autoantibodies and necrotising myopathy, several statin-exposed individuals developed myopathic symptoms that were typically misclassified as toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Many such individuals continued to exhibit muscle weakness and elevated CK levels even after discontinuing statin treatment. The resolution of symptoms with immunosuppressive interventions pointed towards an autoimmune aetiology, ultimately leading to the identification of the HMGCR autoantibody as a key marker of this condition. Despite these discoveries, approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not demonstrate detectable autoantibodies. This seronegativity complicates the diagnostic process and indicates that additional, yet unidentified autoantibodies and causative factors may exist within this population.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade of life, whose past medical history includes obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep venous thrombosis with pulmonary embolism, reported gradual worsening of proximal muscle weakness, along with difficulty swallowing and speaking.

Previously, she enjoyed robust health and maintained full independence in her daily life. Three years before her health declined, she was being assessed in the outpatient setting for elevated transaminase levels. At that juncture, laboratory results revealed mild increases in aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), each approximately twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT exceeding AST. Measurements of alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count were all within their respective normal reference ranges. She reported abstinence from alcohol and denied the use of hepatotoxic drugs. Abdominal ultrasonography identified hepatomegaly accompanied by a globally increased echotexture, findings suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Testing for viral hepatitis markers as well as for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies yielded negative results. Histopathologic examination from liver biopsy revealed steatohepatitis consistent with a diagnosis of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8, and stage 0 fibrosis. She received recommendations to adopt a calorie-restricted diet and engage in regular physical activity.

As her NAFLD persisted alongside obesity 2.5 years post-diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was initiated. Shortly after receiving her initial semaglutide injection (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she began to experience nausea and vomiting. These symptoms did not abate, leading to her hospital admission two days later with ongoing nausea and vomiting, reduced oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal failure, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic medications. She required a brief stay in the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation support. Renal function subsequently improved following hydration, with no requirement for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. At presentation, her creatine kinase (CK) was elevated at 337 U/L; however, she did not report any additional symptoms such as muscle pain, skin rash, or dark urine. During her hospitalisation, she developed intermittent atrial fibrillation, yet her serum troponin remained within the normal range. Discharge occurred after a 5-day hospital stay. Upon discharge, while her gastrointestinal symptoms had resolved, she remained deconditioned. Over the next four months, she noticed gradually worsening weakness. She specifically denied prior statin use, ingestion of mushrooms, red yeast rice, or other supplements commonly linked to IMNM, and did not report taking any other nutritional supplements. There was also no significant family history of myopathy, no recent unusual or prolonged exertion, nor any use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her initial and sole administration of semaglutide, the patient experienced progressive deterioration. She lost the ability to stand or sit unassisted, and over several days developed dysphagia affecting both liquids and solids. These symptoms led her to seek care at an external hospital, where laboratory evaluation revealed an elevated CK level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Management included intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg per day for five days, administered under remote neurology supervision. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for further rheumatologic assessment. On arrival, she was afebrile, maintained normal blood pressure, and displayed no overt signs of distress. Physical assessment identified marked muscle weakness predominantly affecting proximal muscles (see table 1). There were no observable rashes, skin abnormalities, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological deficits.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone therapy, she transitioned to a daily regimen of oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Owing to ongoing muscle weakness and pronounced clinical symptoms, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Over a 2-week period, her CK levels showed substantial reduction (see figure 1). She was discharged following an inpatient stay of almost 4 weeks. However, 12 days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK values remained within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional abilities and general health continued to progress, and her CK levels remained consistently within normal limits. Consequently, her prednisone was tapered quickly and discontinued without complications. Owing both to financial considerations and her significant clinical improvement after stopping semaglutide, she did not undergo further IVIG treatments beyond the initial course administered during her hospitalization.

At her most recent follow-up in the outpatient clinic, which occurred 10 months following her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her prior level of functioning and had been free of immunosuppressive medications for the past 8 months.

Investigations:

The laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a prior peak value of 3992 U/L reported one week earlier by the referring facility. Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) measurements fell within normal parameters. Aspartate transaminase (AST) was measured at 191 U/L, and alanine transaminase (ALT) at 165 U/L, both in the context of increased serum CK and a documented history of fatty liver disease. The autoantibody assessment was remarkable for the absence of anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels were unremarkable, creatinine was normal, and there was no evidence of cytopenia. The comprehensive myositis antibody panel yielded negative results for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. However, she did demonstrate a low titre positivity for SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (normal reference: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised at 22 mm/hour, accompanied by an elevated C-reactive protein level of 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was also increased at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL) and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was significantly elevated at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging assessments comprised MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler studies, high-resolution CT of the chest, and abdominal MRI. Bilateral femur MRI demonstrated mild, symmetric edema within the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, accompanied by subcutaneous edema on the lateral sides of both thighs (see figure 2). The echocardiogram revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated between 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis localized to the mid-anteroseptal and anterior cardiac walls, with associated myocardial edema and an absence of significant late gadolinium enhancement; this pattern may suggest myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or alternatively, a focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No evidence of considerable myocardial scarring, infarction, or major valvular dysfunction was present. Liver ultrasound with Doppler indicated hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest did not show any interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI revealed a steatotic, enlarged liver, but was otherwise unremarkable, with no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Further advanced investigations included a second liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, obtained during her hospital stay, revealed multiple samples of hepatic tissue characterized by both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, involving roughly 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, without any notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her previous diagnosis of hepatosteatosis and indicated mild progression of liver pathology, corresponding to NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. The muscle biopsy taken from the right vastus lateralis exhibited scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis showed positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68)—a marker for macrophages—with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, there was positive staining for the membrane attack complex on both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), indicating an active inflammatory process. Occasional CD3-positive T cells (figure 4A) as well as perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were also identified. Collectively, these muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient displayed a pronounced and rapid onset of symptoms following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. Initially, she presented with gastrointestinal disturbances and developed renal failure, likely due to hypovolemia. At first assessment, creatine kinase (CK) levels were only mildly raised, not aligning with rhabdomyolysis. However, her persistent clinical deterioration after discontinuation of semaglutide, combined with the particular pattern of muscle weakness and a 12-fold escalation in CK over the subsequent 4 months, point towards an IMNM likely related to semaglutide exposure. In addition, biopsy findings consistent with drug-induced IMNM, absence of other signs typical for connective tissue diseases, and ongoing clinical improvement after cessation of semaglutide and initiation of immunosuppression lend further support to a causal relationship.

It is worth highlighting that the patient exhibited elevated transaminase levels before initiating semaglutide, a feature that can occur in various myopathies. Nevertheless, she had been diagnosed with hepatosteatosis via biopsy three years prior to commencing semaglutide, and the mild increase in AST/ALT persisted even after normalization of CK levels. This suggests that her chronic transaminase elevation most likely stemmed from hepatic rather than muscular pathology. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not yield definitive evidence for ongoing myocarditis. The rapid improvement observed also makes a diagnosis of stress-induced myopathy plausible in this clinical scenario.

The presence of a positive SS-B antibody in the absence of both ANA and SS-A was an unusual finding. To date, the literature does not document cases where isolated SS-B positivity, coupled with negative ANA and SS-A, has been associated with IMNM. Regrettably, there was no baseline CK level available before the patient began semaglutide, making it impossible to fully rule out a pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM. Nevertheless, the observed twelvefold increase in CK alongside progressively worsening proximal muscle weakness following initiation of semaglutide strongly suggests a drug-related etiology for this episode of IMNM. Furthermore, the patient’s marked IMNM resolved after a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, in combination with discontinuation of semaglutide. Such a clinical trajectory is more indicative of drug-induced IMNM—which typically responds rapidly to short-term immunosuppressive therapy once the causative drug is stopped—than idiopathic IMNM, which frequently necessitates extended, intensive immunosuppression.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days, in response to ongoing significant weakness and pronounced symptoms. Subsequent follow-up visits in the outpatient clinic demonstrated consistent improvements in both her functional abilities and general health status, with creatine kinase (CK) levels remaining within normal limits throughout. As a result, she was able to discontinue prednisone completely over a 2-month period without complications. Owing to both economic factors and her continued progress without semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered following the initial course during her hospital stay.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, occurring 10 months after her initial assessment and the start of therapy, she had returned to her baseline level of functioning and had been free from immunosuppressive medications for 7 months. Throughout the period after her hospital stay and immunosuppressive therapy, her CK levels consistently remained normal. Repeat cardiac evaluations, including echocardiogram and cardiac MRI, demonstrated resolution of the previously observed abnormalities. Furthermore, she remained free of any episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown proven efficacy in treating metabolic conditions like type 2 diabetes and obesity. While gastrointestinal disturbances remain the most frequent side effects, these agents have also been linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in rare instances, to rhabdomyolysis. In the case presented here, the evolution of symptoms and laboratory findings align more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. Specifically, the patient exhibited a 12-fold rise in CK levels over a four-month period following a single semaglutide dose; by contrast, if a direct toxic effect from semaglutide had been responsible—similar to other documented instances of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis—one would anticipate CK normalization within a few weeks. It is worth emphasizing that, in a previously described case of rhabdomyolysis related to tirzepatide, the clinical progression adhered to the expected pattern: the individual experienced an acute, substantial CK increase (CK=57 014 at presentation), which improved rapidly after discontinuing the drug and providing hydration, all without immunosuppressive intervention.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the relationship between semaglutide administration and IMNM in this case appears to be probable, given the appropriate temporal association, the improbability of alternative causes (either other illnesses or medications), and the marked improvement following drug discontinuation. Importantly, performing a rechallenge would not be ethical in light of the severity of the adverse event attributed to semaglutide. Nonetheless, we recognize that causality cannot be definitively established based on the current evidence. In light of the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, this case report is intended to alert clinicians to this possible side effect and to promote greater pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be necessary to determine if this observed association is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

The factors that may have made our patient susceptible to such a pronounced response to semaglutide are not yet well understood. As GLP-1 receptor agonists gain broader use, future research aimed at identifying predisposing variables will be critical, especially if additional instances with a similar temporal association to semaglutide administration are observed.
