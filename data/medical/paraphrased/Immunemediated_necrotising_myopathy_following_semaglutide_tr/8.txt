Title: Possible contribution of semaglutide treatment to immune-mediated necrotising myopathy

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she exhibited symptoms including progressive dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase. Notably, her clinical presentation began after a single exposure to semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings in line with necrotising myopathy, including inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. She exhibited clinical improvement in response to immunosuppressive treatment comprising corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Ultimately, she made a gradual recovery and was able to resume her baseline functional abilities after discontinuation of immunosuppression, as long as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists were avoided. This is, to our knowledge, the first documented instance of IMNM potentially linked to use of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, which also showed favorable response to a brief course of immunosuppressive therapy and IVIG.

Background:

In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely prescribed for managing metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. While effective, these agents have been associated with certain adverse events, including a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. Notably, a recent case report identified rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotising characteristics linked to the use of tirzepatide, a frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this case, the individual demonstrated a marked elevation in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching several thousand units, during ongoing therapy; importantly, CK levels began to decline within days after the discontinuation of the medication, indicating drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique subset of myopathies, typified by rapidly progressive weakness of the proximal muscles, histologically marked by widespread muscle fibre necrosis with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. IMNM demonstrates strong associations with two autoantibodies: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Statin therapy, frequently administered for metabolic disorders, shows a particularly robust correlation with positivity for HMGCR autoantibodies. Historically, individuals developing necrotising myopathy while on statin medications were commonly misclassified as cases of toxic myopathy or polymyositis before the link to HMGCR autoantibodies was elucidated. Notably, these patients frequently exhibited sustained muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase levels, even after discontinuation of statins, with clinical improvements observed only following immunosuppressive treatment. Such clinical patterns led to the recognition of an underlying autoimmune process and ultimately established HMGCR autoantibody presence as a defining diagnostic feature of the condition. Despite these advances, it is important to recognize that approximately one-third of IMNM patients do not test positive for known autoantibodies. This seronegativity complicates diagnosis and raises the possibility of undiscovered associated autoantibodies or other as-yet-unidentified environmental or pharmacologic triggers.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, whose past medical history includes obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness as well as difficulties in swallowing and speaking.

Previously, she enjoyed robust health and was entirely self-sufficient in her daily routines. Three years before her current decline, she was being seen as an outpatient to investigate elevated transaminases. During that evaluation, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were modestly increased, approximately two times the upper normal threshold, with ALT exceeding AST. Levels of alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts fell within the normal reference ranges. The patient reported no history of alcohol consumption and was not exposed to any medications known to cause liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed an enlarged liver with uniformly increased echogenicity, findings most compatible with hepatic steatosis. Results from testing for viral hepatitis, anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies were all negative. Histological examination of a liver biopsy indicated steatohepatitis in keeping with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), scored at NAFLD Activity Score grade 4/8 and fibrosis stage 0. Recommendations included adhering to a calorie-restricted diet and maintaining a consistent exercise routine.

Owing to ongoing fatty liver and obesity 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was commenced. Shortly after administration of the initial subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg), the patient developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms did not abate, and within 2 days, she required hospitalisation due to persistent nausea and vomiting, reduced oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal insufficiency, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate administration, and antiemetic therapy. She was temporarily admitted to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. With continued hydration, her renal function improved and she did not require haemofiltration or haemodialysis. On admission, her CK was measured at 337 U/L, although she lacked symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark-coloured urine. During her stay, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation occurred, but her serum troponin concentrations remained within normal limits. After 5 days, she was discharged, with resolution of gastrointestinal symptoms, but she was left deconditioned. Over the subsequent 4 months, she noted increasing muscle weakness. She reported no prior exposure to statins, or use of supplements known to be linked to IMNM (including mushrooms or red yeast rice), nor any other nutritional supplements. Furthermore, she denied any relevant family history of myopathy, engagement in excessive or prolonged physical exercise, or use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her initial and only administration of semaglutide, the patient’s condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or maintain an upright sitting position independently and, over the course of several days, developed dysphagia affecting both liquids and solids. These symptoms led her to seek care at an external hospital, where laboratory evaluation demonstrated a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference interval: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. The neurology team, consulting via telemedicine, initiated treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days. Following this, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for assessment by rheumatology. On arrival, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and did not appear acutely ill. Physical examination was notable for pronounced weakness, predominantly affecting proximal muscles (see table 1). No cutaneous eruptions, dermatologic findings, pulmonary crackles, evidence of synovitis or edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurological deficits were observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she commenced daily oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Due to ongoing muscle weakness and significant clinical severity, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg over 4 days. Over the subsequent 2 weeks, her CK levels demonstrated substantial improvement (figure 1). She was discharged following an almost 4-week hospital stay. However, 12 days post-discharge, she required rehospitalisation for 10 days owing to septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK levels remained within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up appointments, during which her functional abilities and general health steadily advanced, and her CK levels remained consistently within normal range. As a result of this sustained progress, prednisone was tapered and discontinued in a relatively short period. Owing to both financial considerations and her significant clinical recovery after stopping semaglutide, no further IVIG treatments were administered beyond the initial regimen provided during her hospitalization.

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months following her initial assessment and the commencement of therapy, she had returned to her pre-illness level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive medication for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory assessment revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L upon admission, with a maximum recorded value of 3992 U/L noted a week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) remained within normal parameters. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured at 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 165 U/L, both raised in the context of increased serum CK and a known background of hepatic steatosis. The autoantibody panel demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and serum creatinine were unremarkable, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. The comprehensive myositis antibody testing returned negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. However, a mildly positive SS-B autoantibody was detected at a low titre of 2.4 (reference value: <1.0). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein measured 22.8 mg/L. High-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was elevated to 62 pg/mL (reference: ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was significantly increased at 4358 pg/mL (reference: 0–125 pg/mL).

The patient underwent extensive imaging, including MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler studies, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The MRI of the femurs demonstrated mild, symmetrical muscular edema bilaterally, most pronounced in the proximal regions of the vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps, accompanied by subcutaneous edema along the outer thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70%, with right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI detected mild hypokinesis affecting the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall segments in conjunction with myocardial edema, but did not reveal definitive late gadolinium enhancement; this was suggestive of myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There were no findings of significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or major valvular abnormalities. The liver ultrasound with Doppler demonstrated evidence of hepatosteatosis. The high-resolution CT of the chest was negative for both interstitial lung disease and lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed hepatomegaly with steatosis, but no other abnormalities and no lymphadenopathy were observed.

Comprehensive diagnostic assessments included a repeat liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, which was conducted during her hospital stay, revealed several cores of hepatic tissue displaying both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, affecting roughly 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma. Importantly, there was an absence of notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These histopathological observations aligned with the patient’s prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, demonstrating a mild advancement of her liver pathology corresponding to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5/8 and stage 1. The right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B), while immunohistochemical analysis showed positive acid phosphatase staining (figure 3D) and strong expression of cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), which is indicative of macrophage infiltration and myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, the presence of membrane attack complex deposition was identified on both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), suggestive of an ongoing inflammatory process. Occasional CD3-positive T lymphocytes (figure 4A) as well as perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were also observed. Collectively, the muscle biopsy findings were in keeping with a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient exhibited an acute and pronounced adverse response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. Her presenting complaints were gastrointestinal disturbances and renal insufficiency, likely attributable to hypovolemia. At her initial assessment, the creatine kinase (CK) levels were only mildly raised, making rhabdomyolysis unlikely at that stage. However, persistent clinical deterioration following discontinuation of semaglutide, the specific pattern of muscle weakness, and a subsequent 12-fold escalation in CK levels over a period of four months post-semaglutide exposure point towards an IMNM that is likely linked to semaglutide use. Additional evidence for this association includes histopathological findings compatible with drug-induced IMNM, the absence of signs suggestive of a systemic connective tissue disorder, and lasting recovery subsequent to cessation of semaglutide and administration of initial immunosuppressive therapy.

It is worth highlighting that elevated transaminase levels were present before she began treatment with semaglutide, a finding that can also occur in cases of myopathy. Nonetheless, she was diagnosed with hepatosteatosis based on liver biopsy findings three years before initiating semaglutide, and her mild increases in AST/ALT continued even after normalization of CK concentrations. Therefore, it is most plausible that her chronic transaminase elevation stemmed from a hepatic source rather than from muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not definitively indicate ongoing myocarditis. Given the clinical context and her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy also represents a plausible explanation.

The observation of an isolated positive SS-B antibody, despite negative ANA and SS-A results, was unexpected in this context. To our knowledge, the literature does not document cases of isolated SS-B positivity with concurrently negative ANA and SS-A associated with the onset of IMNM. A limitation in our evaluation is the absence of a pre-semaglutide CK level, which prevents us from conclusively ruling out the presence of a low-grade autoimmune IMNM prior to commencing semaglutide therapy. Nonetheless, the marked 12-fold elevation in CK and the gradual development of proximal muscle weakness following initiation of semaglutide suggest that this instance of IMNM was likely triggered by the medication. Furthermore, the patient's severe IMNM showed complete resolution after a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG, alongside discontinuation of semaglutide. This recovery trajectory is more typical of drug-induced IMNM, which often responds rapidly to short-term immunosuppression following removal of the causative agent, in contrast to idiopathic IMNM, which generally necessitates more sustained and aggressive immunosuppressive interventions.

Treatment:

She initially received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she transitioned to daily oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, in light of her ongoing pronounced weakness and severe clinical presentation. Multiple subsequent outpatient assessments showed her continuous recovery in both functionality and general well-being, with her CK levels consistently remaining within the normal range. Consequently, her prednisone was able to be tapered off successfully and more rapidly, completing the course over 2 months. In view of financial considerations and ongoing stability following discontinuation of semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered after the initial hospital treatment.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, conducted 10 months following her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her premorbid level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive medications for the preceding 7 months. Her CK levels stayed within the normal range after her hospitalization and course of immunosuppressive therapy. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previously identified abnormalities. Additionally, she remained free of recurrent episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly utilized, with established efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal symptoms are the most frequent side effects, these agents have also been linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in rare cases, to rhabdomyolysis. In this report, the evolution of the patient’s symptoms aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. Specifically, the patient’s creatine kinase (CK) values rose twelvefold over a span of four months after receiving a single dose of semaglutide, a pattern that contrasts with the more rapid normalization of CK typically expected in instances of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. For example, a previously described case of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide exhibited the classical clinical scenario: an acute, dramatic CK elevation (CK=57 014 at presentation) followed by a swift return to baseline within several days after discontinuation of the drug and administration of hydration, all without the requirement for immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case demonstrates a probable association between semaglutide administration and IMNM. This assessment is based on a plausible temporal association, the unlikelihood of alternative etiologies such as other diseases or medications, and notable clinical improvement upon discontinuation of semaglutide. Ethical considerations preclude a rechallenge with the drug due to the severity of the adverse reaction observed. Nevertheless, we recognize that causality cannot be conclusively established in this instance. Given the widespread adoption of semaglutide, the intent of this report is to alert clinicians to this possible adverse outcome and encourage increased pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be needed to determine if this relationship is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

The underlying reasons why our patient developed such a pronounced response to semaglutide are not yet understood. As GLP-1 receptor agonists gain widespread use, it will be crucial for future research to investigate potential risk factors, particularly if additional instances with a strong temporal association to semaglutide administration emerge.
