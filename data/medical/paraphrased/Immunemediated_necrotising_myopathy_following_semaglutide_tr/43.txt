Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over a four-month period, she experienced worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels, all of which appeared shortly after a single semaglutide dose. Diagnostic evaluation showed necrotising myopathy with inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. She exhibited clinical improvement with corticosteroid therapy and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). After gradual recovery, she achieved her baseline functional capacity, remaining off immunosuppressive treatment for several months and abstaining from glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first known instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, demonstrating a favorable response to brief immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have frequently been prescribed for metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Adverse reactions reported with this drug class include a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report documented rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics linked to tirzepatide, another widely used GLP-1 receptor agonist. The patient described developed markedly elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels in the thousands during ongoing therapy, and the CK levels began to decrease within a few days after discontinuing the medication, a pattern indicative of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a distinct form of myopathy, typified by rapidly progressive weakness in proximal muscles, the presence of necrotic myofibres, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration on tissue examination. IMNM is closely linked to two autoantibodies: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Statin therapy, which is frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, has a strong association with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Prior to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibody involvement, several individuals on statins developed necrotising myopathy manifestations and were often diagnosed as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. Notably, in these cases, many patients continued to experience muscle symptoms and elevated CK levels even after discontinuing statins. Clinical improvement following immunosuppressive treatment indicated an autoimmune etiology, eventually resulting in the identification of the HMGCR autoantibody as a key feature of the condition. It should be highlighted that approximately one-third of IMNM patients lack detectable autoantibodies. This seronegative group poses further diagnostic difficulties and indicates the existence of other associated autoantibodies or potential precipitating factors that remain unknown.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties with a past medical history of obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening weakness of the proximal muscles, as well as difficulty swallowing and speaking.

Prior to her decline, she maintained good health and independence in daily activities. Three years before her symptoms worsened, she was assessed in the outpatient setting for elevated transaminase levels. At that time, laboratory testing revealed mild elevations of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), both approximately two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelets were within reference ranges. The patient reported no alcohol consumption and was not using medications known to be hepatotoxic. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echotexture, interpreted as likely indicative of hepatic steatosis. Testing for viral hepatitis, as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, returned negative results. Histologic examination via liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis in keeping with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 4/8, stage 0. Lifestyle modifications, including a reduced-calorie diet and regular exercise, were recommended.

Given ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years after NAFLD was diagnosed, semaglutide therapy was started. Several hours following her initial semaglutide injection (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she began to experience nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, and 2 days later she was admitted to the hospital for persistent nausea, vomiting, limited oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal insufficiency, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate administration, and antiemetic therapy. She required a brief ICU stay for non-invasive ventilatory support. After receiving hydration, her renal function improved, eliminating the necessity for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. On admission, her creatine kinase (CK) was measured at 337 U/L. She exhibited no other related manifestations such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine. During her hospitalization, she also experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though her troponin remained within normal limits. She was discharged after a 5-day admission. At discharge, her gastrointestinal symptoms had resolved, though she remained deconditioned. Over the subsequent four months, she experienced a gradual onset of muscle weakness. She reported no prior statin exposure, substantial ingestion of supplements linked with IMNM—including mushrooms or red yeast rice—nor any other nutritional supplements. Additionally, she denied any significant family history of myopathy, recent intensive or prolonged exercise, alcohol use, or illicit drug intake.

Four months after receiving her first and only administration of semaglutide, the patient’s condition continued to worsen. She could no longer maintain an upright position—standing or sitting—without assistance, and developed dysphagia for both liquids and solids over several days. Consequently, she sought care at an outside hospital, where her creatine kinase was found to be elevated at 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) on admission. She was managed with intravenous methylprednisolone, 1000 mg daily for five days, under neurology consultation conducted via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary facility for further evaluation by rheumatology. On arrival, she was afebrile, normotensive, and appeared comfortable. Examination demonstrated marked weakness, particularly affecting proximal muscle groups (see table 1). No rashes, skin abnormalities, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological abnormalities were identified.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, the patient began oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, in response to her ongoing weakness and severe symptoms. Her creatine kinase (CK) showed significant improvement within two weeks (figure 1). She was discharged following a hospitalization that lasted almost four weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for ten days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection and during this stay, her CK was noted to be normal.

She underwent multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional abilities and general health continued to progress, and her CK values remained within the normal range. As a result, her prednisone was tapered quickly and discontinued. Because of financial considerations and sustained clinical improvement in the absence of semaglutide, she did not receive further IVIG beyond her initial hospital course.

Her most recent outpatient assessment occurred 10 months following her original presentation and initiation of therapy, at which time she had returned to her prior level of function and remained off immunosuppressive medications for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a CK level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a prior peak of 3992 U/L recorded one week before at the facility from which she was transferred. TSH values were within the normal range. AST measured at 191 U/L and ALT at 165 U/L, both elevated, likely influenced by increased serum CK and her background of fatty liver disease. The autoantibody panel showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement and creatinine levels were normal, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. The myositis autoantibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A. However, she did have a low titre positive SS-B autoantibody measured at 2.4 (reference value: <1.0). ESR was raised at 22 mm/hour and CRP at 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin was elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL), while pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) reached 4358 pg/mL (normal range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies comprised MRI of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI scans of the bilateral femurs showed mild and symmetrical muscle edema, most pronounced in the proximal regions of the vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, with subcutaneous edema seen along the lateral aspects of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography indicated an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction between 65%–70%, with a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI demonstrated slight hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, correlated with myocardial edema; no late gadolinium enhancement was identified, which could be suggestive of myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or possibly a focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No notable myocardial scarring, infarction, or significant valvular abnormalities were seen. The liver ultrasound with Doppler revealed evidence of hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest did not show interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI indicated steatotic hepatomegaly without other abnormalities and no lymphadenopathy.

Further advanced diagnostic evaluations included repeating the liver biopsy and performing a thigh muscle biopsy. During hospitalization, the liver biopsy showed multiple sections of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, involving about 70% of the analyzed hepatic parenchyma, without significant portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These results matched the patient’s prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, indicating mild worsening of liver disease with an NAFLD Activity Score of grade 5 out of 8, stage 1. The right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B); immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a macrophage marker, alongside evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, staining for membrane attack complex was observed on both necrotizing and non-necrotizing myofibers (figure 4C), reflecting an inflammatory response. Rare CD3-positive cells, marking T-cells (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory cell infiltrates (figure 3C) were also identified. Overall, the muscle biopsy findings were consistent with a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient had a pronounced and rapid adverse response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal symptoms and renal failure, which was thought to be due to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated at the outset, not aligning with rhabdomyolysis, subsequent progression in her condition despite discontinuation of semaglutide, along with the pattern of muscle weakness and a twelvefold increase in CK levels over four months post-semiglutide exposure, point toward an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely connected to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings consistent with drug-induced IMNM, absence of signs indicative of an underlying connective tissue disease, and lasting improvement following initial immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide further reinforce this relationship.

It is important to note that elevated transaminase levels were present before she initiated semaglutide, a finding that may occur with myopathies. Nevertheless, three years prior to starting semaglutide, she was diagnosed via biopsy with hepatosteatosis, and the mild increase in AST/ALT remained after normalization of CK. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevation is most likely attributable to hepatic pathology rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Considering the clinical context and her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy could also be a plausible explanation.

The detection of SS-B antibody alone, with negative results for ANA and SS-A, was unanticipated. Currently, the literature does not document cases where isolated SS-B positivity, in the absence of ANA and SS-A, is associated with IMNM. Regrettably, a CK level prior to semaglutide initiation was unavailable, thus we cannot completely rule out the possibility of a pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM before semaglutide was started. Nevertheless, the twelvefold increase in CK and the onset of worsening proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide administration suggest a higher probability of IMNM being related to the medication. Furthermore, her pronounced IMNM subsided following a short regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG, coupled with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which typically responds to brief immunosuppression once the causal drug is stopped, whereas idiopathic IMNM generally necessitates sustained and aggressive immunosuppressive management.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Ongoing significant weakness and severe symptoms prompted administration of IVIG at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across four days. She was subsequently seen multiple times as an outpatient, demonstrating steady gains in function and general well-being, with creatine kinase values remaining consistently within normal limits. This clinical stability allowed for a successful and accelerated prednisone taper completed within two months. Because of both financial factors and her continued improvement without the use of semaglutide, she was not administered further IVIG after her initial inpatient treatment course.

Outcome and follow-up:

Her most recent follow-up in the clinic occurred 10 months after the start of her assessment and therapy, at which point she had returned to her baseline functional abilities and had been without immunosuppressive medication for 7 months. Since her hospitalization and immunosuppressive therapy, her CK values stayed within normal limits. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the previously detected abnormalities. Additionally, she did not have another episode of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in managing metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are frequent, there is also evidence linking these agents to reduced muscle mass and, in some cases, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the patient’s clinical trajectory aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient’s CK increased twelvefold over a period of 4 months after a single administration of semaglutide; in comparison, drug-induced rhabdomyolysis typically results in normalization of CK levels within a few weeks if due to direct toxicity, as reported in other cases. Of note, a prior instance of rhabdomyolysis related to tirzepatide displayed the expected clinical course: the individual had a pronounced acute rise in CK (CK=57 014 at initial evaluation) with swift recovery following discontinuation of the medication and hydration over several days, without requiring immunosuppressive treatment.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there appears to be a probable association between semaglutide administration and IMNM development in this patient, given the appropriate temporal correlation, the low likelihood that IMNM resulted from other medications or medical conditions, and the observed clinical improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide. Re-exposure to the drug would be unethical due to the severity of the adverse event experienced. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this case. In light of the increasing usage of semaglutide, this report seeks to alert healthcare providers to this possible adverse outcome and promote enhanced pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be necessary to determine if the observed association is truly causal or merely coincidental.

The factors predisposing our patient to this pronounced reaction to semaglutide are not yet clear. With the rapid rise in use of GLP-1 receptor agonists, investigating potential risk factors will be critical, especially if other reports with similar timelines relative to semaglutide exposure emerge.
