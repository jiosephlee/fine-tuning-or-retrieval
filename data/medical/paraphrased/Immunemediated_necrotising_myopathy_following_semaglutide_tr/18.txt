Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. She experienced progressively worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and increased creatine kinase levels over a four-month period, beginning after a single administration of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation identified necrotising myopathy with evidence of inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. The patient was treated with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulting in gradual improvement and eventual restoration to her prior level of functioning. She remained stable off immunosuppressive therapy for several months while avoiding glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report presents the first documented instance of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, demonstrating improvement following a brief immunosuppressive regimen including IVIG.

Background:

In the past several years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have seen widespread use in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity. Although this medication class is generally well tolerated, reduction in skeletal muscle mass has been documented among its adverse effects. Recently, a published case highlighted an instance of rhabdomyolysis with necrotising changes associated with tirzepatide, another widely utilized GLP-1 receptor agonist. The patient in that case experienced a substantial rise in creatine kinase (CK), reaching values in the thousands during active treatment, with levels beginning to decline within several days following discontinuation of the medication—findings that are indicative of rhabdomyolysis induced by the drug.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a specific type of myopathy defined by rapid-onset proximal muscle weakness, the presence of necrotic muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration on histological examination. IMNM is highly linked with two autoantibodies: anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP). Of note, statin therapy—which is frequently prescribed for metabolic disorders—has a strong association with positivity for HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to recognizing the connection to HMGCR antibodies, multiple patients treated with statins developed necrotising myopathy and were frequently misdiagnosed as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these instances, a significant number continued to exhibit muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels despite discontinuation of statin therapy. The persistence of symptoms and subsequent response to immunosuppressive treatment pointed toward an autoimmune etiology, ultimately facilitating the identification of HMGCR autoantibodies as a defining feature of IMNM. It is important to recognize that roughly one-third of IMNM patients do not have detectable autoantibodies. This seronegative group poses greater diagnostic complexity and raises the possibility that other autoantibodies and causative factors remain unrecognized.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a background of obesity, fatty liver, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening proximal muscle weakness, swallowing difficulties, and voice changes.

Prior to this, she enjoyed good health and remained fully self-sufficient. Three years before her current decline, she was evaluated as an outpatient for persistently elevated liver transaminases. At that point, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were both mildly increased, reaching about twice the upper normal limit, with ALT levels surpassing those of AST. Her alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet levels were all within reference ranges. She reported no history of alcohol intake and was not using hepatotoxic drugs. Ultrasound imaging of the abdomen revealed an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Laboratory workup, including viral hepatitis serologies and tests for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, yielded negative results. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis characteristic of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8, stage 0 fibrosis. The patient was counseled to adopt a calorie-restricted diet and to maintain regular physical activity.

Owing to ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years following her initial NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Shortly after receiving the initial dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she began to experience nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, leading to her hospital admission 2 days later with persistent nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal insufficiency, and severe metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate therapy, and antiemetic medications. She required a brief stay in the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal function recovered with fluid resuscitation, and neither haemofiltration nor dialysis was necessary. Creatine kinase measured on admission was elevated at 337 U/L. There were no accompanying symptoms such as rash, muscle aches, or dark-colored urine. During the same hospitalization, she developed episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, but her troponin levels remained within normal range. She was discharged after a five-day admission. Upon discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had subsided, though she was noted to be deconditioned. Over the subsequent four months, she experienced gradually worsening weakness. She reported no prior statin exposure, no significant use of supplements linked to IMNM—such as mushrooms or red yeast rice—nor any other nutritional supplements. Additionally, there was no noteworthy family history of myopathy, recent excessive or prolonged exertion, or use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to deteriorate. She became unable to stand or sit upright independently and developed dysphagia to both liquids and solids over several days. These worsening symptoms led her to seek care at another hospital, where laboratory evaluation showed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. She received intravenous methylprednisolone 1000 mg daily for five days with neurological oversight provided remotely via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for assessment by rheumatology. Upon arrival, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable. On physical exam, pronounced muscle weakness was observed, predominantly involving the proximal muscles (see table 1). She displayed no rashes, skin lesions, lung crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurologic deficits.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone therapy, she was transitioned to daily oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Because of ongoing significant weakness and severe clinical presentation, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg divided over 4 days. Over the subsequent 2 weeks, her CK levels improved substantially (figure 1). She was discharged following a hospital stay of nearly 4 weeks. Twelve days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this episode, her CK was within normal limits.

In subsequent outpatient visits, she continued to demonstrate recovery in both her functional abilities and general well-being, with her CK remaining normal. Owing to these improvements, she was able to taper off prednisone quickly. Considering the financial burden and her clinical recovery after discontinuing semaglutide, no further IVIG therapy was administered apart from the initial inpatient course.

She was last seen in the outpatient setting 10 months after her initial evaluation and treatment initiation and had regained her baseline functional status, off immunosuppression for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory assessment revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of evaluation, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L reported one week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was found to be within reference parameters. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were elevated to 191 U/L and 165 U/L, respectively, in the context of raised serum CK and a known history of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody testing demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were within normal limits, and no cytopenias were present. A comprehensive myositis panel returned negative for SRP, HMGCR, as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. However, there was a low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was mildly raised at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein measured 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin was elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference ≤53 pg/mL) and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was markedly increased at 4358 pg/mL (reference 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies included MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, Doppler liver ultrasound, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs demonstrated mild, symmetrical edema within the muscles on both sides, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps regions, accompanied by subcutaneous edema laterally on both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated between 65% and 70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls, corresponding with myocardial edema, but no clear late gadolinium enhancement; these findings may suggest myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly a localized, stress-related cardiomyopathy. No significant myocardial infarction, scarring, or major valvular pathology was detected. Liver ultrasound with Doppler demonstrated hepatic steatosis. The high-resolution CT scan of the chest did not show evidence of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI found hepatomegaly with steatosis, but was otherwise unremarkable and did not identify any lymphadenopathy.

Comprehensive diagnostic evaluation included a repeat liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, obtained during the hospital stay, revealed several sections of hepatic tissue displaying both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving about 70% of the sampled liver parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These histopathologic features were in line with the patient’s history of hepatic steatosis, indicating mild progression to an NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8, at stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analyses demonstrated positive acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68)—a marker for macrophages—associated with myophagocytosis (figure 4B). A membrane attack complex (MAC) stain was also positive on both necrotic and non-necrotic muscle fibers (figure 4C), indicative of ongoing inflammation. In addition, only infrequent cells positive for CD3—a T-cell marker—were detected (figure 4A), as well as inflammatory cells surrounding blood vessels (figure 3C). These histological findings in the muscle biopsy supported a diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient had a pronounced and acute response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, which indicated intolerance. She initially presented with gastrointestinal symptoms and developed renal failure that was thought to be due to hypovolemia. At presentation, her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly raised, which did not correspond to rhabdomyolysis; however, her condition worsened despite discontinuing semaglutide. The pattern of muscle weakness and a twelvefold CK elevation over the following four months after semaglutide exposure point toward immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to the drug. Additionally, histopathology showing features of drug-induced IMNM, the absence of signs suggestive of an underlying connective tissue disorder, and persistent recovery following early immunosuppressive therapy combined with discontinuation of semaglutide further strengthen this link.

It is important to note that the patient exhibited elevated transaminase levels before initiating semaglutide, a finding that may be associated with myopathies. Nonetheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years before starting semaglutide confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mildly increased AST/ALT persisted despite normalization of CK levels. Therefore, her chronically raised transaminases are most likely attributable to hepatic rather than muscular causes. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not provide clear evidence of ongoing myocarditis. In this clinical scenario, stress-related myopathy would also be a plausible diagnosis, especially considering her swift clinical improvement.

The detection of SS-B antibody in the absence of ANA and SS-A antibodies was unanticipated. There is a lack of documented cases describing isolated SS-B positivity with negative ANA and SS-A as a precursor to IMNM. We were also unable to obtain a creatine kinase (CK) value from before the initiation of semaglutide, which means the possibility of a previously undiagnosed autoimmune IMNM cannot be completely ruled out prior to starting semaglutide. Nonetheless, a 12-fold increase in CK and the subsequent development of progressive proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide use suggest a higher probability that the IMNM episode was induced by medication. Moreover, the patient's significant IMNM resolved rapidly after a brief course of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), alongside discontinuation of semaglutide. This clinical trajectory is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM—which typically responds to short-term immunosuppression once the causative drug is stopped—compared to idiopathic IMNM, which frequently necessitates more sustained and aggressive immunosuppression.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, which was then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. In addition, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, in light of her ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms. Follow-up visits in the outpatient clinic showed ongoing gains in her functional abilities and general health, as well as consistently normal creatine kinase (CK) readings. Consequently, her prednisone was tapered off successfully and more quickly, over a 2-month period. Given both financial factors and her continued progress without semaglutide, she did not undergo further IVIG treatment after the initial inpatient course.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, conducted 10 months after her first assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her pre-illness level of functioning and had been free from immunosuppressive medication for the past 7 months. Her creatine kinase values remained normal after hospital discharge and immunosuppressive treatment. Follow-up echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previous abnormal findings. Additionally, there was no return of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are being increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in managing metabolic conditions, such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are most frequently observed, there is also potential for these agents to contribute to loss of muscle mass and, in some cases, rhabdomyolysis. In the current case, the patient's presentation is more suggestive of IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. Following a single dose of semaglutide, CK values escalated 12-fold over a period of 4 months; if semaglutide toxicity alone had been responsible, normalization of CK would typically occur within weeks, as documented in other instances of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. Importantly, a previously described case of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide exhibited the expected trajectory: a pronounced acute CK rise (CK=57,014 on admission) that improved rapidly following discontinuation of the drug and supportive hydration, without the necessity for immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, the link between semaglutide administration and the onset of IMNM in our patient can be classified as probable. This assessment is based on the presence of an appropriate temporal relationship, the low likelihood that IMNM resulted from other conditions or medications, and the observed clinical improvement upon discontinuing semaglutide. A rechallenge was not pursued due to the severe nature of the adverse event associated with semaglutide exposure, making it ethically unacceptable. Nonetheless, we recognize that definite causality cannot be confirmed in this instance. In light of the rapidly increasing use of semaglutide, this report seeks to alert clinicians to this possible adverse event and to promote greater pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be necessary to determine whether the association observed is causal or coincidental.

It also remains undetermined what specific factors may have rendered our patient more susceptible to developing such a marked reaction to semaglutide. As GLP-1 receptor agonists become more widely used, research aimed at identifying risk factors will become increasingly important if additional cases with a similar temporal association with semaglutide exposure are reported.
