Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This paper presents a case involving a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. The patient experienced a gradual onset of dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels over a span of four months. These manifestations arose after a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation identified necrotising myopathy accompanied by inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) led to clinical improvement. Over time, she regained her previous level of functioning, maintained for several months without immunosuppressive therapy, and remained free of GLP-1 receptor agonists. This represents the first documented instance of IMNM potentially linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist administration, showing improvement following brief immunosuppression and IVIG therapy.

Background:

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely prescribed for metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes in recent years. Notably, adverse events related to these agents include reductions in skeletal muscle mass. A case report has recently documented an instance of rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotising characteristics linked to tirzepatide, another GLP-1 receptor agonist in common use. In the described scenario, the patient experienced a marked increase in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching the thousands during ongoing therapy; following cessation of the drug, CK concentrations began to decline within several days, consistent with a drug-induced rhabdomyolysis presentation.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique form of myopathy, marked by severe proximal muscle weakness and histological evidence of muscle fiber necrosis with few lymphocytic infiltrates. The autoantibodies anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) are strongly linked with IMNM. Statins, frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, have a significant association with HMGCR autoantibody positivity. Prior to recognition of the HMGCR autoantibody connection, numerous patients exposed to statins developed necrotising myopathy symptoms and were commonly misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these individuals, muscle symptoms and elevated creatine kinase persisted even after discontinuing statin therapy. The response of these cases to immunosuppressive treatment suggested an autoimmune etiology and ultimately led to identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature. Importantly, approximately one third of IMNM patients are seronegative. The absence of detectable autoantibodies in this population presents further diagnostic difficulties and implies that additional relevant autoantibodies and initiating factors may remain unidentified.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a history of obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism presented with gradually worsening weakness affecting proximal muscles, difficulty swallowing, and hoarseness.

Previously, she was in good health and independently able to perform all daily activities. Three years before her current symptoms, she underwent outpatient investigations for elevated liver enzymes. At that time, aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were mildly increased, both approximately two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT being higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count remained within normal values. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any potentially hepatotoxic drugs. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed enlargement of the liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Serologic testing for viral hepatitis, anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies yielded negative results. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis compatible with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and stage 0 fibrosis. She was instructed to adopt a reduced-calorie diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Despite ongoing fatty liver and obesity 2.5 years after being diagnosed with NAFLD, semaglutide therapy was started. Following administration of her initial dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneous), she developed nausea and vomiting within several hours. These symptoms continued, and she was admitted to hospital 2 days later presenting with nausea, vomiting, decreased oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal insufficiency, and severe metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic medications. She was temporarily moved to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. With hydration, her renal function improved and renal replacement therapy, such as haemofiltration or haemodialysis, was not necessary. Creatine kinase measured at admission was elevated to 337 U/L. She did not report other related manifestations, such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine. During her hospital stay, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation developed; however, serum troponin remained within normal limits. After a 5-day admission, she was discharged. At discharge, gastrointestinal symptoms had resolved, although she was physically deconditioned. Over the ensuing 4 months, she experienced increasing weakness. She reported no previous statin use, no significant intake of supplements linked to IMNM—including mushrooms or red yeast rice—or other nutritional supplements. She also denied notable family history of myopathy, recent strenuous or extended exercise, and alcohol or illicit drug use.

Four months after her first and only administration of semaglutide, the patient’s condition worsened further. She became unable to maintain an upright posture or stand without support, and began to experience difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids over a period of several days. These symptoms led her to seek care at an external hospital, where laboratory tests revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (normal range: 12–191 U/L) at admission. Neurology was consulted remotely, and she received 1000 mg intravenous methylprednisolone each day for five days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary medical center for rheumatological assessment. Upon evaluation, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and did not appear to be in distress. On physical examination, prominent proximal muscle weakness was observed (refer to table 1). There were no rashes, cutaneous changes, lung crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological abnormalities noted.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she was started on daily oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Owing to ongoing significant weakness and pronounced symptoms, she also received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a total dose of 2 g/kg, administered over 4 days. Over the subsequent 2 weeks, there was a significant reduction in her CK levels (see figure 1). Her initial hospitalization lasted nearly 4 weeks, after which she was discharged. However, 12 days post-discharge, she was readmitted for a duration of 10 days with septic shock secondary to pseudomonal urinary tract infection; during this hospitalization, her CK level was within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional capacity and general health kept improving, and her CK levels remained within the normal range. As a result, prednisone was tapered and discontinued quickly. Because of financial considerations and her sustained recovery without semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered apart from the initial inpatient regimen.

Her most recent outpatient visit occurred 10 months following the onset of evaluation and therapy, at which point she had returned to her pre-illness functional state and remained off immunosuppressive medication for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a previous peak value of 3992 U/L reported one week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was found to be within the reference range. Liver enzymes were increased, with an aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 165 U/L, in the context of both raised CK levels and a history of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody screening demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjögren’s syndrome type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels were unremarkable, creatinine was normal, and there were no findings of cytopenias. A myositis autoantibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A antibodies. However, a low titre SS-B autoantibody was identified at 2.4 (normal <1.0). Inflammatory markers were raised, with an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 22 mm/hour and a C-reactive protein of 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was elevated at 62 pg/mL (normal ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was increased at 4358 pg/mL (reference range 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies included MRI of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution CT of the chest, and abdominal MRI. The bilateral femur MRI demonstrated mild, symmetric muscle edema, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, as well as subcutaneous edema along the outer portions of both thighs (figure 2). Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesia involving the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall regions, along with myocardial edema, but without definite late gadolinium enhancement. These findings may indicate myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly a localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No substantial myocardial scarring, infarction, or clinically significant valvular dysfunction was observed. Liver ultrasound with Doppler indicated hepatic steatosis. The high-resolution CT scan of the chest did not reveal interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI showed steatotic hepatomegaly, but was otherwise unremarkable and without evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Further advanced diagnostic evaluations involved repeating the liver biopsy and performing a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, carried out during her hospital stay, revealed multiple samples of hepatic tissue displaying both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, which covered roughly 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, showing mild advancement of the existing liver disorder to a NAFLD Activity Score grade of 5 out of 8, stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis identified acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and CD68—a macrophage marker—with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, staining for membrane attack complex was positive in both necrotising and non-necrotising muscle fibers (figure 4C), suggesting an inflammatory reaction. A small number of CD3-positive cells, marking T-cells (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C) were also observed. Collectively, the muscle biopsy findings were indicative of an IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient demonstrated a pronounced and prompt reaction following her first and sole administration of semaglutide, indicative of drug intolerance. Initial manifestations included gastrointestinal disturbances and renal failure believed to be due to hypovolemia. Although creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly increased and did not align with rhabdomyolysis at the first assessment, her ongoing deterioration after discontinuing semaglutide and the pattern of muscle weakness, along with a 12-fold escalation in CK levels over four months post-semaglutide exposure, point to the development of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide. Additionally, biopsy findings characteristic of drug-related IMNM, absence of clinical evidence for an underlying connective tissue disorder, and lasting improvement following immunosuppressive therapy and withdrawal of semaglutide reinforce this association.

It is worth noting that she exhibited elevated transaminases before starting semaglutide, a finding that can occur in myopathies. Nonetheless, a liver biopsy had confirmed hepatosteatosis three years prior to semaglutide initiation, and her mildly increased AST and ALT remained despite normalization of CK values. Therefore, the persistent transaminase elevation is most likely attributable to her liver disease rather than muscle injury. Cardiac evaluation did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. A stress-induced myopathy could also explain her presentation, particularly in view of her swift clinical improvement.

The finding of isolated SS-B antibody positivity, accompanied by negative ANA and SS-A, was unusual in this case. To date, there are no documented cases that link isolated SS-B positivity in the absence of ANA and SS-A to the development of IMNM. We were unable to obtain a creatine kinase measurement predating the patient’s exposure to semaglutide, making it impossible to conclusively rule out the presence of subclinical autoimmune IMNM prior to starting semaglutide. Nonetheless, the twelve-fold increase in creatine kinase and the progressive proximal muscle weakness observed following semaglutide administration suggest an association between the medication and this episode of IMNM. Furthermore, the patient experienced resolution of severe IMNM after a brief treatment with corticosteroids and IVIG, coupled with discontinuation of semaglutide. This pattern of recovery is consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which tends to respond to short-term immunosuppression and withdrawal of the causative drug, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, which typically necessitates sustained and aggressive immunosuppressive intervention.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which oral prednisone was initiated at 1 mg/kg per day. Given her ongoing weakness and pronounced symptoms, IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. The patient was monitored through multiple outpatient visits, demonstrating steady gains in functional capacity and general well-being, with CK values remaining within normal limits. These improvements enabled her prednisone to be tapered quickly, and she discontinued it within 2 months. Considering the financial impact as well as her sustained progress without semaglutide, she was not provided additional IVIG after her initial hospitalization course.

Outcome and follow-up:

She attended her most recent outpatient follow-up 10 months after the original assessment and the commencement of therapy, demonstrating a return to her previous level of function, having discontinued immunosuppressive medications for the last 7 months. Her creatine kinase values stayed within normal limits after hospital discharge and immunosuppressive therapy. Subsequent echocardiography and cardiac MRI showed resolution of earlier abnormalities. Additionally, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation did not recur.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists such as semaglutide are increasingly used and have proven efficacy in managing metabolic conditions including type 2 diabetes and obesity. While gastrointestinal side effects are frequently reported, these drugs have also been linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in rare instances, to rhabdomyolysis. In the case discussed here, the patient's clinical progression is more aligned with immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) rather than rhabdomyolysis. The creatine kinase (CK) rose 12-fold over a four-month period after a single administration of semaglutide, whereas a direct toxic response to semaglutide, as seen in other cases of drug-related rhabdomyolysis, would typically resolve CK elevations within weeks. Of note, a prior report of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide exhibited a classic pattern, with a sharp rise in CK (CK=57,014 at presentation) and rapid normalization after discontinuation of the drug and hydration within a few days, requiring no immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, there appears to be a probable link between semaglutide administration and IMNM in this instance, due to the appropriate temporal association, the absence of other plausible etiologies or medications causing IMNM, and a clinically appropriate improvement following discontinuation of the drug. Furthermore, it would be unethical to perform a rechallenge in this context, given the severity of the adverse reaction attributed to semaglutide. Nevertheless, we recognize that causality cannot be definitively established in this case. With the widespread and increasing use of semaglutide, we present this report to inform clinicians of this possible adverse event and to strengthen pharmacovigilance efforts. Ongoing research will be necessary to determine whether this observed association represents causation or is coincidental.

Additionally, the factors predisposing our patient to develop a significant response to semaglutide remain undefined. As GLP-1 receptor agonists continue to gain popularity, investigating predisposing variables will be crucial should further cases emerge with a similarly close temporal relationship to semaglutide therapy.
