Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following the administration of semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she experienced progressive dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels. Her symptoms began after a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation indicated necrotising myopathy characterized by inflammatory infiltrate observed in muscle biopsy. The patient was treated with immunosuppressive therapy consisting of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), resulting in gradual improvement and a return to her prior functional status. She remained off immunosuppressive therapy for several months, while abstaining from glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This represents the first documented case of IMNM likely linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure, with clinical improvement following a brief regimen of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Recently, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized in the management of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among the reported side effects for this medication class is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. A case report has documented rhabdomyolysis with necrotizing characteristics associated with tirzepatide, another frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In this instance, the patient experienced creatine kinase (CK) levels rising into the thousands during active therapy, and the CK values began to decrease within days after stopping the medication, indicative of drug-related rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a specific form of myopathy marked by rapid-onset weakness predominantly affecting proximal muscles, alongside histopathological findings of myofibre necrosis and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Autoantibodies against hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and signal recognition particle (SRP) exhibit strong associations with IMNM. Statin therapy, frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions, is particularly linked to the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Previously, individuals exposed to statins who manifested symptoms of necrotising myopathy were often diagnosed with toxic myopathy or polymyositis, until the relationship with HMGCR autoantibodies was uncovered. Notably, many of these patients continued to experience myopathic symptoms and sustained elevations in creatine kinase (CK) levels after discontinuing statin treatment. The persistence of these manifestations, in conjunction with favorable responses to immunosuppressive therapy, indicated an underlying autoimmune etiology, ultimately leading to the recognition of HMGCR autoantibodies as a defining feature of IMNM. Importantly, approximately one-third of IMNM cases are seronegative for known autoantibodies. The absence of identifiable autoantibodies in this group not only complicates the diagnostic process, but also implies that additional as-yet-unidentified autoantibodies or environmental triggers may be involved.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixties, whose medical history included obesity, hepatic steatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a remote episode of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with worsening proximal muscle weakness accompanied by dysphagia and dysphonia.

She had previously enjoyed good health and was able to perform all daily activities independently. Three years before her current symptoms, she was assessed as an outpatient for elevated transaminases. At that time, her aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels were mildly increased, with values approximately double the upper limit of normal; ALT was higher than AST. Her alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts remained normal. She reported no alcohol intake and was not using any medications known to be hepatotoxic. Abdominal ultrasound revealed hepatomegaly with a diffusely increased echotexture, suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Testing for viral hepatitis and autoantibodies including anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies yielded negative results. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis in keeping with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with an NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and stage 0. Lifestyle recommendations included adopting a reduced-calorie diet and regular physical activity.

Owing to ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years following the NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Shortly after her initial injection of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, leading to hospital admission 2 days later due to persistent nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, dyspnea, acute renal failure, and severe metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate administration, and antiemetics. She was temporarily transferred to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. Hydration resulted in improvement of renal function, and renal replacement therapy such as haemofiltration or haemodialysis was not required. Admission laboratory testing revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 337 U/L. She did not report accompanying signs like rash, myalgia, or dark urine resembling cola. During this admission, she also developed episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, but her serum troponin remained within normal limits. She was discharged after a 5-day hospital stay. By discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had subsided, though she remained deconditioned. Over the next 4 months, she experienced worsening weakness. She stated she had never taken statins and did not consume other supplements linked with IMNM such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, nor any other nutritional products. She also reported no notable family history of myopathy, no recent vigorous or sustained exercise, and no use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her initial and sole administration of semaglutide, her clinical condition continued to worsen. She was unable to stand or sit upright without support, and for several days had experienced difficulty in swallowing both liquids and solids. This led her to seek care at an outside hospital, where her creatine kinase (CK) level was elevated to 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. Management included daily intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg for 5 days, under neurology guidance via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for rheumatologic assessment. Upon evaluation, she was afebrile, had normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable. Physical examination demonstrated pronounced muscle weakness, predominantly affecting proximal muscle groups (see table 1). There were no rashes, skin changes, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological impairments.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone therapy, she was started on daily oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg. Because her muscle weakness persisted and her clinical presentation remained severe, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Over a 2-week period, there was significant improvement in her CK levels (figure 1). She was discharged following nearly 4 weeks of hospitalization. Twelve days post-discharge, she was re-admitted for 10 days owing to septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, and during this admission her CK was normal.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, demonstrating ongoing gains in both functional capacity and general well-being, accompanied by consistently normal CK results. Consequently, her prednisone taper was completed swiftly without issue. Owing to her clinical progress following cessation of semaglutide and financial considerations, no further IVIG was administered beyond the initial inpatient course.

At her most recent outpatient assessment, which occurred 10 months after her original presentation and commencement of treatment, she had returned to her pre-illness functional level and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a previous peak of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the facility where she was transferred from. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels remained within the reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) was measured at 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) at 165 U/L, in the context of raised CK and a history of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody screening returned negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were both normal, and no cytopenias were noted. A myositis antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. The only positive result was a low-titer SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein was also elevated at 22.8 mg/L. High-sensitivity troponin was measured at 62 pg/mL (reference: ≤53 pg/mL), and proBNP was significantly raised at 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies encompassed MRI scans of both femurs, an echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and an abdominal MRI. The MRI of the bilateral femurs demonstrated mild, symmetrical edema in the musculature of both thighs, most prominent in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous edema on the lateral sides of both thighs (figure 2). The echocardiogram identified an estimated left ventricular ejection fraction between 65% and 70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure of 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI further revealed mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall segments, correlating with myocardial edema, and lacked definitive late gadolinium enhancement—findings that could reflect either myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or alternatively, a localized stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No substantial myocardial scarring, infarction, or significant valvular abnormalities were evident. The liver ultrasound with Doppler detected hepatosteatosis. Her high-resolution chest CT did not reveal interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI noted steatotic hepatomegaly but was otherwise within normal limits, with no lymphadenopathy.

Further advanced diagnostic evaluations included repeat liver biopsy and biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy conducted during hospitalization revealed several fragments of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving approximately 70% of the examined liver parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. This histological picture aligned with her established diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, showing a mild advancement of liver disease corresponding to a NAFLD Activity Score of 5 out of 8, at stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated acid phosphatase staining (figure 3D), CD68 staining—which identifies macrophages—along with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). A membrane attack complex positive stain was detected on both necrotic and intact muscle fibers (figure 4C), suggestive of an inflammatory process. Additionally, rare cells staining positive for CD3, a marker for T lymphocytes (figure 4A), and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were noted. The muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient demonstrated a pronounced and rapid adverse response following her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating a likely intolerance to the medication. She initially manifested gastrointestinal symptoms and developed renal insufficiency believed to be due to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) levels were only mildly increased—making rhabdomyolysis unlikely—her clinical status continued to deteriorate despite discontinuing semaglutide. Over the subsequent four months, her weakness pattern and a twelvefold rise in CK levels pointed to an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM), likely linked to semaglutide exposure. In addition, muscle biopsy findings typical of drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical evidence for an underlying connective tissue disorder, and persistent improvement following immunosuppressive therapy coupled with semaglutide withdrawal further reinforce this connection.

It is significant that transaminitis was present before she began treatment with semaglutide, a feature sometimes associated with myopathies. Nevertheless, liver biopsy conducted three years prior to initiation of semaglutide had confirmed hepatosteatosis, and the mildly increased AST and ALT remained after normalization of CK levels. Therefore, it appears her persistent transaminase elevation stems from the liver and is unlikely to be related to muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessments did not confirm ongoing myocarditis. Given her prompt improvement, stress-induced myopathy remains a plausible explanation in this context.

The presence of a positive SS-B antibody, accompanied by negative ANA and SS-A results, was unanticipated. To date, the literature does not report cases in which isolated SS-B seropositivity with negative ANA and SS-A has resulted in IMNM. Unfortunately, there was no creatine kinase (CK) level available before semaglutide therapy, making it impossible to entirely rule out a pre-existing, low-grade autoimmune IMNM prior to starting the medication. Nevertheless, the twelvefold elevation in CK and development of progressive proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide administration suggest a greater likelihood that this episode of IMNM was induced by the drug. Furthermore, the patient’s severe IMNM remitted following a brief regimen of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) in combination with discontinuation of semaglutide. This trajectory is more consistent with drug-induced IMNM, which may resolve after withdrawal of the causative agent and a limited duration of immunosuppression, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM, where extended and aggressive immunosuppressive treatment is frequently required.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, after which she transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg each day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided across four days, in light of ongoing weakness and significant symptoms. She underwent multiple outpatient evaluations, which demonstrated ongoing gains in her physical function and general condition, along with consistently normal creatine kinase (CK) values. As a result, her prednisone was tapered successfully over a two-month period. Owing to both financial constraints and her stable improvement after discontinuation of semaglutide, no additional IVIG was provided following the initial inpatient treatment.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months after starting therapy, she had returned to her previous level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive medications for 7 months. Her creatine kinase values stayed within the normal limits since her hospital admission and immunosuppressive treatment. Subsequent echocardiogram and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of her earlier cardiac findings. There were no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation during this period.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy for metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Gastrointestinal side effects are common, though these agents have also been linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in some instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the patient’s presentation aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. Following a single administration of semaglutide, CK values rose twelvefold over a span of four months; this is in contrast to the expectation of CK normalization within several weeks in cases where rhabdomyolysis is attributable to a direct toxic effect from the drug, as has been documented in other drug-induced rhabdomyolysis events. Importantly, a previously reported case of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide demonstrated a typical clinical trajectory, including an acute and substantial increase in CK (CK=57,014 at admission), followed by rapid recovery upon cessation of the medication and initiation of hydration therapy within a few days, without a requirement for immunosuppressive treatment.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case demonstrates a probable causal link between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM, owing to a plausible temporal association, exclusion of alternative etiologies or medications, and an appropriate clinical response upon cessation of the drug. A rechallenge with semaglutide would not be ethically justified given the severity of the observed adverse reaction. Nevertheless, we recognize that causality cannot be definitively established in this instance. Given the rapid increase in semaglutide utilization, our report seeks to alert clinicians to this potential adverse event and underscore the importance of robust pharmacovigilance. Further research will be necessary to determine whether the observed relationship is causal or merely coincidental.

In addition, the underlying factors contributing to our patient's pronounced reaction to semaglutide remain undetermined. With GLP-1 receptor agonists gaining widespread use, investigations into possible predispositions will be crucial if additional cases exhibiting a strong temporal association with semaglutide exposure emerge in the future.
