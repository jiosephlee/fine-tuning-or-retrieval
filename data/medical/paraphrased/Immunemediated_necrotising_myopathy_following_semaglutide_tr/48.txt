Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case involves a woman in her sixties who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. Over a period of four months, she exhibited increasing dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels. These manifestations were preceded by administration of a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation demonstrated findings indicative of necrotising myopathy, including inflammatory infiltrates identified on muscle biopsy. Treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) resulted in improvement of her symptoms. She gradually recovered, achieving her previous level of function after discontinuing immunosuppressive therapy for several months and continued avoidance of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This report documents the first case of IMNM possibly linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist usage, with notable resolution following brief immunosuppression and IVIG therapy.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely utilized in the management of metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. One reported complication linked to this medication class is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. Recently, a case report detailed an instance of rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotizing characteristics in connection with tirzepatide, a frequently prescribed GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that report, the patient experienced a marked rise in creatine kinase (CK) levels, reaching into the thousands during ongoing therapy, with CK values beginning to decline just days after stopping the drug—findings indicative of rhabdomyolysis induced by the medication.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a specific form of myopathy distinguished by severe weakness affecting proximal muscles, histologically marked by necrotising muscle fibres and minimal presence of lymphocytic infiltrates. Autoantibodies directed against hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and signal recognition particle (SRP) are closely linked to IMNM. Statins, which are widely prescribed for metabolic conditions, have a strong association with the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to recognizing the connection with HMGCR autoantibodies, multiple individuals who were exposed to statins developed signs consistent with necrotising myopathy and were commonly diagnosed as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these patients, muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels frequently persisted after discontinuing statin therapy. Subsequent improvement following treatment with immunosuppressants indicated an autoimmune process, ultimately leading to the characterization of HMGCR autoantibody positivity as a central feature of the disorder. Nevertheless, it is important to note that approximately one-third of IMNM patients lack detectable autoantibodies. The absence of autoantibody in this group presents further challenges in diagnosis and implies that other related autoantibodies or possible precipitating factors remain unidentified.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s, whose past medical history included obesity, fatty liver, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant history of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening weakness affecting her proximal muscles, as well as difficulty swallowing and speaking.

She had been previously healthy and maintained full independence in daily activities. Three years before her clinical decline, she was being assessed as an outpatient due to elevated liver transaminases. At that time, she had mildly increased aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels—both around twice the upper reference limit, with ALT being higher than AST. Her alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts remained normal. The patient reported no alcohol consumption and was not taking any drugs known to affect the liver. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed an enlarged liver with diffusely increased echogenicity, findings most compatible with hepatic steatosis. Screening for viral hepatitis and testing for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies all returned negative results. Liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis in keeping with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), showing a NAFLD Activity Score of 4 out of 8 and stage 0 fibrosis. Lifestyle modifications, including adherence to a calorie-restricted diet and regular physical activity, were recommended.

Given ongoing fatty liver disease and obesity 2.5 years after her NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was started. Shortly after her initial dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg subcutaneously), she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms continued, resulting in hospital admission two days later for nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal failure, and profound metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate therapy, and antiemetics. She required a brief transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal impairment improved with fluid resuscitation, and she did not require hemofiltration or dialysis. Her creatine kinase on admission was found to be elevated at 337 U/L, but she exhibited no accompanying rash, myalgias, or dark-colored urine. During her stay, she also developed episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, but her serum troponin remained within normal limits. She was discharged home after five days. On discharge, her gastrointestinal symptoms had abated, although she was noted to be physically deconditioned. Over the subsequent four months, she experienced a gradual onset of increasing weakness. She reported no previous use of statins, did not ingest mushrooms, red yeast rice, or other supplements linked with IMNM, and did not use any additional nutritional supplements. She also denied any notable family history of myopathy, excessive or prolonged physical activity, or use of alcohol or illicit drugs.

Four months following administration of her first and sole dose of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or remain seated without assistance, and developed dysphagia involving both liquids and solids over several days. This led to her presentation at an external hospital, where her admission labs showed a creatine kinase level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). Neurology was consulted via telemedicine, and she received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg per day for 5 days. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for rheumatology assessment, where she was noted to be afebrile, normotensive, and without signs of distress. Examination demonstrated marked weakness, particularly in the proximal muscles (see table 1). There were no cutaneous findings, rashes, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurological deficits.

After receiving pulse therapy with methylprednisolone, the patient was placed on oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. She subsequently received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at 2 g/kg, administered over 4 days, in response to ongoing weakness and pronounced clinical severity. Over a 2-week period, her CK levels demonstrated significant improvement (figure 1). She was discharged following a hospitalization of almost 4 weeks’ duration. Twelve days after discharge, she required readmission for 10 days owing to septic shock caused by a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK levels remained within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient appointments where her functional abilities and general health continued to get better, accompanied by consistently normal CK values. As a result, her prednisone was able to be tapered quickly and discontinued. Given her improvement without semaglutide and financial considerations, she did not receive further IVIG beyond her first course during hospitalization.

At her most recent outpatient visit, which occurred 10 months following the onset of treatment, she had returned to her baseline level of functioning and had been off immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory assessment revealed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L upon evaluation, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within normal parameters. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, findings observed in the context of increased serum CK and a history of fatty liver disease. Autoantibody screening demonstrated negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels were within normal limits, as was creatinine, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. The myositis panel was also negative for SRP and HMGCR, as well as for Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-γ, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. She did, however, show a low titer positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was increased at 22 mm/hour, with a C-reactive protein of 22.8 mg/L. Of note, her high-sensitivity troponin was elevated at 62 pg/mL (reference: ≤53 pg/mL), and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Imaging studies performed included MRI scans of both femurs, an echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs demonstrated mild, symmetric muscular edema bilaterally, most pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, accompanied by subcutaneous edema along the lateral sides of both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70%, and a right ventricular systolic pressure measuring 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior walls with associated myocardial edema, lacking definite late gadolinium enhancement; this was considered possibly indicative of myocardial inflammation/early myocarditis or focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial scarring, infarction, or major valvular abnormalities. Liver ultrasound with Doppler showed findings consistent with hepatosteatosis. High-resolution CT of the chest did not show interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI indicated steatotic hepatomegaly, but was otherwise unremarkable and showed no lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced diagnostic evaluations included repeat liver biopsy and biopsy of thigh muscle. The liver biopsy performed during hospitalization revealed several cores of hepatic tissue exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis, affecting about 70% of the sampled liver parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. The results were in keeping with her prior diagnosis of hepatosteatosis, showing mild worsening to NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. Examination of the right vastus lateralis muscle biopsy showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), indicative of macrophages, and myophagocytosis (figure 4B). In addition, staining for membrane attack complex was positive in both necrotizing and non-necrotizing muscle fibers (figure 4C), suggesting an inflammatory reaction. Scattered CD3-positive T cells (figure 4A) and perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C) were also observed. Overall, findings on muscle biopsy were compatible with an IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed a notable and rapid response after her first and sole administration of semaglutide, indicating intolerance. Initial manifestations were gastrointestinal disturbances and renal insufficiency, presumed to be related to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly elevated and did not align with rhabdomyolysis at presentation, the ongoing deterioration in her condition after discontinuing semaglutide—together with the pattern of muscular weakness and a twelvefold increase in CK over four months following exposure—indicate an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely linked to semaglutide. In addition, muscle biopsy findings consistent with drug-induced IMNM, absence of alternative clinical signs suggesting a connective tissue disease, and continued recovery following initial immunosuppression and withdrawal of semaglutide further substantiate this connection.

It is important to mention that she exhibited transaminitis before initiating semaglutide, a phenomenon observed in various myopathies. Despite this, liver biopsy conducted three years before semaglutide administration confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her marginally raised AST and ALT remained unchanged even after normalization of CK levels. Therefore, her chronic transaminase elevation probably stems from a hepatic source rather than muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not definitively indicate ongoing myocarditis. Considering her swift improvement, stress-related myopathy is another plausible explanation in this clinical context.

Her isolated SS-B antibody positivity, accompanied by negative ANA and SS-A, was an unusual finding. To date, there are no documented cases linking isolated SS-B positivity in the absence of ANA and SS-A to IMNM. Regrettably, we were unable to obtain a CK value preceding the introduction of semaglutide, making it impossible to definitively rule out a pre-existing, low-grade autoimmune IMNM before semaglutide initiation. Nonetheless, the 12-fold increase in CK levels and worsening proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide administration support a stronger association with medication-induced IMNM. Moreover, the patient’s severe IMNM resolved following a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG, alongside cessation of semaglutide. This trajectory is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, which typically responds to short-term immunosuppression after discontinuing the causative agent, as opposed to idiopathic IMNM that generally demands ongoing, intensive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for five days, then transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Because of ongoing muscle weakness and significant symptoms, she was also administered IVIG at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over four days. Multiple follow-up visits in the outpatient clinic demonstrated consistent gains in her functional abilities and general well-being, with creatine kinase levels remaining within the normal range. Owing to her clinical improvement, prednisone was tapered rapidly and discontinued over a two-month period. As she maintained her progress off semaglutide and in light of financial concerns, no further IVIG was given after the initial inpatient course.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months after her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, her most recent outpatient visit confirmed restoration of her baseline functional abilities; she had been free from immunosuppressive medication for seven months. Throughout the period following her hospital admission and immunosuppressive therapy, her creatine kinase values consistently stayed in the normal range. Follow-up echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of previous abnormal findings. Additionally, she had no further episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown efficacy in the management of metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. Gastrointestinal symptoms are frequently observed as adverse effects, though these agents might also be linked to reductions in muscle mass and, in some instances, rhabdomyolysis. In this particular patient, the clinical evolution aligns more closely with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. The patient’s creatine kinase rose twelvefold during a four-month period after a single dose of semaglutide; this pattern contrasts with expectations for semaglutide-induced rhabdomyolysis, where normalization of CK would typically occur within a few weeks, based on prior reports of drug-related rhabdomyolysis. Of interest, a previously documented case of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide exhibited the characteristic course: an acute and pronounced CK elevation (CK=57,014 at initial evaluation) with rapid resolution following cessation of the medication and administration of hydration over several days, obviating the need for immunosuppressive therapy.

Applying the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, semaglutide is assessed as having a probable causal relationship with IMNM in this case, based on an appropriate temporal association, low likelihood of alternative etiologies, and a clinically plausible response following drug discontinuation. Re-exposure to semaglutide would not be ethically appropriate, considering the severity of the adverse event observed. Nevertheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. With the rising utilization of semaglutide, our report serves to alert clinicians to this possible adverse reaction and encourages heightened pharmacovigilance. Additional research will help determine whether this association is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

The factors underlying our patient's susceptibility to such a severe reaction to semaglutide remain undetermined. As GLP-1 receptor agonists become increasingly prevalent, investigations into predisposition will be essential should more cases with similar temporal linkage to semaglutide exposure emerge in the future.
