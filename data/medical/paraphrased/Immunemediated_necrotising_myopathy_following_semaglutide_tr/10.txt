Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. She experienced gradually worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels over four months, with these symptoms emerging after receiving a single dose of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation showed findings in keeping with necrotising myopathy accompanied by an inflammatory infiltrate on muscle biopsy. She exhibited clinical improvement following treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG). Over time, she returned to her premorbid level of function and remained stable for several months without immunosuppressive therapy, as long as glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists were avoided. This report presents the first known case of IMNM likely related to use of a GLP-1 receptor agonist, with notable recovery following a limited course of immunosuppressive therapy and IVIG.

Background:

In the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Among their reported side effects is a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report has highlighted rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics in connection with tirzepatide, another widely used GLP-1 receptor agonist. The patient involved exhibited a significant rise in creatine kinase (CK) levels—measured in the thousands—while continuing the medication; notably, CK levels began to decline within several days after the therapy was stopped, aligning with a diagnosis of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a specific form of myopathy, notable for severe proximal muscle weakness, the presence of necrotic muscle fibers, and minimal lymphocytic infiltration on histological examination. Two autoantibodies, anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP), have a strong association with IMNM. Notably, the use of statins—drugs frequently prescribed for metabolic conditions—has been significantly linked to the development of HMGCR autoantibodies. Before recognizing the link with HMGCR autoantibodies, several patients on statins who presented with necrotising myopathy symptoms were often misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these individuals, muscle weakness and elevated creatine kinase (CK) levels often continued even after discontinuing statin therapy. The subsequent positive response to immunosuppressive treatment suggested an underlying autoimmune mechanism, ultimately leading to the identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a key feature of this condition. However, it should be recognized that roughly one-third of IMNM patients do not exhibit detectable autoantibodies. This seronegative group presents further diagnostic complexities and implies that there are other, yet unidentified, autoantibodies and potential triggers contributing to the disease process.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a past medical history including obesity, fatty liver disease, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with worsening weakness of the proximal muscles, as well as difficulties with swallowing and voice changes.

Before this, she was generally healthy and fully independent in daily functioning. Three years before her current decline, she was being assessed as an outpatient for increased transaminase levels. During that evaluation, she was found to have mildly raised aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), both approximately two times the upper limit of normal, with ALT values exceeding those of AST. Results for alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet count were within normal parameters. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using any medications known to be toxic to the liver. An abdominal ultrasound revealed hepatomegaly with diffusely increased echogenicity, findings consistent with hepatic steatosis. Testing for viral hepatitis as well as anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies all returned negative. Liver biopsy findings indicated steatohepatitis in line with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), with an NAFLD Activity Score of 4/8 and fibrosis stage 0. She was counseled to adopt a calorie-restricted diet and to engage in regular physical activity.

Ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity persisted 2.5 years following her initial diagnosis of NAFLD, leading to initiation of semaglutide therapy. She developed nausea and vomiting within hours after her first subcutaneous injection of semaglutide (0.25 mg). These symptoms did not resolve and, two days later, she required hospitalization for continued nausea, vomiting, poor oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal failure, and severe metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate therapy, and antiemetic medications. She was briefly admitted to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal function subsequently improved with fluid resuscitation, and she did not require haemodialysis or haemofiltration. Admission laboratory evaluation showed an elevated creatine kinase (CK) of 337 U/L. She did not report any associated muscle pain, skin rash, or dark-colored urine. During her admission, she experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, but her serum troponin was within the normal range. She was discharged after a five-day hospital stay. By the time of discharge, her gastrointestinal complaints had resolved, though she remained physically deconditioned. Over the next four months, she reported gradually worsening weakness. She specifically denied prior statin exposure, significant intake of mushrooms, red yeast rice, or any other supplements known to be linked to IMNM, as well as any other nutritional supplements. She also denied any relevant family history of myopathy, any history of unusual or strenuous physical activity, and alcohol or recreational drug use.

Four months following her first and only administration of semaglutide, her condition further deteriorated. She became unable to stand or maintain an upright seated position without help, and experienced increasing difficulty swallowing both liquids and solids over several days. Consequently, she presented to an external hospital, where laboratory testing showed an elevated creatine kinase level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) upon admission. She received treatment with intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, managed by neurology via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care facility for evaluation by rheumatology. On arrival, her vital signs indicated she was afebrile and normotensive, with no signs of distress. Physical examination demonstrated marked muscle weakness, particularly pronounced in proximal groups (table 1). No rashes, skin alterations, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or other neurological deficits were noted.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone, she was transitioned to oral prednisone at a dose of 1 mg/kg per day. Because of ongoing muscle weakness and significant disease severity, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Over the following 2 weeks, her creatine kinase (CK) levels improved substantially (see figure 1). She was discharged after nearly 4 weeks in the hospital. Approximately 12 days post-discharge, she was re-admitted for 10 days due to septic shock from a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK was found to be within normal limits.

She continued to be seen in outpatient follow-up, demonstrating ongoing functional and overall health improvements with sustained normalization of CK values. As a result, her prednisone was able to be tapered quickly. Given the financial considerations and her notable improvement without semaglutide, no additional IVIG infusions were administered beyond the initial course received during hospitalization.

She was last seen in the outpatient setting 10 months after her initial evaluation and treatment initiation and had regained her baseline functional status, off immunosuppression for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory investigations revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with a prior peak measurement of 3992 U/L recorded a week earlier at the referring facility. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was within normal parameters. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) measured 191 U/L, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was 165 U/L, both in the context of increased serum CK and a known history of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody testing showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibody type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were within normal ranges, and no cytopenias were detected. A comprehensive myositis antibody panel was negative for anti-signal recognition particle (SRP), anti-3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR), as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. There was, however, a low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference: <1.0). Erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was raised at 22 mm/hour, and C-reactive protein measured 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was elevated at 62 pg/mL (normal: ≤53 pg/mL), and the pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was 4358 pg/mL (reference range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies performed included MRI scans of both femurs, an echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. The bilateral femur MRI demonstrated mild, symmetric edema of the musculature, most prominently in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, as well as subcutaneous edema along the lateral thighs (see figure 2). The echocardiogram showed a left ventricular ejection fraction estimated at 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI identified mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall accompanied by myocardial edema, but lacked definitive late gadolinium enhancement, suggestive either of myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. No evidence of major myocardial scarring, infarction, or significant valve abnormalities was observed. The liver Doppler ultrasound indicated hepatic steatosis. The high-resolution CT scan of the chest did not demonstrate findings consistent with interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI revealed enlarged fatty liver (steatotic hepatomegaly) but was otherwise normal, with no detectable lymphadenopathy.

Additional advanced diagnostic studies included a repeated liver biopsy and a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, carried out during her hospitalization, revealed multiple fragments of hepatic tissue containing both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving approximately 70% of the sampled liver parenchyma. There was no evidence of significant portal inflammation or interface hepatitis. These results aligned with her known history of hepatosteatosis, showing mild advancement of her liver pathology to a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 5 out of 8 and stage 1. Biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle showed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A, B). Immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68), a marker for macrophages, highlighting areas of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Additionally, there was positive staining for the membrane attack complex on both necrotic and non-necrotic myofibers (figure 4C), indicating an active inflammatory process. Occasional CD3-positive cells, indicative of T cells (figure 4A), as well as perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C), were also noted. Overall, the muscle biopsy findings supported a diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM).

Differential diagnosis:

The patient developed a pronounced and rapid response following her first and sole administration of semaglutide, indicating intolerance to the medication. Initially, she presented with gastrointestinal symptoms and developed renal failure thought to be due to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly raised at the time—making rhabdomyolysis unlikely—her ongoing deterioration in the absence of further semaglutide exposure, the pattern of muscular weakness, and a twelvefold rise in CK over four months post-semaglutide suggest immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings typical for drug-induced IMNM, absence of clinical features pointing to a primary connective tissue disease, and persistent improvement following both immunosuppressive therapy and semaglutide discontinuation further corroborate this possible link.

It is important to highlight that the patient exhibited elevated transaminase levels before initiating semaglutide therapy, a finding that can be associated with myopathies. Nonetheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years before she began semaglutide demonstrated hepatosteatosis, and her mildly increased AST/ALT persisted even after creatine kinase levels returned to normal. This suggests that her chronically raised transaminases most likely have a hepatic etiology rather than being secondary to muscle injury. Additionally, her cardiac evaluation did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. In the context of her clinical presentation and swift improvement, stress-induced myopathy is also a plausible consideration.

The finding of isolated SS-B antibody positivity, with both ANA and SS-A being negative, was unanticipated. To our knowledge, isolated SS-B reactivity without accompanying ANA or SS-A antibodies has not been reported in association with IMNM. Regrettably, we lacked access to CK values prior to the administration of semaglutide, so it is not possible to definitively rule out a pre-existing, subclinical autoimmune IMNM before semaglutide was started. Nevertheless, the 12-fold increase in CK and the worsening proximal muscle weakness that followed initiation of semaglutide suggest that the IMNM episode was likely medication-induced. Moreover, her significant IMNM improved after a brief course of corticosteroids and IVIG along with discontinuation of semaglutide. This sequence of clinical events is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM, which can respond to short-term immunosuppression and cessation of the inciting drug, unlike idiopathic IMNM that typically necessitates prolonged and aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days because of ongoing weakness and the presence of severe symptoms. During multiple outpatient follow-up visits, she demonstrated steady improvement in her functional abilities and overall condition, with consistently normal creatine kinase levels. As a result, her prednisone was able to be tapered off rapidly within a two-month period. Because of both financial factors and her continued progress without semaglutide, no further IVIG was given after the initial hospitalization course.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient follow-up, which occurred 10 months after her initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she had returned to her previous level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for the past 7 months. Throughout this period, her creatine kinase values stayed within normal limits after hospital discharge and immunosuppressive treatment. Repeat cardiac imaging with echocardiography and MRI demonstrated resolution of the previously noted abnormalities. She also remained free of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation episodes.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy for metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are common, these agents have also been implicated in reduced muscle mass and possibly in the development of rhabdomyolysis. In this report, the evolution of the patient’s illness aligns more with IMNM rather than rhabdomyolysis. After a single dose of semaglutide, the patient’s CK values rose by a factor of 12 over a 4-month period; if semaglutide’s direct toxicity had been responsible, as observed in other cases of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis, one would anticipate CK normalization within several weeks. Importantly, a prior case of rhabdomyolysis associated with tirzepatide demonstrated the expected clinical progression: the patient presented with a pronounced acute CK increase (CK=57 014 at presentation) and showed a rapid CK decline with cessation of the drug and hydration over a few days, and did not require immunosuppressive therapy.

According to the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case exhibits a probable association between semaglutide administration and the development of IMNM, as evidenced by a plausible temporal link, a low likelihood of alternative etiologies such as other diseases or medications, and a clinically appropriate improvement following discontinuation. Re-exposure to semaglutide would not be ethical in light of the severe adverse effect observed. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. Given the widespread and rapidly increasing utilization of semaglutide, this case report seeks to inform clinicians of this potential adverse reaction and to promote increased pharmacovigilance. Additional research will be necessary to determine whether this observed link is causal or merely coincidental.

Moreover, the factors that may have made our patient more susceptible to this significant adverse event remain unknown. As the use of GLP-1 receptor agonists expands, future studies investigating predisposing factors will be crucial, particularly if additional cases showing a close temporal association with semaglutide arise.
