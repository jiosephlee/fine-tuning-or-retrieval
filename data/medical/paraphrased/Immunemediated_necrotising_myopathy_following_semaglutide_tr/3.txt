Title: Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy following semaglutide treatment: a contributing factor?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report details a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following exposure to semaglutide. The patient presented with worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase spanning four months, beginning after a single administration of semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation revealed findings consistent with necrotising myopathy and inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. She experienced clinical improvement following immunosuppressive treatment with corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), ultimately regaining her previous functional level after discontinuing immunosuppression for several months and continuing to avoid glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists. This is the first documented instance of IMNM likely associated with GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, demonstrating apparent resolution after a brief period of immunosuppression and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have been widely utilized in the management of metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. This medication class has been linked to various adverse effects, including reductions in skeletal muscle mass. A recent case report detailed an occurrence of rhabdomyolysis with necrotising characteristics in a patient treated with tirzepatide, another GLP-1 receptor agonist frequently prescribed. In that case, the individual exhibited marked increases in creatine kinase (CK), reaching levels in the thousands during ongoing therapy; following cessation of the medication, CK values began to decline within a few days, indicative of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a specific type of myopathy, marked by severe weakness of proximal muscles and histological evidence of myofibre necrosis with minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Autoantibodies such as anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) are strongly linked with IMNM. Notably, statins—widely prescribed for metabolic disorders—are closely correlated with the presence of HMGCR autoantibodies. Prior to recognition of the association with HMGCR autoantibodies, several statin-treated individuals who developed necrotising myopathy were often misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these cases, muscle symptoms and elevated CK frequently persisted even after statin withdrawal. The subsequent response to immunosuppressive treatment suggested an underlying autoimmune process and ultimately led to the identification of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining feature of this condition. Importantly, it is estimated that about one-third of IMNM patients lack detectable autoantibodies. The absence of autoantibodies in this population not only complicates diagnosis but also implies that further associated autoantibodies and triggers may yet be unknown.

Case presentation:

A woman in her 60s with a background of obesity, fatty liver disease, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a past episode of deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism presented with worsening proximal muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, and impaired voice.

Prior to onset of these symptoms, she was generally healthy and able to perform all daily activities independently. Three years before her current decline, she was evaluated as an outpatient for elevated liver enzymes. At that assessment, mild increases in AST and ALT were observed, both roughly twice the upper limit of normal, with ALT higher than AST. Alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet levels were normal. She reported no alcohol consumption and was not using hepatotoxic drugs. An abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with a diffusely increased echotexture, suggestive of hepatic steatosis. Screening for viral hepatitis and autoimmune liver disease—including anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies—yielded negative results. Liver biopsy findings indicated steatohepatitis characteristic of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), graded NAFLD Activity Score 4/8 with stage 0. She was counseled to maintain a low-calorie diet and engage in regular physical activity.

Due to ongoing hepatic steatosis and obesity 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, semaglutide therapy was commenced. Shortly after the first subcutaneous injection of semaglutide at a dose of 0.25 mg, she developed nausea and vomiting. These symptoms persisted, prompting hospital admission two days later for nausea, vomiting, inadequate oral intake, shortness of breath, acute renal failure, and pronounced metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids, bicarbonate, and anti-emetic medications. The patient was temporarily transferred to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilation. Renal function subsequently improved with hydration, negating the need for haemofiltration or dialysis. On admission, her creatine kinase level was elevated to 337 U/L, but she did not report accompanying symptoms such as rash, myalgia, or dark urine. During her hospital stay, she experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, although her serum troponin levels remained within normal limits. After a 5-day hospitalization, she was discharged; at that time, her gastrointestinal complaints had subsided, but she remained physically deconditioned. Over the subsequent four months, she experienced gradual worsening weakness. She reported no history of statin use, notable consumption of supplements known to be linked with IMNM, such as mushrooms or red yeast rice, nor other nutritional supplements. She also denied a significant family history of myopathy, recent prolonged or excessive physical exertion, and use of alcohol or illicit substances.

Four months following her initial and only administration of semaglutide, her condition continued to worsen. She became unable to stand or sit upright unaided and, over several days, experienced increasing difficulty in swallowing both liquids and solids. These symptoms led her to seek care at another hospital, where her admission laboratory studies revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L). She received daily intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg for 5 days while undergoing neurology evaluation via telemedicine. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary center for rheumatology assessment. Upon arrival, she was found to be afebrile, with normal blood pressure, and otherwise comfortable. Examination identified pronounced muscle weakness, predominantly affecting her proximal muscle groups (as outlined in table 1). She showed no evidence of rashes, skin alterations, crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurological abnormalities.

After completing pulse therapy with methylprednisolone, the patient continued on oral prednisone at a dosage of 1 mg/kg per day. Because of ongoing weakness and pronounced disease manifestations, she received intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) at a total dose of 2 g/kg, administered over 4 days. Over the subsequent 2 weeks, her CK levels showed significant reduction (see figure 1). She was discharged following a hospitalization of almost 4 weeks. Nearly 12 days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days after developing septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK was normal.

She attended multiple outpatient appointments during which her functional capacity and general health progressively improved, with CK levels remaining within normal limits. Consequently, her prednisone therapy was discontinued quickly without issue. Given both financial constraints and sustained improvement in the absence of semaglutide, no further IVIG was administered beyond the initial regimen provided during hospitalization.

Her most recent outpatient visit occurred 10 months following initial assessment and commencement of treatment, at which point she had returned to her pre-illness functional baseline and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for 8 months.

Investigations:

Her laboratory evaluation revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L upon assessment, with a maximum value of 3992 U/L recorded one week earlier at the referring hospital. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) was found to be within the reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) measured 191 U/L and 165 U/L, respectively, in the context of increased serum CK and a background of hepatic steatosis. Autoantibody testing was significant for negative anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement levels and creatinine were normal, and there was no evidence of cytopenias. Her myositis-specific antibody panel was negative for SRP, HMGCR, Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. She was found to have a low-titer positive SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (reference range: <1.0). The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was elevated at 22 mm/hour, as was the C-reactive protein (CRP) at 22.8 mg/L. Of note, high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) was increased to 62 pg/mL (normal range ≤53 pg/mL) and pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) was 4358 pg/mL (normal: 0–125 pg/mL).

Key imaging studies consisted of MRI scans of both femurs, echocardiography, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution chest CT, and abdominal MRI. MRI of the femurs showed mild, symmetrical edema in the muscles of both thighs, particularly pronounced in the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous edema on the lateral sides of both thighs (see figure 2). The echocardiogram demonstrated a left ventricular ejection fraction of 65%–70% and a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI indicated mild hypokinesis of the mid-anteroseptal and anterior wall regions in association with myocardial edema, without clear evidence of late gadolinium enhancement; these findings were thought to suggest either myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no notable myocardial infarction, scarring, or significant valvular abnormality detected. Liver ultrasound with Doppler was remarkable for hepatic steatosis. High-resolution chest CT showed no signs of interstitial lung disease or lymphadenopathy. Abdominal MRI revealed hepatomegaly due to steatosis, with no other abnormalities and no evidence of lymphadenopathy.

Further diagnostic procedures included performing a repeat liver biopsy along with a biopsy of thigh muscle tissue. The liver biopsy, conducted during her hospitalization, showed several tissue cores exhibiting both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving roughly 70% of the examined hepatic parenchyma, without notable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. This pattern was in keeping with her known history of hepatosteatosis and indicated a mild advancement to NAFLD Activity Score grade 5/8, stage 1. The biopsy of the right vastus lateralis muscle revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating muscle fibers (figure 3A,B); immunohistochemistry highlighted acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68)—a macrophage marker—along with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Furthermore, membrane attack complex positive staining was observed on both necrotic and intact myofibers (figure 4C), suggesting an inflammatory reaction. Occasional CD3 positive cells, indicative of T-cell presence (figure 4A), as well as perivascular inflammatory cells (figure 3C), were identified. These muscle biopsy findings were compatible with IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient had a pronounced and acute adverse response to her first and sole administration of semaglutide, raising suspicion for drug intolerance. Initially, she manifested gastrointestinal symptoms and acute kidney injury, believed to be due to hypovolemia. Although her creatine kinase (CK) was only mildly raised—making rhabdomyolysis unlikely—her persistent deterioration in the absence of further semaglutide, the pattern of her muscle weakness, and a 12-fold rise in CK over 4 months post-exposure all point to an immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy (IMNM) likely related to semaglutide. Additionally, muscle biopsy findings characteristic of medication-induced IMNM, the absence of clinical evidence for a primary connective tissue disorder, and durable clinical improvement after immunosuppression and discontinuation of semaglutide further reinforce this connection.

It is important to note that the patient exhibited elevated transaminase levels before beginning semaglutide therapy, a finding that may occur in myopathies. Nonetheless, three years prior to starting semaglutide, a liver biopsy had confirmed hepatosteatosis, and her mildly elevated AST and ALT remained even after creatine kinase levels returned to normal. Therefore, her chronically raised transaminases are most likely hepatic in origin rather than resulting from muscle injury. Additionally, cardiac assessments did not conclusively indicate active myocarditis. Considering her swift clinical improvement, stress-related myopathy is also a plausible diagnosis in this context.

The presence of a positive SS-B antibody in conjunction with negative ANA and SS-A was unanticipated. To date, there are no documented cases detailing isolated SS-B positivity, in the absence of ANA and SS-A, as a precursor to IMNM. Regrettably, no creatine kinase (CK) value was available prior to the initiation of semaglutide, making it impossible to definitively rule out pre-existing low-grade autoimmune IMNM before semaglutide therapy. Nevertheless, the twelvefold increase in CK and worsening proximal muscle weakness that ensued following semaglutide administration suggest a greater probability that this occurrence of IMNM was associated with medication use. Moreover, the patient’s severe IMNM responded to a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG combined with discontinuation of semaglutide. This clinical trajectory is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM—which typically improves with short-term immunosuppression and cessation of the offending medication—than idiopathic IMNM, which generally requires extended, aggressive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

She received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dose of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. IVIG was administered at 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days, in light of her ongoing weakness and significant disease manifestations. Multiple follow-up appointments in the outpatient setting demonstrated progressive gains in her functional abilities and general well-being, with creatine kinase (CK) levels remaining within the normal range. On this basis, her prednisone was successfully tapered off within 2 months. Given financial constraints and her ongoing improvement in the absence of semaglutide, she did not receive further IVIG treatment after the initial course provided during hospitalization.

Outcome and follow-up:

At her most recent outpatient visit, which occurred 10 months after starting therapy, the patient had recovered her baseline functional abilities and had been free of immunosuppressive medications for 7 months. Throughout the post-hospital period and after immunosuppressive treatment, her creatine kinase values stayed within normal limits. Follow-up cardiac assessments, including echocardiography and MRI, demonstrated resolution of previously noted abnormalities. Additionally, she remained free from paroxysmal atrial fibrillation episodes.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide, are increasingly utilized and have shown benefit in treating metabolic diseases like obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are most frequently observed, these agents have also been implicated in reduced muscle mass and possible rhabdomyolysis. In this report, the patient’s progression aligns more with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. The patient’s creatine kinase (CK) levels rose twelvefold across four months after receiving a single dose of semaglutide; if semaglutide had been responsible for a direct toxic effect as in other drug-induced rhabdomyolysis cases, normalization of CK would typically occur within several weeks. Importantly, a prior report of tirzepatide-related rhabdomyolysis described a classical acute presentation, with the patient developing significant CK elevation (CK=57,014 at onset) and demonstrating rapid improvement within days following medication discontinuation and supportive hydration, without immunosuppressive therapy.

In accordance with the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, our case demonstrates a probable association between semaglutide administration and IMNM. This is based on an appropriate temporal correlation, the unlikelihood that IMNM resulted from alternative diseases or medications, and a clinically plausible improvement following discontinuation of semaglutide. Re-exposure would not be ethical due to the severity of the adverse event attributed to semaglutide. Nonetheless, we recognize that definitive causality cannot be established in this instance. With semaglutide usage rapidly increasing, this case report seeks to alert clinicians to this possible complication and promote heightened pharmacovigilance. Subsequent research will determine whether this relationship is causal or merely coincidental.

Furthermore, the specific factors that may have rendered our patient particularly susceptible to such a profound adverse reaction to semaglutide are still unknown. As GLP-1 receptor agonists continue to gain widespread use, investigations into potential predisposing elements will be crucial, especially if other cases temporally linked to semaglutide are observed going forward.
