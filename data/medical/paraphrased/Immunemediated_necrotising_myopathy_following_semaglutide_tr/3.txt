Could semaglutide administration play a role in triggering immune-mediated necrotising myopathy?

Brian Lam, Eleni Tiniakou, Xinhai Robert Zhang, Shervin Assassi

Abstract:

Summary: This case report presents a woman in her 60s who developed immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) following administration of semaglutide. Over four months, she experienced progressively worsening dysphagia, dysphonia, proximal muscle weakness, and elevated creatine kinase levels. These clinical manifestations emerged after a single exposure to semaglutide. Diagnostic evaluation revealed necrotising myopathy with evidence of inflammatory infiltrates on muscle biopsy. The patient was treated with immunosuppressive therapy consisting of corticosteroids and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), to which she showed improvement. Over time, she gradually regained her prior level of function, remaining well without immunosuppression for several months and refraining from further glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist use. This report documents the first known instance of IMNM potentially linked to GLP-1 receptor agonist therapy, with notable recovery following a brief course of immunosuppressive agents and IVIG.

Background:

Over the past few years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists have become widely prescribed for managing metabolic conditions such as type 2 diabetes and obesity. Despite their therapeutic benefits, these agents have been linked to adverse outcomes, notably a reduction in skeletal muscle mass. Furthermore, a recent case report has detailed an instance of rhabdomyolysis exhibiting necrotising characteristics in connection with tirzepatide, a popular GLP-1 receptor agonist. In that case, the patient experienced a marked elevation in creatine kinase (CK) levels—rising to several thousand units—while receiving the medication. Notably, the CK levels began to decline just days following cessation of the drug, supporting a diagnosis of rhabdomyolysis induced by medication.

Immune-mediated necrotising myopathy (IMNM) represents a unique category of muscle disease, hallmarked by severe weakness predominantly in proximal muscles, with histopathology revealing extensive myofibre necrosis and minimal lymphocytic infiltration. Among the autoantibodies closely linked to IMNM, anti-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-CoA reductase (HMGCR) and anti-signal recognition particle (SRP) are most notable. Notably, HMGCR autoantibody positivity demonstrates a strong relationship with prior statin therapy, a common treatment for metabolic disorders. Before the link between statins and HMGCR autoantibodies was clarified, patients exhibiting statin-induced necrotising myopathy were frequently misclassified as having toxic myopathy or polymyositis. In these instances, muscle symptoms and elevated creatine kinase levels frequently persisted even after discontinuation of statin therapy. The resolution of symptoms following immunosuppressive treatment indicated an underlying autoimmune process and facilitated the subsequent recognition of HMGCR autoantibody as a defining biomarker of IMNM. Importantly, approximately one-third of IMNM cases do not display detectable autoantibodies. This seronegativity introduces further complexity to the diagnostic process and raises the possibility that other, as yet unidentified, autoantibodies or causative factors contribute to disease pathogenesis.

Case presentation:

A woman in her sixth decade of life, whose medical background included obesity, hepatosteatosis, hypertension, hypothyroidism, and a distant episode of deep venous thrombosis/pulmonary embolism, presented with gradually worsening weakness of proximal muscles, alongside difficulties with swallowing and voice changes.

The patient had been in good health and maintained complete independence in performing daily activities. Three years before her condition worsened, she was being evaluated as an outpatient for elevated transaminases. During that period, she exhibited mildly increased levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), reaching roughly double the upper limit of normal, with ALT surpassing AST. Results for alkaline phosphatase, albumin, bilirubin, and platelet counts were unremarkable. She reported no history of alcohol consumption and was not on any medications known to cause liver toxicity. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged liver with a uniformly heightened echotexture, findings most consistent with hepatic steatosis. Screening for viral hepatitis, as well as serologies for anti-nuclear, anti-mitochondrial, anti-smooth muscle, and liver-kidney microsomal antibodies, were all negative. Histological assessment by liver biopsy demonstrated steatohepatitis, compatible with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), and a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 4/8, stage 0. She received recommendations to adopt a reduced-calorie diet and to engage in regular physical exercise.

Owing to ongoing fatty liver and obesity 2.5 years post-NAFLD diagnosis, the patient was started on semaglutide therapy. Shortly after her initial subcutaneous dose of semaglutide (0.25 mg), she developed symptoms of nausea and vomiting. These symptoms persisted, leading to hospital admission two days later for persistent nausea, vomiting, reduced oral intake, dyspnoea, acute renal insufficiency, and marked metabolic acidosis. Management included intravenous fluids and bicarbonate, as well as anti-emetic medications. She required brief transfer to the intensive care unit for non-invasive ventilatory support. Her renal function subsequently recovered with fluid administration alone, negating the need for haemofiltration or haemodialysis. Admission laboratory results indicated an elevated CK of 337 U/L. She had no other symptoms such as rash, myalgias, or dark urine. During her hospital stay, she experienced episodes of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, though troponin assays remained within normal limits. She was discharged after five days; her gastrointestinal complaints had resolved by discharge, but she remained physically deconditioned. Over the ensuing four months, she developed gradually worsening weakness. She reported no history of statin use, nor of intake of other supplements linked to IMNM such as mushrooms, red yeast rice, or any additional nutritional products. Furthermore, there was no significant family history of myopathies, nor did she report recent intense or prolonged physical exertion, alcohol use, or illicit drug consumption.

Four months following administration of her first and only semaglutide dose, her condition progressively worsened. She became unable to either stand or maintain an upright sitting position without support, and experienced several days of dysphagia involving both liquids and solids. Consequently, she sought care at an external hospital, where laboratory studies showed a CK level of 3992 U/L (reference range: 12–191 U/L) at admission. Her management included a daily regimen of 1000 mg intravenous methylprednisolone over a period of 5 days, coordinated by neurology via telemedicine consultation. Subsequently, she was transferred to a tertiary care center for further rheumatology assessment, at which point she was afebrile, maintained normal blood pressure, and appeared comfortable otherwise. On physical exam, notable muscle weakness was identified, particularly affecting the proximal muscle groups (table 1). No skin rashes, dermatologic alterations, pulmonary crackles, synovitis, edema, sicca symptoms, oral ulcers, or additional neurological deficits were observed.

After receiving pulse methylprednisolone therapy, the patient was started on oral prednisone at a daily dose of 1 mg/kg. Owing to ongoing muscle weakness and significant clinical severity, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg, divided across 4 days. Over the subsequent two weeks, her CK levels showed substantial improvement (see figure 1). She was discharged following an inpatient stay of almost 4 weeks. However, 12 days post-discharge, she was readmitted for 10 days with septic shock secondary to a pseudomonal urinary tract infection, during which her CK levels remained within normal limits.

She attended multiple outpatient follow-up visits, during which her functional abilities and general health kept improving, and her CK levels remained within the normal range. Consequently, prednisone was able to be tapered off quickly without issues. Given both the financial burden and her significant clinical progress following cessation of semaglutide, she did not require further IVIG beyond the original inpatient course.

At her most recent follow-up in the outpatient clinic, conducted 10 months following the commencement of her assessment and treatment, she had returned to her pre-illness level of functioning and had been without immunosuppressive therapy for the preceding 8 months.

Investigations:

Laboratory investigations revealed a creatine kinase (CK) level of 1237 U/L at the time of assessment, with the highest recorded value being 3992 U/L one week earlier at the referring institution. Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) levels were found to be within the reference range. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) were elevated at 191 U/L and 165 U/L, respectively, in the context of increased CK and known fatty liver disease. Autoantibody screening showed negative results for anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-Sjogren’s syndrome antibodies type A (anti-SSA), anti-Smith antibodies, anti-U1 ribonucleoprotein antibodies (anti-U1 RNP), anti-double-stranded DNA, ANCA, and anti-mitochondrial antibodies. Complement and creatinine levels were within normal limits, and no cytopenias were noted. The myositis antibody panel tested negative for SRP, HMGCR, as well as Jo-1, Pl-7, Pl-12, EJ, OJ, Mi-2, MDA-5, TIF1-y, NXP-2, and cN-1A autoantibodies. However, she demonstrated a low-titer positive result for SS-B autoantibody at 2.4 (normal range: <1.0). Inflammatory markers were elevated, with an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 22 mm/hour and C-reactive protein at 22.8 mg/L. Additionally, there was an increase in high-sensitivity troponin (HS-Troponin) to 62 pg/mL (normal range ≤53 pg/mL), as well as a markedly raised pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (proBNP) at 4358 pg/mL (normal range: 0–125 pg/mL).

Significant imaging studies consisted of MRI of both femurs, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, liver ultrasound with Doppler, high-resolution CT of the chest, and MRI of the abdomen. MRI scans of the bilateral femurs demonstrated mild, symmetric muscle edema, predominantly affecting the proximal vastus lateralis and distal quadriceps muscles, along with subcutaneous edema located on the lateral aspects of both thighs (see figure 2). Echocardiography showed a preserved estimated left ventricular ejection fraction between 65%–70%, as well as a right ventricular systolic pressure measured at 46 mm Hg. Cardiac MRI revealed mild hypokinesis in the mid-anteroseptal and anterior myocardial walls, accompanied by myocardial edema and absence of definite late gadolinium enhancement; these findings may suggest myocardial inflammation or early myocarditis, or possibly a focal stress-induced cardiomyopathy. There was no evidence of significant myocardial scar or infarction, and no notable valvular pathology. Liver ultrasound with Doppler indicated the presence of hepatosteatosis. High-resolution chest CT was unremarkable for both interstitial lung disease and lymphadenopathy. MRI of the abdomen showed an enlarged, steatotic liver without other abnormal findings and no lymphadenopathy.

Further specialized investigations included a repeat liver biopsy as well as a biopsy of the thigh muscle. The liver biopsy, carried out during the hospital stay, showed multiple cores of hepatic tissue characterized by both small and large droplet macrovesicular steatosis involving roughly 70% of the hepatic parenchyma submitted, without appreciable portal inflammation or evidence of interface hepatitis. These histopathological findings aligned with her documented hepatosteatosis, with a mild advancement of her hepatic pathology to a NAFLD Activity Score of grade 5/8, stage 1. Examination of a muscle biopsy from the right vastus lateralis revealed scattered necrotic and regenerating myofibers (figure 3A,B), and immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated positive staining for acid phosphatase (figure 3D) and cluster of Differentiation-68 (CD68)—the latter being a marker for macrophages—along with evidence of myophagocytosis (figure 4B). Furthermore, membrane attack complex immunoreactivity was detected in both necrotizing and non-necrotizing myofibers (figure 4C), indicative of an underlying inflammatory process. Infrequent CD3-positive T cells (figure 4A), as well as perivascular inflammatory infiltrates (figure 3C), were also noted. The cumulative findings on muscle histology supported a diagnosis of IMNM.

Differential diagnosis:

The patient demonstrated a rapid and pronounced adverse response to her first and only administration of semaglutide, indicating possible intolerance to the drug. She initially developed gastrointestinal disturbances and acute renal failure, likely due to hypovolaemia. Although creatine kinase (CK) levels were only mildly increased and not indicative of rhabdomyolysis at presentation, her persistent clinical deterioration in the absence of further semaglutide exposure, combined with the pattern of muscle weakness and a twelvefold escalation in CK over four months following the semaglutide dose, are indicative of IMNM probably linked to semaglutide. Additionally, the presence of histopathological findings typical of drug-induced IMNM, absence of symptoms suggestive of another underlying connective tissue disorder, and lasting improvement following both cessation of semaglutide and initial immunosuppressive therapy, further substantiate this correlation.

It is important to note that the patient exhibited transaminaemia before commencing semaglutide therapy, a finding that may occur in cases of myopathy. Nevertheless, a liver biopsy conducted three years prior to the initiation of semaglutide confirmed hepatosteatosis, and the mild increase in AST/ALT levels remained even after normalization of CK values. Therefore, it is probable that the chronic elevation in her transaminase levels stems from hepatic causes rather than from muscle degradation. Additionally, her cardiac assessment did not provide definitive evidence of ongoing myocarditis. Given the context and her swift clinical improvement, stress-induced myopathy is also a plausible consideration.

The presence of a positive SS-B antibody, despite negative results for ANA and SS-A, was unanticipated. To date, there are no documented instances of IMNM arising from isolated SS-B positivity with concurrent negative ANA and SS-A. A limitation in this case is the absence of a baseline CK measurement prior to initiating semaglutide, making it impossible to completely rule out a preexisting, subclinical autoimmune IMNM. Nonetheless, the marked 12-fold elevation in CK, together with the emergence of progressive proximal muscle weakness following semaglutide initiation, supports the notion that the episode of IMNM was linked to medication exposure. Furthermore, the patient's severe IMNM remitted following a brief regimen of corticosteroids and IVIG, in addition to discontinuation of semaglutide. Such a clinical trajectory is more characteristic of drug-induced IMNM—which frequently resolves after short-term immunosuppression and cessation of the causative agent—compared to idiopathic IMNM, which typically demands extended and intensive immunosuppressive treatment.

Treatment:

The patient received intravenous methylprednisolone at a dosage of 1000 mg daily for 5 days, after which she was transitioned to oral prednisone at 1 mg/kg per day. Owing to ongoing significant weakness and pronounced symptoms, IVIG was administered at a total dose of 2 g/kg, divided over 4 days. Multiple outpatient follow-up visits revealed steady improvement in her functional capacity and general well-being, with consistently normal CK values. As a result, prednisone tapering was accomplished successfully within a 2-month period. Additionally, given both financial factors and her continued stability after discontinuing semaglutide, no further IVIG was required following her initial hospital course.

Outcome and follow-up:

Ten months post initial assessment and commencement of therapy, she was most recently evaluated in the outpatient clinic, having returned to her baseline level of functioning and remaining free from immunosuppressive therapy for seven months. Her CK concentrations consistently stayed within normal limits after her hospital admission and course of immunosuppressive treatment. Follow-up echocardiography and cardiac MRI demonstrated resolution of the abnormalities previously noted. Additionally, there was no recurrence of her paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Discussion:

GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide are increasingly prescribed and have shown efficacy in treating metabolic conditions such as obesity and type 2 diabetes. While gastrointestinal side effects are commonly reported, these agents may also lead to reductions in muscle mass and, on rare occasions, rhabdomyolysis. In this case, the patient’s progression aligns more closely with IMNM than with rhabdomyolysis. Following a single dose of semaglutide, creatine kinase (CK) values rose twelvefold over a period of four months. This contrasts with the expected clinical course if semaglutide’s direct myotoxicity were responsible, as CK normalization typically occurs within weeks in cases of drug-induced rhabdomyolysis. For instance, in a previously published report of tirzepatide-associated rhabdomyolysis, the patient presented with a dramatic acute CK increase (CK=57 014 at admission) followed by a prompt decline in CK upon cessation of the medication and supportive hydration—achieved in a matter of days and without requiring immunosuppressive therapy.

Based on the WHO-Uppsala Monitoring Centre (UMC) Causality Categories and Criteria, semaglutide use in our patient likely has a probable causal association with IMNM. This determination is supported by the temporal proximity between drug initiation and symptom onset, the low likelihood of alternative etiologies (either from other diseases or medications), and clinical improvement upon discontinuation of semaglutide. Given the severity of the adverse event, a rechallenge was not considered ethically justifiable. Nevertheless, we recognize that a definitive causal link cannot be confirmed in this instance. With semaglutide’s widespread and increasing usage, this report intends to alert clinicians to this possible side effect and to promote vigilant drug safety monitoring. Further research will be needed to establish whether this relationship is indeed causal or merely coincidental.

The factors that may have contributed to our patient’s pronounced response to semaglutide are still undetermined. As GLP-1 receptor agonists continue to gain widespread use, investigating potential predisposing factors will be increasingly important, especially if additional cases with a similar temporal association to semaglutide administration arise.
